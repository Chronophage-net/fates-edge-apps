The Wilds, Dungeons, and Ways Between

The Ways Between

Spiritways & Veilways.

“The Ways Between do not measure miles. They measure choices. A wrong turn might lead to yesterday. A right turn might lead to a version of yourself who made a different decision – and is still alive to regret it. The mist does not hide landmarks. It hides destinations.”
— The True-Mason

What do people eat for breakfast? Nothing. There is no breakfast in the Ways Between. No morning, no hunger, only the walking. The walking is sustenance.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To forget. Children born in the Ways Between do not learn. They remember the path they took to be born – eight turns, then the ninth turn is the birth. The birth is a choice. The child does not know they chose.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their direction. A stranger walking toward you may be coming from your future. A stranger walking away may be going to your past. A stranger walking beside you may be yourself from a timeline where you did not turn left. Do not ask.

What is the most common crime? Stealing – but stealing is a paradox. A thief steals a memory from a version of you that no longer exists. The memory never belonged to you. You cannot miss it. You will miss it anyway.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Ways Between are not between anything. They are the only thing that exists. The solid ground you remember – cities, roads, hearths – is the dream. The Ways are the waking. The waking is the walking. The walking never ends.

Where do people go when they die? Deeper. The Ways have no surface, no bottom. The dead walk deeper. The deeper they walk, the less they remember. When they remember nothing, they become the path. The path is the walking. The walking is the dead.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You cannot. Apologies require memory. Memory requires a self. The Ways erase both. The only apology is to stop walking. Stopping is not an option.

What is the most important festival? There are no festivals. There is only the moment between heartbeats when you realize you have been walking for nine years and you do not remember why. That moment is the festival. Celebrate it by taking the next step.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Wayfinder is not a person. It is the road itself, dreaming of a traveler who will finally reach the end. The end does not exist. The road knows this. The road dreams anyway.

The Ways Between — Obishaal, the Realm of Dreams and Death

Quick Reference

Tagline: Every dream is a door. Every door is a judgment. The dead dream forever; the living borrow their sleep.

The Wayfinder (Elite): “I exist only in the moment of choice, the breath between one path and another. Obishaal is not a place—it is a state. To guide here is to understand that every dream leads somewhere, but only the virtuous know which somewhere is worth the waking.”

Lost Pilgrim (Commoner): “The dead don’t leave. They just change addresses. Obishaal is their new neighborhood, and the scales are balanced with virtue. If you’re lucky, you’ll only visit. If you’re not…you’ll find yourself climbing stairs that never end, and every landing offers a bargain you can’t refuse.”

Genre / Mood: Dream-death metaphysical, virtue-based ascent, temptation and fall, folk-surrealism.

Starting Location: A crossroad shrine of black glass stones, where travelers meet their reflections coming the other way. The shrine is silent except for the soft hum of the stones. A Backwards Pilgrim stands at the crossroads, facing the direction you came from. “The Thirteenth Stone speaks,” they say. “But the path chooses who arrives. What did you bury on the road here? The stones can smell it. And in Obishaal, every buried thing dreams of waking.”

The Ways Between is a transit way, the eternal crossroads through Obishaal, the element of Dreams and Death, the eighth element after Fire, Life, Air, Fortune, Earth, Fate, Water, Dreams/Death. It is the realm where the settled dead go not to oblivion but to an ideal afterlife—a dream that reflects the life they lived. A farmer dreams of endless harvest; a warrior dreams of eternal battle; a merchant dreams of scales that never tip. These dreams are real, and they are paradise or prisons.

The living may enter the Ways through thresholds of sleep, near-death, or deliberate ritual. Once inside, they find a landscape that shifts with their own psyche—but also with the weight of their choices. To ascend through the layers of Obishaal, a traveler must demonstrate virtue: honesty, courage, mercy, self-sacrifice. To fall is to yield to vice: greed, cowardice, cruelty, despair. Each layer is a test, and each test is a dream that tempts you to fail.

At the very bottom, pressed into the silt of the deepest dream, lies Ikasha, She Who Sleeps. Her sisters Inaea and Isoka imprisoned her there eons ago, for Ikasha dreamed of summoning and confronting the Clockwork Monad—a confrontation that, in their judgment, would unmake reality. Now Ikasha dreams fitfully, and her nightmares seep upward, infecting the layers above. Sometimes a traveler who falls far enough can hear her voice, offering escape in exchange for service—a service that would free her.

Between the layers drift the dead who have not yet found their final dream. They are the Unsettled, and they will trade memories, secrets, and virtues for a chance to ascend—or to pull you down with them.

What you notice first: The air tastes of copper and old keys. Your footsteps echo half a beat too late. The waystones are warm—and they pulse like hearts.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never answer a question asked by a reflection. The answer will bind you to the path you did not choose.

Regional Mood: The Dream That Remembers

Here, the dream is not a thing—it is a witness. It remembers every promise made at a deathbed, every lie told to a dying ear, every virtue you failed to keep. Obishaal does not forgive, but it does not forget either. It offers passage in exchange for truth. The longer you stay, the more the dream learns about you. By the time you reach your destination, you may find that the destination knows you better than you know yourself. Some travelers never leave. They become dream-echoes, their faces pressed into the glass of a forgotten dream, their names still warm on the lips of the next pilgrim.

On any path in the Ways Between, the ninth step you take in a straight line will land on a threshold you did not see. The wise take eight steps, then pause to look around. In Obishaal, the ninth step is always a dream of falling. Those who fall hear a voice ask, “What did you owe?” Answering costs a memory; silence costs a name.

Thin Places — Where the Veil Wears Thin

Not every threshold is a door. Some are a crack—a place where the world forgot to finish its seam. The Pereshi call them breath-holes, where the mountain’s exhalation smells of yesterday. The Tulkani knot a thread and leave it untied, marking where the Hollow’s attention wanders. The Aeler do not name them at all. They simply count to eight and leave the ninth breath unspoken.

A thin place is a location where the boundary between the waking world and the Ways Between is unusually permeable. Crossing is not a matter of ritual or intent—it is a matter of being in the wrong place at the wrong angle. A wrong step. A miscounted breath. A name spoken at the wrong hour.

When Does a Thin Place Open?

The GM may declare a thin place when:

  • A character rolls a critical failure (all 1s) on a navigation or survival test in a liminal region (Valewood, Mistlands, high passes, deep mines, ancient ruins).

  • The party camps at a crossroads, bridge, or ford during a new moon, an eclipse, or the ninth hour.

  • A character breaks a taboo (pours the ninth cup, takes the ninth step, speaks the ninth name) in a region where the Hollow is strong.

  • The GM spends 3+ Story Beats to introduce a supernatural complication, and the fiction supports a threshold crossing.

  • A player willingly seeks a thin place (rare—most who seek them never return unchanged).

Signs of a Thin Place (d6)

Roll or choose when the party enters an area that might be thin:

  1. Echoes answer wrong. Your voice returns half a heartbeat later, or in a different pitch. Sometimes the echo speaks words you did not say.

  2. Shadows drift. Your shadow moves when you stand still. Cast by a light source that isn’t there.

  3. The ninth step appears. A stair, a bridge plank, a stone—where no ninth should exist. Step on it, and the ground shifts.

  4. Bells ring without clappers. A distant chime, a temple bell, a levee alarm. The sound comes from a direction you cannot find.

  5. The air tastes of copper and old keys. The same taste Saikou Ira describes before every exorcism. The same taste the Aeler name breath of the deep.

  6. A door stands where no door stood. Gray wood, warm handle, no frame. It does not open for those who knock. It opens for those who do not.

Accidental Crossing (Mechanics)

When a character is in a thin place and the conditions align, the GM may call for a Spirit + Endurance test (DV 4–6, depending on the thinness). On a failure, the character crosses into the Ways Between—not by choice, but by accident.

  • Partial success (0 < successes < DV): The character feels the pull. They may choose to step back (mark 1 Fatigue) or let themselves cross (gain 1 Boon, but enter the Ways).

  • Miss (0 successes): The character crosses involuntarily. They arrive in a random location in the Ways Between (roll on the table in §).

  • Critical failure (all 1s): The character crosses—and something crosses with them. A Oath-Keeper, a Counting Wraith, or worse.

Once crossed, the character must navigate the Ways Between using the standard travel procedures (Chapter 46). They cannot simply “turn back”—the threshold that opened may close behind them. Finding the way home requires a balance: a story, a memory, or a name offered to the road.

Accidental Crossing Destination (d6)

When a character crosses into the Ways Between accidentally, roll to determine where they emerge:

  1. The Mist-Ford of First Doubt (Class III Procession site). Water runs uphill. Footsteps echo backwards. The dead march here at midnight.

  2. The Bone Corridor of Unfinished Business (Anchor graveyard). Shadows walk the walls but never touch the floor. A Poltergeist may be forming.

  3. The Crossroads of the Ninth Choice (Threshold nexus). All four paths lead to the same destination—but different yesterdays. A Trow sits at the center, smoking.

  4. The Promise Bridge of the Unsettled (Oath-Keeper territory). Cross with a vow, and the bridge remembers. The toll is a story you have never told.

  5. The Long Mile of Temptation (Vice’s testing ground). The road stretches differently for each traveler. A Walking Scale appears at the halfway mark.

  6. The Thirteenth Stone (Ikasha’s Seal). The path ends at a warm stone. The stone whispers. Beneath it, She Who Sleeps dreams of freedom. A Deep Drake may be coiled nearby.

Thin places are not traps. They are complications. A party that accidentally crosses into the Ways Between should not feel that they have “failed.” They have simply entered a different kind of scene—one where negotiation matters more than steel.

  • Offer a way out. The Ways Between always offer a path home. The path may demand a toll (a memory, a story, a name), but it exists.

  • Use the travel rules. Treat the Ways Between as a leg of a journey. Draw cards. Set timers. The destination is “home” or “the next threshold.”

  • Let players use their bonds. A character who calls on a bond while lost in the Ways may hear a familiar voice—or see a familiar face—guiding them. The road remembers who weighs what.

  • The ninth is always watching. In any scene set in the Ways Between, the ninth card drawn (or the ninth roll made) triggers a manifestation of the Hollow. Not a fight—a question. The players must answer truthfully, or the road takes a memory.

  • Diamonds are codified outcomes (true names/safe passages/waywisdom) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo dream-road motifs—reflections that lie, paths that judge, destinations that choose the traveler. Also, the presence of an Ace hints that Ikasha’s dream is particularly close.

Additional Features

  • Dream-Logic Navigation: Any Ace introduces a metaphysical requirement (a sacrifice, a taboo, an exchange) that must be satisfied to pass. Name it at the table; the road will enforce it.

  • Reflection Points: Face cards (J, Q, K) show alternate selves or unlived choices. Treat them as temporary NPCs or scene tags that can help, hinder, or tempt.

  • Memory Currency: Diamonds may be traded for knowledge or safe passage as if they were memories. Describe the memory paid; the path (or its keeper) pays in kind.

  • Virtue and Vice Timers: Each character in Obishaal tracks a small personal Virtue [3] and Vice [3] timer. Virtue advances when they act with honesty, courage, mercy, or self-sacrifice. Vice advances when they act with greed, cowardice, cruelty, or despair. When one timer fills, the character ascends (Virtue) to a higher layer of Obishaal or falls (Vice) to a lower one. Ascension offers a boon; falling offers a temptation (a cursed gift from the layer’s vice-spirit). The timers reset after each layer transition. Reaching the bottom (Vice filled in the deepest layer) allows Ikasha to speak directly to the character.

  • Ikasha’s Whisper (Patron Intervention): When a character falls to the lowest layer (or deliberately seeks her out), they may hear Ikasha’s voice. The Wilds

Roads, Ruins, and Weather.

“Between towns and treaties, the land breathes with patience. Old roads keep the step of armies in their dust; cairns hold the names that no page can contain. Law here is not written in books but in the marks we leave and the debts we owe to the earth – not in coin, but in the weight of a story. To travel is not to conquer the land, but to ask its leave, and the land answers not with silver but with the memory of our passage. The hollows are not holes in the world; they are the lungs of the earth, breathing deep and slow. They do not judge; they do not give. They simply are, and in their being, they teach us to be.”
— The Bravo of Silkstrand

What do people eat for breakfast? Whatever they found – berries, roots, a rabbit snared at dawn. The wilds provide. The wilds also take. The taking is breakfast for something else.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read the sky. Wilds children are taught the eight winds by taste. The ninth wind has no direction – it is the land’s own breath. The land’s breath is a warning.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their pack. Heavy pack means traveling long. Light pack means just started. No pack means a fool or a ghost. The wilds eat fools. Ghosts eat the wilds.

What is the most common crime? Forgetting – but forgetting is a survival strategy. The wilds are full of things that want to be forgotten. A traveler who forgets them may pass safely. A traveler who remembers must pay a toll – a story. The memory is the trap.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The wilds are not empty. They are full of watchers – spirits, beasts, old things with no names. They do not interfere. They wait. They remember every footprint.

Where do people go when they die? Into the land. The dead are left where they fell. The land absorbs them. The land’s memory grows longer. The dead become the land’s memory. The memory is the wilds.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You leave an offering – a coin, a button, a lock of hair. If the offering is gone by morning, you are forgiven. If it remains, you are not. If it has been moved, the land is testing you. The test is the walk. The walk is the apology.

What is the most important festival? The Turning of the Leaves (autumn). The wilds change color – red is anger, gold is warning, brown is patience, green is forgetfulness. This year the leaves are red. The land is angry. Do not ask why.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The wilds have a heart. A stone buried beneath the oldest oak. The stone is warm and beating. If it stops, the wilds die. Nothing left to remember. Nothing left to wait.

The Wilds — “Roads, Ruins, and Weather”

Quick Reference

Genre / Mood: Hexcrawl wanderlust, ruin-haunted silence, weather as character.

Starting Location: A shelter hollow carved into a fallen giant’s ribcage, moss glowing faintly under starlight. A Forager Child offers a route-song: “The Bad Ground shifts, and the weather walks behind it. Sing with me, or walk alone.”

Between towns and treaties lies the patience of the land. Old roads remember armies; cairns remember names no book keeps. In the Wilds, law thins to trail-marks and favors, and the weather keeps its own counsel. This is the country of the hexcrawl: a patchwork of hidden valleys, forgotten ruins, beast lairs, and the bones of failed settlements. No single authority rules here—instead, the land itself is the measure, and every crossing writes a new line. Walk soft, read the wind, bargain with whatever still listens, and keep one eye on the horizon. The next hex may hold water, treasure, or teeth.

What you notice first: The silence after a bird stops singing. The way a cairn seems to have shifted since you passed it last. The smell of rain on dry stone, even when the sky is clear.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never sleep in a hollow that has soot on the ceiling without adding your own to the fire. The land knows the difference between a guest and a thief.

The Wilds Timer

The land has a memory. Track it with a visible timer:

The Wilds Timer [6]: Tracks the land’s growing attention. Ticks when:

  • The party ignores a cairn offering (+1)

  • The GM spends 2+ SB on environmental complications (+1)

  • A character speaks a true name aloud in an unmarked place (+2)

  • The party takes the ninth step without pausing (+1)

At 3 segments: Navigation rolls gain +1 DV. Paths begin to loop. At 6 segments: The land manifests — roll on the Manifestation table (see below).

Regional Mood: The Land That Watches

Here, the wilds are not empty—they are patient. Every ridge has been climbed by someone who never came down; every spring has been drunk from by a hundred generations of the lost. The land remembers every footstep, every campfire, every promise whispered into the wind. It does not judge, but it does not forget. Power in the wilds is not given by crowns or charters—it is earned by reading the weather, by leaving the right offering at the right cairn, by knowing when to move and when to wait. Those who rush are swallowed; those who listen are guided. The wilds are not a destination. They are the conversation between the map and the untamed.

In any Wilds scene, the ninth cairn you pass in a day will be warm to the touch. Leave an offering—a pinch of salt, a copper coin, a single word spoken to the wind—or the path will forget you. Those who forget to pay find that the next crossing leads back to the same cairn, and the cairn has grown a new stone. The stone has their name on it.

Travel at a Glance (Fast & Loose)

A journey through the Wilds is a series of choices, not a checklist. Before each leg (a day, a stretch of road, a single hex), the party picks two priorities from Speed, Stealth, Safety, Survey. Those become your edge; the others become vulnerabilities. The GM then frames a single roll based on the terrain and the weather’s mood (see Weather Whims, below). Success means you find what you sought; a mixed result trades progress for a cost; a miss throws a complication from the Clubs table onto the path.

Weather Whims (d6, one per leg)

  1. Glass-clear – long sight, easy navigation. +1 to Survey actions.

  2. Low fog – stealth advantage, speed suffers. +1 to Stealth, –1 to Speed.

  3. Trickle rain – tracks wash, cold bites. First navigation or tracking roll at –1 die.

  4. Dry cold / heat – resources drain faster. Mark +1 supply strain at end of leg.

  5. Shifting wind – smoke and scent lie. Reroll one die on a weather-related check, but the GM may bank 1 SB.

  6. Omen sky (aurora, sun-dog, three moons) – the land is listening. The GM may ask one player to answer a single question about the party’s future.

Making Camp

When you stop, choose one boon and accept one risk:

  • Boon: heal a piece of gear; clear 1 Fatigue; scout tomorrow’s weather; share a story with a road spirit (gain a Truce Cord).

  • Risk: thin shelter (next weather hits harder); smoky fire (attracts prowlers); exposed approach (ambush more likely); hungry neighbors (locals or beasts appear).

A road spirit’s favor requires a small offering—a pinch of salt, a dropped coin, a whispered truth. Be polite.

  • The Wilds Timer should be visible to players — the land’s attention is a pressure they can feel.

  • Position Lost: You lose advantageous ground/cover/stealth due to weather shift or route blockage.

  • Foe Appears: A war-band or territorial beast arrives on scene.

  • Gear Trouble: A piece of equipment becomes Compromised/Neglected.

  • Lock/Barrier: A simple obstacle now requires a test to overcome.

Serious Trouble (3 SB)

  • Reinforcements: Additional wardens, war-bands, or prowlers arrive.

  • Key Gear Breaks: A crucial tool/weapon becomes temporarily unusable.

  • Major Twist: The situation fundamentally changes - weather turns/catastrophe strikes/quarantine declared.

  • Rail Tick: Advance a relevant campaign/front timer by 1 segment.

  • Condition Applied: Mark Fatigue 1/Harm 1/Condition appropriate to fiction.

Major Turns (4+ SB)

  • Trap Springs: A prepared danger activates with full effect.

  • Authority Arrival: Claimant chief, quartermaster, or The Stranger intervenes.

  • Scene Shift: The environment changes dramatically - weather shifts/blockage clears/catastrophe hits.

  • Patron Omen: Divine/arcane forces take notice - omen appears/blessing lost/curse manifests.

  • Narrative Pivot: The story takes an unexpected turn that reframes objectives.

Dungeons

Living Infrastructure.

“Beneath the surface, ancient places breathe with rusted lungs and dream in currents of forgotten speech. The walls do not forget a step; the air holds the echoes of words we wish we had not spoken. Dungeons are not broken things – they are machines that have learned to live. Their lungs are the vents that sigh, their veins the pipes that throb, their jaws the doors that yield without touch. Some were forged by hand; others grew like mold in the dark. They are not ancient; they are patient, and they remember every flaw in the design as if it were a feature.”
— The True-Mason

What do people eat for breakfast? Fungus bread and cave water – the fungus grows on the walls, the water drips from the ceiling. Both taste of stone. Both are all there is.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count footsteps. The eight sounds of the deep: Drip, Creak, Groan, Whisper, Scrape, Echo, Silence, and the ninth – the dungeon’s own heartbeat. The heartbeat is a door opening. The door is always behind you.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their shadow. Dungeon shadows are long, even in bright light. A shadow too short means they have been here too long. A shadow that moves independently means they have been touched by the deep. No shadow means they are not a stranger – they are the dungeon’s guest. The dungeon never lets guests leave.

What is the most common crime? Map-making – but map-making is heresy. The dungeon changes its corridors when it feels watched. A map that is accurate at dawn is a lie by dusk. The punishment is to walk the lie until it becomes true.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The dungeon is not a place. It is a creature that dreamed itself into stone. The corridors are its veins. The chambers are its organs. The prisoners are its thoughts. The thoughts are not its own.

Where do people go when they die? Into the walls. Bones are ground into mortar and used to patch the cracks. The cracks always reappear. The dead are never finished being used.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a memory – a true one, witnessed by the dungeon itself. The memory is added to the walls. The walls whisper it back when you sleep. The apology never ends.

What is the most important feature? The Turning of the Keys (whenever the dungeon decides). Locks change, prisoners move, corridors rearrange. There is no date, no warning, no end. It is happening now. You did not notice. That is the point.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The dungeon’s architect is still alive, sealed in the lowest chamber. The dungeon does not want to be alone. The architect is its only friend. The architect has not spoken in nine centuries. The dungeon does not need words.

Dungeons — Living Infrastructure

Quick Reference

Tagline: Beneath the surface, ancient places breathe with mechanical lungs and dream electric dreams. The walls remember every footfall; the air carries whispers of forgotten conversations.

The Dungeon’s Architect (Elite): “I am a Scholar-Prisoner. The walls here have a memory like old parchment—they remember every scream, every spell, every drop of blood spilled on the stones. And sometimes, when the air’s just right, they whisper it back to you. The dungeon listens. The question is whether it learns. I have been here for nine years. I have heard it learning. It started with single words. Then sentences. Now it speaks in paragraphs. Last week, it finished my thoughts before I did.”

Cavern-Crawler (Commoner): “Dungeons are not ruins—they are systems. Ventilation shafts that breathe, water channels that pulse, doors that open and close without any hand to move them. The Aeler built many of them; the Covenant sealed others; the Sidhi mined a few; and some were never built at all. They grew. Like fungus in the dark. Like a story told too many times. Like a debt that compounds.”

Genre / Mood: Survival horror underground, infrastructure as adversary, eco-horror, forgotten machinery.

System Type: Portable overlay — apply to any dungeon, ruin, or deep place. Not a fixed geography.

Dungeons are not empty holes. They are living infrastructure—semi-sentient systems that remember, learn, and hunger. Their corridors shift when you aren’t looking. Their doors open to places you did not intend. Their air tastes of copper, old keys, and the breath of something that has been waiting a very long time.

Beneath the surface, ancient places breathe with mechanical lungs. The Aeler built many of them as overflow vaults and under-roads; the Covenant sealed others to contain what should not walk; the Sidhi mined a few and left behind ghost-workings. Some were never built at all. They grew from pressure, from forgotten oaths, from the weight of unresolved debts. A dungeon is not a place. It is a process. Like a fungus in the dark. Like a story told too many times. Like a curse that compounds.

When you enter a dungeon, you are not entering a room. You are entering a system—and systems have rules. Air must be counted. Light must be rationed. Noise is the first enemy. Attention is the last. The dungeon learns your patterns and adapts. It offers shortcuts to the desperate and dead ends to the confident. It remembers your name after you leave.

What you notice first: The air tastes of copper and old keys. Your footsteps echo half a beat too late. The walls are warm—and they pulse like hearts.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never ignore a door that opens when you’re not looking. The dungeon is offering you a choice. Refuse, and the next door may not open at all. Accept, and the door may lead to a room that was always there, waiting for someone to finally walk through. The room has been waiting for nine thousand years. It is not tired of waiting.

In any dungeon, the ninth door you open (counting only doors that required conscious choice) will lead to a room that is not on your map. The room is always warm. Something inside knows your name. If you enter, you may find treasure—or become part of the dungeon’s memory. The wise open eight doors, then backtrack.

  1. Air-Sealed Chamber — A room with no obvious vent; the air is still, warm, and tastes of iron. ‘[SURVIVAL]‘ Testing Air each scene; on a miss, start Suffocation [4]. Hook: Each scene spent here requires a group Endurance test (DV 3). On failure, the GM may tick Air +1. The walls have tally marks in eights—someone counted their breaths here. They stopped at eight. The ninth breath is carved into the stone as a single line: “I ran out of chalk.”

  2. Whispering Gallery — A long corridor where every sound echoes nine times. ‘[STEALTH]‘ Any roll that produces noise ticks Noise +1; on a miss, the echo speaks a name. Hook: Stealth rolls are Desperate. A successful Crafting test (DV 4) can muffle the echoes for one scene. On a failure, the echo speaks a name from your past. That name is now in the dungeon’s memory—tick Attention +1. If you speak the name aloud, the echoes will stop for one scene, but the dungeon now knows your voice.

  3. Flooded Vault — A submerged antechamber; pressure doors block the way. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ Crossing requires a Body+Athletics test (DV 4) or lose 1 Light (wet lamp). Hook: The water is cold—colder than it should be. A successful Wits+Lore test (DV 3) reveals a current that pulls toward a grate at the bottom. If you follow the current, you find a hidden passage (reduce Distance by 1) but lose 1 Fatigue from the cold. The water tastes of salt, even far from the sea.

  4. Clockwork Junction — Gears turn in the walls; brass conduits hum. ‘[MAGIC]‘ A successful Crafting test (DV 3) can shift one segment of any dungeon timer. On a 1, start Anomaly [4]. Hook: The gears turn in a rhythm—eight ticks, then a pause. If you can match the rhythm (Wits+Performance DV 4), the junction will reveal a hidden door. If you fail, the gears will shift in the wrong direction—tick Noise +1 and start Anomaly [4]. The Anomaly is always a door that leads somewhere it shouldn’t.

  5. Spore Garden — Bioluminescent fungi carpet the floor; soft light, softer danger. ‘[HEAL]‘ Forage DV 2 (glow-spores, medicinal moss). On a miss, inhale spores → Weariness +1. Hook: The fungi pulse in a pattern—eight glows, then a pause. A successful Wits+Lore test (DV 3) identifies the pattern as a counting song. If you sing along, the spores will not harm you (ignore the Weariness penalty). If you harvest without singing, the fungi will remember your face—tick Attention +1.

  6. Bell-Line Corridor — Ropes of copper bells hang from the ceiling; any movement rings them. ‘[STEALTH]‘ Stealth rolls start Desperate. A successful Crafting test (DV 4) silences them for one scene. Hook: The bells have a pattern—eight rings, then a pause. If you ring the ninth bell, the corridor will fall silent—for one scene. But the ninth bell will also alert the dungeon’s Voice (tick Attention +2). The bells were hung by someone who counted in eights. The ninth bell is a trap.

  7. Keystone Arch — A stone doorway carved with ancient Aeler runes. ‘[AUTHORITY]‘ Present a Keystone Courtesy or spend 1 Boon to open it without rolling. Hook: The arch has a seal—break it, and the dungeon notices (tick Attention +1). If you present a Keystone Courtesy, the arch will open silently. If you try to force it, roll Body+Athletics (DV 5). On failure, the arch collapses (Harm 1 to all in Close) and ticks Noise +2. The runes spell a name—the builder’s name. If you speak it, the arch will open for you.

Mirror Hall — Walls of polished obsidian show reflections that move independently. ‘[TRUTH]‘ Any lie spoken here is answered by a reflection that speaks the truth—at cost of 1 Fatigue to the liar. Hook: The reflections are getting closer. Each time you pass a mirror, the reflection takes one step forward. If you pass nine mirrors, the reflections will step out of the glass (treat as a Cap 3 adversary). To avoid them, you must speak a truth about yourself that you have never told anyone. The reflections will listen—and step back.

  9. Pressure Vent — A narrow shaft with rhythmic blasts of hot air. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ Crossing requires timing (Wits+Lore DV 4). On a miss, take Harm 1 (burn). Hook: The vent breathes—eight blasts, then a pause. If you count the blasts and move on the ninth, you will cross safely. If you count wrong, the vent will scorch you (take Harm 1) and tick Noise +1 (your scream echoes). The vent is a lung of the dungeon—it is breathing you.

If you speak the name, the forge will accept your offering without Obligation. If you don’t, you owe the dead a story. The dead will collect.

  12. Wyrm-Bound Knight — A fallen knight bound by a geas to a sleeping dragon. ‘[OATH]‘ Will aid in a single combat if you promise to break their oath afterward. Hook: The knight’s armor is rusted, but their blade is sharp. The geas is written on their shield—eight words, then a pause. If you promise to break the oath, they will fight beside you. After the fight, they will demand you break the geas—by speaking the ninth word. The ninth word is the dragon’s name. If you speak it, the knight will be free. The dragon will wake.

  13. The Dungeon’s Voice — A whisper from the walls, speaking in your own voice. ‘[MYSTERY]‘ It offers a shortcut or a treasure in exchange for a promise. Breaking it costs a memory. Hook: The voice counts your words—eight, then a pause. If you speak the ninth word, the voice will answer. The answer is always the truth. If you make a promise to the voice, you must keep it—or lose a memory. The voice is the dungeon’s attention given form. It is always listening. It knows your name.

Clubs — Complications/Threats (Dungeon Hazards)

Environment, Attention, Betrayal, Unstable

  1. Micro-Collapse — A ceiling cracks; stone dust fills the air. ‘[DISASTER]‘ Each PC tests Body+Athletics (DV 3) or takes Harm 1. Tick Noise +1. Hook: The dust has a pattern—eight particles, then a pause. If you hold your breath through eight counts, you avoid the worst of it. If you breathe on the ninth, you cough (mark 1 Fatigue). The dust settles in a shape that looks like a door. The door is not on your map.

  2. Lantern Sputter — Your light source gasses or gutters. ‘[LIGHT]‘ Reduce Light by 1. On a miss, the lamp goes out entirely—start Stumble in Dark [4] timer. Hook: The darkness is warm. If you stand still and count to eight, you will see a faint glow from a direction you didn’t notice. The glow is a Lantern-Eye (see Bestiary). If you follow it, you may find a way out—or a way deeper. The lantern sputters because the dungeon is breathing. It is trying to extinguish you.

  3. Pocket Gas — A burst of foul air from a crack. ‘[AIR]‘ Tick Air +1. Anyone in the scene who fails an Endurance test (DV 3) marks 1 Fatigue. Hook: The gas smells of iron and old keys—the dungeon’s breath. If you light a flame, the gas will ignite (Harm 2 to everyone in Close). If you hold your breath, you avoid the worst. The gas has a rhythm—eight pulses, then a pause. The pause is when it’s safe to breathe.

  4. Echo-Lure — The dungeon mimics a voice you know, calling from a side passage. ‘[TRAP]‘ Whoever follows must test Wits+Endurance (DV 4) or be separated from the party for 1 scene. Hook: The voice is counting—eight, then a pause. If you answer on the ninth, the voice will respond. If you follow the voice, you will be led to a room that is not on your map. The room is always warm. Something inside knows your name. If you resist the lure, the voice will grow angry—tick Attention +1.

  5. Leech Bloom — A patch of carnivorous fungi awakens. ‘[COMBAT]‘ Cap 2 adversary; if ignored, ticks Supplies –1 (they eat rations). Hook: The fungi have a rhythm—eight pulses, then a pause. If you move during the pause, they will not notice you. If you move during the pulse, they will attack. The fungi are the dungeon’s teeth. They are always hungry.

  6. Silk Strand Snaps — A Lethai-ar web bridge collapses. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ Distance –1; anyone on the bridge tests Body+Athletics or takes Harm 1. Hook: The strands are warm—they were woven by the dungeon itself. If you fall, you land in a chamber that is not on your map. The chamber is full of webs. Something is watching you from the ceiling. If you cut the strands, the dungeon will notice—tick Attention +1.

  7. Oath-Magnet — A spoken promise in this zone becomes magically binding. ‘[OATH]‘ Any vow made here ticks a Promise Timer [4] that tightens on a lie. Hook: The dungeon is witness—it never forgets a promise. If you break your word, the Promise Timer will fill. When it fills, the dungeon will claim something from you—a memory, a name, or a breath. The oath-magnet is the dungeon’s way of collecting debts. It is always collecting.

  8. Drake Whisper — A distant roar echoes; the dungeon’s attention sharpens. ‘[ATTENTION]‘ Tick Attention +1. On a miss, also mark Fatigue (fear). Hook: The roar has a rhythm—eight roars, then a pause. If you count them, you can predict when the drake will pass. If you are in its path, you must hide (Stealth test DV 4) or face the drake. The drake is the dungeon’s pet. It is always hungry.

  9. Patrol Net Tightens — Sentinels or cultists adjust their routes. ‘[AUTHORITY]‘ Next Stealth roll starts at Desperate Position. Hook: The patrols move in eights—eight steps, then a pause. If you move during the pause, you can avoid them. If you are caught, you must pay a toll—a story, a breath, or a name. The patrols are the dungeon’s enforcers. They never sleep.

  10. Lose the Bell-Wire — Your guide-rope is cut; you are lost. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ Distance +2 segments; all navigation rolls suffer –1 die until you find a landmark. Hook: The rope was cut with a sharp knife—it was intentional. Someone wanted you to be lost. If you follow the cut rope, it leads to a side passage. The passage is not on your map. If you take it, you find a cache (roll on Diamonds). If you don’t, you must navigate by instinct (Wits+Lore DV 4). The rope was cut by the dungeon itself.

  11. Task Loop — The party repeats the same corridor; time loops. ‘[MAGIC]‘ Tick Weariness +1. On a miss, a character forgets why they came (lose one Clue). Hook: The loop is a counting song—eight corridors, then a pause. If you count the corridors and take the ninth turn, you will escape the loop. If you don’t, you will repeat the loop until you make the right choice. The loop is the dungeon’s way of testing your patience. It is always testing.

  12. Autonomous Spasm — A clockwork mechanism runs wild. ‘[DISASTER]‘ A random piece of gear becomes Compromised; on a critical, it explodes (Harm 1 to wielder). Hook: The mechanism ticks in eights—then it stops. If you can find the source (Wits+Crafting DV 4), you can stop it. If you can’t, it will continue to run wild—and it will tick faster. The mechanism is the dungeon’s muscle. It is always moving.

  13. Convergence — The dungeon’s Attention fills. The boss manifests immediately. ‘[BOSS]‘ Treat as a Major Turn (4+ SB); roll on the Boss Seed table. Hook: The boss has been waiting. It knows your name. It has been counting the seconds until you arrived. The Convergence is the dungeon’s answer to your presence. It is not a coincidence. It is a judgment.

Diamonds — Treasures & Discoveries

Each Diamond is a physical object with a cost and a story.

  1. Pressure Map — A leather chart marked with safe routes. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ DV –1 to navigate collapses or gas pockets for one delve. Hook: The map is drawn in blood—the guilders’ ink. It shows routes that no longer exist. If you follow it, you may find a passage that has collapsed since the map was made. If you find a new route, the map will update itself—the blood will shift to show the new path. The map is alive.

  2. Bell-Wire Spool — A coil of copper wire that rings when taut. ‘[STEALTH]‘ Lay a witness line; convert one False Order/Echo Lure into a Confusion only. Hook: The wire has a tone—eight rings, then a pause. If you use it to lay a witness line, you will know when someone crosses it. The tone will tell you who. The wire remembers every footstep.

  3. Keystone Courtesy — A carved stone token. ‘[AUTHORITY]‘ Once per leg, raise or lower a portcullis “by the book” without a roll—if you can name the builder. Hook: The builder’s name is carved on the back. If you read it aloud, the keystone will obey. If you can’t read it, the keystone will ignore you. The builder was an Aeler under-mason. They have been dead for a century. Their name is still law.

  4. Web-Token — A silk charm from a Lethai-ar warden. ‘[SOCIAL]‘ Enter parley with any faction in the dungeon from a position of safety once. Hook: The token is warm—it was woven by the dungeon itself. If you present it, the faction will listen. The web-token is a promise of safe passage. If you break the promise, the web will tighten. The token has a pattern—eight threads, then a pause. The ninth thread is a strand of the warden’s hair.

  5. Shed-Skin Writ — A parchment signed in Isoka’s rite. ‘[LAW]‘ Voids one coercive clause (contract, geas, oath) at the cost of 1 Obligation (a future favor). Hook: The writ is written on snakeskin—it’s still warm. If you use it to void a clause, the obligation will shift to you. You will owe a favor to Isoka. The writ is a way to escape a bond, but the bond is never truly broken. It is only transferred.

  6. Reed-Charm (under-river) — A water-resistant talisman. ‘[TRAVEL]‘ Treat one blackwater reach as neutral terrain for a crossing. Hook: The charm has a rhythm—eight knots, then a pause. If you wear it, the water will not harm you. The water will remember your face. The charm was made by a Sidhi sailor who never returned from the deep. The water is still waiting for them.

  7. Cinder Pearl — A drake’s tooth still warm. ‘[MAGIC]‘ Add IGNITE to one small action (e.g., light a torch, spark a fuse) once. Hook: The tooth is warm—it’s still growing. If you use it, it will grow colder. When it goes cold, it will crumble. The drake will know who took it. Tick Attention +1.

  8. Gleam Reed — A phosphorescent stem. ‘[LIGHT]‘ Remove one Root/Slow effect; also restores 1 Light segment. Hook: The reed hums—eight notes, then a pause. If you break it, the light will last for one scene. The reed is the dungeon’s way of saying: “You are not alone.”

  9. Cold Scale — A frost-wyrm scale. ‘[WARD]‘ Ignore one chill or terror effect for a scene; then crumbles. Hook: The scale is cold—it never warms. If you use it, the cold will protect you from one fear. The wyrm will know who took its scale. Tick Attention +1.

  10. Proof-Mote — A floating mote of amber light. ‘[TRUTH]‘ Ask the GM: “What here is misfiled?” The answer is a clue about a hidden treasure or trap. Hook: The mote is warm—it knows you. If you ask it a question, it will answer in the voice of the dungeon. The answer is always true—but it is always a riddle. The mote is a fragment of the dungeon’s memory.

  11. Scale Draught — A vial of diluted wyrm blood. ‘[FEAR]‘ Resist fear in a tribunal or horror scene; after use, tick Attention +1 (the wyrm notices). Hook: The draught is warm—it pulses with the wyrm’s heartbeat. If you drink it, you will feel no fear for one scene. The wyrm will notice your courage. Tick Attention +1.

  12. Rime Crown — A circlet of frost-shaped bone. ‘[WARD] [COLD]‘ Once per session, gain COLD and WARD for a scene; then mark 1 Fatigue. Hook: The crown is cold—it remembers the wyrm’s last breath. If you wear it, the cold will protect you. The crown will drain your warmth—mark 1 Fatigue at the end of the scene. The crown was made from the bones of a frost-wyrm. The wyrm is still dreaming.

  13. The Egg That Will Not Hatch — A cold, glass-smooth egg containing an idea. ‘[LEGEND]‘ Whisper a question; the egg answers in dreams. It will only hatch when someone dies willingly for it. Hook: The egg is warm—it’s counting. If you whisper a question, it will answer in your dreams. The answer is always a riddle. The egg will only hatch when someone dies willingly for it. Until then, it will wait. The egg is the dungeon’s last secret.

Dungeon Mechanics (Visible Tracks)

Every delve tracks four resources. Keep them visible.

  • Air [6] – Stale pockets, smoke, gas leaks. When Air ≥ 4, all physical actions start –1 Position unless you have a bellows or mask. At Air = 0, suffocation begins—each PC tests Endurance (DV 4) or takes Harm 1.

  • Light [6] – Lamps, glow-silk, bioluminescence. At Light = 0, all actions are Desperate; wandering monsters find you automatically. If Light ≤ 2, all actions suffer -1 die from shadows and uncertainty.

  • Noise [4] – Clatter, echoes, spells. When Noise = 4, a patrol or predator arrives (GM chooses). Each point of Noise above 2 gives +1 die to any creature trying to track you.

  • Attention [6] – The dungeon’s focus. When Attention = 6, the boss manifests or a catastrophic trap triggers. Reset to 0 after. At Attention ≥ 3, the dungeon begins to adapt—doors that were open are now closed, paths that were clear are now blocked.

Intent Dial (per zone)

Choose one before entering a new zone:

  • Stealth – Low Noise, slower. +1 Position to Hide Sign, but Distance advances slower.

  • Speed – Tick Noise to push distance; invites mishaps.

  • Survey – Read the stone/web/flow. On a hit, reduce Air or Light by 1.

  • Communion – Parley with place-spirits. Risky boons (a clue, a shortcut) but may tick Attention.

Three-Timer Rule

At most three active timers in a scene. The GM selects which of Air/Light/Noise/Attention are active. Long-term timers (Hoard’s Attention, Promise Timer) are tracked between sessions.

Boss Seeds (Three-Phase Foes)

Roll d6 or choose a boss that fits the dungeon’s heart.

  1. The Hoard-Wyrm (Crystalline Drake) — Phases: Greed’s Gleam → Scale Horde → Awaken the Hoard. Cracks: feed it a cursed treasure, speak a true regret, or shatter its heart-crystal with a memory of generosity.

  2. The Unburnt Cult (Isoka Fanatics) — Phases: Shedding Rite → Trial of Venom → The Fiery Dawn. Cracks: refuse the cup three times, expose a false priest, or force a Bowl (fairness) before a Fang (verdict).

  3. The Deep Drake (Pressure Below) — Phases: Whisper → Knock → Invitation → Collection. Cracks: offer a memory of sunlight, speak a true regret, or allow a willing sacrifice to walk into the deep in your place.

  4. The Iron Avenger (Aeler Feud-Keeper) — Phases: Blood Ledger → Toll Demanded → Reckoning. Cracks: pay the wergild in public works, expose a false claimant, or complete the feud with a duel of witnesses (not blades).

  • Knot the Bell-Wire: Tie a single knot in a guide rope. Cancel Echo-Lure or take –1 die on your next navigation roll (your choice). The knot is a promise to the dungeon. If you break it, the dungeon will know.

SB Menu (Dungeons)

Minor (1 SB)

  • Exposure: A patrol or warden notices you.

  • Noise: Your footsteps echo; a nearby denizen stirs.

  • Trace: Scratched stone or spent lamp oil marks your route.

  • Delay: A door jams, a ladder breaks—lose time.

  • Air Strain: Tick Air +1 (stale pocket).

Moderate (2 SB)

  • Alarm Raised: A sentinel sounds a chime; Attention +1.

  • Foe Appears: A minor drake, a fossil juror, or a cultist squad arrives.

  • Gear Trouble: A lamp sputters (Light –1) or a weapon snags.

  • Lock/Barrier: A keystone door now requires a test to open.

Serious (3 SB)

  • Reinforcements: A warband of Iron Avengers or a Hoard-Wyrd appears.

  • Key Gear Breaks: A bell-wire snaps, a pressure map tears.

  • Major Twist: The dungeon shifts—a corridor loops, a new path opens.

  • Rail Tick: Advance a campaign timer (e.g., Hoard’s Attention, Manifestation).

  • Condition Applied: Mark Fatigue 1 or Harm 1 from gas or fall.

Major (4+ SB)

  • Trap Springs: A prepared seal activates (Harm 2, Area).

  • Authority Arrival: The Dungeon’s Voice, a Herald Gargoyle, or the boss interrupts.

  • Scene Shift: A collapse seals the exit; the Heart chamber opens prematurely.

  • Patron Omen: A draconic echo or a Lethai-ar omen appears.

  • Narrative Pivot: The dungeon reveals a forgotten truth—a secret door, a hidden geas, a broken oath.

  • Ykrul Steppe: A Cold Scale counts as a Salt Allotment in a kurgan negotiation; the dead will demand a story.

In a dungeon, power flows through breath, light, and the careful management of the dungeon’s attention. The true authorities are those who understand its systems—the Aeler under-masons who know keystone law, the Lethai-ar silk-wardens who read web-law, the Mazereth pressure guilders who count every exhalation. But the dungeon itself is the final patron. It offers shortcuts, treasures, and secrets—always at a price. Accept a shortcut, and it may close behind you. Take a treasure, and the Hoard’s Attention ticks. Speak a true name, and the dungeon learns your voice.

For the GM:

  • Use the four tracks (Air, Light, Noise, Attention) as visible pressure. Let players buy relief with Strings or etiquette, not just dice.

  • Enforce the Intent Dial each zone; switching intent costs a scene or a tick of Noise.

  • Offer one Diamond that could solve the current scene—but only if the party accepts a future Price (a geas, a curse, an obligation to a faction).

  • Spend SB as environment first (collapse, sputter, echo) before monsters. The dark should be the first antagonist, the denizens the second, the boss the third.

  • Use the Boss Seeds as capstones for major delves. A boss is not a random encounter; it is the dungeon’s answer to the party’s intrusion.

The under realms are not a pit to be cleared but a commons to be negotiated. Where lights are counted and breaths are tallied, procedure is mercy—and every crossing is a promise that must hold under weight.
