The Spaces Between

“The Ways Between do not measure miles. They measure choices. A wrong turn might lead to yesterday. A right turn might lead to a version of yourself who made a different decision – and is still alive to regret it.”
— The True-Mason

Ubral

The Stone Between Spears.

“Ubral is a land of upland mists, stone cairns, and clan oaths held tighter than iron. Caught between the Aelerian mountains and the lowland courts of Viterra, its hills are scarred by old raids and crowned by watch-fires. Law is written not in charters but in cattle, blood-price, and songs. A guest’s word can buy more than a sword, but one broken vow can spark a feud lasting generations.”
— Lady Isolde of the Scar

What do people eat for breakfast? Oatcakes and skyr – the oats are grown in highland clearings, the skyr is cultured in stone crocks buried in the peat. The peat remembers the old kings. The old kings are buried beneath it.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count cattle. Ubral children are taught the eight breeds of the hills. The ninth breed has no color – it belongs to the cairn-folk. The dead do not pay taxes.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their guest-token. A token given by their last host. A warm token means recently welcomed. A cold token means traveling long. No token means a spy. Spies are given a rope.

What is the most common crime? Cattle raiding – but raiding is a sport. A stolen cow is a stolen child. The return is a negotiation. The negotiation is a feud. The feud is a song.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Lady of Tor is not the ruler. The cairns rule. The cairns vote at each full moon. Their votes are carried by the wind. The wind speaks to the hearth-aunts. The clan obeys.

Where do people go when they die? To the cairns. Their bones are added to the cairns. The cairns grow taller each year. The oldest cairn has no name. It remembers the first clan.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a wergild – payment in cattle. The cattle are driven to the cairn. If they graze peacefully, you are forgiven. If they scatter, you are not. If they refuse to enter the covenant’s shadow, your claim is blood.

What is the most important festival? The Gathering of the Clans (autumn). The clans meet at the Great Cairn. The cattle are counted. The songs are sung. The feuds are settled. The dead are named. The hills remember.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Great Cairn is a prison – the tomb of a Vritann king who tried to conquer Ubral and failed. He was buried alive. His ghost votes with the cairns. He votes for war. The cairns have not yet agreed.

Ubral is a land of knife-edged ridges, mist-veiled glens, dark lochs, and stone cairns that mark the bones of old kings. Caught between the Aelerian mountains to the north and the lowland courts of Viterra to the south, its hills are scarred by centuries of raiding and reprisal. The clans speak a tongue thick with swallowed consonants and older grudges. They measure wealth in cattle, honor in wergild, and time in the generations since a Viterran banner last burned in the high passes. To the east, the gray waters of the Dolmis Sea crash against rocky shores, where fishing villages pay tribute to whichever clan controls the nearest pass—and sometimes to a different one each season.

Exiled Aeler—dwarves who could not abide the Deep Vaults’ suffocating tradition—have found refuge here, sharing their cunning with forge and toll-road, and their outcast bitterness with any clan willing to listen. The lowland lords of Viterra claim suzerainty over Ubral; the clans call those claims “ink on wet stone.” Every pass holds a watch-fire, every ford a memory of a battle, every hearth a knife waiting for a guest who stays too long.

The clans do not fight for land. They fight for honor—a geometry of obligation that stretches back centuries. An insult is not forgiven; it is weighed. A wound is not healed; it is counted against a blood-price. Every death opens a new angle in the feud, every reconciliation closes one—but the shape is never forgotten. The Aeler outcasts, with their breath-counters and bell-codes, understand this geometry better than most. They call it Kon’reh: the game of exits and obligations, where the ninth move is never made.

The old folk still speak of the sluagh—the host of the unforgiven dead that flies through the night sky, snatching souls who have no cairn to call their own. They whisper of the baobhan sith, the blood-drinking woman who dances with travelers before draining them dry, and the each uisge, the water horse that lures the unwary onto its back and drags them into the loch. The clans leave offerings of iron nails at fords to ward off the kelpies, and they never cut the ninth stalk of heather for fear of waking the uruisg, the wild spirit of the glen.

What you notice first: The smell of peat smoke and wet granite. The constant lowing of cattle on distant hills. The way every threshold has a knife hidden nearby—for guests, not against them. The way old men trace patterns in the dirt with sticks, arguing over angles that only they can see.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never refuse a cup from a hearth-aunt. Hospitality is a contract sealed in salt and bread. Refusal is an insult that blood must answer. And never count to nine in a cairn’s shadow. The dead are counting with you, and they stop at eight.

What Players Actually Do Here

The Ubral highlands are about honor, feud, and survival. A party in Ubral will most often:

  • Settle or escalate feuds between clans, using wergild, guest-right, and cairn votes.

  • Travel through contested passes, negotiating tolls with Aeler outcasts and avoiding reivers.

  • Deal with the dead—cairns that speak, ghosts that count, and the Hollow’s attention.

  • Choose between honor and pragmatism, with consequences that echo through generations.

Key hooks:

  • The Wolf Road wakes. Viterran tax men are asking which passes you used. Which clan will you offend?

  • A Mac Tern groom cannot pay the wergild for an O’Creagh bride. The bride has fled to the Pass of Ashes.

  • A cairn on Three-Fires Ridge is weeping water that tastes of salt. The dead are agitated.

In Ubral, the ninth stone in any cairn is always warm. Touch it, and you will remember an oath you never swore. Those who touch the ninth stone three times are said to become a cairn themselves—silent, still, and waiting for the next hand. The Aeler outcasts say the ninth stone is a door to the Deep Vaults they fled. They do not touch it. They count to eight and leave.

The ninth step on any path that leads to a loch is said to belong to the each uisge. Those who step on it hear the sound of hooves beneath the water and feel a cold breath on their neck. If they take a ninth step into the water, the water horse will claim them.

Regional Mood: Cairn, Tor, and the Geometry of Grudges

Here, the past is never past. Every cairn holds a voice, every burn scar on a hillside remembers a raid. Viterra has tried to conquer Ubral a dozen times; each attempt ended with supply lines cut by reivers, passes sealed by Aeler outcast engineers, and lowland knights lost in mist. The Utaran Imperium itself broke its leg on these hills and limped away. Now the clans watch the lowlands with patient eyes, and the exiled Aeler of Khaz-Vurim weigh every traveler’s honor on iron scales. A guest here is kin—until they prove otherwise. And kin here will die for you—or kill you over a stolen cow. The Lady of Tor rules by consent of the Council of Cairns, and the Council speaks for the dead as much as the living. Every nine years, a moot is called at Gray Tor to settle the great feud—a dispute between the two oldest clans that no one can remember the origin of, but everyone remembers the blood.

The clans do not write their laws. They carve them—into stone, into memory, into the bones of their enemies. A feud is not a quarrel; it is a balance, an equation that must be solved in cattle or blood. A clan chief who cannot count the weight of an insult is a chief who will not last the winter. The Aeler outcasts, with their strange obsession for eights and their fear of the ninth breath, have found kindred spirits here. The clans count in eights too—eight cattle for a wound, eight winters for a grudge, eight generations for a blood-feud. The ninth is the Hollow’s share.

Along the eastern coast, the sea cliffs drop sheer into the Dolmis. Fisherfolk in black-hulled boats speak of drowned cairns beneath the waves—ancient stones that mark the graves of clans who lost everything to the sea. Some say the Hollow rises from those waters on the ninth wave of the ninth tide. The fisherfolk do not go out on the ninth tide. They tie knots in their nets and wait. In the dark glens, the bean-sidhe (banshee) keens when a chieftain is about to die. The clans do not fear her; they listen, for her cry is the only honest herald of what is to come.

Faction Timers (Campaign Engines)

  • The Viterran Yoke [6] – Lowland crown’s creeping control. Advances when a chief accepts a Viterran title, the party enforces lowland law, or a garrison is established. Retreats when a tax collector is sent home empty (or headless) or a claim is exposed as fraud. On full: a Queen’s Justiciar arrives with a circuit court; lowland law becomes default. The cairns fall silent—the dead are watching.

  • Tor’s Hold [6] – Lady Uria’s personal authority. Advances when she wins a moot, brokers peace, or gains an ally. Retreats when she loses a feud or a client clan defects. On 0: she is deposed; civil war follows. The ninth cairn will have a new stone.

  • Ancestral Grudge [8] – The dead’s patience. Advances when oaths are broken, wergild unpaid, or cairns defiled. On 4: the dead grow restless—navigation rolls in Ubral gain +1 DV. On 8: a cairn-wight (an undead guardian of the stones) rises and demands blood. The dead do not negotiate. They count.

  • The Forge Weight [6] – Khaz-Vurim’s economic leverage. Advances when they secure toll contracts, sell iron, or gain a clan’s weight of obligation. On full: they can close a pass to enforce terms. Their iron is cold, but their memory is colder.

  • The Deep Road [8] – Aeler outcasts’ secret ambition. Advances as they survey, bribe, or blast. On 4: they find a promising route. On 8: construction begins on a dwarf under-way that would destroy several clan cairns. The mountain will remember. The party can stop it by exposing their plans, sabotaging their survey, or offering a better route.

  • Mac Tern’s Revenge [6] – Advances when Mac Tern suffers an insult or loses cattle. On 4: they call a moot to plan a raid. On 6: they launch a devastating raid. The raiders count their steps in eights and leave a single white stone at each burned stead—a promise.

  • O’Creagh’s Patience [6] – Retreats when O’Creagh is wronged. When it hits 0, they strike without warning. Their war-cry is a single word: Remember.

The Hollow’s Cairn (Legendary Site)

Hidden in the high mist, a ninth stone circle with no clan markings. The dead do not vote here; they listen. Once per arc, a character may climb the cairn and ask one question of the Hollow. The answer is always a weight.

Mechanics:

Spirit + Endurance (DV 6). Success: the GM answers truthfully (one sentence). Failure: the character gains the Condition Marked by the Hollow (the GM may spend 2 SB to have an Oath-Keeper appear). If the character counts to nine while on the cairn, the Hollow answers without being asked—and takes a memory.

Starting Hooks

  • The Wolf Road wakes: Viterran tax men are asking which passes you used. The clans are counting your steps. One clan has offered a bounty for the tax men’s heads—in cattle, not coin.

  • The Bride-Price Default: A Mac Tern groom cannot pay the wergild for an O’Creagh bride. The bride has fled to the Pass of Ashes. Retrieve her—or pay her price yourself. The groom has offered his sword. The bride’s clan wants his head.

  • The Toll-Master’s Burden: Khaz-Vurim has doubled tolls on the Steps. A clan chief suspects they are skimming to fund the Deep Road. Infiltrate the counting-house and find the hidden reckoning. The toll-master counts in eights. Do not let him reach nine.

  • The Cairn that Weeps: A shepherd reports that a cairn on Three-Fires Ridge is weeping water that tastes of salt. The dead are agitated. Find out why before the clan blames a rival. The water has a rhythm—eight drips, a pause, eight drips. Something is counting.

  • The Kelpie’s Ford: A child has gone missing near the ford of Loch Dubh. The elders say the each uisge has taken her. They offer a fortune in cattle to whoever can retrieve the child—or drive the water horse back to its dark loch.

Cairn Vote (d8)

When a cairn is asked to judge a dispute, roll 1d8:

d8 Outcome Mechanical Effect
1–2 Dead vote for war Advance the related Feud or Conflict timer by +2 segments.
3–4 Dead are divided No change, but the GM gains 1 SB (the cairn is watching).
5–6 Dead vote for peace Retreat the related timer by  − 1 segment.
7 Dead demand tribute The cairn will not judge until an offering is made (1 Supply or a story).
8 Dead vote for blood-price The losing party must pay a wergild of 3 cows (1 Supply) or the feud continues.

Guest-Right Violations (d6)

When a PC breaks guest-right (refuses a cup, insults a host, draws steel at table), roll 1d6:

d6 Consequence
1 The host demands a wergild of 1 Supply immediately.
2 The cairn nearest the hall votes against you—start a Grudge [4] timer.
3 A witness spreads the story. Gain a Black Mark ( − 1 die on all social rolls in Ubral for one session).
4 The host’s kin challenge you to a duel to first blood (Body+Melee, DV 3). The loser owes a story.
5 The hearth-stone cracks. The hall is now Desperate for all social rolls until you make an offering (1 Supply or a story).
6 The Hollow takes notice. GM gains 2 SB to spend on supernatural complications. The host’s family will remember—for generations.

  9. Gray Tor Fort — Earthen rampart, timber crown, smoky cook pits. ‘[COMMUNITY]‘ The cook pits have been burning for three hundred years. The ash is deep enough to bury a secret—and more than one Viterran scout. The fort has eight gates. The ninth is a wall.

  10. Black Broom Bog — Stepping-logs; wrong one drinks you into silence. ‘[DEATH]‘ ‘[FEAR]‘ The logs are painted with warding runes. One is unpainted—that one is the quick way. The bog exhales names of the drowned. There are eight logs. The ninth is a whisper.

  11. Bride’s Causey — Raised road to a valley kirk; ribbons hang like warnings. ‘[SOCIAL]‘ The ribbons are from wedding gowns—cut short when the groom fell in a feud. The kirk is neutral ground, but the path is not. The causey has eight spans. The ninth is a promise.

  12. Three-Fires Ridge — Watchposts that see Viterra and Vhasia both; news travels fast. ‘[WATCH]‘ The fires are layered: birch for peace, pine for raid, oak for invasion. A fourth fire—yew—means the Hollow walks. The watchmen count in eights. The ninth signal is never sent.

  13. Pass of Ashes — When snow closes here, the upland becomes an island. ‘[WEATHER]‘ ‘[DEATH]‘ The ash is from old sacrifices. The pass remembers every offering. The last sacrifice was a Viterran herald. His voice is still in the wind. The pass has eight turns. The ninth is a cairn.
    Cairn vote: The pass is watched by the ghost of the herald. Any oath sworn here is witnessed by the dead. Break it, and the cairn votes for war automatically.

Hearts — People & Factions

  2. Hill Guide — Thorn-staff and ten quiet shortcuts; the land as a map. He taps each stone before stepping. The stones talk to him. He learned the tongue from an Aeler outcast who went deaf and learned to listen with his feet. He never takes a ninth step in the same direction.

  3. Feud-Broker — Knows the weight of a life in cattle and coin; peace as trade. His record is a row of notched sticks. Each notch is a death—or a birth. He has notched both sides of the same feud for forty years. He counts in eights. The ninth notch is a lie.
    Service: He will settle any dispute for a wergild of 3 cows (or 1 Supply). If you pay, gain +1 Position in the next negotiation. If you cheat him, the cairns remember—start Broker’s Grudge [4].

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Guest-Law: Breaking hospitality stains a name worse than breaking a sword. Guest-right tokens guarantee shelter, but abuse invites a blood feud. A Viterran lord who broke guest-law at Gray Tor had his name struck from the Council’s cairn. His own clan disowned him. The cairn has eight names. His is not among them.

  • Wergild System: Every injury has a price in cattle or coin. Wergild keepers maintain reckonings that outlive the injured; weights pass to kin. The lowland courts have no equivalent; they imprison the poor and hang the desperate. The clans consider that barbaric. The clans count in eights. The ninth is the Hollow’s.

  • Dwarf Toll Roads: Khaz-Vurim gates charge for passage, but their roads are sure and swift. Pass-rings grant priority, but dwarf law is strict. The Aeler outcasts who run the tolls are exiles—they fled the Deep Vaults because they could not abide the counting. Now they count for the clans, and they are gentle with the weary. They never count to nine.

But trade is the veneer. The real power moves through older channels: the client–patron relationships that stitch the city together from the waterline up. The old patrician families — Contarini, Morosini, Dandolo — hold charters signed by emperors who are now dust. The Matron rules from her palazzo stairs with velvet and iron, but she rules because she balances these threads, not because she cuts them.

What you notice first: The clash of water against ancient stone. The constant rumble of cart wheels on bridge planks. The way every handshake lingers a heartbeat too long — as if both parties are checking for hidden blades — and the way every favor is named aloud, so witnesses will know who stands in whose shadow.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never name a rival while crossing a bridge at night. The water remembers, and the curse you send may come back wrapped around your own throat. But the deeper rule: never accept a favor without speaking its terms. An unspoken kindness is a thread that can be pulled from either end — and the other end is always held by someone who has been playing longer than you.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Measure of a Favor (Patron–Client Bond): In Silkstrand, favors are the true measure of standing. Each favor has a measure — a sense of what it cost to grant and what it will cost to call in. Favors are remembered not in scrolls but in threads — a red thread tied around the wrist of the client, a black thread around the wrist of the patron. When the favor is answered, the threads are cut. A character may start with 1d3 favor threads (minor promises owed or owed to them). The GM may award threads for significant aid. A thread can be spent to gain +1 Position on a social roll with the patron’s faction, or to demand a minor service (safe passage, a night’s shelter, a warning). Holding more than nine threads is dangerous — the ninth thread is always the Hollow’s.

    • Minor Favor (1 thread): A night’s shelter, a message delivered, a blind eye turned.

    • Significant Favor (3 threads): An introduction to a patron, an altered record, a small stake.

    • Major Favor (6 threads): A change in policy, a life spared, a charter voided.

    • Unpayable Measure (9 threads): The Ragman’s favor. You do not want to carry this. The ninth thread is never cut.

  • Patron–Client Mini-Generator (d6): When the party seeks a patron or encounters a client relationship, roll to determine the nature of the bond.

    1. Blood Bond: The patron and client share a family tie — or a family feud. The promise is old, deep, and bitter.

    2. Silk Bond: The patron holds the client’s bond in the form of a silk charter. The terms are written, but the reckoning is not.

    3. Shadow Bond: The patron is a crime lord (the Ragman, the Tide Father, the Silk Mother). The client owes a service that cannot be named aloud.

    4. Stone Bond: The patron is a patrician (Contarini, Morosini, Dandolo). The client holds a charter or a seal. The promise is public but the measure is private.

    5. Bridge Bond: The patron and client met on a bridge at night. The favor was sworn without witnesses. Only the water remembers.

    6. Ninth Bond: The patron is the Hollow. The client does not know they are bound. The ninth thread is already tied.

  • Duel Etiquette: Duels fought on bridges draw crowds eager to wager on blood. Refusing stains a name worse than losing. The winner may claim the loser’s bridge token (if any) as a trophy. A duel may also be fought to settle a favor’s measure — the loser’s bond is transferred to the winner’s patron.

  • Canal Omens: Canals run strange colors at dawn; red foretells riots, blue brings calm, black means a curse has awakened. A successful Wits + Lore test (DV 3) allows a character to interpret the omen and gain +1 die on the next roll to avoid or exploit the coming event. Green water means the Hollow is near.

  • Matron’s Seal: Any charter bearing her seal is law — but the Matron is known to rescind seals without warning, leaving promises tangled. A seal may be challenged by producing an older Matron’s seal; the older one always holds. The ninth seal is the Matron’s own — she has never used it.

  • Exchange Volatility Timer [6]: Tracks market instability. Each time a major cargo is seized, a trade route is cut, or a merchant falters, tick the timer. At 3: prices of all goods increase by 1 Supply. At 6: a panic hits — the Exchange closes for 1d3 days, and all standing agreements are voided unless witnessed by the Matron. The ninth time this timer fills in a decade, the Ragman collects.

In Silkstrand, power flows through commerce, connections, and the careful weaving of influence — but the loom belongs to the patrons, not the merchants. The Matron maintains control through a web of guilds, factors, and condottieri, each bound by charters and customs that shift like the tides. The old patrician families hold the long view — a promise recalled after twenty years is still a promise. The crime lords hold the short view — a knife in the ribs settles a score by morning.

For the GM: Patronage in Silkstrand revolves around access — to bridges, to warehouses, to the Exchange floor itself — but access is granted through trust, not purchased with silver. Rewards often take the form of tokens, seals, or safe passage that can be leveraged into greater influence, but each such reward carries a thread — a promise owed to the patron who granted it. To emphasize the layered client–patron dynamic:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (seals, tokens, charters) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided — and that carry a thread of promise to a specific patron (a Contarini seal, a Ragman’s knot, a Matron’s ribbon).

  • Let rival patrons (Contarini vs. Ragman, Morosini vs. Tide Father) issue conflicting charters, forcing players to choose whose favor matters more — and whose measure they are willing to carry.

  • Use the bridges, markets, and Exchange as arenas for social contests, where reputation and connections determine success — and where a well-timed favor can turn the tide.

  • Track favor threads as a tangible resource. Holding threads is power; holding too many is danger. The ninth thread is always the Hollow’s.

In Silkstrand, your word is your bond, and your bond determines whether you rise or drown. But your bond is also a thread, and every thread has two ends. One end is in your hand. The other is held by someone who has been playing longer than you.

The Almanac of Ordinary Days

A commoner’s view, overheard at the Silver Stall Stable.

  • What do people eat for breakfast? Curd cheese and flatbread — the cheese is from the Ykrul herds, the bread is baked in dung-fire embers. It tastes of smoke and the open sky. The poor dip it in salt; the rich dip it in honey stolen from a border hive.

  • What does a child learn before they can walk? To count horses. Midh Ahkaz children learn the eight gaits of a healthy mount: walk, trot, canter, gallop, pace, amble, rack, and the ninth — the death-sprint that only a stolen horse knows. The ninth gait is never taught; it is discovered when the rider’s life depends on it.

  • What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their braids. Ykrul warriors braid their hair with clan colors; Vilikari traders wear oiled braids with silver beads. A stranger whose braids are loose has just fought. A stranger whose braids are cut has lost a blood-price. A stranger with no braids is either a slave or a ghost. Neither stays long.

  • What is the most common crime? Grass-right forgery — but forgery is not a crime, it is a negotiation. The Oasis Compact issues branded sticks for grazing rights. A clever carver can duplicate the brand. The penalty is exile into the tall grass at dusk. The grass does not have a court of appeals.

  • What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Khatun is not the only ruler. The Serpent Cult’s high priestess, the Shedder, knows the price of every sin. Her followers gather in the sunken pit behind the Dust Market, and the venom they drink either kills or transforms. The three powers — the Khatun, the Dust Market auctioneer, and the Iron Well keeper — meet once a season. No one records what they say. The grass records it.

  • Where do people go when they die? To the grass. Bodies are carried beyond the caravan rings and left for the wolves and the wind. The grass grows tall and green where a body falls. A patch of deep violet is a sign that the dead were not properly mourned. The locals avoid those patches.

  • How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a blood-price in silver, weighed on the Bronze Court’s scales. If the scale tips in your favor, you are forgiven. If it tips against you, you owe a horse. If the scale balances, you owe a story — and the story must be true, and the true story must make the wronged party weep. If it does not, you owe a finger.

  • What is the most important festival? The Grass Moon (autumn equinox). Violet seed-heads glow under moonlight. The tribes ride in a great circle, singing the names of the dead. At midnight, the Khatun pours a cup of water onto the earth. If the water sinks, the winter will be mild. If it pools, the winter will be hungry. If it steams, the Hollow is walking. The festival ends at dawn. No one sleeps.

  • What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Oasis Compact’s original seal was not lost. It was stolen by a Vilikari factor who wanted to control water rights. The factor is still alive, living in the Foreign Quarter under a false name. The seal is hidden in a hollow horse skull buried under the Grass Ring. The skull belonged to the Khatun’s favorite stallion. The stallion did not die of old age. And beneath the Iron Well, the First Shedder waits—a serpent that has not eaten in three hundred years, feeding on the cult’s offerings.

  14. Cup of Renewal (Cult Favor) — A single sip from the Shedder’s cup. You may reroll any failed Body or Spirit roll once, but gain a permanent snake-scale mark on your palm. The mark will itch when the cult calls. [MAGIC] [DANGER]

Regional Mechanics & Lore

  • Kon’reh Arbitration: Disputes are settled on a board painted on hide. Winners gain seasonal rights. Losers yield grass or labor — or blood if they have neither left to give. The board is set up in the Grass Ring. The spectators bet on the outcome.

  • Grass Omens: The steppe flickers violet when rain is near. When the seed-heads droop at noon, a blood feud approaches. When they stand straight at midnight, the Hollow walks. No one stays outside when the seed-heads stand straight.

  • Khatun’s Braid: The chieftain’s hair holds the memory of every oath she swore. Cutting a braid voids a promise — but the severed braid becomes a curse token. The current Khatun has nine braids. She is saving the tenth for something important.

  • The Law of the Rope: Midh Ahkaz has no jail. Serious offenders are tied to a post in the Grass Ring and left for the steppe. If they survive the night, they are free — and must answer to the family of their victim. No one has survived the night in seven years.

  • The Shedding (Cult Rite): Once per month, the Serpent Cult holds the Rite of the Cup. Participants who survive the venom gain +1 die to resist poison and disease for one month, but also gain a random quirk (nightmares, a phobia of open flames, an insatiable hunger for raw meat). The GM may offer this as a bargain — but the cult always asks for something in return later.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Wind Father — The Traveler. He who reads the grass and knows the safe routes — for a share of the cargo.

    • The Scale Mother — Mykkiel. She who weighs the oath and the old score; her clerks carry knives.

    • The Grass King — Grimmir. The wild that pushes back against the ring of tents; he demands a sacrifice of horseflesh each spring.

    • The Toothless One — Malachai. A silver coin in the dust market that guarantees a fair deal — until the grass remembers.

    • The Shedder (Cult) — Isoka. She who teaches that weakness can be burned away. Her cup is poison. Her mercy is a fang.

    • Crom (Vilikari Barbarian Cult) — Unknown. A few Vilikari outcasts worship a god of uncivilized strength, called Crom. They say he sits on a mountain and does nothing, but that he laughs when you kill your enemy with your bare hands. His shrine is a pile of skulls behind a ruined bathhouse. No one knows if he exists. No one argues with his worshippers.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Bitter Root: Daughters of the Cord run a “free soup” wagon near the Dust Market. They ask no coin — but they measure your shadow. A renegade Sister hides among the horse-braid apprentices, trying to untie her cord from a blood feud.

  • The Threshold Compendium: Saikou Ira once tracked a ghoul to the Under-Arcades. He left a silver mirror in the wall. It still shows the ghoul’s face. The ghoul is still watching.

  • Malachai, the Chained Angel: A broken Kon’reh board at the Iron Well is said to compel truth. Players who touch it gain +1 die to resist lies — but mark 1 Obligation to Malachai. The board was broken by a merchant who lied about a horse. The horse was a demon.

  • The Gray Wanderer’s Grimoire: Midh Ahkaz psions are called “Wind-Readers.” They sense lies by the tremor in grass. The Khatun employs two; they never speak to each other. They communicate by writing in the dust. The dust blows away after each message.

  • Isoka’s Serpent Cult: The cult’s venom is said to be harvested from a great snake that lives in a sealed cave beneath the Iron Well. The snake is called the First Shedder. It has not eaten in three hundred years. It does not need to. It waits.

  • The Ninth: In any Midh Ahkaz scene, the ninth horse table in the Violet Bazaar sells nothing but bad advice. Spend a Boon to reroll a failed negotiation — but the advice may haunt you. The table is always empty except at midnight. At midnight, a hooded figure sits there. No one knows who.

Quick use notes

Draw until you have all four suits. Highest rank sets main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10). Diamonds are narrative permits (grazing rights, oaths, witnesses) that shift Position. If any A appears, echo grass and wind motifs — shifting alliances, old claims that ride with the seasons, the sky as witness, a body that vanishes into the tall grass by morning. The violet seed-heads of the steppe are a recurring omen: when they glow under moonlight, a spirit walks; when they shed, a caravan will be late — or never arrive. If a Serpent (S) entry appears, introduce a cult encounter, an omen of shedding, or a venomous complication.

In Midh Ahkaz, power flows through horseflesh, water rights, and the geometry of oaths. The Khatun rules by consensus, but the true authority rests with the Oasis Compact — a loose council of clan elders, Vilikari factors, and Steppe Rangers who interpret the Wind Law. When the Compact fails, the Grass Ring becomes the courtroom, and the duel is the verdict.

But a new power coils beneath the old: the Serpent Cult of Isoka. They offer a different kind of power — transformation through poison, renewal through death. The weak are shed like skin; the strong rise reborn. The cult does not seek to rule Midh Ahkaz. It seeks to own the people who will rule after the old ways die.

For the GM — Actionable Examples:

  • A player who wins a Kon’reh match earns a Grass Right (Diamond). That right could be contested by a rival clan the very next session — forcing a rematch or a chase across the prairie. The grass will be cut for the rematch. The loser’s grass will be salted.

  • A Water Share token is stolen. The thief leaves a violet seed-head as a signature — and a single snake scale. Tracking them leads to a seasonal camp where the Serpent Cult is holding a secret Rite of the Cup. The party must decide whether to interfere — or to drink.

  • The Khatun’s illness (Club) is not natural. A Vilikari factor (Heart) knows the poisoner’s name but demands a Burned Mark (J♢ Diamond) in exchange — a dangerous witness the player must then protect. The witness is a child who cannot speak. She draws in the dust. Her drawings keep showing a serpent eating its tail.

These hooks turn the generator entries into immediate playable situations.

Sword & Sorcery — The Serpent’s Favor and the Steppe’s Code
Serpent’s Favor (GM Eyes Only)

Isoka’s attention is a subtle currency. Track it secretly for each PC.

  • Gain Serpent’s Favor: Accept a boon from the cult, drink the Cup of Renewal, or roll a natural 1 on a Spirit roll in the cult’s presence. The GM may also award it when a PC shows weakness the cult can exploit.

  • Effects (the PC thinks they’re becoming legendary):

    • 0–3: Nothing yet. A strange luck, a coincidental escape.

    • 4–7: +1 die once per scene from “instinct” (the serpent’s nudge).

    • 8–12: +1 die to all rolls involving “serpent intuition” (notice, poison, deceit).

    • 13+: +2 dice, but civilized folk give you strange looks. Your shadow sometimes moves a half-step behind you.

  • Corruption Score (visible to the player): Track separately. At 6+, gain Serpent’s Senses (+1 die to Notice in the dark). At 11+, gain Scaled Skin (+1 Armor). At 16+, gain Cosmic Insight (ask one question about cosmic threats per session) — but you also hiss when you speak.

  • The Price: When the GM spends 3+ SB, Isoka may call in a favor. Refusal costs 1 Corruption.

The Steppe’s Code (Visible Timer)

The opposite of the serpent’s whisper: the hard law of the open grass.

  • Track the Code [0–6]: Advances when a PC keeps a blood-oath, pays a wergild, shows hospitality, or defies the cult openly. At 3, gain +1 Position on all parley with Ykrul and Vilikari. At 6, the sky itself will bear witness to one oath you swear — breaking it invokes Kuva’s Vengeance.

  • Erosion: The Code erodes when you break an oath, refuse a blood-price, or accept a gift from the Shedder. At 0, you are “grassless” — no clan will shelter you. The wind no longer carries your name.

Sword & Sorcery Cult Encounters

Roll d6 when the Shedder’s influence manifests:

  1. A cup of warm milk appears on your saddle. There is a scale floating in it.

  2. A child you helped now has snake-scale tattoos on her arms. She smiles with too many teeth.

  3. The Dust Market auctioneer offers you a “gift” — a silver coin that is warm to the touch. Isoka wants a name.

  4. Your horse refuses to cross a patch of violet grass. Beneath the soil, something sheds its skin.

  5. The Iron Well Keeper writes a name in ash. The ash floats toward you. It is not your name. It is the name of someone you love.

  6. A whisper in the dark says, “The Shedder invites you to dine. Bring a story worth remembering.”

Blood and Steel — Quick Duels

When a dispute is settled in the Grass Ring, use this fast procedure:

  1. Each duelist chooses a stance: Guard (defense, +1 armor this exchange), Lunge (attack, +1 die, but -1 armor), Feint (trick, test Wits vs. opponent’s Wits to gain +2 dice next exchange).

  2. Roll Body + Melee (or relevant skill). The winner claims the round.

  3. Three rounds: best of three wins the dispute. The crowd bets, the grass drinks the blood, and the dead remember who fought true.

Serpent’s Coil Set-Piece Generator (For when the cult is central)

When the party enters the Serpent Pit or a cult stronghold, roll 1d6 for the chamber’s nature:

  1. The Shedding Floor: A pit of writhing serpents. Crossing requires a Body + Athletics test (DV 4). On a failure, the serpents bite (Harm 1, and mark a Serpent’s Favor tick). On a success, you find a shed skin that can be used as a Cult Token (Diamond).

  2. The Altar of Names: A stone slab covered in carved names. A character who touches it must test Spirit + Endurance (DV 4) or have a name (a memory, a bond, a secret) pulled from them and carved into the stone. The GM gains a String on that character.

  3. The Whispering Gallery: The walls speak in the voices of those who drank the Cup and failed. They offer warnings — but each warning costs a story. The GM may offer a clue about the Shedder’s weakness in exchange for a memory.

  4. The Hatching Chamber: Dozens of eggs pulse in the warmth of a central brazier. The cult is breeding a new generation of serpents. A successful Craft or Lore test (DV 4) can sabotage the brazier, destroying the eggs and gaining 2 Serpent’s Favor (but also gaining 2 Corruption as Isoka’s anger burns through you).

  5. The Vault of Skins: Shelves of shed skins, each labelled with a name. Some are decades old, some are fresh. Reading a name grants a clue about the person who wore that skin — but the skin will remember your touch. Mark 1 Serpent’s Favor.

  6. The First Shedder’s Chamber: A sealed cave beneath the cult temple. The air is hot, and the walls pulse with a heartbeat that is not your own. The First Shedder sleeps here. If you enter, test Spirit + Endurance (DV 5) or gain 2 Corruption. If you survive, you may take one Scale of the First Shedder (Diamond, can be used to rebless the Oasis Compact or to bargain with Isoka herself).
