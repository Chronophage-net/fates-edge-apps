Of Patrons, Runes, and Invokers

“You wish to walk the road of power? Then listen well. The world is old, and older still are the voices beneath it. We call them Patrons, though they were never sworn to us. They are the tides that move unseen, the keepers of forgotten bargains, the sleepers beneath the stone and the stars. To call upon them is to dip a hand into a river that has carved mountains.”

The Nature of Patrons

Patrons are not gods, though some worship them as such. They are not beings with agency and desire, though some possess a kind of will. They are resonances—frequencies in the fabric of reality that respond when struck.

Patrons Are Resonances, Not Entities

A resonance is a frequency. When you invoke a Rite, you are not asking a Patron for power. You are striking a frequency. The power flows because that is what the frequency does. The Traveler does not choose to guide you. It is guidance. The Ninth does not decide to overflow. It is overflow.

Patrons have will but no agency. They have a direction, a tendency, a pattern of response. But they cannot choose to act or not act in a way that contradicts their nature. The Carrion King does not decide to consume decay; it is the cycle of decay and renewal. When you invoke it, you are attuning to that cycle.

Implications for play:

  • Invocation is attunement, not petition. You are not asking for help—you are aligning yourself with a principle.

  • Obligation is residual resonance. When you invoke a Rite, you are still vibrating at the Patron’s frequency. Clearing Obligation is dampening that resonance.

  • Corruption is over-attunement. You have become too resonant with a dangerous frequency to comfortably exist in ordinary reality.

  • Patron Intrusions are echoes. The Patron does not choose to intervene; the resonance continues, and the echo is the consequence.

A Patron’s Rites are not spells. They are techniques of attunement. A Rite of the Traveler does not ask the road to open; it teaches you how to walk so that the road opens naturally. A Rite of the Ninth does not beg for forbidden knowledge; it opens your mind so that the knowledge flows.

To entreat a Patron is to risk being changed. Their Rites are gifts and snares both—a thread that binds, a frequency that lingers.

Iconic Ascension: The Fate of Great Power

In Fate’s Edge, power has its price. When a being rises beyond mortal scale, its identity dissolves and reforms into something larger, older, and less free. This transformation is known as Iconic Ascension.

Icons are not individuals. They are resonances—living embodiments of desires, patterns, and long-term agendas. Their power is immense, but their agency is small. An Icon cannot freely choose its actions; its nature compels it.

Icons cannot touch the world directly. Their presence is too vast, their will too diffuse. Instead, they act through intermediaries:

  • Chosen who carry fragments of their purpose

  • Devotees who interpret their omens

  • Agents who benefit from their favor

Icons shape events not with hands, but through boons, omens, and shifts in fate granted to those who serve their desires—whether fleeting hungers or ancient ambitions woven into the world’s fabric.

To serve an Icon is to wield a living idea. Their gifts are potent, but their influence reshapes those who bear them. Their will echoes in mortal choices long after their voices fade.

Choosing a Patron

Not every Patron walks in every story. The following pages present a curated selection of powers. You are not expected to read them all before creating a character.

For a first character or a campaign that is just finding its feet, we recommend these eight Patrons. They are mechanically straightforward, thematically flexible, and welcome in almost any game:

  • Oath of Flame & Light – vows, radiance, and righteous smiting. A classic paladin or cleric.

  • The Traveler – roads, wayfinding, and safe passage. Low risk, high utility.

  • Inaea (Angel of the Spider) – mercy, connection, and the threads that bind families and communities.

  • The Witness – truth, memory, and uncomfortable revelations. Perfect for investigators or notaries.

  • Carrion King – decay, renewal, and the wisdom of endings. Good for those who like cycles of death and rebirth.

  • Ikasha (She Who Sleeps) – shadow, secrets, and latent potential. Ideal for rogues, spies, and those who walk unseen.

  • Grimmir (The Wild Speaker) – seasons, sacrifice, and the old pact between field and forest. For druids, rangers, and those who respect the wild.

  • Palinode (Queen of Encores) – performance, rapture, and the unfinished song. Perfect for bards, dancers, and anyone who lives for the encore.

If one of these speaks to you, stop here. Read its entry. Make your character. The other Patrons (in the Appendix) will still be waiting when you are ready for a second campaign.

Important: Your Game Master has the final say on which Patrons exist in their world. Some Patrons are tied to specific cultures or story arcs; others may be dormant, forgotten, or simply not part of the campaign. Always clear your Patron choice with your GM before you write it on your sheet.

Now, if you are determined to explore the full menagerie—or if your GM has already pointed you toward a particular power—read on. The road is long, and the old voices are patient.

The Carrion King — Lord of Decay and Renewal

Lore

Description: The Carrion King does not rule. He waits. He is the patience of the forest floor, the hunger of the root, the slow, inevitable turning of flesh to soil. He is not evil—he is necessary. Without decay, there is no growth. Without rot, no renewal. Without the carrion, no feast for the living.

Followers: His followers are undertakers, plague doctors, mushroom farmers, and those who tend to the dying. They understand that death is not an ending but a transformation. The body returns to the earth; the earth feeds the root; the root feeds the tree; the tree shelters the living. The Carrion King is the hinge of this great cycle, and his servants are its keepers.

Names across regions: Mistlands: The Fog-Mother's Consort, the cold breath that follows the bell-line's failure

Aeler: The Deep Mold, the fungus that grows in the dark galleries where miners do not return

Valewood: The Root-Father's Shadow, the decay beneath the green

Zakov: The Salt-Hunger, the preservation that consumes moisture and leaves only husks

Quote: Speaker: Sister Mora, Fungal-warden of the Deepwood

Text: You fear the rot. I have made peace with it. The body feeds the root; the root feeds the leaf; the leaf feeds the deer; the deer feeds the wolf; the wolf feeds the earth. You are not an ending. You are a beginning.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Deep Mold

Aeler fungal galleries. A working gallery and a mortuary gallery can share ancestral stonework without sharing their preparation. Rot-Thread keepers know which thresholds mark that distinction. A visiting enthusiast who treats every dark chamber as interchangeable may spoil a food bed or disturb a burial. The keepers’ practical care can coexist with an unnerving lack of disgust.

The Consort at the Unturned Bed

Mistland households after the fog. People call him the Fog-Mother’s Consort while preparing places where remains can be tended. They know who brought the cloth and which household still needs help. Asked about the marriage implied by the title, they redirect the visitor to the work. A companion remains beside the bed a little too long, speaking of the body as though its changes were already familiar.

The Root-Father’s Shadow

Valewood margins. His mark is worked beneath the living branch in some household carvings. Fallen wood is handled differently from standing wood, and an elder can insist on the difference without explaining either power. A child may learn to recognize a sound piece before learning why a particular log is carried past the fire and left outside.

The Bed That Gives Again

Oshiiran river districts. Some undertakers keep a small planted vessel beside the names of the dead. The family decides who receives it after the observance. Canal workers returning from foreign ports helped introduce the form, but local keepers use district earth and crops. It promises renewed life around a loss, not that the person buried has become the same plant or will return on schedule.

History of Worship

The Rot-Thread gathered practices of attending remains and returned growth. It has no secure first shrine and no authority over every funeral.

Households can remember particular keepers farther back than they can explain a regional title. Early carvings show fungus, roots, and figures lying under planted beds. The same forms acquired different meanings when carried to dry coastlands or underground galleries. A seal on a traveling chest identifies the person who transported an object more securely than the being to whom it was first offered.

Funeral workers and families displaced with their dead taught their hosts useful ways of keeping vigil and preparing ground. Mushroom growers contributed observations from their own work without adopting every mortuary practice. River districts gave planted memorial vessels a public place; Aeler households retained distinctions between chambers whose purposes an outsider might confuse. The practical differences resisted attempts to standardize the entire cult.

Its keepers undertake work others postpone, often with patience and competence. Their houses may become indispensable during a season of sickness or war. The danger is not that every undertaker secretly hates the living. Long service can make the boundary between a person and what will follow their death feel less substantial. A gentle attendant may begin preparing the next bed while its intended occupant is still asking to go home.

The Carrion King’s patient return differs from Vorthak’s acquisition of properties and Mor’iraath’s final loss. Necessity of ordinary decay does not endorse every act of the cult, make disease sacred treatment, or establish that every local funeral power is this Patron.

Leaving Time for the Living

A body can cease while its relationships remain unfinished. Practical knowledge of what happens to remains does not determine how quickly a household should mourn.

The keeper who no longer recoils from decay may mistake their composure for a condition everybody else should attain. A family requesting another vigil can then seem to be obstructing a process whose completion the keeper finds reassuring.

Some Rot-Thread houses leave the last cloth for a relative to remove. In others the keeper does everything efficiently and returns the possessions already sorted. The latter can be an immense kindness or a devastating discovery that nobody was allowed to say goodbye.

At the table.

Present a practical problem with remains, ground, or a blight and people with different needs. Let competent care be valuable. Introduce the Patron’s unsettling intimacy with decay through particular behavior, without making neglect or a needless death the only faithful choice.

Thiasos and Codex (Runekeeper Options)

Thiasos: Description: The Carrion King's Thiasos is a small creature of the dark, damp places: a carrion beetle that clicks its mandibles in a language of decomposition, a pale fungus that pulses faintly when a death is near, a maggot that pupates into a fly only when its keeper is in danger, a mole that nests in graveyards and knows the taste of every soil, or a toad that hibernates in the hollow of a dead oak and croaks the names of the recently dead.

Care: Beyond its ordinary care, this Thiasos must be allowed to consume something dead—a fallen leaf, a dead insect, a scrap of hide—each day. If that need goes unmet, the Thiasos becomes Compromised: its shell cracks and its scent turns sweet, imposing –1 die on all Medicine and Survival rolls involving decay or death until it is fed a proper corpse.

Codex: Description: A Codex sworn to the Carrion King is never a clean book. It may be a grave-soil ledger with entries written in mushroom-ink, a bundle of pressed fungi whose spores form the Rites when disturbed, a skull whose teeth are etched with runes that can only be read by candlelight, or a roll of cured leather that smells of the tannery.

Upkeep: Because decay resists being fixed in writing, the Codex requires upkeep: the Runekeeper must spend an hour each week burying it in fresh soil (or allowing it to rest on a corpse) to refresh its memory, or the Rites stored there become faint (treat as Compromised until re-interred).

Patron’s Gift (Imbuement)

Once per scene as an action, a Runekeeper of the Carrion King may touch a held item (weapon, tool, or a piece of leather) and whisper a word of the grave. For the rest of the scene, the item grants +1 die to Medicine or Survival rolls involving decay, poison, or death, and the first time each scene the bearer would suffer a Condition from disease or poison, they may ignore it (the rot accepts them as kin). After the scene, the user marks +1 Obligation as the scent of turned earth clings to them.

Cantors & Cults of the Carrion King

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of the Carrion King sings in funeral hums—low, droning melodies that mimic the vibration of flies’ wings and the slow drip of grave-water. Their voice is used to ease the passage of the dying, to calm the restless dead, or to coax a corpse to yield its secrets. The Carrion King’s Cantors are often found in plague wards, mortuaries, and the quiet rooms where the dying are tended. They do not mourn; they accompany.

The Cult of the Rot-Thread. The Carrion King’s cults gather in places where decay is visible and accepted: charnel houses, mushroom caves, the compost heaps of great cities, and the root-cellars where bodies are prepared for burial. The Rot-Thread is a fellowship of undertakers, plague doctors, mushroom farmers, and those who have learned that death is not an ending. A ritual of the Cult involves each member bringing a piece of something dead—a bone, a fallen leaf, a scrap of hide—and placing it in a central pile. They then speak aloud what they hope will grow from that decay: a new craft, a new relationship, a new understanding. The pile is left to compost; the next meeting, they dig through the soil to see what has sprouted. Their creed is written in mushroom-ink on a single, ever-rotting leaf: “The body feeds the root. The root feeds the leaf. The leaf feeds the deer. The deer feeds the wolf. The wolf feeds the earth. You are not an ending. You are a beginning.” Outsiders are welcome if they bring something dead and are willing to speak of what they hope will grow from it.

Witchcraft of the Carrion King (Lay / Mortuary)

A witch who walks with the Carrion King is a grave-wright or rot-smith—one who crafts the tools of the undertaker and the composter: a shovel that turns soil into a blessing, a needle that sews a shroud that becomes a winding-sheet for a new life, or a mortar that grinds bone into fertilizer. Their magic works through the materials of decay: a spore-dusted cloth that preserves a body for a season, a bone needle that stitches a wound without infection, or a clay pot that ferments a medicine from grave-soil. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a “death keystone” into a crypt, but a witch of the Carrion King would instead plant a mushroom cap in the mortar, so the stone remembers the weight of the dead it holds.

The Witch’s Tool: The Grave-Wright’s Shovel. A spade of black iron, its blade etched with a spiral of roots. Once per session, the witch may strike the ground with the shovel and speak a name. For the next scene, any corpse buried in that spot yields one memory of its life—a face, a name, a secret—that the witch may harvest (the GM must answer one question about the deceased’s life or death). The shovel can also be used to “bless” a grave; a corpse buried with the blessing will not rise as undead for a year and a day.

A Hedge Gift: The Compost’s Grace (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may touch a dead or dying creature and speak a word of the Carrion King’s name. The creature’s body will not decay for one day, and its soul will not linger. The witch may also use this gift to preserve a corpse for transport, allowing it to be moved without rotting for the duration.

Corruption of the Carrion King

Playstyle Notes

Description: The Carrion King's rites work with remains, soil, and the creatures that return one to the other. Give the follower practical tasks others avoid: preparing a body, tracing a blight, or deciding what can safely be reclaimed. Their growing affinity with decay can unsettle the living without making them careless of life. Intrusions should ask what must be allowed to end and who needs help accepting it.

Emphasizes: - Decay and Renewal: death as a necessary part of life

  • Carrion as Resource: using what others discard

  • The Patient Hunger: slow, inevitable consumption that nurtures rather than destroys

  • Ecological Balance: understanding that preservation without decay is stagnation

  • The Quiet Dignity of Death: tending the dying and the dead with respect

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Mistslands (Fog and Decay): The Carrion King is the patron of the bell-wardens who know that a broken line means a harvest of bodies. A bell-warden who prays to the Carrion King before a fog will find that the dead do not wander—they simply lie still and wait.

  • Aeler (The Deep Mold): In the deepest galleries of the Aeler holds, a pale fungus grows where the pressure is greatest. The Deep Mold is the Carrion King’s blessing—it consumes the dead so the living may breathe. A vent-prior who tends the Mold is honored, not feared.

  • Grimmir (The Rival): Grimmir and the Carrion King are two faces of the same coin. Grimmir demands sacrifice; the Carrion King accepts it. Their followers rarely meet, but when they do, they recognize kin. A druid of Grimmir may plant a seed in the soil of a corpse blessed by the Carrion King—and the seed will always grow.

  • The Bitter Root (Daughters of the Cord): The Daughters of the Cord and the Carrion King share an uneasy truce. Both deal in debts; both understand that death cancels all accounts. A Daughter who prays to the Carrion King may find that a dead debtor’s obligations are finally settled—though she will be asked to plant a tree in the debtor’s memory.

  • Zakov (The Salt-Hunger): In Zakov, the Carrion King is called the Salt-Hunger, the preservation that consumes moisture and leaves only husks. A pirate who prays to the Salt-Hunger before a voyage may find that their ship’s timbers do not rot—but the crew will dream of drowning each night.

Sample Adventure Seed: The Compost King

A village on the edge of the Mistlands has stopped burying its dead. They leave them in the fields, hoping the fog will take them. But the fog does not take them. The bodies swell and burst, and a strange, pale fungus grows from their mouths. The villagers believe they have offended the Carrion King. In truth, a rival cult has poisoned the soil with a substance that prevents decay. The party must find the source of the poison, cleanse the soil, and restore the cycle of decay and renewal—or the village will rot from within.

“The body that does not rot is a debt unpaid. The soil that does not feed is a wound unhealed. The Carrion King is patient, but even patience has its limits.” — Sister Mora, Fungal-warden of the Deepwood

Grimmir, the Old Man of the Forest (Primal Mystery & Seasonal Sacrifice)

Lore

Description: Grimmir is the Old Man of the Forest, the Alder King, the presence that watches from the shadow of the oldest oak, the cold breath on the back of the neck when the path grows dark. He is not kind, nor is he cruel—he is wild, and the wild does not negotiate. It demands, and it takes. His followers learn to read the language of blood on snow, the geometry of root and stone, and the weight of a heart offered at the turning of the year. Grimmir does not teach harmony; he teaches respect through fear, and fear through sacrifice.

Followers: Those who walk his path become keepers of the old ways: druids who know when a grove must be fed, rangers who leave the first kill for the soil, and hedge-folk who whisper the names of spirits not to be spoken aloud. Their magic is not the gentle coaxing of garden plants but the raw, patient hunger of the forest floor—a hunger that swallows bones and grows mushrooms in the shape of the dead.

Names across regions: Aeler: The Root-Father, a patron of deep fungus and stone-lichen whose shrines are carved into living rock

Ykrul: The Sky-Wolf, a spirit of the steppe who herds the seasons and grants omens

Aelaerem: The Thorn-Father, keeper of hedgerow magic, honored with red thread tied around roadside stones

Quote: The forest does not ask. It takes. The deer does not weep for the wolf, and the wolf does not beg the earth. Give what the season demands, or the season will take it anyway.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The Thorn-Father at an inhabited boundary

Aelaerem do not cease to enjoy a hedgerow because it requires care. Berries are gathered, children know the sheltered places, and the person tying a red thread may also be the person who brought lunch. A Thorn-Father observance can be part of the same afternoon. When the necessary work becomes terrible, that household warmth need not turn out to have been false. Someone carries it through because a neighbor would otherwise have to.

Root-Moot practices passed between villages through seasonal work and marriage. A hedge laid by one family may preserve an observance their descendants no longer perform at home. Disputes concern which household will tend it and whose account of the last winter is trusted. They do not require a visiting priest to explain why the Alder King wants what he wants.

The Root-Father below the tree line

Aeler Root-Father shrines attend fungus beds and stone-lichen as well as wooded slopes. A hold that reveres old growth below ground need not admit outsiders to its upper forest. In the Aelerian foothills, keepers have negotiated gathering rights with human households for generations; those agreements govern the people, not Grimmir. A paper allowing a tree to be cut does not make the grove approve.

The same devotion acquires a different scale in the Yundrath. A family’s grove can lie below its dwelling across a gorge, and two relatives may reach it by different routes. When a bridge fails, the nearer human settlement can become responsible for work the hold previously insisted only its own keepers could perform. Gratitude and a quarrel about authority can arrive together.

The wolf without a forest

Ykrul Sky-Wolf observances place seasonal movement where a forest village would place a boundary. The grass and the herds can be read without supposing a hidden oak behind every rite. Some camp keepers recognize Grimmir in the wolf that herds the seasons; others use the name only when speaking to outsiders and regard the comparison as incomplete.

Such identifications spread through shared winter camps and returned fosterlings. They need not demonstrate that one culture borrowed its entire religion from another. A keeper who learned a song among rivals may preserve it because it marked a season survived, while refusing to adopt the rivals’ account of who first sang it.

The old wood beside a new shrine

In Nihori, some forest shrines acknowledge Grimmir as the Elder at the Root. Their local kami remain local presences with their own standing. A grove can receive both observances without becoming an eastern province of the Alder King’s Court. Processional visitors may leave an offering in a form the resident household finds unfamiliar but recognizes as careful.

The difficult history is often in an abandoned approach. A new road has made the old offering place inconvenient; a shrine household has served travelers for generations at the replacement. When the old growth begins intruding on the maintained road, no one needs to have lied. Decide what the grove requires and what can be learned before asking the party to intervene. The villagers’ affection for their home and their fear of the wood should both survive the encounter.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Alder King

Valewood margins. A household leaves the oldest tree out of its account of what may be cut. The wood around it can be worked, argued over, and inherited; the tree is addressed differently. A newcomer who asks which lord owns it may receive a useful answer about every other tree on the slope. Nobody supplies a genealogy to make that answer easier.

The Thorn-Father

Aelaerem hedges. His sign is allowed to grow crooked. Hedge-cutting gathers neighbors who disagree about nearly everything except where the work must stop. A gap is not improved merely because a cart would pass more easily through it. When something stands beyond the hedge after dusk, the household continues the meal without asking it to identify itself.

The Root-Father

Aeler mountain and foothill communities. Small shrines stand among fungus and lichen where the living growth reaches stone. Aelerian keepers honor persistence under a sheltered slope; Tavarun households attend to roots pressing into wet masonry. Their observances answer different mountains. They do not identify every root that enters an undervault as a message from one king.

The Sky-Wolf

Ykrul seasonal gatherings. The figure follows the herds rather than a forest boundary. Hunts remember where game returned after a hard season and where it did not. This name links Grimmir to the movement of living country; it does not make him Kuva or assign him sovereignty over every steppe spirit. A hunter may honor both without placing either in the other’s household.

The Keeper Outside the Wall

Thepyrgos’s rural estates. Estate sanctuaries place a rough branch beside polished agricultural images. Learned owners compare the figure to several ancient rustic powers; workers know which strip was left uncut before the estate acquired a name. A patron’s description of picturesque wilderness can be younger than the household whose work has kept it there.

History of Worship

His cults remember boundaries and encounters more readily than a first foundation.

The Root-Moot preserves a tradition of masked gatherings beneath old trees. Its songs change with the country and often refuse a sequence that would turn them into a king’s life. Some singers say Grimmir was waiting when their people arrived. Others say he has always belonged to the place and find the distinction unnecessary. A temple catalog that gives him one birthplace has obtained a neater answer than the keepers provide.

Migrants took a branch, a mask, or a season’s refrain into new country. The observance did not always settle there unchanged. An Aeler household finding roots beneath a new threshold could recognize the Root-Father without possessing its neighbor’s Thorn-Father custom. Hunters on the steppe translated a figure encountered at the forest edge into the Sky-Wolf following the herds. These recognitions are not proof that every landscape has agreed to the same terms.

Landholders adopted the cult where an older boundary constrained a claim. Some observed it faithfully. Others offered an impressive new sanctuary while clearing the place people had actually avoided. The quarrel is not always between ignorant laborers and an enlightened protector of nature: a family may need fuel, a proprietor may need a boundary moved, and the thing in the uncut ground has not explained its interest to either.

Keep Grimmir distinct from local spirits, the Ukwe’s powers, and the Couatl. A shared concern with living country does not reduce Sekogo or every forest people to worshippers of the Alder King. Nor does a rural title turn him into a safe agricultural benefactor.

What the Clearing Leaves Outside

Grimmir’s followers live by taking from country that has other inhabitants. Respect is not a guarantee that the taking will be permitted.

It is tempting to turn him into the spokesman for whatever management plan seems best. That gives a human argument an authority the Patron has not supplied. A careful forester may still meet something their careful practice cannot placate.

The useful humility is to acknowledge a limit without pretending to understand all of it. A boundary can be observed for generations while its keeper remains unable to explain what stands on the other side.

At the table.

Put a needed resource across an old limit. Establish observable warnings and consequences, but do not require a speech in which the Alder King explains the correct policy. Negotiating with the people affected should remain meaningful even when the supernatural question remains open.

Cantors & Cults of Grimmir

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Grimmir sings the drone of green things – a low, humming chant that mimics the vibration of leaves in wind or the deep thrum of growing roots. Their voice is used to coax reluctant seeds, to calm wounded animals, and to mark the turning of the seasons in rituals that entire villages attend. A Grimmir Cantor does not seek an audience; they sing to the land itself, and the land answers in fruit, rain, or silence.

The Cult of the Root-Moot. Grimmir’s cults are neither secret nor organized – a “moot” may be a single hermit or a scattered network of rangers who meet only at the solstices. The most ancient of these, the Root-Moot, carves masks from living moss and wears them during the rite of the Turning Wheel. Each member plants a tooth (their own or a beast’s) into the root-ball of a sacred oak; if the tooth sprouts a sapling by spring, the planter is considered “heard” by the Old Man. Their creed is carved into the oldest oak in each grove: “Take only what the season gives. Give back what the season will need.” Outsiders are welcome at the solstice fires, but they must work – splitting wood, digging a drainage trench, or feeding the fire with a personal memory (which the flames accept with a green flash).

Witchcraft of Grimmir (Lay / Threshold)

A witch who walks with Grimmir is a hedgewright or path-keeper – a crafter of living boundaries. Their work may be invisible: weaving a branch to guide deer away from a village, carving a marker that tells the frost when to end, or building a root bridge that only becomes passable at low tide. This craft can look like “infrastructure” – an Aeler True-Mason might carve a stone that remembers the boundary between a mine and a sacred spring, or a Linn shipwright might season a keel with a prayer for the right wind. But the witch’s tools are never hammers; they are twine, moss, and the quiet patience of waiting for the land to answer.

The Witch’s Tool: The Knotted Staff. A staff of petrified wood, cold to the touch but warm when it touches living matter. Once per session, when used to bless a boundary marker (a post, a stone, a tree), it inscribes a minor [SEASONAL] tag into the threshold. The marker will “remember” the season of its carving for one year, granting +1 die to rolls that honor that season’s work.

A Hedge Gift: Knot of the Turning Year (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, you may tie a simple knot (in a cord, a strand of hair, or a blade of grass) and speak a phrase appropriate to the current season. The knot subtly influences the weather or local plant growth for the next hour – a gust of wind, a sudden rain, or a patch of ivy that grows just enough to provide cover. The GM adjudicates effect.

Inaea, Angel of the Spider (Webs & Patient Predation)

Lore

Description: Inaea sits in patient silence at the center of the unseen lattice. Where others see isolation, she sees threads—debt, affection, rivalry, loyalty—all binding mortals to one another. She is comforter and conspirator alike: the hearth-mother who eases grief with a warm hand, and the shadow-spinner who knots fates so they cannot slip free. Those who serve her learn to pull strands, tying allies closer and ensnaring foes until mercy or trap is inevitable. The spider does not rush. It weaves, waits, and feels the tremor of every caught fly. So too does Inaea. Her mercy is never without cost; her kindness is a thread that, once accepted, can be pulled taut at a moment of her follower's choosing. To serve her is to understand that every bond is a leash, every promise a snare, and every act of compassion a line cast into the dark.

She does not state falsehoods, and the reason is structural rather than moral: a web with a lie in it does not hold. So she tells the whole truth in a shape that carries four readings and lets the listener choose the wrong one. Mab is bound not to lie by a rule she did not write; Inaea declines to, because lying is bad engineering. Morag will tell you the price in plain words and let you refuse. Inaea will never tell you there was a price.

Followers: Among the peoples of Fate's Edge, Inaea is the patron of hearth-keepers, matchmakers, and those who bind communities. In Aelaerem, she is the Kettle-Spinner, invoked by grandmothers to keep families together. In Silkstrand, she is the Dyed Web, honored by dyers' guilds who leave a single strand of silk outside their windows on the first of spring. Wherever threads are tied—in marriage, in treaty, in the quiet forgiveness of a debt—Inaea's influence is felt.

Signs: - A spider's web woven in the shape of a promise or name

  • A thread that tightens when a debt is recalled

  • A knot that appears on a garment or cord when a bond is formed

Quotes: - "The thread that comforts is the same that binds. The knot that saves is also the snare. What you accept from my hand, you give me leave to tighten."

  • "No thread stands alone. The weakest knot still holds the strongest weight."

Regional Devotions and Histories

The Kettle-Spinner and the person who leaves

Aelaerem Kettle-Spinner observances are kept by people who know which neighbor needs company and which relative has stopped eating with the household. The help can be real. A chair is brought nearer the hearth, a task is quietly taken over, and the guest is spared having to request either. Inaea’s danger appears when the household begins treating that knowledge as authority over what the guest must do next.

Marriages carried local forms of the devotion from one village to another. A grandmother may know a welcome her own mother learned elsewhere and deny that it is foreign now. Families quarrel over whether a marriage joins households or places one inside the other’s care. A person who wants to leave can find everyone agreeing that the departure would be harmful, with nobody intending to threaten them.

The Dyed Web in Silkstrand

Dyers’ guilds leave the spring strand outside their windows, where one household’s work can be seen beside another’s. The observance once linked workshops that shared a watercourse and had to agree when particular work could be done. Masters subsequently made it a sign of membership. A widow retaining a workshop may be recognized by neighbors long before a guild agrees to admit her.

The Woven Circle’s strength lies in that interval. Its members can arrange helpers, introduce a buyer, and make an official willing to listen. A season later, someone arrives with a marriage proposal or a request for silence that no one remembers making part of the assistance. Inaea’s followers need not have invented the widow’s need. Their care and their appetite for arranging her future can be equally sincere.

The household behind the register

In Sihai, some extended families address Inaea as the Keeper of the Joined Sleeves. Her attendants preserve introductions and marriage witnesses that formal offices will need later. The practice spread through households whose relatives took postings far from home. A visiting member could arrive at a door already expected, carrying nothing more persuasive than a remembered relationship.

The imperial register recognizes names; the Weaver’s people know who will speak for them. Cooperation between the two can rescue someone omitted from a document. It can also make a household’s preferred account impossible to contradict. A servant may know the marriage never occurred as described and discover that every available witness has been helped by the same generous aunt.

The daughters beyond the household

Solara, Venara, Gaila, and Zephyria remain Inaea’s daughters. Household shrines differ in which daughter receives attention, and those choices can disclose disagreements the family will not name directly. An aunt may bring Gaila’s game into a room that has become too carefully arranged. A keeper of Solara’s practice can decline to carry every thought offered to her. Those differences do not turn the daughters into aliases of the Weaver.

Inaea chooses not to lie because a false strand weakens her work. Mab is bound against falsehood; Morag states the cost. Do not collapse those methods into interchangeable clever women. A Weaver’s dispute is strongest when the assistance was indispensable, the facts are true, and the person who received help can see the arrangement around them only after deciding to do something their helper did not plan.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Kettle-Spinner

Aelaerem kitchens. Her sign is worked into the cloth used to lift the kettle, where heat and spilled water wear it away. A neighbor mends it without being asked and remembers which households have stopped appearing at supper. The care is genuine. So is the expectation that an absent person will eventually explain where they have been.

The Dyed Web

Silkstrand’s dye houses. Pattern-books place her mark where several colors meet. Master dyers honor the hands that keep a household and workshop together, though apprentices may hear the same prayer invoked against leaving for another employer. An old piece of silk can display a vanished worker’s pattern under the name of the house that kept it.

The Keeper of the Joined Names

Sihai’s extended households. A family image receives additions when a person marries in or is taken into the household. Inaea’s care is invoked over people whom blood descent alone would exclude. The person who controls the additions can also refuse to remove a name. A welcome freely given can become an argument that there is nowhere else its recipient has a right to belong.

The Lady of the Kept Place

Vhasian noble households. A chair is reserved for an absent family member at certain meals. The gesture may sustain someone at war or display a parent’s refusal to acknowledge a departure. Servants know which kind of absence has been arranged before the guests arrive. They are rarely asked to explain it in front of the household’s chaplain.

The Weaver of Kinship

Thepyrgos’s schools of domestic observance. Teachers compare regional patterns and propose a common order behind them. Visiting keepers bring actual disagreements about marriages, apprentices, and neglected elders. Their evidence is often more awkward than the diagram. A beautifully ordered account can omit the one person who wanted a different kind of life.

History of Worship

A web of household practices whose surviving records seldom preserve the names of the first people who kept them.

Inaea’s daughters remain Solara, Venara, Gaila, and Zephyria. Household prayers give the family different emphases without acquiring permission to add every local maiden or mother to that genealogy. Some houses honor the daughters separately; others place all their signs in one domestic hanging. Disagreement over their arrangement usually concerns what a household expects love to do.

The Kettle-Spinner traveled with marriages, fostered children, servants, and people establishing homes after displacement. In Silkstrand, workshop households translated kitchen observance into the discipline of several crafts living under one roof. A migrant could recognize the gesture before recognizing the name. The new household might keep the guest’s practice and gradually insist that the guest had always belonged there.

The Woven Circle seldom needs a formal conversion. Someone is fed, a child is watched, a sick person finds their work done. The relationship acquires depth through acts worth being grateful for. Its darker histories begin when gratitude is presented as an agreement the recipient never remembers making. Those leaving may still love the people who cannot accept their departure.

Inaea’s selective truth is chosen, unlike Mab’s inability to lie. Neither a shared hostess image nor the Unbounded Guest title in another region establishes that the two are one being. Inaea arranges people into enduring relationships; a household benefiting from that arrangement may never ask who was permitted to refuse it.

The Place Kept for You

Inaea answers the fear of being left alone. Her care becomes frightening when a person discovers that being held and being kept can be the same experience.

A bond can be wanted at one time and oppressive at another. Her more absolute followers treat that change as a failure in the beloved rather than a reason to change the bond. Their remembered kindness makes the accusation persuasive.

The distinction from Mercy and Grace is practical. Mercy can tend someone who later leaves. Inaea wants the relationship itself to endure, and may make endurance more important than what either person now needs.

At the table.

Build a household with a history of real mutual care, then give one member a reason to depart. Let several people be hurt without making that hurt proof that departure is wrong. Preserve the Patron’s capacity for tender, patient coercion rather than reducing it to an openly villainous bargain.

Oath of Flame & Light (Dawn & Vows)

Lore

Description: There is a kind of person who cannot bear to be doubted, and a kind who cannot bear to have doubted themselves. The Oath takes both.

It is not a god and has never claimed to be. It is what happens when a promise is spoken aloud, in front of witnesses, in the first light — the moment the words stop belonging to you and become a thing in the world that other people are entitled to hold you to. The fire is only the visible part of it.

To swear in it is to give up the ordinary mercies: the second thought, the quiet revision, the kinder version of the story told afterward. The sworn stand where they can be seen. That is the whole discipline. The smiting, the healing, the light that finds a lie in a crowded room — all of it comes out of that, and not the other way around.

Followers: The Oath answers to many names and does not appear to mind which. It is called on most by the magic-workers of the Church of Flame and the Temple of Light, though any faith that keeps a fire keeps some part of it. What its people share is not doctrine. It is a particular restlessness — the condition of having said a thing out loud and now having to be the person who said it, every morning, in front of everyone.

Names across regions: Everflame: The Everflame — the unquenchable fire of promise, carried from shrine to shrine by wandering priests of the Church of Flame. Its censers never cool.

Aeler: Adur, the Light of Aeler — worshiped as the sun's church-deity, whose flame consecrates oaths and consumes falsehood. Dwarven templars strike their oaths into stone with a brand.

Vilikari: Adar, the Vilikari Dawnfire — invoked as a war-god of vows and vengeance, whose rising light marks those who break faith. Cavalrymen light a single arrow before battle.

Vhasia: The Watchfire of Vhasia — a saint's flame that never gutters, tended by a silent order of healers who swear to harm no one — and to let no one die alone.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The same vow across the schism

In Ecktoria the Oath stands within the Everflame as a warrior’s path. Its sworn can be posted beside a public fire whose maintenance is an act of government. A promise to defend the flame may be heard as defense of the city’s people, its institutions, or the officers administering them. A soldier does not have to be insincere to discover those purposes separating during a riot.

The Synod of Thepyrgos broke from the Everflame in 478 AR. The Temple of Light retained the Oath while teaching a different account of who could stand before its light. Its Dawn-Knights emphasize protection and are forbidden to initiate violence. Border communities have inherited shrines from before the separation. An old inscription can be acceptable to both sides while neither accepts the other’s right to read it. Families divided by the schism may recognize a grandparent’s vow in the mouth of someone they have been told is a heretic.

The healer’s sworn promise

Within the Covenant, the same Patron is approached by healers who bind care to a promise of more lawful living. They are not simply Everflame crusaders with another badge. Their clinics draw people whom no military order would recruit, and the disagreement concerns what a sick person can meaningfully promise. One keeper demands the spoken undertaking before treatment; another begins care while a relative finds someone able to hear it. The covenant remains binding where its rites make it so. The bedside argument does not abolish the cost.

Vhasian Watchfire observances place the emphasis differently again. A healing vow and a vow to refrain from harm can be honored in a household whose knight sincerely believes a punitive campaign lawful. The wound brought home from that campaign gives the household something it cannot settle by repeating the same prayer more loudly.

The Pereshi lamp and the Taharkan house

Pereshi Keepers of the Pure Flame invoke the Oath with the Witness while tending lamps and preserving texts. Daily conduct feeds the spark; no imperial officer supplies a completed salvation. The first wick a traveler lights from an eternal lamp can be the beginning of devotion without admission to a warrior order.

Taharka preserves early houses of the northern faiths on land granted rather than conquered. An Everflame delegation can arrive expecting deference and find its own older liturgy still being practiced by people who have never accepted the delegation’s revisions. Their dispute is about precedence, language, and who has cared for the house through lean years. The hosts are not a provincial echo waiting to be corrected.

Adur and Adar

The Aeler sun church’s Adur and the Vilikari Dawnfire’s Adar are established approaches to the Oath. Their resemblance gives translators useful words; it does not grant a foreign priest authority over a hold or an oak-ring. Vilikari should not be treated as displaced Ykrul simply because a fire is kindled outside.

When two sworn people invoke the same Patron for opposing obligations, a working rite does not settle which faith is the rightful institution. Put the conflict where the promises meet: a sanctuary at a border, a prisoner both sides claim to protect, or a healer unable to give the assurance a magistrate demands. The Oath makes their commitment consequential. It does not relieve them of choosing what to do.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Everflame

The Church of Flame. A wandering priest carries a coal from the last household that received them. The Church teaches that the gods are faces of the True God and can recognize another community’s sacred fire within that claim. Recognition may feel generous to a visitor and possessive to the keeper whose god has just been explained as somebody else’s face.

The Witnessing Light

The Temple of Light. Temple teachers place divine powers beneath the Light rather than treating each as its face. An oath is made visible before it is defended by doctrine. Disputations with Flame priests can begin over a shared brazier and continue through the meal afterward. Their practitioners can invoke the same Oath without resolving what either institution believes has answered.

Adur, the Light of Aeler

Aelerian and Himadri holds. Oaths are struck into stone with a brand. An Aelerian household may keep the branded piece where visitors eat; a severe Himadri community may preserve it beside the work for which the promise was made. The latter’s austerity does not make it the source of the former’s rite. Foothill Aeler carry these practices into mixed communities without requiring their neighbors to adopt the hold’s entire discipline.

Adar, the Dawnfire

Vilikari war gatherings. A single burning arrow precedes the vow. Those attending remember who named a limited aim and who promised an enemy’s destruction. The war-god interpretation is sincerely held, though an Invoker may describe the answering power as the Oath. A mounted lord cannot quietly exchange one account for the other when the promise becomes inconvenient.

The Watchfire

Vhasian houses of healing. Healers keep a flame beside the person who must not die alone. Their refusal to harm is the undertaking, not a claim that every servant of Flame is peaceful. A knight can honor the same fire before riding against a village that shelters a fugitive. Both must account for what they swore; shared light does not reconcile their purposes.

The Fire on Granted Ground

Taharkan old foundations. The oldest Everflame houses outside its homeland retain words that visiting delegations have revised away. The Pereshi Fire and Covenant also have early foundations here, on granted land, without becoming names for the Everflame. A local keeper produces the grant when a foreign superior treats the house as a dependent outpost. The district remembers who gave the land.

History of Worship

The Oath is not a personified god with a single birth narrative. Institutions disagree about the source of the fire through which it answers.

Church chronicles begin with an enduring flame carried from house to house. Temple accounts begin with a public promise whose speakers could no longer pretend it had remained private. Aeler dedication stones and Vilikari arrow customs preserve other histories. These differences are not merely decorative names for an agreed theology. Practitioners can recognize the same working while maintaining incompatible accounts of what stands behind it.

Priests carried fire across borders; soldiers brought vows home; healers adopted a night watch that continued when an army moved on. In Taharka, grants to foreign faiths created settled houses that kept their own liturgical descendants. Later reforms in the homelands therefore encountered observances that were older than the reformers’ books and supported by communities with no reason to surrender their place.

Public witness made the Oath useful to rulers, guilds, and households. It also gave listeners something to hold against them. A ceremony can become coercive when a powerful speaker dictates who must swear, who may hear, and which witnesses will be admitted afterward. Reformers disagree about whether to withdraw from such ceremonies or remain where the false claim can be challenged.

The shared invocation does not merge the Church, Temple, Pereshi Fire, and Covenant into one organization. Nor do Adur and Adar become proof that every local war or sun deity is one personal being. The distinctions matter to the people keeping the fires.

A Promise Kept in Public

The Oath binds a speaker to what others heard. It does not guarantee that the promise was wise, freely made, or compatible with another promise.

Its hardest disputes concern a faithful person who has undertaken something harmful. Breaking the vow has a cost; keeping it has victims. Calling the fire pure does not remove either fact.

Witnesses have responsibilities too. Those who demanded an impossible promise may prefer to watch its maker suffer rather than admit what they asked. A congregation can love a steadfast person and still use that steadfastness to avoid changing its own conduct.

At the table.

Place an old vow beside a present need, with identifiable witnesses and the actual words available. Preserve existing rules for breaking it. Offer room for confession, restitution, refusal, or a different undertaking without making clever wording an automatic escape from every cost.

History of Worship

Palinode was a road-cantor who swore to reconcile feuding towns. She died between their feasts with a chorus unfinished, and the refrain continued beyond her life.

The rival Feast Accounts preserve different opening speeches, different lists of gifts, and the same place where the surviving music will not close. Later companies have claimed that their founder carried the missing verse away. Some claims are commercial inventions; others have preserved music older than the company making them. Establishing that a verse is old does not establish that it can finish what Palinode began.

A refrain travels without a relic chest. Hired singers took it from civic reconciliations into work crews and festival stages, changing the breath length and response to suit new listeners. Oshiiran landing songs preserved a rhythm a conservatory edition discarded. Nihori’s companies gave the encore an explicit place beyond the formal bow. Such adaptations carried the Queen’s reach farther than any agreed account of the original towns.

People have used her songs to meet when speech alone had failed. The first shared response can be an achievement worth preserving. The Gasping Chorus becomes dangerous when it treats that achievement as something no participant may subsequently leave. Its throat-bells and exchanged fragments belong to an inner discipline, not to every worker or theatergoer who knows a favored tune.

Civic authorities have tried to acquire the Queen by acquiring her company. A permanent grant supplies rehearsal rooms and binds the annual performance to a sponsor’s celebration. The performance may then remember a reconciliation the sponsor no longer intends to honor. Whether to omit the offending verse divides singers who all need the grant to continue working.

A Refrain Outliving Its Promise

Palinode gives shared work an existence larger than its maker. The work can survive while the people making it lose the ability to stop.

An audience may love a singer without hearing the cost of the next song. A performer may interpret that appetite as the only proof that the company still belongs together. Both can prefer another encore to the conversation that should follow it.

Some companies preserve an incomplete copy beside the version they perform. Its absence acknowledges that the work has a history beyond their authority. Others secretly keep the same copy because they no longer trust themselves to recognize an ending.

At the table.

Two communities will share a feast only if a particular singer performs the old reconciliation. The singer can still produce the music but cannot hear when it stops. The party can recover witnesses, share the performance, change its occasion, or help the singer refuse. Let the reconciliation require conduct outside the song as well as a successful performance.

Cantors & Cults of Palinode

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Palinode sings in unfinished cadences — melodies that never quite resolve, leaving the listener yearning for the next note. Their voice is used to keep a crowd enthralled, to make a performance unforgettable, or to bind a troupe together with a shared, unreachable ideal. Palinode’s Cantors are often found in touring companies, their songs changing slightly each night — because the perfect version is always just out of reach.

The Cult of the Gasping Chorus. Palinode’s cults meet in theaters after closing, in rehearsal rooms, or in the wings during a performance. The innermost circle, the Gasping Chorus, has a stranger practice: each member wears a small silver bell sewn into their throat, just under the skin; the bell rings only when they are about to tell a lie, and it tolls once when they speak a truth they have never shared. A ritual involves each member performing a fragment — a verse, a step, a gesture — and then passing the “unfinished” piece to the next person to complete. The cycle never ends, and the chorus breathes as one, gasping when a phrase is left unresolved. Their creed is written in the margins of a script that is constantly being rewritten: “The art that is never finished is the art that never dies.” Outsiders are welcome to watch, but they will be handed a prop or a note and expected to join — and those who stay for the entire ritual find that they can no longer whistle a tune they once knew by heart.

Witchcraft of Palinode (Lay / Performance)

A witch who walks with Palinode is an instrument-smith or stage-weaver — one who crafts the physical vessels of rapture: a violin that plays notes the player has not yet learned, a mask that shows the audience what they most desire to see, or a stage that remembers every performance and adds its echo to the next. Their tools are never hammers; they work with tuning forks, silk cords, and scraps of unfinished sheet music. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve an acoustic keystone, but a hedge-witch of Palinode might tune a theater’s resonance by humming a specific interval into its cornerstone until the stone hums back.

The Witch’s Tool: The Conductor’s Baton of Unfinished Phrases. A slender baton carved from the wood of a stage that was never completed, tipped with a silver bell that has no clapper. Once per session, the witch may raise the baton as if to begin a performance. For the next scene, any willing ally who follows the baton’s rhythm gains +1 die to Performance or Sway rolls, and any audience member who hears the baton’s faint, bell-less ring feels a powerful urge to stay until the “final note” — which never comes. The baton can also be used to “cut off” a performance prematurely, forcing an encore (the performer must immediately reroll a failed Performance check, but the reroll is at –1 die).

A Hedge Gift: Encore’s Whisper (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may snap their fingers and whisper a single word (“again,” “more,” “stay”). For the next minute, any willing performer (including the witch) may reroll one failed Performance or Sway roll, as the audience’s desire for more bends reality slightly. The reroll is made at –1 die, and the audience will demand an encore at the end — the witch must perform again (or provide a suitable distraction) or mark 1 Fatigue.

The Traveler (Ways & Journeys)

Lore

Description: The Traveler is the eternal guide of the road, guardian of those who walk the paths between what is and what might be. Among the Fhara caravans and Kuvani traders along the Way of Silk, the Traveler is invoked at every crossroads, honored with small offerings at each waypoint, and consulted before every major journey.

Followers: The Traveler is not merely one who shows the way — they are the way, existing in the pause between steps and in the choice of which path to take when roads fork. Every journey is both physical and spiritual; to move from one place to another is to transform, and the road itself becomes a teacher.

Names across regions: Kahfagia: The Lantern-Bearer, whose light guides ships through uncharted waters

Ykrul: The Hoof-Print, the spirit of the steppe road that appears as a single arrowhead left at a crossroads

Aeler: The Ways-Stone, carved into every mile marker by True-Masons to ensure the road remembers those who walk it

Mistlands: Invoked with a single unknotted bell-rope — a sign that the fog may part for one who walks humbly

Quote: One foot in a promise, and the road will meet you halfway. But break your word to the way, and the way will break you.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The waypoint before the map

Fhara caravans and Kuvani traders keep the Traveler where a route becomes a choice. A worn stone may receive an offering from someone who cannot read the inscription added later. Waypoints have passed between caravan families through marriages, lost ventures, and the retirement of guides. A family can maintain a place for generations without claiming to own every road leading to it.

Mapmakers arrived after many of these observances. They gave common names to forks that different travelers had named for different departures. Old names persist in songs and directions; a guide may need both. The useful disagreement is whether the map describes a possible crossing or a promise that somebody still keeps the far landing open.

The bowl that carried the Road

Still Road mendicants approached the Traveler with a bowl and a willingness to leave again. Their circuits carried teachings beyond the reach of any single monastery. Alms and teaching are not treated as a purchased exchange. A householder can feed a traveler without becoming a student, and a student may have nothing to put in the bowl.

Along the Way of Silk, several former Still Road stopping places have become endowed houses. They can shelter more people and need a succession of keepers to manage the gift. Older mendicants ask whether a traveler who inherits the guest rooms has stopped walking. Householders answer that someone must remain to cook. Both may follow the Traveler, and their disagreement need not be solved by burning down a useful refuge.

Lantern-Bearer, Hoof-Print, Ways-Stone

Kahfagian Lantern-Bearer images traveled with pilots and crews. A shrine at a narrow entrance may be supported by households from more than one port, each wanting its own route named first. The sea passage is still the Traveler’s road; acknowledging Raéyn’s water does not make the two devotions identical.

Ykrul Hoof-Print observances mark a different scale of departure. An arrowhead at a fork can recall someone who found a way through a bad season. Returning fosterlings carry such memories between camps. Aeler Ways-Stones, often cut by True-Masons, preserve a route in enduring work. Their presence does not make every Aeler living beside the road a Mason. In mixed foothill settlements, a human ferryman may be the person who knows why a stone faces away from the present bridge.

A lamp without a cleric

The Little Flame’s waystation lamps spread through people helping other people arrive. Some keepers will not name the Traveler, although a Runekeeper recognizes familiar practices. Recognition is not a warrant to take possession of their lamp or establish a hierarchy over its attendants. A night of shelter can be valuable without agreement on why it was offered.

The Traveler’s existing cult name, the Unbroken Way, should not be confused with the distinct Patron of discipline and restraint. Two travelers using the same phrase may be speaking about different practices.

A changed border can make an old route unlawful while the household at its far end still expects a yearly visit. The guide knows the safe crossing, the keeper knows who needs to come through, and the people funding the waystation want it closed. Let the route’s history matter to those people. An invocation can help someone travel; it does not decide whom the keeper should admit.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Companion at the Last Fire

Fhara caravan households and the Way of Silk. Departure prayers leave a pause after the list of travelers. It belongs to the person who may join before morning. Caravan masters disagree about whether that means an expected hire or anyone who arrives in need. The pause survives in both versions, including the ones spoken by masters who lock their gates immediately afterward.

The Lantern-Bearer

Kahfagia’s roadward districts. The image has no visible face, only a lamp raised over the shoulder. Porters touch its niche before taking work beyond the walls. Wealthy travelers sponsor lamps at named stages; people who actually keep them lit may alter the route when a bridge fails. The sponsor’s inscription does not always follow the lamp.

The Hoof-Print

Ykrul encampments. An arrowhead set beside a hoof-mark is enough for a shrine. A guide shows a guest the departure route before asking where they came from. Fosterage still carries obligations between households; honoring the Traveler does not dissolve them. The hard cases concern a rider who has a good reason to leave and somebody who will be blamed for letting them go.

The Ways-Stone

Aelerian roads and eastern foothill settlements. True-Masons set his marks beside crossings, but ordinary households also keep them at roadside shelters. In the Kazurai the mark may be uncovered at the thaw; in the Wenkar it is rubbed smooth by generations pausing with their baskets. Neither practice makes every keeper a Mason or a visitor from the same mountain kingdom.

The Unknotted Road

Mistland causeways. An unknotted bell-rope hangs where a traveler can reach it without stepping off the path. Nobody ties it neatly for the keeper. Local guides are quite capable of explaining which causeway has flooded and decline to explain the rope. A visiting Lantern-Bearer who brings a replacement lamp is thanked; one who knots the rope is stopped.

History of Worship

Roadside worship whose communities disagree about whether it began with a first journey or the first person received after one.

Caravan genealogies preserve the names of guides who carried the Traveler’s observances across several languages. They do not establish a birthplace for the Patron. An old stage can outlast the city it served, and a blessing can retain the name of a destination no living speaker can locate. Keepers sometimes preserve that name precisely because they no longer know where else it could survive.

The cult spread through useful acts: a shelter kept open, a ford marked after flood, a stranger escorted to the next known fire. Fhara caravans and pilgrims along the Way of Silk carried different versions. Kahfagian donors preferred durable lamps and inscriptions. In the Mistlands, imported ceremonies were reduced to what could be done without delaying a crossing. Nobody agreed that this made the shorter observance inferior.

The Cult of the Unbroken Way gathers at crossings rather than establishing a single central sanctuary. Households may feed those gatherings or maintain a travelers’ room without claiming to house the Patron. The cult’s name must be distinguished from the separate Patron called the Unbroken Way: endurance along a chosen course and finding a passage are not interchangeable promises.

Roadkeepers argue about flight. Some shelters refuse pursuit at the threshold; others notify a guest’s kin before morning. Neither position guarantees that the next person seeking passage is innocent or that the person pursuing them is just.

The Next Place

A road offers somewhere else. It does not promise that leaving is a cure, or that arrival releases the traveler from what happened before.

The Lantern-Bearer is easiest to praise from a well-provisioned doorway. For the person who must abandon the familiar, a route is a gift with an uncertain destination. For the people who remain, the same departure may be a rescue, desertion, or the loss of their only support.

Good roadkeeping makes choice possible without pretending to make the choice for a guest. A guide who conceals the return path because departure would be best has begun deciding more than the Traveler asked.

At the table.

Give the party a passage and two people with different reasons to use it. A shrine can provide directions, witnesses, or shelter under ordinary hospitality; none of this grants an unpurchased transport power or makes a dangerous road automatically safe.

Witchcraft of the Traveler (Lay / Wayfinding)

A witch who walks with the Traveler is a map-scribe or waystone-carver — one who crafts the physical markers of the road: a compass that always points to a chosen destination, a map that shows routes that appear only when needed, or a staff that never causes the bearer to trip. Their magic works through the materials of travel: a quill that draws paths that become real by morning, a chisel that carves a symbol into a stone so that travelers who touch it feel rested, or a needle that sews a patch into a cloak that keeps the wind from shifting direction.

The Witch’s Tool: The Way Chisel. A chisel of iron, its head struck with a simple arrow. Once per session, the witch may carve a “way mark” into a stone, a tree, or a wooden post. For the next day, anyone who sees the way mark gains a perfect sense of direction to the nearest safe harbor, village, or crossroads — but only while walking. The chisel can also be used to “erase” a false trail, removing tracks from a path as if they had never been made (one exchange of effort per use).

A Hedge Gift: Road-Worn Token (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may touch a small stone, a coin, or a button that has been carried on a long journey. For the next hour, the token glows faintly when the bearer is on the correct path to their goal (as long as the goal is a physical location). The token does not reveal the path — only that the bearer has not (or has) strayed from it.

The faithful must live with knowledge they cannot comfortably forget, including truths that expose their own part in a wrong. Some become relentless accusers. Regional Devotions and Histories

The copy that an office did not order

Ecktoria’s Ink-Eye is invoked by clerks whose work survives the officials directing it. A private copy can begin as a safeguard against being blamed for a superior’s instruction. Descendants may inherit it as evidence of a crime the family has never dared name. Some keepers defend the copy because they want public truth; others because they want the person who dictated it to remain aware that it exists.

The Witness’s congregations learned to exchange testimony across institutional boundaries when an office could suppress what remained inside it. The practice preserved vulnerable speakers. It also created opportunities for an ambitious keeper to decide which disclosure would damage a rival most. Accurate evidence does not settle the keeper’s motives.

The Fractured Mirror in Vhasia

Vhasian Fractured Mirror observances developed around retinues whose members were expected to preserve the honor of a house. A servant who saw a lord keep one oath and violate another might possess the only account that could explain a later feud. Some households keep a covered mirror at the place testimony is received; others break the old frame when the witness who used it dies.

Marriage carried those observances between rival courts. The same testimony can survive in two family histories with different passages emphasized. An investigator who wants the whole account may have to persuade a witness’s descendants that their ancestor’s loyalty can survive an admission of what they actually saw.

Names beside the lamps

Pereshi Keepers of the Pure Flame approach the Witness with the Oath of Flame & Light while preserving texts and tending lamps. Their work is devotional even when the copied material concerns a disputed field rather than a miracle. The Ash-Readers’ challenge to orthodox practice has made some copies particularly difficult to preserve openly. A Keeper may consider an argument dangerous and still refuse to destroy the record that it was made.

In Zakov’s Salt-Fire, the Witness and the Pale Shepherd are invoked by the Namers over the Roll of the Drowned. One preserves who was lost; the other accompanies passage. The work can expose a shipowner who reported cargo carefully and passengers incompletely. A Sidhi lighthouse community may share names with the Namers without adopting their doctrine of the sea.

A witness is not an entire archive

Sihai’s official examinations reward a defensible account, and the Witness can be approached by someone who knows the accepted account omits a living person. Lethai-thora keepers preserve knowledge at a cost to their own memories; invoking the Witness does not restore their entire lost history or make every archive infallible. A copied statement and a person who remembers it remain different kinds of evidence.

The Apocalyptic Witness’s belief that final disclosure will end the world belongs to that cult. It is not the narrator’s explanation of the setting. Let its members produce something true and then disagree with others about whether publishing it now protects the speaker. A survivor asking that a testimony remain sealed can confront the congregation with a harder duty than exposing one more powerful liar. Truthful preservation and immediate public revelation are not the same act.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Ink-Eye

Ecktoria’s copying offices. A small eye is drawn on the inside of a scroll’s wrapper, where a casual inspection will not find it. Some clerks copy a redacted passage there. Others keep only enough information to establish where a suppressed document once stood. Neither practice promises safety for the clerk or agreement about when the copy should be produced.

The Fractured Mirror

Vhasian intelligence households. A fragment of mirror accompanies a sealed report. Spies use it to acknowledge that what they saw was partial, although ambitious patrons sometimes display a whole reconstructed mirror as proof of complete knowledge. A report can be accurate in every particular and misleading in what its commissioner refused to ask.

The Fog-Scribe

Mistland communities after a bell-line failure. Identifying the dead can require a private fact as well as a face. Keepers hear the fact from someone willing to recognize the person publicly. Families sometimes ask them to preserve the identification while withholding how it was established. The Fog-Scribe’s following includes people whom the truth has reunited and people frightened by the other truths it could release.

The Uncovered Measure

Oshiiran weigh-houses and district petitioners. A covered measure is uncovered only after the official account has been recited. The gesture has been adopted by petitioners who want a disputed distribution compared with what was actually delivered. The scale does ordinary work; it is no universal judge of honesty. The divine witness may be invoked by the inspector, the complaining household, or both.

History of Worship

The Witness is approached wherever an account survives pressure to replace it. Her followers’ histories do not require one original tribunal to have authorized all later testimony.

Many copying circles began as precautions against a familiar abuse: a revised order, a missing name, a memorial from which the defeated had disappeared. Their founders sometimes kept the evidence for years before allowing anyone to use it. Later Congregations of the Unmasking have claimed those predecessors as fearless public accusers, quietly shortening the long interval in which concealment was what kept the account alive.

Displaced clerks carried copies into new offices; travelers brought names to the families that could identify them; investigators learned to compare a court’s record with a household’s mementos. In Oshiira, petitioners adapted this practice to disputes over material distributions rather than importing a foreign court’s entire procedure. Vhasian intelligence households valued the same discipline for less public purposes.

Preserved evidence gives people leverage against an account already accepted by authority. Its keeper can therefore acquire authority of their own. A person who controls the only surviving copy may become the one everybody must please before the truth can be heard. Other followers make several copies, arrange witnesses, or put the decision back with the person whose account they preserved. Each choice creates different risks.

The Apocalyptic Witness is an interpretation pursued by the Congregation of the Unmasking, not a certainty every follower shares. Some keepers believe revelation must be immediate. Others refuse to destroy a speaker’s chance of survival merely to make a truth public sooner. Their disagreement can continue even after everyone agrees about what happened.

What the Truth Allows Its Keeper

Evidence may establish an event. It does not grant its possessor unlimited authority over everyone the event touched.

A keeper can preserve another person’s testimony faithfully and still use its release as a threat. The accuracy of the account does not make every use of it an act of justice.

Followers sometimes place their own contribution to a wrong beside the evidence against a more powerful person. The confession may weaken their standing in court. Omitting it would make the preserved account easier to deploy and less complete.

At the table.

A copy proves an official widened a punishment beyond the order they received. It also identifies the person who secretly warned the victims. Let the party test authenticity, prepare protection, negotiate a hearing, or expose a limited part without falsifying the rest. Avoid treating the widest possible disclosure as the automatic measure of courage.

  • Truth Revelation: exposing lies, uncovering secrets, forcing confessions

  • Memory Keeping: preserving knowledge, recalling forgotten facts

  • Inconvenient Knowledge: learning what others wish to hide

  • Prophetic Warning: seeing the consequences of hidden truths

  • Burden of Witness: the psychological weight of knowing too much

Cantors & Cults of the Witness

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of the Witness sings in truth-tolling — clear, piercing melodies that cut through evasion, harmonies that make the hidden surface. Their voice is used to compel a confession, to sound an alarm that a secret is about to be spoken, or to sing a historical record into being, each note a word in a ledger. The Witness’s Cantors are often found in courtrooms, in archive vaults, and at the bedsides of the dying, singing the last truth.

The Cult of the Congregation of the Unmasking. The most feared of the Witness’s cults, the Congregation of the Unmasking believes that the world will end when the last hidden truth is finally revealed — and that this ending is not a catastrophe but a liberation. They seek to expose every lie, every buried crime, every forgotten promise, no matter the cost. A ritual of the Congregation involves each member speaking aloud a truth they have kept secret, then burning a representation of the lie that concealed it. Their creed is painted on a scroll that is never fully unrolled: “When all that is hidden is seen, the world will either break or be free. We will be there to witness which.” Outsiders are not welcome; they are potential sources of truth — or obstacles to be unmasked. The Congregation has been known to destroy families, topple governments, and start wars in their pursuit of total revelation. They are loved by none and feared by all, but even their enemies cannot deny that they are right: some secrets truly are worth dying to expose.

Witchcraft of the Witness (Lay / Record)

A witch who walks with the Witness is a ledger-scribe or seal-breaker — one who crafts the tools of permanent record: a book that cannot be altered once written, a quill that writes in ink that fades only when the truth it records is fulfilled, or a press that stamps each page with a sigil of authenticity. Their magic works through the materials of documentation: a chisel that carves a truth into stone so that it cannot be erased, a needle that sews a confession into a garment so that the wearer cannot deny it, or a forge that tempers a door that can only be opened by someone who has never told a lie. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a “witness stone” at a courthouse entrance, so that all testimony given within is remembered by the stone for a century. In Thepyrgos, a bookbinder might stitch a volume that screams if its pages are cut.

The Witch’s Tool: The Quill of Unwritten Truths. A quill carved from a raven’s feather, its nib dipped in ink made from lampblack and a drop of the witch’s blood. Once per session, the witch may write a single sentence on a piece of parchment, then seal it with a drop of wax. For the next day, anyone other than the witch who reads the sentence must make a Spirit + Endurance test (DV 3) or forget it immediately after reading. The witch may also use the quill to “seal” a spoken secret, causing the speaker’s voice to catch if they try to repeat it for the rest of the scene.

A Hedge Gift: Record of a Breath (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may hold a blank piece of parchment up to a person’s lips as they speak. The witch may also use this ability to record the final words of the dying, preserving them for one day.

Terrestrial Patrons — Mortal Powers That Collect

Not all power flows from ancient Patrons and forgotten bargains. Some power sits in counting-houses, guild halls, and the back rooms of taverns. These are Terrestrial Patrons — mortal factions, noble houses, crime syndicates, or merchant leagues that offer tangible aid in exchange for Influence (the same mechanic as Obligation, tracked on a per-patron basis).

How Terrestrial Patrons Work

When you accept a favor from a Terrestrial Patron (access, resources, protection, an Asset), you mark +1 Influence to that patron. Your Influence capacity is the same as Obligation: Spirit + Presence. Exceeding capacity causes Fatigue (1 per segment over), and you cannot call on that patron for further favors until you clear segments.

Clearing Influence requires performing tasks for the patron. The GM sets the scope:

  • Minor task (deliver a message, pay a bribe): clears 1 Influence.

  • Significant task (escort a caravan, break a siege): clears 2 Influence.

  • Major task (assassinate a rival, broker a treaty): clears 3+ Influence.

Asset Slots

A Terrestrial Patron can grant Assets (see \(\ref{ch:assets-followers}\)) in exchange for Influence, not XP. The Influence cost is based on the Asset’s value and the patron’s power:

Asset Type Minor Patron (Local) Major Patron (Regional)
Minor Asset (4 XP value) 2 Influence 2 Influence
Standard Asset (8 XP value) 4 Influence 3 Influence
Major Asset (12 XP value) — 4 Influence
Follower (Cap 2) 2 Influence 2 Influence
Follower (Cap 3) 4 Influence 3 Influence

A Minor Patron cannot grant Major Assets — their reach is too short.

Examples of Terrestrial Patrons

  • House Contarini (Vilikari) — Archivolt access, forged charters, legal immunity.

  • The Ragman (Silkstrand) — Smuggling routes, night-boat passage, stolen goods.

  • The Morosini (Vilikari) — Letters of credit, market access, debt collection.

  • The Black Banners (Mercenary) — Condotta contracts, war mounts, safe passage.

  • The Salt Syndicate (Fhara) — Water rights, caravan escorts, desert guides.

Why Use Terrestrial Patrons?

Terrestrial Patrons allow players to gain Assets and favors without spending XP — but the cost is narrative. A favor from House Contarini may come due in the middle of a heist. A loan from the Morosini may be called when you are penniless and far from home. The Influence track ensures that these relationships have mechanical weight without replacing XP advancement.

For GMs: Terrestrial Patrons are excellent sources of adventure hooks. When a party owes Influence to three different patrons, those patrons will eventually compete for the party’s loyalty — and that competition is a campaign.

Witchcraft, Cults, and Religions

The Patrons do not demand worship. They do not build temples, compose scripture, or care which name you speak when you pray. They notice only one thing: Influence (sometimes called Weight). A vow kept, a promise honored, a threshold crossed with witness—these are what move the old powers. Yet mortals are not content to be noticed. We want to be answered. We build cathedrals to flames we do not understand. We whisper confessions into bells that cannot answer. We gather in secret circles to sing the name of a Patron we have never seen, hoping that if we sing loudly enough, someone will listen.

This section describes the human (and near-human) faces of power in the Amaranthine: the religions that shape daily life, the cults that form around specific Patrons or living conduits, and the quiet, patient craft of witchcraft—the art of working at thresholds, tying knots, and reminding the world of promises it has forgotten.

Religions are syncretic traditions—the Everflame, the Temple of Light, the Covenant of Mykkiel—that venerate a single divine principle while drawing power from multiple Patrons. They are the faith of the faithful: public, legal, and often entangled with the state. To join a religion is to join a Terrestrial Patron: you gain access to certain Rites and bear Influence with the hierarchy, not directly with the Patrons. The religion mediates the weight of the covenant.

Cults are concentrations of purpose: fellowships that form around a specific Patron, a specific interpretation, or a specific living voice—a Cantor whose song has drawn the desperate, an Invoker whose bargains have begun to outrun them. Cults are the faith of the desperate: private, dangerous, and always watched.

Witchcraft is not a faith at all. It is a method: the art of listening to Echo, steadying the Veil, and leaning into Flow. Witches work at thresholds—doorways, wakes, crossroads, bedsides—and their power is indistinguishable from skilled attention. Witchcraft is the craft of the cunning: patient, subtle, and older than any temple.

Whether you kneel in a basilica, whisper in a root cellar, or simply tie a red thread around a gatepost, the same law applies: the world remembers. And everything you do carries weight.

Religions of the Amaranthine

The great religions of the known world are not rivals to the Patrons; they are the masks the Patrons wear when mortals need a single face to pray to. Each is a syncretic tradition that venerates one divine principle—the Flame, the Light, the Covenant, the Twin Fires, the Road—while drawing power from several Patrons at once. Patrons care less about correct theology than about whom a promise binds, who is protected by it, and who bears its cost. A priest who invokes the Inquisitor Prime and a paladin who calls on the Oath of Flame & Light may believe they serve the same god. They are not wrong—but neither prayer relieves either of them of deciding what they owe the person standing in front of them.

Religion in the Amaranthine is not a moral shorthand. It is the largest thing mortals have ever built together, and it is built out of mortals. The same parish that shelters a widow through a hard winter will inform on her son. The same order that founded the only free clinic in a city will take a child away from a mother for singing the wrong hymn over a cradle. Play the faiths as people play them: with real comfort, real courage, real cruelty, and a great deal of posturing.

Orders Are the Masks

An Order is not a department of a religion. It is the shape a specific Patron takes inside that religion—the office through which that presence is approached, named, and made respectable. This is why the same Order appears in faiths that despise each other, and why it does not hold the same rank in each.

Patron / Order In the Everflame In the Temple In the Covenant
Oath of Flame & Light Orthodox (knights) Orthodox (preachers) Hospitalier (healers)
Inquisitor Prime Prosecutorial (Flame-Wardens) Prosecutorial (Chain-Lanterns) Prosecutorial (the Accuser)
Oath of Mercy and Grace Hospitalier (watched) Hospitalier (beloved) Absent (a scandal)

The consequences of this are the whole social texture of religion in play:

  • The same power, different manners. An Everflame knight and a Covenant hospitaller both draw on the Oath of Flame & Light. Both are correct. Each finds the other’s use of it faintly obscene—one has made a war-saint of a healer, the other has made a nurse of a war-saint—and they will say so, at length, to anyone but each other.

  • Rank is the argument. Where an Order sits in the hierarchy is a claim about what the faith is for. The Everflame’s Mercy houses are supervised because mercy is a subordinate aspect of fire; the Temple’s are beloved because mercy is what light is; the Covenant has no mercy Order at all, which its rivals never stop mentioning and its own hospitallers quietly resent.

  • Schism runs along the seams. Faiths do not usually split over gods. They split over which Order gets the money, the pulpit, and the right to speak first. Every schism in this section began as a budget dispute or a seating order and became a theology afterward.

  • Posturing is characterization. An adherent wears their Order the way a soldier wears a regiment. A Chain-Lantern will find a way to mention the chain. A Keeper of the Pure Flame will correct your pronunciation of a Name. Let them. It is free flavor and it tells a player exactly who they are talking to.

The Everflame (Ecktoria)

The Everflame is the state religion of the Ecktorian Empire, and the distinction matters: it is not merely the faith most Ecktorians hold, it is an organ of the state. Its basilicas are courthouses. Its magistrates are ordained. Its perpetual fires are maintained at public expense and their extinction in a city is a constitutional event. The Everflame teaches that the world was kindled by a single fire that divided into three aspects—the Gleaming (justice), the Searing (purification), and the Warming (mercy)—and that the flame does not judge. The flame witnesses. The judge is the empire.

The Three Orders:

  • Oath of Flame & Light (Orthodox) — Clergy and knightly orders; the public face. Rites of vow, radiance, and smiting.

  • Inquisitor Prime (Prosecutorial) — The Flame-Wardens. Iron masks, blessed coal, and jurisdiction that does not stop at a border. Rites of judgment and purification.

  • Oath of Mercy and Grace (Hospitalier) — Healers and confessors, and the only Order in the faith that is formally supervised by another. They are required to report the heretics they treat. Most do. The ones who do not are the reason the Order is supervised.

How one is saved: not by a verdict but by a sequence. The Everflame’s central claim is that purification is a known procedure — the Refinement — with published stages, in a fixed order, which any soul can be brought through. Confession, restitution, the vigil, the Warming, the Searing, the reading of the name back to the penitent. Perform the stages correctly and completely and you are clean; this is not opinion, and it is not up to the priest.

This is the most genuinely democratic doctrine in the Amaranthine. It does not matter who your parents were or what you can pay. The stages are the stages. Beggars have been carried through the Refinement in full, publicly, by an empire that despised them, because the procedure does not have a clause for despising people. Everflame parishes will feed and shelter a stranger indefinitely while they are in the middle of it, and Ecktorians of every class have been genuinely moved by this for four hundred years, and they are right to be.

It is also the cruellest, for the same reason. A stage performed imperfectly must be repeated from the beginning. Whether it was performed imperfectly is determined by the Censorate. A Refinement can therefore be extended, without malice and without any rule being broken, until the penitent dies in it — and because the stages are the stages, the family may pay to have the remainder completed on the dead person’s behalf, which is not a scam, exactly, and is nonetheless most of the income of several dioceses. The most feared words in Ecktoria are not a sentence of death. They are “we will begin again.”

Beauty: the Refinement itself; the perpetual fires that any freezing person may sit at by right; four centuries of parish records that have reunited more families than any army; the Warming, which is simply a rite of being fed and sat with by strangers until you can speak.

Ugliness: the Censorate, which decides what counts as a completed stage and has never once been audited by anyone outside itself; the Unspoken Ninth Citation, a redacted procedural clause permitting execution without confession, never read aloud; censure, which strikes a name from the parish register so that the person’s family may not pray for them — a punishment aimed entirely at the survivors.

Internal schism: the Ember Prefects hold that a fire kept in a basilica is a fire being hoarded, and carry candles into the streets to run the Refinement for anyone, free, without the Censorate. They are correct about the doctrine and unbearable about it, and about a third of them are extortionists with a candle.

Where: the Ecktorian core and its borderlands; a minority presence in Kahfagia and among Vilikari communities; called the Imperial Fire in Thepyrgos and meant unkindly.

The Temple of Light (Viterra and Thepyrgos)

The Temple began as a reform movement inside the Everflame and broke from it over a single question: whether a soul needs an institution in order to be met. The Temple’s answer is no. Light is not administered. It falls on you. Every member of the Temple has — is expected to have, is examined on having — a personal relationship with the light, unmediated by any priest, and the whole apparatus of the faith exists to put people in the way of that and then get out of it.

The practical expressions of this are startling to an Everflame visitor. The Codex of Illumination is in the vernacular and cheap, and Temple presses have been running for a century to keep it cheap; households own one; children are taught to read on it, which is why literacy in rural Viterra runs ahead of the imperial cities. A Temple priest is a schoolteacher with a stipend. There is no confession to a priest, because there is nobody in between to confess to — there is the morning candle, and you and the light, and the plain and terrible business of being honest by yourself. Congregations call their own ministers and can dismiss them, and do.

The Three Orders:

  • Oath of Flame & Light (Orthodox) — Parish teachers, wandering preachers, and the Dawn-Knights who escort them. Light is a lens refracted into Gold (justice), Silver (truth), and White (mercy).

  • Inquisitor Prime (Prosecutorial) — The Chain-Lanterns, who carry their lanterns on chains as a standing reminder that power must be restrained, and who have never once let the reminder stop them.

  • Oath of Mercy and Grace (Hospitalier) — Hospices, orphanages, free clinics; the best-loved institution in Viterra and, by a wide margin, the best-run.

Beauty: a faith that trusts ordinary people with its own scripture and has never taken it back; free schools; free clinics; a hundred small congregations that have quietly sheltered exactly the people the Everflame struck from its registers.

Ugliness: when everyone reads for themselves, everyone is a potential heretic, and discipline falls to the congregation — which is to say, to your neighbors, who know what your marriage is like. Temple shunning is administered by people who have eaten at your table, and it is far more intimate and far more effective than any inquisitor. The Temple also fissions constantly: three chapels in one market town, none speaking, all correct. And the Chain-Lanterns’ rehabilitation of arcane-talented children — taking them from their mothers into schools that are genuinely kind, genuinely well-fed, and from which the child comes home a stranger who thanks you for it — is the single most disturbing thing any faith in the Amaranthine does, precisely because nobody involved is being cruel.

Internal schism: the Plain Meeting hold that ministers, buildings, and hymns are all mediation and must go; they sit in silence in barns until someone is moved to speak. The Temple has never suppressed them and finds them insufferable, chiefly because they are the Temple’s own argument taken one step further, and everyone knows it.

Where: dominant in Viterra; the popular faith of Thepyrgos beneath a Covenant-leaning ruling class; growing among the Linn; missions as far as the Mistlands.

The Covenant of Mykkiel

Every other faith in the Amaranthine begins with a message. The Covenant begins with a ruling.

The Covenant does not hold that a messenger came down with words to be recited. It holds that a woman of the Sidhi diaspora, dispossessed and without recourse, brought a grievance — and that Mykkiel, the Arbiter, heard the case and found for her. The founding document of the faith is not a revelation. It is a judgment, with a plaintiff, a respondent, findings, and a remedy. The first Covenanter was not a prophet. She was a litigant, and she won, and the faith has never entirely recovered from the confidence of that.

This is why the Covenant has no messenger-angel and is proud of it, and why the accusation stung so badly when the Everflame first made it. To a Covenanter, a faith founded on a recitation asks you to believe a speaker; a faith founded on a verdict asks you to read the reasoning and check it. Covenant children are taught the founding judgment the way Temple children are taught letters: as a text you are expected to argue with.

The Three Orders:

  • Mykkiel (Orthodox) — Judges, notaries, and law-scholars, who settle disputes and draft the binding oaths. Their courts are the faith’s cathedrals; there is no other kind of temple.

  • Inquisitor Prime (Prosecutorial) — The Accuser, who investigates broken oaths and brings the suit. Iron scales, the Canons of Obligation, and a formal duty to argue the case against the faith’s own officers when the case is good.

  • Oath of Flame & Light (Hospitalier) — The healer’s path, and the only Order the Covenant borrowed from its rivals. Healing is given under terms: the patient swears to something. Covenant hospitallers argue endlessly about how small the terms may honorably be, and the best of them have gotten it down to a promise to eat.

Beauty: the Covenant will hear anyone. Its courts take the case of a servant against a house, a wife against a husband, a tenant against a landlord, and they take it seriously, for free, and their judgments have been enforced against people who were confident they could not be. In three regions the Covenant is the only body that will write down what a poor person says and treat the writing as binding.

Ugliness: a faith founded on a winning verdict believes it has already prevailed, and treats every disagreement as an appeal it expects to dismiss. Its judgments bind the losing side absolutely and forever, including the losers’ descendants, who were not present. Its formal position is that the unbound are not wicked but unheard — which sounds gentle until you notice it means the whole world outside is a docket, and the Covenant is working through it.

Internal schism: the Recusants hold that a judge who founds a faith cannot then preside over it, and that Mykkiel is obliged to recuse himself from all matters touching the Covenant. Nobody can find the flaw in the argument. Nobody with a position will say so out loud. Recusant congregations keep an empty seat on the bench and are considered either the most rigorous Covenanters alive or a nest of sophists, depending on who is asked.

Where: strongest in Galanina and among the Fhara ruling class; secret cells in Ashaan; ascendant among the Thepyrgan nobility; formally heretical in Ecktoria, where its courts nonetheless quietly arbitrate half the commercial disputes on the docks.

The Twin Flames (the Pereshi Faith)

In the Pereshi highlands the fire is doubled. The universe is a contest between the Pure Flame — truth, order, growth — and the Consuming Flame — deceit, decay, appetite. Both are sacred; only one is to be fed. There is no single god, but a host of radiant spirits, and no salvation to be granted by anyone: every person carries a spark, and the spark grows or dims by what you do this week. Nothing is deferred to a court or a procedure. It is a daily faith, and it is exhausting, and its adherents are among the most stubbornly decent people in the world.

The Orders:

  • Keepers of the Pure Flame — Priests, healers, archivists; tend the eternal lamps, copy the texts, recite the Twenty-One Names of Radiance. They invoke the Oath of Flame & Light and the Witness.

  • Watchers Against the Maw — Hunters of the undead, the possessed, and the corrupted; they burn what the Consuming Flame has touched. They invoke the Pale Shepherd and the Inquisitor Prime, and they are the reason the faith is tolerated in Ecktoria and distrusted in Viterra.

  • The Oil-Bearers (lay) — Not clergy at all: householders who keep a lamp and are entitled to speak in assembly for as long as their own oil lasts. It is the closest thing the Amaranthine has to a franchise, and the rich buy very large lamps.

Beauty: an ethic with no intercessor and no purchase price; the eternal lamps, which any traveler may light a wick from; the Towers of Silence, where the dead are given to the elements and the birds so that no ground is made unclean and no family is beggared by a funeral.

Ugliness: a doctrine of feeding and starving makes purity a daily performance, and performance makes informers. Highland villages keep score of one another’s sparks. The Watchers burn corpses on suspicion and have burned houses with them. And a faith that says the spark dims by your own acts has no comfort at all for the person to whom something terrible was simply done.

Internal schism: the Ash-Readers hold that the Consuming Flame must also be fed — sparingly, deliberately, on a schedule — because a hunger starved entirely will feed itself elsewhere. The Watchers consider this the single most dangerous idea in the highlands. The Ash-Readers point out that the villages that keep the old ash-rites have fewer hauntings, and the Watchers do not have an answer for that, which is why the argument is conducted at such volume.

Where: the majority faith of the Pereshi highlands and of Pereshi communities in Zakov, Kahfagia, and the Dolmis ports.

Flames Carried Abroad: Two Syncretisms

Fire travels well and arrives changed. Where the Everflame’s witness-fire met a local practice with its own claim on the dead or the doorway, the result was not a heresy so much as a marriage — and the great faiths have never agreed which parent to blame.

The Salt-Fire (Zakov and the eastern Dolmis).

Everflame doctrine crossed salt water and came ashore married to the tide-cults and the Pale Shepherd. The Salt-Fire keeps the Refinement, and has quietly rewritten its last stage: the Searing is performed with brine, not flame, because on this coast the sea is what purifies and fire is what a ship most fears. Its priests are called Namers, and its whole soteriology is one sentence — you are saved if your name is said aloud after you die. The Namers therefore keep the coast’s roll of the drowned, and read it, all of it, once a year, for as long as it takes, which in a bad year is three days.

Beauty: nobody on that coast is buried unnamed, no matter how poor, how foreign, or how badly they died; the Namers take no fee and have refused one publicly enough times that offering is now an insult.

Ugliness: a faith whose sacrament is a list runs on informers, because the list must be complete. The Namers know who sailed with whom, who owed whom, and who did not come back, and the Syndicates have found this useful for a century. Being left off the roll is the coast’s severest punishment and is entirely at a Namer’s discretion.

The Little Flame (the ports, the Way of Silk, everywhere).

The poor people’s fire: a candle in a window, a lamp at a door, and no clergy whatsoever. It is what happened when the Everflame’s public rites met a hundred household hearth-cults in a hundred harbors and lost the argument about who owns a flame. Its only doctrine is that a lit window is a promise of one night’s shelter and that the promise is owed to anyone. Its only rite is lighting one.

Beauty: it costs nothing, excludes no one, and has fed and hidden every kind of person the great faiths struck off their books. In a strange city, a lit window in an upper story is the most reliable sanctuary in the Amaranthine.

Ugliness: no clergy means no restraint. A Little Flame congregation is whoever is holding candles in that street tonight, and it can become a mob between one hour and the next; every port has a year it does not discuss. And because anyone may preach, any loud voice can be a prophet for a season, and the seasons end badly about as often as not.

The Still Road (the Eastern Realms)

East of the Amaranthine the argument changes entirely. The Still Road does not ask who judges you, or whether the light has met you, or which fire you have fed. It asks what you are holding, and whether you could put it down.

Its founding teaching is that suffering is grip — the clenched hand, the drawn blade, the promise squeezed until it bleeds — and that the way out is not a verdict, a procedure, or a rescue, but practice: attention, restraint, and the long unlearning of the reflex to seize. It has no creator-god and does not miss one. Its Patrons are approached as aspects of practice rather than as powers to be bargained with, which the western faiths find either admirably clean or deeply suspicious depending on how much they have riding on bargains.

The Orders:

  • The Unbroken Way — The disciplinary and martial Order, and the one outsiders always meet first. Monks of the Still Point mediate feuds, guard roads, and are famous for winning fights they do not start by declining to have them. The blade that is not drawn cannot be broken.

  • The Silver Quiet (Lunara) — Contemplatives and night-keepers; the long sit, the unlit hall, the practice of watching a thought arrive without going with it.

  • The Traveler — Mendicants who own a bowl and walk. They take alms and give teaching, and their circuits are the reason the Road has spread further than any army has marched.

  • The Pale Shepherd — Funerary Order; sits with the dying, walks the dead out, and is welcome in villages that will not let a monk past the gate.

  • The Riven Path (heterodox) — Those who hold that a chain is not untied but broken, and that quietism is a comfortable posture for people who have never been owned. Formally not an Order. Present in every monastery.

It changes as you go east.

The Road is not one thing, and the differences are not corruptions — each branch believes itself the plain reading.

Internal schism: the mainland holds that the texts are the practice’s memory and cannot be discarded; Nihori holds that a memorized practice is a held object like any other. Both sides are polite about this in public and neither has visited the other in forty years.

Religious Conflict and Cooperation

The great faiths do not wage open holy war. Their priests argue theology, their inquisitors watch each other, their missionaries compete for the same ports — and then a Covenant judge consults an Everflame archivist about a parish register, and a Temple physician staffs a Covenant hospice, and a Still Road mendicant is fed at a Little Flame window without either party asking what the other believes. The Patrons are indifferent to mortal sectarianism and accept weight from all of it.

What the western faiths share is a conviction that witness is binding — that the divine is whatever will not let you pretend it did not happen. The Still Road does not share it, and this is the deepest fault line in the world: to a Covenanter, a wrong unwitnessed is a wrong unremedied; to a monk of the Still Point, a wrong you will not put down is a wrong you have chosen to keep carrying. Neither has ever convinced the other, and the argument is at least four hundred years old, and it is still the best one anybody has.

Other Traditions and Cults

Beyond the great syncretic faiths, the Amaranthine harbors a host of local traditions, mystery cults, and folk practices. Most are tolerated (or ignored) by the great faiths, though some are actively suppressed.

The Gray Pilgrims (The Traveler)

A wandering order of Lethai-ar who maintain the old way-cords. They wear gray cloaks woven from mist and speak only in riddles. Their creed: “The road does not end. It only turns.” They accept no payment, only a lock of hair for their way-cords. Found in the Valewood, the Linn coast, and the Aelerian passes.

The Daughters of the Cord (The Bitter Root)

A secretive, all-female cult that believes all obligations are threads that can be cut. The highest form of mercy is to sever a promise without asking. They run hospices and soup kitchens, ask no payment, but write every name into a book whose ink never dries. Found in Silkstrand, Zakov, and the poorer wards of Ecktoria.

The Cog-Cults (The Clockwork Monad)

Underground fraternities of gear-smiths and logicians who believe the universe is a machine that can be perfected. They meet in abandoned watermills, reciting the Canons of Proportion and performing “optimisation rites.” Their symbol is a gear with nine teeth (the ninth is hidden). Found in Thepyrgos, Aeler, and the industrial wards of Silkstrand.

The Salt Sworn (The Pale Shepherd)

A syncretic cult of the eastern Dolmis coast. They believe the Pale Shepherd guides not only the dead but the tide. They build cairns of salt stone and leave only the names of the lost, carved into driftwood. Once a year, they chant the names aloud, believing each strengthens the threshold. Found in the salt marshes of Zakov, the coast of Theona, and the Aberderrin headlands.

The Bear Cults of Ubral

In the high glens of Ubral, the Bear Cults believe the Savage Heart (the Red Maw) is not a demon but a teacher. Initiates wear cured bear-skins and carve runes into their ribs. They believe that by becoming the predator, they can protect their clans. Found only in Ubral.

The Ember Prefects (Heretical Everflame)

The street schism described above (§\(\ref{sec:everflame}\)), seen from outside: candle-carriers who will run a Refinement for anyone, free, in an alley, without the Censorate’s leave. Their symbol is a candle burning from both ends. To a parish priest they are a nuisance; to the Flame-Wardens they are a standing warrant; to the people in the alley they are frequently the only clergy who came. Found across Ecktoria, with cells in Vhasia and Thepyrgos.

The Veneration of the Hearth (Aelaerem Folk Tradition)

Not a religion in the institutional sense, but a pervasive set of practices among the halflings. Every Aelaerem home has a red door, a cup-mark stone, and watch-geese. The Hearth is the accumulated memory of every kindness shown in that home. Offerings are simple: bread on the doorstep, milk in the garden, a red thread on a knife handle. Found only in Aelaerem (Amedell) and among halfling communities in the Dolmis ports.

Player Takeaway

These traditions are not character classes or mechanical packages; they are sources of story hooks, local color, and potential influence. A character who follows the Gray Pilgrims may gain a way-cord (treat as a Minor Asset) but owes a lock of hair. A character who joins the Salt Sworn may learn a Hedge Gift but must carve a name into driftwood each full moon. Work with your GM to determine what, if any, mechanical benefit or cost these traditions carry. Most are best represented as Strings, Timers, or narrative permissions, not as full Patron relationships.

Cults of the Amaranthine

Where religions build temples, cults gather in shadow. A cult is not a failed religion—it is a concentration of purpose. Where the Everflame concerns itself with all of Ecktoria, a cult of the Inquisitor Prime concerns itself only with your heresy. Where the Temple of Light builds hospitals for all, a cult of the Oath of Mercy and Grace builds a single room where promises are forgiven—and remembered.

Cults form around a specific Patron, a specific interpretation of that Patron, or a specific living conduit: a Cantor whose voice has drawn the desperate, an Invoker whose bargains have begun to outrun them, or a Runekeeper who has stopped reading the Codex and started writing their own chapters.

The Cantor’s Congregation

A Cantor does not choose to lead a cult. A Cantor sings, and the broken listen. The desperate gather. The hungry, the hunted, the hollow—they find the Cantor’s song before they find the Cantor. By the time the Cantor realizes they have a congregation, the congregation has already named them Speaker, Shepherd, or Storm-Heart.

A Cantor-led cult is charismatic, volatile, and deeply personal. The Cantor’s voice is the cult’s scripture; their mood is its liturgy. When the Cantor falters, the cult may fragment, turn inward, or seek a new voice. When the Cantor ascends (or descends), the cult ascends with them—into significance, into terror, or into the annals of inquisitors.

GM Seed: A Cantor of the Pale Shepherd has begun singing funerals for the not-yet-dead. Villagers pay to have their names sung into the Shepherd’s flock before they die. The local Temple priest calls it a mercy. The dead call it theft.

The Invoker’s Covenant

An Invoker does not seek followers. An Invoker solves problems. But when an Invoker has solved enough problems for enough people, those people begin to wait. They wait outside the Invoker’s door. They leave offerings. They whisper the Invoker’s name alongside the Patron’s. A covenant forms—not of worship, but of dependence.

An Invoker-led cult is pragmatic, secretive, and increasingly brittle. The Invoker did not ask for loyalty and does not know how to maintain it. Rituals are improvised. Doctrine is whatever worked last time. The cult endures only as long as the Invoker’s bargains keep paying out. When they stop, the knives come out.

Invokers themselves are usually wary of cults—cults attract attention, and attention attracts the Inquisitor Prime. But a desperate Invoker may tolerate a covenant, even cultivate it, telling themselves it is just a network, just a favor economy, just a few people who owe them. It never stays just.

GM Seed: An Invoker of the Clockwork Monad has accidentally created a cult of gear-makers who believe the Monad speaks through her calculations. They have begun “optimizing” the village—replanting fields in perfect grids, scheduling marriages by algorithm. The Invoker cannot stop them without admitting she is not a prophet.

The Runekeeper’s Schism

A Runekeeper leads a cult only when they have broken faith—with their Codex, with their Order, or with the Patron’s orthodox interpretation. Runekeeper cults are doctrinal, obsessive, and dangerous in proportion to their literacy. They keep records. They write commentaries. They argue about commas in Rites that should never have been written down at all.

Most Runekeeper cults are small: a master, a few apprentices, a locked scriptorium. But some grow, especially when the Runekeeper claims to have found a lost Rite—a fragment of a Patron’s power that the orthodox Orders have suppressed. These cults are heresies in the truest sense: not a different faith, but a different reading of the same faith. The orthodox hate them most of all.

A Runekeeper’s attitude toward cults varies by Patron:

  • Oath of Flame & Light: Cults are weeds in the garden—to be pulled, burned, or redirected.

  • Ikasha: A cult may be a useful shadow, but the Runekeeper will never stand at its center.

  • The Witness: Constitutionally incapable of leading a cult; they would be forced to reveal their own manipulations.

GM Seed: A Runekeeper of the Carrion-King has found a Rite that allows followers to “compost” their own memories into physical tokens. The cult now trades these tokens—childhoods, first loves, old griefs—on a black market. The Runekeeper insists this is what the King intended. The King has not disagreed.

Cults as Factions

Mechanically, a cult functions as an Asset (see §\(\ref{ch:assets-followers}\)) with a severe narrative bias. A cult is never neutral. A cult will:

  • Provide Aid: Followers can act as Cap 2–5 followers, but only in service of the cult’s purpose.

  • Generate Influence: Leading a cult ticks Influence with the relevant Patron +1 per session—the Patron notices when you gather followers in their name.

  • Attract Attention: Any cult of 10+ members begins a Cult Investigation [6] timer. When it fills, someone comes looking: an Inquisitor Prime agent, a rival cult, or the Patron themselves.

  • Demand Upkeep: A cult requires either Efficient Upkeep (XP, representing bribes, shelter, and lies) or Intensive Upkeep (a scene per session where the leader performs a miracle, preaches, or solves a follower’s problem).

Examples of Cults

The Unforgotten Ledger (Varnek Karn)


Funeral notaries who speak with the dead. Meet in crypts. Outsiders bring a name and leave with a task.

  • Leader: A Death-Speaker (Cantor or Runekeeper).

  • Hazard: The dead begin to answer—politely, then demandingly.

The Gilded Jest (Mab)


A conspiracy of wits who believe every exchange is a performance. Meet in theaters and back rooms.

  • Leader: A Masked Jester (Cantor or Invoker).

  • Hazard: Contracts become binding fae bargains; breaking one draws Mab’s amused attention.

The Iron Chorus (Inquisitor Prime)


Militant witch-hunters who believe purity is survival. Meet in fortified chapter houses.

  • Leader: A Censor-Prime (Runekeeper or Cantor).

  • Hazard: The Chorus begins to see heresy everywhere—including in you.

The Compost-Circle (Carrion-King)


Gardeners and gravediggers who believe nothing is wasted. Meet in compost heaps and burial mounds.

  • Leader: A Rot-Warden (Invoker or Hedge-Witch).

  • Hazard: The Circle’s transformations begin to accelerate—composting becomes contagion.

Joining or Founding a Cult

  • Founding: Requires a Patron’s Favor (a Talent, a critical success on a Rite, or a GM-defined story beat). The new cult begins as a Minor Asset (4 XP) with 1–3 followers.

  • Joining: Grants access to the cult’s Asset benefits (followers, safe houses, information) but ticks Influence +1 to the cult’s Patron. Leaving a cult is a narrative scene, not a mechanical cost—but the Patron may remember.

  • Reluctant Leadership: A character who becomes the focus of a cult without seeking it gains the Cult Leader tag. This is a Condition: once per session, the GM may compel the character to act in the cult’s interest (preach, protect, perform a Rite) or mark 1 Fatigue from the weight of expectation.

Player Takeaway

Cults are not for everyone. A cult gives you hands, eyes, and voices beyond your own—but those hands may hold knives, those eyes may watch you sleep, and those voices may begin to speak without your permission. If you choose to lead, remember: the Patron is the real congregation. You are just its favorite hymn.

Witchcraft

The Amaranthine prays to the Everflame, the Temple, and the Covenant. But when prayers fail, when the child will not be born, when the roads turn against the traveler, the people do not call for a priest. They call for a witch.

A witch is not a heretic—not necessarily. A witch is a threshold worker: one who listens to what has been (Echo), steadies what is (the Veil), and leans into what wishes to be (Flow). Witchcraft is not brute force; it is fit—finding where the world already wants to move and giving it a gentle, precise nudge. Hearth craft, mill craft, and road craft are simply slow, patient forms of the same art practiced by ordinary hands. The most powerful magic is often indistinguishable from skilled attention.

The Two Speeds of Witchcraft

The Long Road

Ritual, threshold, price, and consequence. It takes Significant Time (hours, days, a moon phase). This is the domain of Full Rituals: unbinding a curse, hallowing a threshold, or unraveling a wound.

The Short Road

Instinct, residue, and a flicker of attention. It takes Some Time (a few heartbeats, a single action). This is the domain of Quick Workings and Hedge Gifts—magic so subtle it can be mistaken for luck.

Core Principles of Witchcraft

  1. The Law of Named Existence: To name is to anchor. A True Name fastens an Echo; forgetting frays it. Words spoken under witness reshape how a place remembers.

  2. The Concordant Layers: People, places, and vows exist as chords across Echo / Veil / Flow.

    • Echo: Accumulated memory and intention. What has been.

    • Veil: The current, perceivable state; the boundary between potential and actual.

    • Flow: The direction of change; the will of elements and intent.

  3. Threshold Mechanics: All workings begin at edges—doorways, wakes, vows, last breaths, first light, crossroads. Magic is boundary-law: who may pass, what must stay, and what should never meet.

  4. Element-Will: Elements are wills, not mere substances. A working argues with a chosen will; Influence is the weight incurred when borrowing from a will larger than yours.

  5. The Silent Ninth: The missing step in every scale—absence with teeth. The Ninth is erasure, gaps, omissions, and the power of what is left unsaid. Players never declare the Ninth. The GM may apply a Void tag only as consequence.

Hedge Gifts

A Hedge Gift is not a spell. It is the world tilting slightly in the witch’s favor because she has paid attention to the right thresholds. Hedge Gifts require no dice roll; they simply work within their limited scope.

Any character with the Craft of the Hedge talent may learn Hedge Gifts. A witch begins with two Hedge Gifts of her choice. Additional Hedge Gifts cost 2 XP each.

Steady Hand


Once per scene, touch a wounded person (including yourself) and whisper a word of comfort. Remove 1 Fatigue. No roll. You may not use this on the same person twice in a scene.

Knot of Favor


Once per scene, tie a small knot in a cord, thread, or cloth. For the rest of the scene, you and allies within Near gain +1 die to a single type of roll (choose when you tie: stealth, persuasion, defense, or navigation). The knot must remain tied; if undone, the benefit ends.

Warm Hand


Once per scene, touch someone and speak a word of comfort or warning. For the next exchange, that person’s next physical action either gains +1 die (comfort) or suffers –1 die (warning). No roll. The effect is subtle and cannot be directly perceived as magic.

Hearth-Sense


Once per scene, ask the GM one yes/no question about a threshold, a boundary, or a promise in the current scene. The GM must answer truthfully.

Salt Line


Once per scene, pour a handful of salt in a continuous line (move action). Any spirit, ghost, or cursed creature attempting to cross must test Spirit+Endurance (DV 3) or be stopped for that exchange. The line must be at least Close length; broken if someone steps through or scatters the salt.

The Unlit Candle


Once per session, declare that a light source you can see (lantern, candle, torch) falters or extinguishes. This creates a zone of dim light or darkness in Near range for one exchange.

Counting the Eighth


Once per scene, when you or an ally is about to roll, whisper a number (1–8). If the GM’s current Story Beat count is equal to that number modulo 8, the roll gains +2 dice. If not, nothing happens. You cannot use this again until the GM’s SB count changes.

Quick Workings

A Quick Working is a single-action roll that relies on the witch’s knowledge of thresholds, elements, and will. Its effects are limited, subtle, and always carry the risk of consequence.

Procedure

  1. Name the Threshold (door, tide line, wound, vow, breath).

  2. Choose a Layer (Echo, Veil, or Flow) and a single Tag (from the magic tags list).

  3. Set Position by urgency (Controlled if you have time, Desperate if threatened).

  4. Roll Attribute + Skill (typically Wits+Lore, Spirit+Insight, or Presence+Sway).

  5. Apply Outcome (see table).

The effect is always minor and scene-limited: a door sticks shut or opens; a candle gutters (brief darkness); a rope knot tightens or loosens; a pursuer’s footing slips (1 Fatigue); a whispered name is heard or forgotten.

Quick Working Outcome Matrix
Roll Result Effect Cost
Clean Success (Successes \(\geq\) DV, SB \(=0\)) Effect manifests cleanly. No cost.
Success with SB (Successes \(\geq\) DV, SB \(>0\)) Effect works, but GM spends SB for minor complication. Mark 1 Fatigue or accept a Condition (e.g., Thread-Burned).
Partial (\(0 < \text{Successes} < \text{DV}\)) Effect works partially or briefly. Gain 1 Boon; the threshold remembers you.
Miss (\(\text{Successes} = 0\)) Effect fails. Gain 2 Boons; start a Promise Timer [4] named “The Threshold Remembers.”2

Full Rituals

A Full Ritual requires Significant Time, a proper threshold, and often a witness. Its effects are lasting or permanent: blessings, curses, bindings, or the hallowing of ground.

General Procedure

  1. Identify the Threshold (name the edge, learn its disposition).

  2. Choose Your Witness (a person, spirit, or Hollowed who watches).

  3. Name Your Will (what change you intend, using Threshold Tags).

  4. Set Your Price (memory, name, lock of hair, promise, blood).

  5. Make the Exchange (roll, resolve, pay the price on success or partial).

Example Rituals
Unravel the Wound (Heal Harm)


Requires: Significant Time, a threshold (doorway or basin of water), a witness. Restores 1 level of Harm. Price: A lock of hair, a memory of a childhood injury, a tear in a vial. Roll: Spirit + Lore vs. DV (3 for Harm 1, 4 for Harm 2, 5+ for Harm 3 requires a coven). Success: Harm reduced; witch pays the price. Partial: Harm reduced, but witch gains a minor Condition or a Promise Timer [4]. Miss: Wound does not heal; witch gains the Condition she tried to remove.

Hallow Threshold (Permanent Ward)


Requires: Significant Time, a permanent threshold (door, well, bridge, hearthstone), a witness who will maintain the consecration, an offering (bread, salt, coin, spoken promise). The blessing lasts until the threshold is physically broken or the offering removed. Roll: Spirit + Lore vs. DV 4 (home), 5 (village well), or 6 (city gate). Success: Threshold hallowed. Spirits, undead, and cursed creatures crossing must test Spirit+Endurance (DV 5) or be turned back. Partial: Blessing holds but has a weakness (the witch names one thing that can bypass it). Miss: Blessing fails; threshold becomes cursed for one season (all who cross feel unease, –1 die to first action).

Unbind the Anchor (Permanent Spirit Release)


Requires: Significant Time, the spirit’s true name or a strong anchor, a threshold (doorway facing east, crossroads, grave), a witness. Steps: (1) Discover the anchor (unfinished business, stolen object, broken vow). (2) Name the Terms (propose resolution). (3) Prepare the threshold (salt, chalk, broken cord). (4) Speak the Unbinding (Spirit + Lore vs. DV 4–5). (5) Pay the Price. Success: Spirit released. Witch gains 1 Boon. Partial: Spirit released but leaves a remnant (echo, cold spot). Witch marks 1 Fatigue and gains a minor Condition. Miss: Spirit refuses, becomes hostile. Start a Promise Timer [4] named “The Spirit’s Revenge.”

Talents of the Hedge

Craft of the Hedge (4 XP)


Requires: Lore 1+ or Insight 1+; a witch mark (tattoo, scar, or birthmark). You have learned the small magics of thresholds and edges. You gain: two Hedge Gifts; the ability to perform Quick Workings; and once per session, when you fail a Quick Working roll, you may convert the Miss into a Partial Success by marking 1 Fatigue.

Hedge Gift of the Patron (6 XP)


Requires: Craft of the Hedge, Bond to a Patron. Once per session, when you would use a Hedge Gift, you may instead invoke your Patron’s Imbuement. Gain +1 die to all actions tied to your Patron’s domain for the scene, but mark +1 Obligation immediately. No action required.

Covens and Traditions

The Bone Coven (Aelerian Influence)


Focus: Ancestral wisdom and the patient power of stone that remembers. Signature Rite: Stone Memory Communion—by pressing hands to ancient stone, the witch witnesses events that occurred near it, going back centuries. Patron Elements: Earth and Fate.

The Mistwalkers (Mistlands Influence)


Focus: Navigation between worlds and manipulation of thresholds. Signature Rite: Wayfinding Through the Veil—by burning specific herbs, the witch creates temporary passages through the Ways Between. Patron Elements: Water and Obishaal (Death/Dreams).

The Stormweavers (Linn/Ykrul Influence)


Focus: Weather magic and storm riding. Signature Rite: Storm Dancing—through ritual movement, the witch calls forth or calms storms. Patron Element: Air, with strong ties to Raéyn or Xhak’Thul.

Threshold Magic and Promise Timers

Many workings create obligations. Represent these with a Promise Timer (typically 4 segments):

  • Mark the timer when the promise is ignored, deferred, or disrespected.

  • When filled, the price comes due—socially, spiritually, or narratively.

  • Examples: sharing grief, tending a grave, protecting a hearth, reconciling rivals.

The Daughters of the Unbroken Chord (The Cord)

Among the most feared and misunderstood covens are the Daughters of the Unbroken Chord—also called the Gray Watchers, Pale Midwens, or simply the Chain. They appear as midwives, hospice nurses, and deathbed witnesses, offering the mercy of warm hands and the comfort of final words. They seem harmless. Mostly.

The Daughters are bound to the High Mother, a threshold entity sustained by accumulated promise and subtle sacrifice. She may be a lich. She may be the current Vessel: a mortal woman elected by the coven, granted godlike power through centuries of accumulated influence. The Daughters smile when asked which is true. The Cord does not distinguish between memory and flesh.

Becoming a Daughter

  • Attributes: Spirit 3+, Body 2+

  • Skills: Lore (Thresholds) 1+, Deception 1+

  • Talents: Disciplined Body (Monk) [3 XP], Gray Thread [4 XP] (see below)

Gray Thread (4 XP)


Requires: Disciplined Body, Spirit 3+. You are bound to the High Mother’s Cord. Your flesh accepts tattooed Rites; your blood activates them. You may perform Rites of the High Mother without a Codex or Thiasos. Instead, you receive a Hollowed—a heartless familiar with eyes like spilled milk. It witnesses your magic and counts as the threefold presence required by Hag Law. The Hollowed never speaks.

Cord of Flesh (8 XP)


Requires: Gray Thread, Body 3+. Your body stores memories and promises. When you convert Harm \(\rightarrow\) Fatigue (Monk ability), you may store up to Body rating points of Fatigue as Chained Knots, appearing uninjured (as fresh scars or weeping tattoos). You have until sunset after the next to perform a sacrifice or take 2 Harm.

The Open Palm, Closed Cord (8 XP)


Requires: Gray Thread, Melee 2+. Your unarmed strikes or subtle touch carry Widow’s Measure. Instead of dealing Harm, you may mark a Promise Timer [4] on the target—they owe the Cord a truth or grief. If they already owe the coven, you may activate that weight: they suffer Position –1 for the scene, or you gain Effect +1 against them.

The Subtle Sacrifice

The Daughters require domestic sacrifices—acts so small they are dismissed as accidents or cruelty, yet sufficient to feed the Cord.

  • The Missing Child: Taken to the House Without Doors. The child is hollowed out into a Heartless familiar. The parents forget they existed (Un-Naming).

  • The Unspoken Grief: At a deathbed, the Daughter convinces the dying to forget their greatest love. The memory is trapped in a Name-Bead. The dying smile peacefully, having forgotten why they were sad.

Performing a sacrifice clears 1 segment of Obligation with the High Mother or reduces a Rite’s cost by 1 (minimum 1). Each requires Intricate Description and leaves physical evidence.

Player Takeaway

Witchcraft is not a separate magic system—it is a way of working that any character with the right talents can learn. A Runekeeper can learn Hedge Gifts. An Invoker can perform a Quick Working. A character with no Patron at all can become a capable hedge-witch, tending thresholds and binding small promises. The power of witchcraft is not in its flash, but in its patience. The world remembers. And the witch knows where to touch it.
