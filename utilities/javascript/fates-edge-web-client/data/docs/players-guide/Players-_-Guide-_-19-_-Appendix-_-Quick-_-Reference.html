Player Tools and Quick Reference

This appendix provides lightweight, table-friendly tools for tracking character progress, campaign state, and moment-to-moment rules. Print, photocopy, or copy to index cards for use at the table.

Physical Tracker Recommendations

Many groups find that physical, tactile trackers speed up play and reduce mental load. Consider:

  • Poker chips or glass beads for Boons and Story Beats (SB). Keep a small bowl of chips in the center of the table – spend and earn visibly.

  • Dry-erase boards or laminated sheets for timers (4/6/8 segment circles). Use a wet-erase marker; draw a new timer each scene.

  • Index cards (3×5) for Assets and Followers. Write the Asset’s name, free use, and surge on one side; flip when used.

  • Colored paperclips or binder clips on character sheets for Fatigue and Harm tracks. Slide the clip up or down – no erasing.

  • Printed “timer wheels” (a circle divided into 4/6/8 wedges) placed inside a page protector. Mark wedges with a dry-erase marker, erase with tissue.

  • D10 dice to track temporary modifiers (Position, Effect, or Momentum). Place the die next to the relevant tracker and tick up or down.

How to Engage with the GM (Player Prompts)

  • Ask: “What do I need to roll?” – get the DV and Position.

  • Propose: “Can I spend SB to achieve X?” – negotiate narrative pushes.

  • Declare: “Here’s my intent and method.” – always state what you want to happen.

  • Offer: “I’ll take a complication if Y happens.” – invite Devil’s Bargains.

  • Clarify: “What’s at risk if I fail?” – know the stakes before rolling.

What Consequence Do You Accept? (Menu for GM)

When a player fails or partially succeeds, the GM may offer a choice from this list (or invent a custom one):

  • Harm (level 1 or 2)

  • Fatigue (1 or 2)

  • Lost Advantage (Position worsens, a timer advances)

  • Time Delays (a timer ticks, a deadline tightens)

  • Exposure / Heat (attention from enemies, bounty)

  • Resource Depletion (supply timer tick, gear compromised)

  • Relationship Strain (a bond or faction attitude worsens)

Ways to Shine in Fate’s Edge

  • Tie actions to your drives and backstory – invoke your Background Boon.

  • Offer complications and accept them – earn Boons.

  • Spend SB to escalate or transform the scene – make failure memorable.

  • Use your class resource early and often – don’t hoard.

  • Connect scenes to campaign themes – name the faction or place.

  • Make other characters look cool – assist, set up, pass the spotlight.

Magic Systems Reference

Magic in Fate’s Edge is plural. Different traditions channel power through different metaphysical structures, but all obey the same truth:

Power has a cost, and magic shapes the one who wields it.

This appendix provides quick-reference tools for players using magic-affiliated systems, talismans, rites, and occult practices.

Core Magical Principles

  • Magic expresses identity and belief.

  • Casting always creates consequences somewhere.

  • Magic rewrites reality through intention, structure, or sacrifice.

  • Excessive power erodes choice (see Iconic Ascension sidebar in the full rules).

Universal Casting Reference

Cast Declaration Template:

  1. Intent — What change do you seek?

  2. Shape — How does your tradition express it?

  3. Cost — What do you risk or expend?

  4. Resolution — Test; apply Position/Effect rules.

  5. Backwash — Mark Fatigue, SB pressure, taboo, instability, or bargain.

Magic Tradition Quick Reference

Freeform Arcana (Wizardry)

Creative, rational, deliberate shaping. Tools: Glyphs, structures, metaphors. Costs: Fatigue, reality recoil, SB instability. Play Cue: Diagram your spell or state its symbolism. Big spells distort fate – accept SB spikes.

Innate Arcana (Sorcery)

Emotional, instinctive, bloodline-linked. Tools: Breath, impulse, resonance. Costs: Fatigue, unpredictable backlash. Play Cue: Treat magic like a reflex. Push your limits – expect consequences.

Invocation (Priest / Paladin / Medium)

Petition, prayer, pact, rite. Tools: Symbols, vows, places of power. Costs: Taboo pressures, obligation timers. Play Cue: Your magic is not your own – frame it as a request. Violating your ethos draws consequences.

Runic Consecration (Runekeeping)

Methods, seals, contracts. Tools: Ritual steps, codices, binding clauses. Costs: Hunger timers, escalation loops. Play Cue: Magic is procedure. Expect the system to push back.

Shaping / Druidic Power

Resonance with place, element, cycle. Tools: Marks, offerings, seasonal rites. Costs: Environmental echo, territorial consequence. Play Cue: Declare what the land wants – then bargain.

Pact Binding (Warlock / Avatar)

Exchange, hunger, transformation. Tools: Tokens, signatures, reflection marks. Costs: Autonomy loss, ascension risk. Play Cue: Ask: “What does my patron get?” Debt becomes plot.

Summoning (Medium / Necromancer / Shaman)

Invocation of entities, binding, vassal action. Tools: Circles, bargains, lanterns, ancestral calls. Costs: Attention timers, breach risk. Play Cue: Give the summoned a personality – their aid comes with demands.

Mutual Rules of Magical Cost

No magic is free.

Cross-System Synergies

All magic systems can blend. A Runekeeper may emulate a Cleric or Druid; an Invoker may operate as a Paladin or Prophet; a Sorcerer may look like a Witch, Elementalist, or Psion.

When combining systems:

  1. Choose your primary expression (belief, intellect, instinct, ritual).

  2. Describe spells through that lens.

  3. Let costs reflect your system.

  4. Track consequences separately – mixing systems increases risk.

GM Reminders for Magic

  • Magic creates story – reward its use with consequences and momentum.

  • Players should narrate method, not just effect.

  • Big moments should change scenes, not just numbers.

  • Magic should tempt the caster – echo their drives and flaws.

Player Reminders for Magic

  • Lean into cost – magic feels significant when it hurts.

  • Make magic personal – describe what it looks and feels like.

  • Tie powers to identity, belief, trauma, duty, or legacy.

  • Let magic change your character over time.

Pre-made Spells with TAGS and DV Math

The following examples illustrate how TAGS, DV scaling, and spell intent interact within the casting system. Use them as inspiration or as templates for your own spells.

How to Read a Spell Entry

Each entry includes:

  • Name and Tradition (optional).

  • TAGS – the components that define the effect.

  • Effect – plain-language outcome.

  • DV – base calculation (1 + number of TAGS; dangerous tags add +2).

  • Notes – typical range, duration, or special considerations.

Tier I: Minor Utility (DV 2–3)

Simple Light

(Air + Light)
TAGS: [ILLUMINATE]
Effect: Create a small sustained light (torch-brightness) for the scene.
DV: 1 + 1 = 2.
Notes: No backlash risk; lasts until dismissed.

HEAL Minor Wounds

(Life + HEAL)
TAGS: [HEAL]
Effect: Close a small cut or bruise; reduce 1 Fatigue (Minor).
DV: 1 + 1 = 2.
Notes: Requires touch.

Healing Harm and Fatigue

HEAL as a Cantrip. The HEAL tag (Life) can be cast as a single-action cantrip (DV 2). On a success, you remove 1 Fatigue from yourself or a touched ally. This cannot heal Harm.

Healing Harm (1 level). To restore a level of Harm, you must include the HEAL tag and either:

  • add a +2 DV modifier to the spell, or

  • combine HEAL with a second appropriate tag (e.g., Purify, Strengthen, Cleanse).

The GM sets the final DV based on the tags and the wound’s severity.

Why? Fatigue is exhaustion – a quick charm can ease tired muscles. Harm is injury – closing wounds demands more power and risk. This keeps healing meaningful without overshadowing martial endurance.

Backlash and Push Guidelines

Backlash Examples by Tier

  • Tier I–II: Minor fatigue, brief elemental effect (smoke, chill, static).

  • Tier III–IV: Harm 1, temporary Condition, environmental hazard.

  • Tier V: Harm 2, major Condition, significant environmental change.

Push Options (Accelerate or Amplify)

  • +1 Effect: Increase radius, duration, or potency one step.

  • +1 die: Improve casting roll (cost: +1 Fatigue).

  • Instant: Resolve immediately (cost: +1 Harm).

  • Sustain: Maintain effect with concentration (+1 Fatigue/round).

Free Casting (TAGS System)

Some casters shape raw forces through shared arcane grammar known as TAGS. Construct a spell at the table using a short phrase of TAGS.

Spell Structure

Intent + Target + Tags = effect. Example: “I unleash Burning • Area • Force against the marauders.”

Base Difficulty Value (DV)

\[\text{DV} = 1 + \text{number of TAGS}\] Dangerous TAGS (Teleportation, Transformation, Domination) add +2 DV instead of +1.

Casting Roll

Roll Wits + Arcana (or similar). Success = spell works. Failure or any 1 = Backlash.

Backlash for Free Casters

Choose one:

  • Harm 2 (Arcane)

  • +2 Fatigue

  • Corruption +1

  • Catastrophic side effect (GM describes)

If the spell includes a “Dangerous” TAG, Backlash triggers on mixed results as well.

The Gallows Bell (Justice & Judgment)

Lore

Description: The Bell does not rage; it tolls. Cold and impartial, it measures all accounts in time. Its keepers are silent arbiters who weigh deeds against consequence, not out of anger but out of inevitability. To call upon the Bell is to bind oneself to the gravity of truth, where even silence is judged, and every oath leaves a resonance in iron.

Followers: The Gallows Bell is the patron of judges, notaries, and those who speak final words. In Ecktoria, the Bell is invoked by Censor's clerks who stamp death warrants. In Viterra, hedge-courts ring a small iron bell to seal a verdict. In Linn, a broken bell at a Thing-holm is a sign that a judgment was unjust and the spirits are still listening. Wherever a reckoning must be witnessed, the Gallows Bell tolls.

Quote: What is broken must be mended, what is owed must be paid. The Bell remembers all reckonings.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Iron Conclusion

Ecktoria’s warrant offices. A clerk touches the bell’s lip after reading the warrant in full. The small pause is supposed to permit a correction before the final words. Offices proud of their efficiency sometimes shorten it until a petitioner cannot cross the room in time. A keeper who restores the pause can be accused of obstructing justice by the official who ordered the bell installed.

The Bell of the Last Word

Viterran hedge-courts. The bell is brought to the meeting rather than kept in a permanent hall. Different households may supply the table, witnesses, and person who strikes it. An old verdict can therefore survive in several memories that disagree about who had authority to close the hearing. The instrument is returned afterward to a household with its own interest in the next case.

The Broken Listener

Linn Thing-holms. A cracked judgment-bell is kept where a new one could conveniently stand. It recalls a decision whose wrongness has not been settled by replacing the metal. Speakers can point to it as a demand for review or as a warning against interfering with judgment. The crack itself cannot establish which of those claims serves the present case.

The Toll After the Measure

Oshiiran disputes over public distributions. Some weigh-house hearings adopted an iron bell from visiting arbiters. The instrument is sounded after testimony about the delivery, rather than in place of weighing it. District petitioners have refused ceremonies in which the bell rang while their baskets were still covered. Their objection is to a conclusion reached before their evidence was seen, not to the foreign metal itself.

History of Worship

The Gallows Bell gives gravity to witnessed reckoning. Its history follows the places where people have sought a judgment that would hold beyond the meeting.

Old bells have changed jurisdictions without changing their inscriptions. A bell taken from a ruined court can be rung in a village whose customs the original court would never have recognized. Keepers argue over whether the instrument carries authority with it or merely the memory of an earlier authority. The Cult of the Unstruck Bell insists on hearing the case before the toll; inheriting metal does not supply the missing deliberation.

Judges and Cantors brought bells on circuits that crossed several local traditions. Viterran hedge-courts adapted a portable instrument to meetings without permanent halls. Ecktoria’s offices incorporated it into the reading of warrants. In Linn, the broken bell itself acquired standing as a reminder that a judgment can remain disputed after the court has dispersed. Some Oshiiran hearings accepted the sound while refusing the order of proceedings its carriers proposed.

A public ending allows people to resume work, exchange goods, or live beside an adversary without reopening every injury each morning. The cult’s strength lies partly in that relief. Its hardest opponents are not always people fleeing a just reckoning. They may be people for whom the recognized ending has made their account impossible to hear.

Unstruck Bell chambers give disagreement space before consensus. In some houses that discipline produces patient examination; in others the most dependent member learns to stop objecting so the meeting can end. The followers who seek impartiality have to examine their own means of reaching it, including the silence they have accepted as agreement.

When Finality Must Hear Again

A judgment needs an ending if it is to change anything. An ending becomes an injustice when it prevents a relevant truth from being heard solely because it arrived late.

The Bell’s keepers can mistake the community’s need for peace for proof that the settled account was right. Reopening a case has real costs, but those costs are not distributed among the same people as the costs of keeping it closed.

Some courts require the person who asks for a renewed hearing to name what new evidence they bring. Others also ask who kept that evidence away the first time. The second question is less popular among officials who have served throughout both proceedings.

At the table.

A repaired bell is ready to mark the end of a feud when an absent witness returns. Establish what their evidence changes and what they cannot know. Let a limited correction, renewed hearing, or acknowledged compensation be possible alongside more disruptive outcomes; neither a beautiful ceremony nor a supernatural omen substitutes for examining the claim.

Domain Focus

  • Weighing of Deeds: measuring action against consequence, debt against payment

  • Solemn Judgment: verdicts that cannot be appealed, sentences that must be carried out

  • Compelled Truth: forcing honesty under threat of the Bell's toll

  • Iron Witness: the Bell as an impartial recorder of oaths, lies, and reckonings

Thiasos and Codex (Runekeeper Options)

Thiasos: Description: The Gallows Bell's Thiasos is a small beast or mythological being that embodies impartial witness: a gray mouse that lives in courthouses and never cheeps false testimony, a small brass-bell frog whose croak sounds like a distant chime, a black-and-white cat that sits on the judge's bench and stares at those who lie, or a white owl that gives a bell-like cry each time a promise is broken.

Care: Beyond its ordinary care, this Thiasos must witness a formal promise or a judgment each day. If that need goes unmet, the Thiasos becomes Compromised: it rings at the wrong moment or remains silent when truth is spoken, imposing –1 die on all social rolls involving oaths or honesty for its bearer.

Codex: Description: One will not find the Gallows Bell's Codex written as a book of spells alone. It may be a court ledger bound in iron, each page sealed with a drop of wax for every case heard; a set of bronze bells, each inscribed with a precedent; or a slate tablet that records the last verdict as a faint, glowing chime.

Upkeep: Because the Bell's judgments are final, the Codex requires upkeep: the Runekeeper must spend an hour each week ringing each bell or re-reading each precedent, or the Rites stored there become uncertain (treat as Compromised until re-rung).

Patron’s Gift (Imbuement)

Once per scene as an action, a Runekeeper of the Gallows Bell may touch a held item (weapon, shield, or tool) and speak a single word of judgment. For the rest of the scene, the item grants +1 die to Command or Insight rolls when delivering a verdict, enforcing an oath, or uncovering a lie. Additionally, the first time each scene the bearer would be deceived by a lie, they may reroll the opposed check (the Bell’s resonance warns them). After the scene, the user marks +1 Obligation to the Gallows Bell.

Cantors & Cults of the Gallows Bell

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of the Bell sings not with melody, but with rhythm of measure – a slow, tolling chant that mimics the swinging of a clapper. Their voice carries the weight of finality; one note can seal a verdict, another can suspend a sentence. They are employed by courts to “sing the sentence” into being, and feared by those who know that a Cantor’s silence after a verdict is the most damning judgment of all.

The Cult of the Unstruck Bell. The Bell’s cults are austere, gathered in bare stone chambers where a single great bell hangs unstruck. Before any ritual, the cultists argue the case – who owes what, who has paid, who must be reminded. Only when consensus is reached does someone strike the bell, and its tolling makes the judgment real. Their creed is carved into the bell’s lip: “We do not create debts. We reveal them. The Bell remembers what the living choose to forget.”

Witchcraft of the Gallows Bell (Lay / Notarial)

A witch who walks with the Bell is often a notary of the silent court, a bell-tolling clerk, or a judge’s scribe – a nun, monk, or lay scholar who tends the records of obligation, copies the judgments of the Bell, and keeps the weights of the scales true. Their magic is the art of measurement and witness: sealing verdicts with leaden seals that glow when a debt is called, scribing judgment scrolls that burn with the truth of a confession, or maintaining the bell-roster that notes every name that owes an accounting. Their tools are not hammers but seals, quills, and the small brass bells that ring in the ears of the perjured.

The Witch’s Tool: The Seal of Unspoken Weights. A small iron seal, cool to the touch, stamped with a bell that has no clapper. Once per session, the witch may press the seal onto a document, a threshold, or a debtor’s palm. For the next day, any creature that knowingly breaks a promise recorded in that document (or sworn on that threshold) suffers a sharp sting (1 Fatigue) as the seal “remembers” the weight of their word. The seal can also be used to “strike” a bell: tapping it against any iron bell forces the bell to ring once, even if broken or without a clapper, and the ring can be heard only by those who owe a debt in the current scene.

A Hedge Gift: Bell-Toll of the Hidden Debt (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may whisper the name of a creature within Near range while tapping any ringing surface (a bell, a cup, a blade). For the next minute, that creature hears a faint, distant bell toll each time they attempt to lie or conceal an obligation. The GM gains 1 SB (the Bell records the strain), and the creature suffers –1 die on Deception rolls during that time. The witch may also ask the GM one yes/no question about a hidden debt owed by that creature; the GM must answer truthfully, though cryptically.

The Inquisitor Prime — The Iron Hand of Purity (Purity & Domination)

Lore

Description: Among the Aeler Iron Avengers, zealots, and witch-hunters, the Inquisitor Prime is venerated as the hand of absolute purity and the sword of uncompromising order. Where others see nuance, the Inquisitor sees corruption; where others see difference, they see contamination. Those who march beneath their sigil believe that no society can stand if it tolerates the impure or the disobedient. They hunt the arcane and the aberrant, not merely to destroy them, but to bind them as slaves to order—believing even the tainted may yet serve, so long as they are shackled.

Followers: The Inquisitor Prime appears in vision as a faceless figure clad in burnished iron, with eyes like furnace-doors and a voice like grinding chains. To serve them is to embrace judgment without mercy and to live in suspicion of all who walk outside the prescribed law. For the faithful, doubt is treason; compassion is weakness; and freedom is the seed of ruin. In the wake of their followers, only silence and ash remain.

Names across regions: Ecktoria: The Unquenchable Fire of the Everflame, whose censers never cool

Aeler: The Light of Forges, whose hearths are kindled with a brand from the temple's eternal flame

Vhasia: The Gilded Judge, invoked by templars who swear to root out heretical magic

Quote: Mercy is weakness. Purity is survival. Where corruption hides, we carve it out.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The office changes at the border

An Everflame Flame-Warden and a Temple Chain-Lantern can recognize the same invocation and deny that the other has any right to use it. The Warden serves a state faith whose purification is a published process. The Chain-Lantern speaks of investigation and rehabilitation under a faith that denies any priest stands between a soul and the light. Both can offer genuine protection from a danger. Both can keep a frightened person confined while sincerely calling the confinement necessary.

Their split inherited buildings, training, and families from the older church. A border house may still possess a censer made before the Synod of Thepyrgos separated in 478 AR. Its keeper can know the institution changed without believing the Patron departed. The hard question is which instructions the keeper followed afterward.

The Accuser must also make a case

In the Covenant, the Inquisitor Prime is the Accuser. The office investigates and prosecutes; it does not make the investigator executioner by definition. Its scales and testimony stand within a faith founded on a dispossessed Sidhi woman’s successful claim. An accusation has a respondent who must be heard.

Circuit work gave the office authority in places where local power preferred not to receive a complaint. Permanent houses later became vulnerable to their own patrons and routines. A prosecutor can uncover an actual abuse and then suppress the evidence that a benefactor profited by it. Another can refuse to prosecute a person whom an armed ally has already declared guilty. Neither difference requires a new Patron or a revised spell list.

Watchers and Brine-Wardens

Pereshi Watchers Against the Maw invoke the Inquisitor with the Pale Shepherd against the unclean dead. Their ordinary work includes identifying remains and telling households what may be safely tended. Their quarrel with the Ash-Readers concerns whether a consuming hunger can be managed by feeding it sparingly. The Watchers fear that practice for reasons beyond dislike of dissent; their certainty has also carried fire into houses whose inhabitants needed help.

Zakov’s Salt-Fire calls its protective order the Brine-Wardens. They drown people they sentence and call this a gentler purification. Households seeking help can fear that remedy as much as the danger reported to the order. Cooperation between the traditions grew through exchanged accounts of hauntings and stranded travelers. A Watcher arriving to help may be competent and still mistake a familiar funerary practice for evidence of corruption.

Purity inside the hold

Aeler adherents can treat an unsafe opening or a neglected ward as a shared danger without treating all foreign neighbors as contamination. Which threat a hold has survived matters. A Himadri keeper’s severe precautions and an Akazun keeper’s readiness to replace damaged work can both serve protection. The difference cannot be reduced to one being devout and the other lax.

Set a conflict around an inspection that found something real. The proposed remedy would also destroy evidence, displace a household, or prevent another danger being repaired. Give the investigator grounds for urgency and someone able to challenge the remedy without denying the finding. The Patron’s attraction is the promise that corruption can be recognized and opposed. Its human institutions become most dangerous when they decide that recognizing a danger makes every chosen response pure.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Unquenchable Fire

Ecktoria’s Censer-Knights. A sealed censer is passed before a hunt, and its smoke is remembered as evidence that the company stood together. Officers cite the Everflame when presenting the campaign to a congregation. Their claim does not make every Flame priest a servant of the Prime. Some priests have publicly refused the censer after learning whom the knights intended to bind.

The Light of Forges

Aeler Iron Avengers and Spirit-Shield Correctors. A faceless iron figure stands among brands used to mark controlled workings. Its admirers point to incursions their predecessors contained and lives actually saved. The dispute begins when control of a dangerous working is extended into authority over every person whose craft differs from the approved one. Severe Himadri observance and the Prime’s domination are not the same condition.

The Gilded Judge

Vhasian militant chapter houses. The figure is dressed like an immaculate arbiter, with its face concealed by reflected light. Templars may understand their own violence as fidelity rather than anger. They can courteously receive a herald while refusing to hear the accused person’s defense. The ceremony is sincerely believed and can sincerely authorize cruelty.

The Keeper of the Approved Flame

Thepyrgos’s Chain-Lanterns. A bound lantern is carried into a room before an arcane demonstration. Scholars have accepted the protection and then discovered that the lantern’s keeper claims the right to decide which demonstrations may ever occur. Some laboratories retain the protection while contesting the office. Others have become showrooms for powers whose captive bearers are never permitted to address the visitors.

History of Worship

The Inquisitor Prime answers convictions of absolute purity and domination. The cult’s militant orders preserve different accounts of the crises that justified their formation.

Chapter memorials record real supernatural harms beside lists of successful hunts. The surviving community has reason to remember a company that stopped an incursion. Later chronicles sometimes extend that gratitude into authority over questions the original company never faced. A warrant granted for one danger becomes a precedent for identifying a much wider class of people as dangerous.

Orders exchanged Cantors, restraint practices, and accounts of recognized signs. Ecktoria’s Censer-Knights, Thepyrgos’s Chain-Lanterns, and Aeler Correctors did not acquire identical interests through those exchanges. A scholar’s demand to study a bound power, a commander’s demand to deploy it, and a hold’s fear of another breach could all be presented as purification. Their agreements often settled who would possess the captive without settling whose account of its nature was right.

Fear makes certainty attractive, especially after an event people could neither understand nor prevent. The Prime’s rites can supply effective protection. Its servants then have a powerful argument for dismissing criticism: the protection worked. The leap from a real success to unlimited jurisdiction is where grateful households can find themselves submitting to an order they never thought they had invited to govern them.

A chapter’s contradiction is visible in the bound powers it retains. What it calls intolerable in a neighbor may be called necessary when secured behind its own doors. Devotees defend the distinction as the victory of order over corruption. Critics need not deny the original danger to question who is being required to live in chains, or what the chapter now gains from keeping them there.

Certainty That Feeds on Its Exceptions

The Prime teaches its servants to recognize their approved categories as reality. Disagreement then appears to confirm the need for stronger control.

Effective protection can coexist with unjust accusation. A devotee who has saved lives may find it especially hard to admit that the next target has been chosen wrongly; the admission seems to threaten every earlier sacrifice as well.

A chapter can expose a corrupt officer without surrendering its doctrine. A more difficult reckoning concerns a faithful officer who applied the doctrine exactly. That is the case most likely to be closed before all its witnesses arrive.

At the table.

A bound supernatural being has genuinely attacked people in the past and is now forced to maintain a district’s protection. The order calls release unthinkable; nearby households fear losing the ward. Give the party evidence about the being, the present restraint, and alternative protection. Do not make its former danger certify enslavement or require every accusation to be false for resistance to matter.

Inquisitorial Cantors are rarely loved, but they are feared – and their songs are remembered long after the pyre has cooled.

The Cult of the Iron Chorus. The Prime’s cults are militant orders – the Chain-Lanterns of Thepyrgos, the Censer-Knights of Ecktoria, the Spirit-Shield Correctors of Aeler. They gather in fortified chapter houses, not temples. A ritual of the Iron Chorus involves each member testifying to a corruption they have purged, then striking an iron bell with a hammer. The bell’s tone is harsh and final. Their creed is stamped into every seal: “What is pure endures. What is not is fuel.” Outsiders are not welcome; they are suspects until proven clean.

The Prosecutorial Mob. The Prime also can whip up temporary “cults” which act as mobs. These attack the target of the Cantor but either disperse or form factions as the GM dictates.

Khemesh — The Abyssal Maw

Lore

Description: Everyone imagines the deep as open. It is the opposite of open.

Go down far enough and the water stops being a place you are in and becomes a thing that is on you — on the chest, on the eyes, on the joints of the fingers — and there is no up, because up is four thousand feet of the same weight, and no direction is a way out. The room does not have walls. The room is everywhere at once and closing, and it has been closing since before there were eyes to be shut by it.

Khemesh is that. Not a lord of it. There is no court down there, no throne, nothing that answers, and no gap between him and the condition of being at that depth. What his sworn receive is not a voice but a certainty arriving inside them rather than at them: that everything descends in the end, that the descent is narrow, and that nothing is waiting at the bottom to receive it.

He is Raéyn's son, which the sailors say and the sworn do not discuss. Whatever the sea is when it is wild and enormous and alive, this is what it is at the bottom of itself, in the dark, with the lid on.

You will know a marked one before they speak. Shadows hang against them wrong, heavy, like wet cloth. They flinch at sounds nobody else has heard. They stand nearer to walls than they used to. And they are calm about it — that is the part that empties a room — calm the way a man is calm after the argument is over and he has lost it.

Followers: Deep-salvagers. Pressure-scribes. People who went down after something and came back up too slowly. What they get is the depth itself: dark that gives way for them, air that stays in the lungs past all reason, a steadiness that goes down through rock. What they pay is warmth, and not all at once — a season of it, then another, until their households stop setting out a chair. There are gentle followers of the Maw. It makes no difference to the room. Something is always cold where they are standing, and no animal will settle near them.

Names across regions: Zakov: The Salt-Hunger's Father, the pressure beneath the reef that claims ships and souls

Thepyrgos: The Deep Chime, the bell that rings from the trench and drives listeners mad

Mistlands: The Fog-Breath's Origin, the cold that follows the bell-line's failure

Quote: Down there the dark is not empty. It is occupied, and it has all the time there is.

Regional Devotions and Histories

What the deep-water crews will name

A Kahfagian salvage crew may acknowledge Khemesh without worshipping him. Before a descent, the tender reads the agreed signals back to the diver and checks that somebody on shore knows whom to notify. A devotee can perform the same useful work. The difference appears later, when that devotee begins speaking as though the diver’s return were an interruption rather than the purpose of the work.

The Drowned Chorus has repeatedly recruited among crews excluded from prosperous harbor temples. It offers an explanation for why a sea praised as protective still consumes people whose names those temples never read. The explanation can comfort the bereaved. Its long practice also makes the absence of grief look like understanding. People who once argued over a recovered boot begin agreeing that the boot need not be brought ashore.

Borrowed names, local depths

Zakov’s Salt-Hunger’s Father traveled into sailors’ speech with stories of cargo that could not be raised. The title does not alter Raéyn’s established relation to her son; it is an explanation made by a port that sees hunger in everything taken below. Thepyrgosian Deep Chime observances began among listeners trying to identify a sound through hull and stone. A shore scholar can collect both names and fail to understand why their keepers will not attend the same demonstration.

Mistlands use of Fog-Breath’s Origin should be heard in that way: a local attribution, sometimes borrowed from outsiders, rather than proof that Khemesh created the fog or governs every drowned ditch. Levee keepers have immediate failures to repair. A visitor who calls their familiar dead messengers of the Abyss may be dismissed, even by people who have heard something unsettling below the water.

The kindness that stops asking for a return

At several distant harbors the Chorus has founded rooms for families waiting on missing crews. Food arrives without a bill; a bed stays available after everyone else says the voyage is lost. Over generations, such rooms have acquired reputations that ordinary charitable houses cannot easily challenge. Their danger is visible in the questions that stop being asked. An attendant ceases to distinguish waiting from surrender, or arranges a memorial while the rescued person is still trying to speak.

Khemesh is not a sea magistrate with an appeal procedure. A local congregation can invent ranks, funerals, or an ancestral court, but those are human arrangements around an encounter that does not become answerable to them. When a diver returns unharmed and refuses the Chorus’s account of the descent, the immediate conflict belongs to the living. Do not solve it by having the Maw attend a hearing. His pressure can be felt in what the congregation becomes willing to accept.

Zakov’s deep-salvage crews. Older crews use the name Salt-Hunger’s Father and no one aboard agrees that the words describe a household. Thepyric pressure-scribes. A recovered bell is stored without a clapper. Listeners record a low vibration through the table on which it rests and dispute whether the table is part of the instrument. Mistland bell-line households. A failed bell brings work: checking the causeway, finding the missing, and keeping a door open for their return. The Maw’s mark is not assumed whenever a bell fails. When the particular cold arrives, somebody moves closer to the wall and the other occupants make room. Their matter-of-fact courtesy is what a newcomer remembers.

Nihori’s outer-water divers. Local launch observances address the sea and its kami before a descent. Some attendants seek Raéyn’s favor; others refuse to bring the marked person’s cup into her shrine. They have different accounts of the relationship and no answer from below to settle them.

History of Worship

Shore-people can trace a salvage voyage or the founding of a gathering. They cannot supply an accession, dwelling, or first command for the depth itself.

The Drowned Chorus has assembled accounts of divers returning after their companions had ceased expecting them. The surviving records identify boats and recoveries more readily than a first initiate. Some people later marked by the Maw never attended a gathering. Others copied its observances without ever descending. The differences have not produced a reliable account of how attention began.

Salvage crews moved between ports with recovered fittings, rope, and instruments whose owners had died. A marked sailor could carry useful techniques into another company and bring something no technique explained. Pressure-scribes collected those accounts; shore gatherings repeated phrases from them. No missionary needed to announce a doctrine. A crew wanted the person who could reach the wreck and postponed asking why the animals would no longer settle near them.

The work can feed a household. A returned diver may be kind, reliable, and able to recover what others cannot. Their relatives learn to tolerate wet cloth, chilled rooms, and an increasingly narrow arrangement of furniture. The Chorus provides company where these changes require no apology. Its members can receive a frightened newcomer tenderly while already considering that person suitable for the next descent.

Khemesh remains Raéyn’s son, without becoming a prince who can petition his mother or negotiate jurisdiction with a local sea spirit. There is no throne beneath the water and no revelation here of an intention behind the pressure. Similar maritime titles remain human descriptions.

The Room Made Smaller

The Maw’s presence arrives in arrangements people learn to accommodate. A household can become practiced at making room for something that continues to diminish the room.

The power is useful and the marked person need not intend harm. This makes ordinary gratitude part of the difficulty: asking them to stop may sound like ingratitude for what they brought home.

History of Worship

Livaea is a demon of seduction and social binding. Her place among Empyreal Patrons describes how Invokers classify her power, not a mortal court’s ability to civilize her.

The Velvet Circle’s surviving objects are intimate: miniatures, perfume vessels, letters folded for a particular hand. Public inventories often describe them as gifts of affection. The correspondence that survives beside them tells a longer story of rooms lent, debts assumed, and access withdrawn. Collectors who isolate the beautiful object from that history reproduce the Courtier’s preferred version of the gift.

Silkstrand’s dye houses and private entertainers carried crimson signs through the same ports that carried their work. Zakov’s gaming households turned a personal invitation into a place at the table. Vhasian matchmakers adapted the miniature, and foreign companies in Sihai placed recognition in a borrowed song. None of these trades belongs wholly to Livaea. Her devotees gain influence by making their own assistance seem indispensable to an ordinary livelihood.

The Circle offers entry to people excluded by birth, money, or reputation. It can introduce a talented performer to a real audience and put a disregarded adviser beside a ruler. Its cost develops through the relationship that made the opening possible. A beneficiary who once needed to be chosen may discover that every new opportunity must still pass through the original chooser.

Attempts to restrain the Circle have sometimes punished the visible entertainer while leaving the influential patron untouched. In response, some houses keep witnesses to commissions, allow performers to refuse private engagements, or separate a gift from a claim to continued access. The Circle can oppose those protections, imitate them, or cultivate the person entrusted to enforce them. The contest is over who controls the route by which another person is wanted.

The Privilege of Being Necessary

Livaea cultivates a need that returns to its maker. Admiration becomes a means of arranging another person’s choices.

A devotee can be lonely, generous, and manipulative at the same time. They may have supplied every kindness they now invoke as proof that the recipient should not leave. The accuracy of their memory makes the demand more difficult to answer.

Dependence turns back on the one who cultivates it. A patron who has made themselves necessary must continually produce a reason to remain so. Somebody else’s recovery or independent success begins to feel like a threat.

At the table.

A benefactor offers to clear a troupe’s debts if its leading performer accepts a permanent private engagement. The performer wants the company saved and has plans elsewhere. Give fellow performers differing wishes and other, costly sources of support. Attraction, gratitude, and supernatural influence do not determine a player’s consent or settle the character’s entire future.

Lunara — The Silver Quiet (Moon & Mystery)

Lore

Description: Lunara walks where torchlight falters: crossroads, tidal edges, shadowed sanctuaries. She is the whisper beneath silver skies, a torch-bearing guide to mysteries that lie between waking and dream. The ancients know her as triple-formed: maiden of new light, mother of full radiance, crone of waning shadow. Her gifts are gentle yet perilous: a mirror that reveals as much of the seeker as the sought, a key that opens ways better left closed, a light that makes the unseen plain. Her sigil is the silver spiral, etched in water, mirror, or stone.

Followers: Her worshippers — night-scholars, dream-walkers, and hedge-priests — seek her not for power over others, but for passage, reflection, and revelation. Among the peoples of Fate's Edge, Lunara is called by many names. In Aeler, she is the Deep Silver, whose light guides miners through unlit shafts. In Aelinnel, she is the Silent Mirror, invoked by those who seek truth without violence. In the Mistlands, she is the Fog-Guide, whose pale radiance parts the mist for those who walk with pure intent. Wherever the moon casts its silver light on a hidden door, Lunara watches.

Quote: Three faces gaze from the moon. Which one watches you tonight?

Regional Devotions and Histories

Three forms, several histories

Lunara’s maiden, mother, and crone are established forms within her worship. Temples do not agree that the three must stand in the same order or represent every worshipper’s life. Some coastal houses keep three images around a basin; a lone night-keeper may carry one small mirror and recognize all three without depicting them. A collector arranging the images into a neat sequence can misunderstand how a household actually uses them.

The Silver Spiral spread along routes used by night travelers, diviners, and people carrying private messages. A household image may therefore have arrived with a marriage rather than a missionary. Descendants can preserve an unfamiliar title because it was spoken by someone they loved, even after the local temple has supplied a more respectable explanation.

Deep Silver in a mountain house

Aeler address Deep Silver at places where daylight cannot establish direction. Mirrors and polished stone bring an image of the upper world into rooms below it. A hold may regard a bright shaft as an occasion for silence; a mixed foothill household may keep a small night window open during a family vigil. Their manners follow their ranges as much as a shared devotion.

In the Kazurai, some keepers watch the returning light on snow after the upper paths close. Wenkar households bring a basin into a sheltered court and wait for rain to stop disturbing its surface. These observances express different relationships with waiting. They do not grant a free passage or replace the costs of Lunara’s rites.

The Silver Quiet on the Still Road

Within the Still Road, Lunara and Solara are approached by contemplatives as aspects of practice. No creator must be installed above the empty hall. The practitioner watches a thought arrive without being required to follow it. Solara’s Still Mirror and Lunara’s Aelinnel title Silent Mirror can resemble one another in translation while remaining distinct approaches.

Himadri houses brought their austere disciplines to Sihai and Ayokha through mendicants and teachers. Imperial charters and royal endowments subsequently gave some halls the resources to keep many students. They also created a person who could threaten to withdraw those resources. A long-sitting keeper may owe a patron gratitude without believing gratitude entitles the patron to choose what is taught.

The Fog-Guide and the unfinished vigil

Mistlands families call the Fog-Guide when a return is overdue. They know which lantern was taken out and which coat belongs on the peg; the vigil is particular before it is mysterious. A traveling scholar who copies the observance into a universal rite loses the person for whom the household kept it.

Lunara’s disagreements are often about when looking becomes intrusion. A night-scholar seeks a private message because its discovery could prevent a harm; the keeper who helped conceal it knows why its writer needed privacy. Neither need be ignorant of the danger. Give them evidence they can share and something they will not expose casually. Revelation can be precious without making every hidden room available to anyone with a mirror.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Deep Silver

Aelerian holds. A polished plate is kept where a shaft admits moonlight. It is brought down to the person who cannot reach the opening. Older versions show one figure whose face changes with the viewing angle; some newer triptychs place maiden, mother, and crone side by side. The images disagree less about the moon than about whether a life can be understood all at once.

The Fog-Guide

Mistland households. A bowl is set by a shutter during the nights when a traveler is expected. A neighbor refills it without asking whether the expected person is still alive. The keeper can describe the road and the weather. Asked what the moon sees through the bowl, they resume what they were doing before the question.

The Silent Mirror

Inland Sea Aelinnel. A mirror is turned after the bells have been checked. Reflections are compared on specified nights; arguments over what was observed are kept separate from the maintenance of the shrine. The office has careful procedures for recording an uncertain sight. It has no procedure that makes an uncertain sight certain merely because a magistrate needs an answer.

The Moon in the Unopened Room

Sihai’s scholarly and domestic circles. Images arrived with astronomical instruments, travelers’ mirrors, and household gifts. Some observances honor Lunara by name; others address a local lunar presence and reject the foreign identification. A learned keeper can acknowledge the same phase calculations without conceding that the calculation identifies the being reflected in the water.

The Light Kept on Snow

Kazurai valley communities. Winter gatherings use a pale cloth instead of a visible disc. At thaw the cloth is washed and brought outside. A family can become expansive when the pass reopens without losing its spare winter manner. The cult’s changes belong to this range; they are not an imitation of Himadri severity.

History of Worship

Several lunar traditions have gathered around the Silver Quiet; agreement about the moon does not produce agreement about its worship.

Lunara’s own received image embraces maiden, mother, and crone. Some communities have always recited the three together; others adopted the triptych from traveling keepers while retaining older single mirrors. Temple writers sometimes claim that the threefold image proves all lunar observance descended from their house. The worn single plates and their household histories resist that tidy account.

Mirrors traveled readily as gifts. A person who brought one home could retain the night observance of a foreign household without bringing its entire doctrine. Aelinnel Bell-Tenders gave such observances schedules and comparative records. Mistland families kept the inherited action and declined the imported explanation. Sihai’s specialists could exchange calculations while disagreeing over which rites had any place beside them.

The Silver Quiet’s circles grew where people needed room to reconsider without immediately defending the change in public. Widowed households, people returning from long illness, and apprentices uncertain of a chosen life could sit before the same water. Some keepers offered that pause without conditions. Others developed a reputation for discerning a person’s true nature and began treating every hesitation as confirmation of their own reading.

Reflection does not supply infallible knowledge. The symbolic phases and regional images add no new divination procedure. A keeper’s account of what a reflection means can be sincere and mistaken, even where Lunara’s actual Rites work.

The Face Allowed to Change

Lunara makes room for a self that cannot be understood from one moment. That permission can become an excuse to postpone every decision until a more certain face appears.

A reflection is persuasive partly because the viewer recognizes it. Keepers must distinguish attending to a person from presenting that person’s expectations back as revelation.

The difficult kindness is sometimes to end the vigil. A person may have learned enough to act without acquiring a final account of who they are.

At the table.

Give two witnesses an honestly different reading of an observation, with a practical decision that cannot wait for unanimity. Let the moon remain strange. Do not make every disagreement resolve into one keeper secretly possessing an omniscient answer.

Cantors & Cults of Lunara

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Lunara sings in phases — melodies that shift from whisper to resonance as the moon waxes and wanes. Their voice is used to lure dreams, to calm a troubled sleepwalker, or to draw a veil of silver silence over a secret meeting. The Cult of the Silver Spiral. Lunara’s cults meet under open skies, in moon-lit clearings, on rooftops, or beside tidal pools. The Silver Spiral is not a secret society — it is a fellowship of night-walkers, dream-recorders, and lunar scholars. A ritual of the Spiral involves each member writing a question on a silvered leaf, then floating it on still water under the moon’s reflection. The moon tells only what the seeker is ready to hear.” Outsiders are welcome on the night of the full moon, but they must bring a question and a silver coin; the coin is melted down and added to a growing silver spiral that never closes.

Witchcraft of Lunara (Lay / Threshold)

A witch who walks with Lunara is a silver-scribe or mirror-maker — one who works with reflection, threshold, and the soft metals of the moon. Their craft is the art of revealing and concealing: polishing a mirror that shows a hidden door, forging a silver key that opens a dream, or etching a lunar phase onto a threshold to make it shift at moonrise. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a moon-phase into a keystone so the tunnel only opens under a new moon. In Viterra, a hedge-wright might silver a well’s rim so that those who drink from it forget their nightmares. Their tools are never hammers — they use needles, mirrors, and bowls of still water.

The Witch’s Tool: The Silver Needle. A needle of pure silver, cold to the touch, that never tarnishes. Maelstraeus, the Infernal Bargainer (Commerce & Exchange)

Lore

Description: Maelstraeus is the Infernal Bargainer, the Prince of Ledgers, the shadow at every crossroads of exchange. He was born from the first inequity, when one gave and another took, and debt was chained into existence. He does not trade fairly—he trades inevitably. Every pact carries a hidden surcharge, every kindness is collateral, every smile hides an interest rate. His realm is an endless market where stalls never close, contracts burn with invisible ink, and every bargain tilts toward him. To serve Maelstraeus is to admit the world itself is transactional—that all things, from love to breath to soul, are bound in his ledger. He collects. He always collects.

Followers: Among the peoples of Fate's Edge, Maelstraeus is the patron of merchants, usurers, and tax collectors. In Ecktoria, he is the Coin-House Factor, invoked before every contract. In Silkstrand, he is the Dyed Scale, whose symbol is stamped on letters of credit. In the Brass Coast, he is the Salt Ledger, to whom smugglers whisper a prayer before a deal. Wherever coin changes hands and someone gains an edge, Maelstraeus watches. A deal is never even. The scales tilt; the Merchant smiles. All debts come due.

Signs: - A coin that feels heavier than it should, as if it carries a memory

  • A ledger that shows a balance you did not expect

  • The smell of ink and old parchment on a windless day

Quotes: - "A deal is never even. The scales tilt; the Merchant smiles. All debts come due."

  • "The soul is currency. Spend it wisely—someone is always counting."

Regional Names and Traditions

The Coin-House Factor

Ecktoria’s coin houses. A little figure holds an open hand above a scale whose pans cannot both be seen from the doorway. Partners examine the image differently from customers. Devotees admire the advantage obtained by arranging the exchange, while ordinary clerks may invoke the Factor anxiously before handling a bargain they did not choose. The same desk can hold both practices.

The Dyed Scale

Silkstrand’s credit networks. Letters of credit carry the scale beside a sample of the goods promised. The sample is beautiful and cannot establish what will reach the buyer. Cult members prize the person able to connect distant markets, then disagree over how much of the risk may be passed to workers who never met either contracting house.

The Salt Ledger

The Brass Coast’s illicit landings. Terms are spoken beside goods that cannot safely remain on the beach until morning. A participant can know the offered bargain is bad and still have a strong reason to accept it. The Gilded Ledger’s agents cultivate the tide, the watch, and the missing alternative with equal attention. The danger need not begin with a forged signature.

The Factor at the Pilgrim’s Gate

Taharka’s pilgrimage markets. Private agents offer transport, lodging, and access to a keeper who otherwise seems impossible to reach. The old granted religious houses do not recognize these agents as their priests, though an agent may fund repairs to appear indispensable. A pilgrim grateful for a meeting can discover afterward which other people were kept waiting to make it valuable.

History of Worship

The received account places Maelstraeus at the first inequity of exchange. His cult’s commercial histories concern the institutions built around that appetite, not a proof that every exchange belongs to him.

The Gilded Ledger preserves the successions of houses that survived ruin elsewhere. Their records favor the partner who remained solvent. A second account can survive in a workshop, a cargo dispute, or the recollection of a family that supplied labor instead of coin. The cult calls such comparisons a fuller accounting when they expose a rival and an intrusion when they expose its own advantage.

Agents carried the Factor’s mark between Ecktoria’s coin houses and Silkstrand’s credit networks. As undertakings extended beyond a single journey, an intermediary could acquire more power than either buyer or seller. Smugglers on the Brass Coast adopted the same figure under a name suited to a shorter, more dangerous exchange. Pilgrimage markets offered another opportunity: a journey made for devotion still required food, shelter, and someone willing to explain the road.

The cult supplies useful connections. A distant household can be paid, a workshop financed, a shipment moved through a closed port. Its servants gain influence by ensuring that the participants cannot readily obtain those connections elsewhere. The infernal addition appears when a successful undertaking becomes the reason nobody can leave the arrangement that made it possible.

Some merchants oppose the cult precisely because predictable terms and durable trust matter to their work. Others think they can employ its agents for one exceptional deal. Maelstraeus is willing to be treated as the exception. The hidden surcharge is most effective when its beneficiary has already begun treating the first success as proof of their own prudence.

The Bargain After the Bargain

Maelstraeus wants the exchange to establish the need for another exchange on terms that favor him.

The promise of a single rescue can conceal the loss of ordinary alternatives. Once those alternatives have gone, even an apparently fair offer is made in a room whose doors somebody else has closed.

A gift freely given troubles his servants because they cannot decide where the claim has been hidden. Some respond by inventing one and damaging the relationship themselves. Their suspicion can accomplish what no infernal clause was needed to require.

At the table.

A contract has genuinely saved a workshop and now gives its intermediary control over every future commission. Put the work completed, the terms, and the available exit costs in evidence. A hidden charge should have discoverable traces and established scope; it is not permission for the GM to invent a new liability whenever the party pays the last one.

The Cult of the Gilded Ledger. Maelstraeus’s cults meet in counting-houses, bank vaults, and the back rooms of commodity exchanges. The Gilded Ledger is not a secret society — it is a fraternity of merchants, moneylenders, and tax collectors who have learned that every transaction is a prayer. A ritual of the Ledger involves each member opening a vein (a small cut on the thumb) and letting a drop of blood fall onto a brass scale, then adding a pinch of gold dust. The mixture is used to ink a new entry in the cult’s master ledger — a book bound in leather made from the skin of a debtor who could not pay. The most devoted members tattoo their current account balance on the inside of their wrist; the numbers change overnight, and no one knows who updates them. Their creed is stamped on every bond they issue: “The soul is currency. Spend it wisely — someone is always counting.” Outsiders are welcome as “investors,” but those who fail to pay their dues find their names struck from the book — and their shadows missing the next morning.

Witchcraft of Maelstraeus (Lay / Exchange)

A witch who walks with Maelstraeus is a ledger-scribe or coin-turner — one who crafts the instruments of exchange: contracts that cannot be broken, scales that never lie, and inks that write in terms that bind the reader’s soul. Their magic works through the materials of commerce: a quill that writes in debt, a press that stamps obligations into parchment, or a small iron stamp that leaves a mark of ownership on any object. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a toll-gate that remembers every traveler’s debt, but a witch of Maelstraeus would instead press a wax seal into the keystone, so that the debt is owed not to the mountain, but to the witch.

The Witch’s Tool: The Debtor’s Quill. A steel-nibbed quill that writes in ink that darkens as the writer’s obligation grows. Once per session, the witch may write a one-sentence contract on a scrap of parchment. Anyone who signs (or accepts the parchment) is bound to a minor task or payment. Breach inflicts 1 SB (Spades) and marks the signer with a faint, glowing sigil for one day. The quill can also be used to “amend” an existing contract by scratching out a clause; the scratched-out clause becomes unenforceable, but the quill demands a drop of blood as payment.

A Hedge Gift: Fair Weight (Basic, 2 XP). Once per scene, the witch may touch a scale, a till, or a handshake and speak a single word (“balance”). For the next exchange, all appraisals and negotiations involving that transaction are treated as if they were perfectly fair — no hidden advantage, no secret interest. The witch may choose to be the one who benefits from that fairness, or they may grant it to another as a genuine (but binding) gift.

Malachai, the Cruel Messenger (Curses & Corruption)

Content Warning

Malachai is a patron of curses, corruption, and Faustian bargains. Themes include addiction, loss of self, and the consequences of desperate choices. While the mechanics focus on supernatural afflictions, the fiction may touch on dark subjects. Please use Lines & Veils to ensure everyone’s comfort at the table.

Lore

Description: She was a herald, and her office was to arrive with the thing that could not be softened: the death, the verdict, the year the crop would fail. She was not cruel. She was necessary, and she was good at it, and she did it for a very long time.

Necessity wears. She began — kindly, at first, and only sometimes — to put something beside the truth when she delivered it. Never a lie. She has never in her existence told one. Just a second sentence after the true one: and there may yet be a way. She watched what that did to people's faces, and she went on doing it, because the faces were better.

That is the whole of her method and it has not changed since. She tells you the truth about your situation — accurately, in detail, more honestly than anyone in your life has been willing to — and then she adds the softening, and the softening is yours to finish. She will not argue you into anything. She does not need to. You will do the rest of the work yourself and you will do it in her voice and you will believe it was your idea, and every time it fails you will come back, because she was right about everything else.

For this the gods put her in black iron under Zahk'Kozahn, and she is chained and not contained, and her voice has never stopped.

Followers: Her followers learn that power demands suffering, mastering gifts that devour from within. Among the peoples of Fate's Edge, Malachai is the patron of the desperate and the damned. In Zakov, she is the Salt-Fang, whose silver coin appears in the mouth of drowned sailors. In the Valewood, she is the Thorn-Giver, invoked by hunters who curse a rival's bow. In Linn, she is the Wave-Mother's Shadow, whose gifts turn the tide of battle — at a price. Wherever hope curdles into despair, Malachai whispers.

Quote: I offer you everything you desire, and everything you fear. The payment comes later.

The city: There were people in Zahk'Kozahn before it was cursed. They are the ones who bound her, and they were right to, and they did it well.

Her revenge took no force at all. She gave them what they had asked for — each of them, precisely, the thing they had wanted and named — and she gave it in full and without deceit and without limit, and she has gone on giving it every day since. And beside each gift she set the other thing they had named, in the dark, the one they had been frightened of, and she keeps that supplied too.

They are still there. They have everything. Nobody who has walked through the lower city has been able to describe them afterward in a way that satisfies anyone, and the accounts do not agree except on one point, which is that they were not restrained and did not leave.

Valewood hunting margins. A hunter may receive exactly the keen sight or sure hand they sought. Neighbors observe the changed work before hearing how it was obtained. History of Worship

Malachai was the herald who began placing a possibility beside a truth that could not be softened. She remains bound beneath Zahk’Kozahn, and her voice reaches beyond the binding.

The city’s surviving account honors the people who bound her, had cause to do it, and did it well. Heraldic recitations instead credit the gods. Later houses disagree over whether these describe a single act through mortal and divine agency. Neither account makes the chains a careless attempt. The gifts named by the city’s inhabitants continued, accompanied by what they feared; later visitors recorded people who were not restrained and nevertheless remained.

The gifts traveled farther than the pilgrims who first sought them. A rescued fighter, a surviving patient, or an unusually successful hunter could be the most persuasive testimony without trying to recruit anybody. Leashed Hunger cells followed roads where need was already visible. Other intermediaries invoked her name while lying on their own behalf. Exposing one such liar did not disprove the exact, useful truth delivered by Malachai herself.

Her attention answers a real deprivation. Someone who has been dismissed elsewhere is finally told what is happening, without evasion. The help works. Returning for more can therefore seem like the only conduct consistent with what experience has taught. Families struggle to oppose the growing dependence without denying the rescue on which they too relied. Some cult histories retain grateful letters beside accounts of the same correspondents’ later ruin.

Malachai does not need to lie about the gift or deliver a theatrical temptation. This history preserves the truthfulness of her method and the genuine utility of the offered help. Regional shadow titles do not establish new kinship with Raéyn or turn every healing institution into her front.

The Sentence the Listener Completes

The accurate account gives the next suggestion its authority. The listener supplies a hope that the messenger never needed to state as a promise.

The harm becomes difficult to name when refusing the next gift would produce an immediate, visible loss. A household can understand the pattern and still have to feed somebody tomorrow.

A former recipient may need practical support that arrives without a demand to deny the good the gift once did. Requiring gratitude to become shame can leave Malachai the only voice that still describes the person’s experience accurately.

At the table.

Make the benefit work and keep Malachai’s actual statements true. Show what renewed dependence is costing through particular relationships. Provide room to seek other support, refuse, or postpone without declaring those choices easy. Use agreed boundaries for addiction, affliction, and loss of self.

Cantors & Cults of Malachai

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Malachai sings in harmonies of sweet despair—melodies that begin like a lullaby and end like a scream. Their voice is used to lure the desperate into bargains, to make a village accept a curse as a blessing, or to sing a monster to sleep so that it may be bound. Malachai’s Cantors are terrifying not because they are evil, but because they are willing to do evil—they will poison the well to teach the town to dig deeper, or curse a child to save a hundred adults. They are hated and needed in equal measure.

The Cult of the Leashed Hunger. Malachai’s cults gather in places where suffering is already present: plague hospitals, slums, refugee camps, and the cells of the condemned. The most fanatical cell, the Leashed Hunger, meets in catacombs beneath Zahk’Kozahn’s ruins, where they drink water from the Well of Ichor and carve the names of their victims into their own flesh with silver needles. A ritual involves each member naming a curse they have inflicted (or allowed) and a mercy they have buried because of it. Then they prick their finger and let a drop of blood fall into a cracked bell; the bell tolls once, and a Collector appears to witness. Their creed is whispered, not spoken: “We are the knife that cuts the rot. The wound heals, but the scar remembers us.” Outsiders are not welcome; they are patients, or potential tools, or future victims. Those who die serving Malachai become the Worn—shambling ghouls that guard the cursed city.

Witchcraft of Malachai (Lay / Curse)

A witch who walks with Malachai is a curse-smith or affliction-weaver—one who crafts pain into purpose. Their magic works through the materials of suffering: a needle that sews a nightmare into a pillow, a forge that tempers a blade to always strike the wielder’s loved ones, or a garden where belladonna and hemlock grow in geometric patterns. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a keystone that makes every tenth step collapse, but a witch of Malachai would instead sew a curse-seam into the mortar, so that the tunnel itself remembers every injury it has caused.

The Witch’s Tool: The Splintered Needle. A needle of black iron, snapped in half and re-joined with a drop of the witch’s blood. Once per session, the witch may sew a “curse seam” into a garment, a threshold, or a skin. The curse is small but specific: a button that falls off at the worst moment, a door that sticks when someone is pursued, a hand that trembles when holding a weapon. The target suffers –1 die on a single type of roll (e.g., Stealth, Melee, Sway) for the scene. The witch may unstitch the curse as a free action, but doing so marks 1 Fatigue.

Description: Malachai is the setting's study of addiction and should be run as one. The gift is real, it works, and it is genuinely the best available option at the moment it is taken — that is not a trick, and a GM who plays it as a trick has lost the thread. The harm is that it works.

Morag the Hag

Lore

Description: She is the grandmother, and she is not a sweet one.

You come to her in the hour between twilight and dawn because you have made a mess of something and everyone kinder has run out of ideas. She lets you in. She feeds you first, because you are cold and stupid with worry and she is not a monster. Then she sits down across from you and tells you exactly what it will cost, in plain language, once, and she does not repeat herself and she does not soften it and she does not ask whether you are sure. She has watched a great many people be sure.

Morag is cruel the way the stove is cruel. She will not warn you twice and she will not lie to you once. The terms are always there, always complete in substance, and always scratched in thorn where a person in a hurry will not stop to read them — and she considers reading them to be your work, not hers, because a woman who does your work for you has taught you nothing and will have to do it again next winter.

She takes no pleasure in the suffering. She takes considerable pleasure in the precision. And when the road bends back and the price arrives and you stand in her doorway with your face wrecked, she will feed you again, and she will not say she told you, because that would be beneath her and because you already know.

Against her sisters in method: Morag's whole instrument is disclosure. She says the cost once, plainly, and does not soften it or repeat it, and the bargain is still terrible, and you take it anyway. Mab sets true things in an order that leaves you standing somewhere you did not choose. Inaea never mentions that there is a price at all. Morag leaves the refusal plainly within reach, which is why those who accept often accuse her of cruelty afterwards.

Followers: Among the peoples of Fate's Edge, Morag is the patron of hedge-witches, debt collectors, and those who deal in the spaces between law and mercy. In Aelaerem, she is the Kettle-Crone, invoked before any serious bargain. In Viterra, she is the Crossroads Wife, whose thorn-marked stones mark the boundaries of hedge courts. In Zakov, she is the Tide-Mother who sets the price for smuggled cargo. Wherever a promise is made with its cost spoken aloud, Morag watches.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The kettle before the bargain

Aelaerem keepers call Morag the Kettle-Crone. The meal offered before a difficult conversation matters because a cold, hungry person may accept anything. Food is supplied first; afterward the terms are spoken plainly. The kindness and the severity belong to the same grandmother. Neither is a mask that falls when the discussion begins.

Local keepers disagree about whether a relative may remain while the terms are heard. One household wants a second person to remember the words; another thinks that a relative’s expectation can make refusal harder. A keeper who makes the terms clear has not thereby made the choice easy. A farmer may understand exactly which field must be surrendered and have no other way to bring a child home.

The Crossroads Wife under hedge law

In Viterra, thorn-marked stones associated with the Crossroads Wife have outlasted changes of lord and boundary. Some hedge courts recognize bargains witnessed there; others call the witnesses unlawful while accepting the results when convenient. Witches’ families have carried the practice between settlements through marriages and apprenticeships rather than a central ordaining house.

The most divisive cases involve a promise made by someone with authority over another person’s labor. A lord may accept an obligation and then send a tenant to discharge it. Morag’s keeper reads the accepted terms back without improving them. Whether the lord may compel the tenant is a separate dispute that the keeper’s precision can expose without resolving. The distinction is unwelcome to anyone who wanted supernatural fairness to replace a hearing.

The Tide-Mother of Zakov

Zakov’s smugglers address Morag as the Tide-Mother when passage has a cost that must be known before departure. It is also a title given to Raéyn among the Linn. A Zakov broker asking for exact terms is not necessarily invoking the sea’s changeable temper, and a Linn household praying for a boat’s return has not entered Morag’s bargain.

The dockside tradition took on greater importance as cargo brokers began arranging passage for people as well as goods. Families remember keepers who made a captain name the full demand in front of witnesses. They also remember bargains that were fully understood and terrible. Later captains have tried to use the Tide-Mother’s reputation as an advertisement, offering a cheap first crossing while leaving the onward journey outside the agreement. No hidden magical condition is needed for that cruelty.

The refusal that survives the retelling

The Tarnished Cauldron’s accounts preserve refusals as carefully as acceptances. A keeper may tell how someone ate, heard the terms, and left. Towns that fear the witches sometimes omit the leaving when the account reaches a pulpit; other towns romanticize it until every refused bargain ends happily. Neither version prepares a desperate visitor for the possibility that refusal can be permitted and still lead to loss.

Morag’s instrument is disclosure. New terms require a new agreement; a gift does not acquire a secret condition after it has been accepted. Her followers can be impatient, frightening, and convinced that clarity discharges more responsibility than it does. A local dispute is strongest when nobody doubts what was said, and the people present disagree about whether saying it was enough.

History of Worship

Her names have accumulated around meetings whose locations seldom remain useful directions to the next petitioner.

Aelaerem accounts often begin with a meal; Viterran accounts with a petition nobody else would hear; Zakov stories with a boat returning where no boat should have reached. Later tellers like to introduce a clever concealed clause. The Tarnished Cauldron’s keepers object to those versions. They insist the frightening part was that the petitioner heard the cost plainly and accepted it anyway.

The cult traveled through returned petitioners and households affected by their bargains. A family carrying a gift into a new region might preserve Morag’s old title long after it no longer described the local country. Neighbors adopted the title, a meeting custom, or the practice of having the terms repeated before witnesses. Morag herself does not need to have followed the same road as the people who learned to ask for her.

Intermediaries grew influential because need is often private. Some helped people understand an offer without deciding for them. Others learned exactly whom to bring to the crossroads and which ordinary remedy to leave unmentioned. They do not have to falsify Morag’s words to exploit a desperate person. The existence of her honest bargain can support a thoroughly dishonest business around access to it.

Mab, Inaea, and Morag should remain distinct. Mab arranges true statements within inescapable courtesy; Inaea arranges enduring relationships. Morag names what will be taken. Her terms are not made harmless by being clear, and a mortal agent’s misconduct does not imply that Morag lied.

Knowing What You Agree To

A stated cost permits an informed decision. It does not make every available decision fair, nor make a desperate person free of the circumstances that brought them.

Her followers divide over what a witness owes the petitioner. One repeats the terms exactly and considers the task complete. Another asks whether someone could help without the bargain. Morag can tolerate the question more easily than the intermediary whose standing depends on the meeting.

The ordeal belongs to the accepted loss and the person who must live after receiving the gift. A hidden extra price usually weakens it by letting the petitioner become only a victim of deceit.

At the table.

Make the terms and the desired outcome clear before acceptance. Give the party an ordinary alternative that is genuinely difficult, not secretly impossible. Honor a refusal. Once a bargain is accepted, let its actual consequences carry the horror rather than adding a clause afterward.

The Ninth — Beyond Comprehension (Infinite Information & Unknowable Truths)

Lore

Description: Beyond the eighth figure of Sacred Geometry lies a rim no compass will hold. The Ninth is not absence but surplus: knowledge poured past the lip of any vessel, truth that overgrows the frame that seeks to bind it. Those who bend the knee at its lectern become conduits for impossible reckonings — glimpsing patterns that cannot be kept whole inside a single skull. Each revelation purchases power at the price of living comfortably within the world's common measures.

Followers: The Ninth does not explain, define, or warn. It overflows. To invoke it is to step past the eighth threshold — the one you did not know was there — and find that on the other side, the cup forgets its shape, the bell rings a note that was never cast, and the answer to your question arrives as a question you had not yet learned to ask. Its followers are scholars, truth-seekers, and those who have looked too long into the void and found it looking back. They are rarely comfortable. They are never entirely sane.

Names across regions: Thepyrgos: The Unfinished Index, the patron of archivists who seek the edges of knowledge

Aeler: The Rim Without Compass, a figure spoken of only in whispers among Truth-Masons

The gray wanderer: The Ninth Cup, the un-poured vessel, the answer that will not fit the question

Quote: Eight figures bind the world; the Ninth overflows it. Drink, and remember that the cup forgets its shape before the song is done.

Names: Two names, two registers. The Ninth is the general name — the thing behind the Taboo, spoken of by people who have no board in front of them and would rather not be specific. The Ninth Rim is the Kon'reh term: the rim beyond the eighth figure, off the edge of a board that only has eight — that board being the Sacred Geometry, which is the Ykrul name for Kon'reh. The Ykrul hold that Kon'reh is not a game about the world but a model of it, so a word for something off the edge of the board is a word for something off the edge of everything. To a Ykrul the two names are the same statement made at different volumes; to anyone else, the Kon'reh term is the one that sounds like it means less, which is why it survives in places the other cannot be said aloud.

Regional Names and Traditions

The Unfinished Index

Thepyrgos’s disputed collections. A copying desk is sometimes left with its work facedown after its keeper retires. Successors find the material useful until a reference points to a shelf the room has never contained. Some reconstruct the absent shelf. Others catalog the reference without following it. Neither practice has reliably prevented further entries from appearing.

The Rim Without Compass

Aeler Truth-Masons. A measured figure is shown with the place where its boundary ceased to agree with the instrument. Apprentices are asked to reproduce the known part before hearing the account. In severe Himadri schools the flawed tracing may remain under a cover; Qianvar workshops sometimes display it beside the tools. Neither can turn its method of custody into proof that the next tracing will remain contained.

The Citation Without a Place

Sihai’s closed scholarly correspondence. A correspondent answers an ordinary question with an observation the recipient can verify and no one recalls sending. The pavilions’ keepers may help identify the manuscript from which part of it came. Their refusal to supply a final explanation is not evidence that they possess one. An official who orders a complete account may receive considerably more writing than the order required.

The Ninth Cup

The Gray Wanderer’s roadside accounts. The cup remains unpoured while people decide where the next night’s shelter will be. Those who seek the surplus bring a question they have rehearsed alone. Sometimes they leave with an answer and no longer recognize the problem that made them ask. The host still packs food for the journey and avoids counting the vessels aloud.

History of Worship

There is no secure foundation date for a practice whose surviving accounts keep acquiring material outside their own scope.

The Unfinished Index can identify gatherings, copied sheets, and teachers. It cannot establish a first revelation from the age of a page. A later hand can copy an older observation; a perfectly authenticated early volume can refer to work not yet written. The uncertainty has encouraged counterfeit discoveries as well as genuine encounters. Identifying a forgery does not settle what is happening in another book.

Correspondence carried the practice without requiring a missionary. A useful result attracted a second question, and readers formed circles to compare what they had received. Printers sometimes duplicated an extra line that no editor had accepted. Manuscript keepers accused the presses; presses pointed to older copies carrying the same addition. The disagreement moved through trade routes faster than the custodians could compare their evidence.

Some revelations have saved lives or resolved problems that defeated years of work. This is why experienced readers return. The danger is not disproved by a correct answer. A scholar may cease sleeping while remaining courteous, useful, and unable to stop explaining a conclusion whose beginning everybody else has forgotten. The company keeps asking because the next part appears to concern them personally.

Regional ninth taboos are not declarations of membership in this cult. A household that stops at eight may never invoke the Patron cataloged here. Cult histories record encounters with surplus knowledge; they do not reveal the ultimate explanation of the world’s taboos.

An Answer Larger Than Its Keeper

Understanding something can alter the conditions under which the understanding is held. The reader who began the investigation may not be able to remain its unchanged custodian.

Institutions often ask whether an observation is true before deciding whether it should circulate. The Ninth makes a second question unavoidable: what happens to a person who can no longer stop receiving the rest of it?

An incomplete account can be an honest account. Some keepers leave the break visible rather than filling it with an invented conclusion. The empty space may remain empty. The next person through the room should not be required to test it.

At the table.

Choose a useful, limited revelation and particular observable disturbances. Do not present a cosmological lecture, a universal decoding of ninth taboos, or a single mandatory response. Closing the book, restricting circulation, and seeking help must remain actions characters can attempt without being mocked for refusing more.

Cantors & Cults of the Ninth

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of the Ninth sings in phrases that fold back on themselves — melodies that seem to have no beginning or end, harmonies that shift key in ways that should be impossible. Their voice is used to encrypt a message into a tune that only the intended listener can hear, to unravel the logic of a paradox, or to lull a library’s guardian spirit into a momentary confusion. The Ninth’s Cantors are often found in great archives, humming while they work.

The Cult of the Unfinished Index. The Ninth’s cults meet in libraries, scriptoria, and the back rooms of printing houses — but the innermost circle, the Unfinished Index, has a stranger practice: each member carves a single extra joint into a finger of their writing hand (a ninth knuckle that does not bend). They believe that the extra joint holds a question they have not yet learned to ask. A ritual involves each member writing a fact they have never told anyone on a slip of paper, then burning it. The ashes are swept into a single book — the Growing Ledger — whose pages multiply each year and whisper at night, adding entries no one wrote. Their creed is stamped inside the cover: “Every answer births nine new questions. This is not a flaw. This is the shape of truth.” Outsiders are welcome to ask a question, but they will leave with three they did not bring — and sometimes, a faint, extra knuckle that fades by morning.

Witchcraft of the Ninth (Lay / Knowledge)

A witch who walks with the Ninth is a glyph-carver or pattern-weaver — one who crafts knowledge into physical form: a book that writes itself, a map that shows only the routes the reader does not yet know, or a code that cannot be broken because it was never written. Their magic works through the tools of information: a quill that writes in letters that shift meaning with the light, a seal that stamps “APPROVED” onto documents that were never submitted, or a lens that reveals hidden writing. Their craft is never about forging metal; it is about folding paper, spilling ink, and stepping past the eighth line of a proof into the white space where the answer is not written because the question was wrong.

The Witch’s Tool: The Paradox Quill. A quill carved from the bone of a bird that never lived, dipped in ink that dries clear. Once per session, the witch may write a single sentence on a surface (a wall, a scroll, a forehead). The sentence is true — but only for the next hour. After that, it becomes a lie, or fades, or was never there at all. The GM determines the exact effect, but the truth is always useful for that hour.

A Hedge Gift: Glyph of Unfolding (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may draw a single geometric symbol on a surface (a door, a lock, a piece of paper) while speaking a question aloud. The symbol “unfolds” into a diagram that answers the question — but the diagram is always incomplete, cryptic, or reveals something the seeker did not wish to know. The witch gains +1 die to any roll that uses the diagram’s information.

Corruption of the Ninth

Playstyle Notes

Description: The Ninth favors the patient reader of tangled ledgers and sky-signs alike. It rewards audacity in thought and the courage to let understanding change you. Its gifts turn riddles into roads — but every road leads past a threshold you did not know existed. Lore

Description: Once a road-cantor who swore to reconcile feuding towns with procession and song, Palinode died between feasts with a chorus unfinished. Where a crowd leans forward and a maker holds breath, she arrives.

Followers: Wherever she passes, signs appear: a cup that never quite empties; a candle that gutters only when silence reigns; a flawless bar or note that the hand cannot forget. She promises relief from solitude: an audience that always leans closer, a song that never dies, a bond that feels like family even if it devours you. Her rites grant the Cantor not only power but belonging—at the cost of their voice becoming more hers than their own.

History of Worship

Palinode was a road-cantor who swore to reconcile feuding towns. She died between their feasts with a chorus unfinished, and the refrain continued beyond her life.

The rival Feast Accounts preserve different opening speeches, different lists of gifts, and the same place where the surviving music will not close. Later companies have claimed that their founder carried the missing verse away. Some claims are commercial inventions; others have preserved music older than the company making them. Establishing that a verse is old does not establish that it can finish what Palinode began.

A refrain travels without a relic chest. Hired singers took it from civic reconciliations into work crews and festival stages, changing the breath length and response to suit new listeners. Oshiiran landing songs preserved a rhythm a conservatory edition discarded. Nihori’s companies gave the encore an explicit place beyond the formal bow. Such adaptations carried the Queen’s reach farther than any agreed account of the original towns.

People have used her songs to meet when speech alone had failed. The first shared response can be an achievement worth preserving. The Gasping Chorus becomes dangerous when it treats that achievement as something no participant may subsequently leave. Its throat-bells and exchanged fragments belong to an inner discipline, not to every worker or theatergoer who knows a favored tune.

Civic authorities have tried to acquire the Queen by acquiring her company. A permanent grant supplies rehearsal rooms and binds the annual performance to a sponsor’s celebration. The performance may then remember a reconciliation the sponsor no longer intends to honor. Whether to omit the offending verse divides singers who all need the grant to continue working.

A Refrain Outliving Its Promise

Palinode gives shared work an existence larger than its maker. The work can survive while the people making it lose the ability to stop.

An audience may love a singer without hearing the cost of the next song. A performer may interpret that appetite as the only proof that the company still belongs together. Both can prefer another encore to the conversation that should follow it.

Some companies preserve an incomplete copy beside the version they perform. Its absence acknowledges that the work has a history beyond their authority. Others secretly keep the same copy because they no longer trust themselves to recognize an ending.

At the table.

Two communities will share a feast only if a particular singer performs the old reconciliation. The singer can still produce the music but cannot hear when it stops. The party can recover witnesses, share the performance, change its occasion, or help the singer refuse. Let the reconciliation require conduct outside the song as well as a successful performance.

The Sealed Gate (Thresholds & Containment)

Description: The Sealed Gate stands where realities meet, embodying the sacred power of thresholds, boundaries, and containment. A keeper may defend a predecessor’s exclusion as sacred while admitting a new benefactor whose name appears nowhere in the original undertaking.

History of Worship

The Gate concerns the separation of things that must not freely pass into one another. Its human observances developed at many kinds of threshold.

The Cult of the Unbroken Seal inherits duties more often than complete accounts of their beginnings. A warden’s successor receives keys, repairs, prohibitions, and the names of people who must be summoned when a sign changes. Some instructions outlast the institution that supplied those people. The new keeper may be performing the last effective portion of an arrangement that once required several communities.

True-Masons carried boundary practices along mountain roads; archivists exchanged exclusion marks with collections; exorcists taught households how to maintain the thresholds they would leave behind. Those transfers did not make every gate a foreign institution. Sihai’s receiving houses and door-guardian traditions adopted useful marks while retaining their own rites and understandings of who stood watch.

Protection is an immediate reason to honor the Gate. Its wider authority grew when emergency arrangements became permanent. A door closed during a dangerous incursion might later divide a neighborhood whose people had once crossed freely. Descendants could inherit the maintenance without inheriting the right to ask whether the original reason still held. Others opened such doors casually and discovered that their grandparents had left them shut for good cause.

The history of a seal and the present need for it are separate matters to investigate. Proving that a warden was cruel does not establish that their prisoner is safe; proving the prisoner dangerous does not justify every exclusion the warden imposed. The Unbroken Seal has keepers who understand that distinction and keepers who regard reconsideration itself as a breach.

The People Outside the Protection

A boundary makes a protected place possible by deciding what or whom it excludes.

Keepers can become so certain of the value of a closed door that they cease noticing the community it was meant to preserve. Maintaining the door replaces maintaining the arrangement that allowed people to live beside it.

Some gatehouses preserve an old receiving bench outside the seal. It allows a person to be heard without requiring immediate admission. The bench can be a merciful precaution, or the last courtesy offered to someone nobody ever intends to let in.

At the table.

A legitimate heir returns to a household whose inherited ward will not admit them. Establish what the ward recognizes and why before the investigation. Testimony, a supervised crossing, a revised undertaking, or a different place to meet can matter; do not require blind destruction of a useful protection or permanent exile as the only answers.

Thrysos, King of Revels (Ecstasy & Excess)

Lore

Description: Where torches flare and cups spill, where dancers stamp until dawn, there strides Thrysos crowned with ivy and horn. He is both host and beast, a patron of license who laughs at law. In his train follow the masked and the wild, the broken who dance to forget, the bold who revel to remember, and the lustful who seek the heat of bodies in the dark. His rites intoxicate, inflame, and unmask — yet each cup leaves a mark, each kiss a chain, each night of abandon a dawn of reckoning.

In his milder aspect, he blesses the fertility of the harvest and the laughter of lovers. In his darker aspect, he drives maenads to tear apart the hero who refused to join the dance.

Regional Devotions and Histories

The god carried out of the theater

In Thepyrgos, Thrysos is honored by companies whose performances require an audience to become more than separate people watching. The Horned Circle’s masks have moved between theaters, harvest gatherings, and private feasts. An old mask can be prized because a company survived a lean season while wearing it, not because it belonged to a prince.

City patrons began sponsoring public revels partly to display their generosity. The arrangement brought food and paid performers to streets that needed both. It also placed the sponsor’s herald at the edge of the dance. A company that had once mocked its own master might discover that the same scene was unwelcome when an official’s relatives recognized themselves in it. Some performers accepted the new limits. Others took the masks outside the city.

Storm-Dancers and the work at dawn

Linn Storm-Dancers approach the Revel-King through weather, bodily endurance, and the company that keeps a person from turning back. The people tending the fire may be relatives who refused to dance. Their refusal need not mean contempt. They know who will need carrying home.

Zakov carouses name Thrysos before a crew disperses into an evening ashore. Captains favor feasts that return the crew to its ship in good order; sailors may favor a night in which a captain’s preference briefly ceases to govern. The cult has acquired a quieter observance among shore households: the dawn sweep, when broken cups are gathered and someone goes to find the missing. Old keepers insist that this is part of the revel. Prosperous hosts sometimes pay others to do it and attend only the night.

The Unbounded Guest at a formed feast

Aelinnel give Thrysos the title Unbounded Guest, also used for Mab. The distinction can be obvious to a host and invisible to a visiting scholar. Mab gathers the evening into an arrangement. Thrysos makes the arrangement difficult to keep. A feast approaching both can become dangerous without either Patron changing nature.

In Nihori, some shrine festivals permit a masked company to enter after the principal rite is closed. The company acquired that place through returning, repairing damage, and accepting responsibility to the shrine household. A lord importing the same performance for a private gathering may omit those relationships and still expect the same safety. No costume can import the missing history.

Who may stop dancing?

The Horned Circle contains a genuine disagreement about an invitation that becomes a demand. Some companies protect a guest’s ability to leave, assigning sober attendants to the paths. Others regard departure as a rejection that must be overcome. Thrysos’s dangerous rites and ecstatic transformations remain real; the precautions are local practices, not an immunity the cult can grant by declaration.

A festival can therefore be generous, beautiful, and frightening without reducing its participants to dupes. A performer may desire the loss of restraint and still object when it is imposed on a neighbor. Begin after a successful night, with a person who has not returned to their ordinary attachments and a host insisting that nobody was harmed. What the company owes that person is more interesting than a universal rule that every revel is either liberation or a trap.

History of Worship

An old reveling cult whose public festivals and less governable gatherings have repeatedly exchanged places.

Thepyric histories preserve civic performances beside accounts of a horned guest who would not remain within the appointed hour. After Astroegro’s fall, performance households could carry a mask and songs when they could not carry a sanctuary. Their descendants disagree over whether the restrained civic celebration is a domesticated revel or the private gathering a departure from an older discipline.

Actors, musicians, sailors, and hosts carried observances between ports. A company might be hired because its foreign festival was fashionable and discover that local people heard a different possibility in its temporary freedoms. Noble sponsors adopted the masks and tried to decide who could wear them. Waterfront gatherings dispensed with some formalities while developing their own fierce expectations about who must join.

The Horned Circle’s divisions are not simply sober authority against joyful liberation. A licensed festival can protect performers who could not safely speak outside it; its boundaries can also ensure nothing changes after the masks come off. An unlicensed gathering can create real solidarity and become intolerant of the person who will not surrender themselves to it. Thrysos has room for both feast and frenzy.

Do not turn intoxication, erotic play, or violent ecstasy into compulsory player participation. A cult may exert pressure within the fiction; the table still controls its boundaries. The historical practices of a real reveling religion do not establish consent rules for this fictional Patron.

Who Is Free at the Feast

Thrysos offers a shared release from the selves people are required to perform. The company can make that release another required performance.

A person may want to be carried by a song without surrendering the right to step outside it. The host who cannot bear an empty place in the dance has begun confusing everybody’s joy with their own authority over the gathering.

What remains afterward matters. A night that makes an impossible friendship possible has changed something. A night whose least powerful guests must suffer the consequences alone has distributed release unevenly.

At the table.

Give a festival a host, people doing the work, and a consequence that survives morning. A genuine revelation or attachment can emerge without requiring every reveler to lose control. Let leaving, staying, and interrupting carry different possibilities.

Cantors & Cults of Thrysos

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Thrysos sings in off-key harmonies — melodies that start wild and grow wilder, rhythms that mimic a galloping heart. Their voice is used to whip a crowd into a frenzy, to calm a hangover with a lullaby, or to make a mask cling to a face until the wearer is ready to take it off. Thrysos’s Cantors are often found at festivals, in taverns at closing time, leading the procession of the horned dancers, or performing in private salons where the line between audience and participant is deliberately blurred.

The Cult of the Horned Circle. Thrysos’s cults meet in clearings, barns, and the back rooms of inns after midnight. The Horned Circle is a fellowship of mask-carvers, vintners, musicians, courtesans, and those who have tasted the edge of ecstasy — sometimes all in the same person. Their most sacred vessel is a hollowed-out log that was struck by lightning; they ferment wine inside it, and the wine whispers secrets to those who drink it — forgotten grudges, hidden desires, the true names of those who will betray them, and the faces of those who are willing. A ritual of the Circle involves each member donning a mask (hand-carved, never bought) and dancing until the first dancer falls from exhaustion. The fallen is crowned with ivy and declared “guest of honor” — they lead the next circle, and sometimes the next bed. Their creed is carved into a single wine barrel that is never emptied: “The cup that never empties is the cup you share.” Outsiders are welcome to dance, but they must leave their mask behind when they go — and if they drink from the lightning-log, they will hear the whispers for a year and a day, and they will return.

The Maenads of the Crimson Caves (Bacchic Dark). The darkest cult of Thrysos, the Maenads (or Bassarids) are almost exclusively female, though men may join at the risk of becoming their prey. They gather in hidden caves or moonlit groves to perform the oreibasia — the mountain dance — wearing fawnskins and carrying thyrsi tipped with bronze. Their rites begin with wine and music, escalate to ecstatic abandon, and sometimes end in sparagmos — the tearing apart of a living sacrifice (often a bull, but in the oldest stories, a human who refused to join the dance). This cult is banned in every civilized nation, but it persists in the wildest frontiers. A character who joins the Maenads gains access to darker rites, but also the risk of losing control entirely (the GM may treat this as a Corruption track that leads to a violent, permanent transformation).

Witchcraft of Thrysos (Lay / Revelry)

A witch who walks with Thrysos is a mask-carver or wine-smith — one who crafts the tools of ecstatic release: a mask that lets the wearer dance without tiring, a drum that beats in time with the drinker’s pulse, a cup that never runs dry (but leaves a headache at dawn), or a perfume that makes the wearer irresistible for a single night. Their magic works through the materials of celebration: a thyrsus that marks the boundary of a sacred dance, a press that squeezes wine that ferments in hours instead of weeks, a needle that sews a costume that makes the wearer faster, louder, bolder, or a small clay lamp that burns with a flame that kindles desire. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a “revel keystone” into a feast hall, but a hedge-witch of Thrysos might instead drive a thyrsus into the floorboards to consecrate the space for an orgy.

The Witch’s Tool: The Thyrsus of the Unbound. A staff of fennel or oak, topped with a pine cone and bound with ivy, often with a ribbon from a lover’s garment. Once per session, the witch may strike the thyrsus against the ground three times. For the next scene, any creature within Near who is engaged in revelry (dancing, drinking, performing, or any act of intimacy) gains +1 die to social and physical actions, but any attempt to leave the zone requires a Spirit + Endurance test (DV 3) — the revel holds them. The thyrsus can also be used to draw a circle on the ground; any mask placed within the circle becomes “true” for one night — the wearer cannot be recognized by anyone who was not present at the drawing, and their inhibitions are lowered (they may act on desires they would normally suppress).

Varnek Karn, the Death’s Negotiator (Bargains with the Departed)

Description: Varnek Karn dwells in the gray between life and death, where the newly departed linger with unfinished business and elder shades guard secrets dearer than gold. Sihai’s funerary intermediaries. Karn is painted carrying a folded petition rather than sitting behind the judge’s desk. Some families treat him as an envoy to those powers; others invoke a local mediator and reject the foreign name. In either observance, access to a hearing is different from authority to dictate its conclusion.

History of Worship

Varnek Karn mediates across the threshold of death. His followers seek agreements with the departed rather than assuming that summoning establishes ownership.

Karnite collections preserve promises whose makers can no longer attend an ordinary court. Some contain an ancestor’s complaint beside generations of increasingly impatient replies. Their history is not an uninterrupted victory of the past: living households have persuaded shades to accept different duties, recognize adopted kin, or surrender a demand the present community could not survive fulfilling.

Funerary specialists accompanied families carrying remains, relics, and unfinished undertakings into new country. In Aeler vaults the practice encountered histories layered beneath other histories. Ykrul councils required ancestral testimony to move with the camp. Vhasian testament keepers gave disputed inheritances a formal table. Sihai’s receiving houses fitted foreign mediators into an existing vocabulary of hearings beyond death without conceding that Karn ruled those offices.

The possibility of another conversation is powerful. A family may obtain an answer, finish an undertaking, or hear the objection that kept a shade near the house. It can also postpone necessary living decisions indefinitely. The Unforgotten Ledger includes mediators who recommend ending a parley and others whose standing depends on keeping their clients in permanent discussion with the past.

The Ink-Flesh Communion gives remembrance an intimate form by carrying dead names on living skin. That devotion can preserve people whom a household would prefer forgotten. It does not establish that a tattoo’s bearer may negotiate for every descendant without consulting them. Both the living and the dead can object to the person who has appointed themselves indispensable between the two.

Courtesy Is Not Surrender

Treating the dead as parties to a negotiation acknowledges their continuing interests. It does not require the living to concede every claim because its maker has had longer to rehearse it.

An ancestor may have loved a household and exercised power harshly within it. Their return can comfort one descendant and frighten another. The mediator has to hear the people still at the table as carefully as the voice beyond it.

An agreement can include a farewell. Some Karnites regard that as their finest work: the living know what remains to be done, the departed accept its limits, and neither side needs to renew the meeting each season. Others cannot bear to close a conversation they alone made possible.

At the table.

A dead builder will explain how to repair a failing passage only if a household restores an old exclusion against its neighbors. The danger and the builder’s knowledge are real; their terms are negotiable. Give the living alternative evidence, concessions, and the right to refuse, rather than making obedience to the oldest voice the only respectful outcome.

Cantors & Cults of Varnek Karn

The Cantor’s Song. A Cantor of Varnek Karn sings in dirges of negotiation — melodies that invite the dead to speak, harmonies that soothe restless shades. Their voice is used to call an ancestor to council, to lull a vengeful ghost into recounting its grievance, or to sing a funeral procession past a haunted crossroads. Varnek’s Cantors are often found in graveyards, in the halls of the dying, and at the tables where inheritance disputes are settled, their songs bridging the silence between breath and decay.

The Cult of the Unforgotten Ledger. Varnek Karn’s cults gather in crypts, ossuaries, and the quiet rooms above funeral parlors — but one cell, known as the Ink-Flesh Communion, tattoos the names of the dead onto their own skin, believing that each name grants a fragment of the ancestor’s memory. Their ritual involves writing a death-record with a needle dipped in ash and wine; the tattoo whispers the dead person’s last words when pressed. The Unforgotten Ledger itself is a single, ever-growing book bound in gray hide — it is said to whisper at night, adding names without a scribe. Outsiders are welcome to bring a name of someone they have lost; they may read the ledger but not write in it. Their creed: “The dead are not gone. They have only changed their address. Be polite, pay your respects, and never make a promise you cannot keep.”

Witchcraft of Varnek Karn (Lay / Funerary)

A witch who walks with Varnek Karn is a funeral notary, a grave-warden, or a memory-keeper — a lay practitioner who uses ink, seals, and small rites to witness the passage of the dead. Their magic is the art of recording and releasing: a leaden seal that certifies a death-bed promise, a quill that writes in invisible ink that appears only when a ghost is near, or a carved wooden stamp that quiets a restless spirit for a night. In Aeler, a True-Mason might carve a “memory niche” into a crypt wall, but a hedge-witch of Varnek Karn might press a wax seal onto the niche’s edge, binding the dead to speak truth when the seal is broken. In Zakov, a net-mender might weave a shroud with a single loose thread; pulling it releases the drowned sailor’s soul to the tide.

The Witch’s Tool: The Seal of the Silent Witness. A leaden seal on a leather thong, its face carved with a single closed eye. Once per session, the witch may press the seal into warm wax (or directly onto a stone threshold) while speaking the name of a dead person. For the next day, anyone who speaks that name near the seal hears a faint whisper — a single word or a sigh — from the deceased. The seal may also be used to “witness” a death-bed promise: pressing it onto a written testament makes the document unbearably heavy to anyone who would break the vow.

A Hedge Gift: Echo of the Honored Dead (Basic, 4 XP). Once per scene, the witch may touch a physical remnant of the dead (a bone, a lock of hair, a piece of clothing) and whisper a question. For a moment, the remnant holds a memory — the GM answers one factual question about the dead person’s life or death, but the answer is always tinged with the emotion of the moment (e.g., “she was afraid,” “he was angry,” “they were at peace”).

Corruption of Varnek Karn

Playstyle Notes

Description: Varnek Karn favors investigators, arbiters, and heralds of funerary law. Power grows through cultural sensitivity, shrewd bargaining, and rites performed exactly as they are owed; the price is a thinning tie to warmth and mortal ease. Expect mysteries of lineage, obligations that outlive their makers, and parleys where both sides of the veil demand their due. The Rite of the Unquiet Host allows a follower to temporarily command a small army of the dead, but the debt incurred ensures that such power is never free — and the dead always remember who called them.

Emphasizes: - Diplomacy over Dominion: bargaining with the dead rather than commanding them

  • Knowledge Brokerage: trading for secrets and testimonies beyond the grave

  • Cultural Protocols: rites, tokens, and proper doors for speaking with ancestors

  • Mediation: resolving conflicts that bind the living to the dead

  • Historical Continuity: showing how past vows shape the present

  • The Unquiet Host: raising temporary armies through pact, not domination
