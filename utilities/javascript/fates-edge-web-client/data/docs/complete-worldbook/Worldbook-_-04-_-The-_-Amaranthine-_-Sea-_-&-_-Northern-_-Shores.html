The Amaranthine Sea & Northern Shores

“The sea keeps a better ledger than any scribe. The reef remembers every secret whispered above its depths. Do not ask which flag flies highest – ask which hull leaks slowest.” — Dusana of the Raven Road

Kahfagia

The Flag of Storms.

I was born on the tide, which is to say I was born owing. My mother was a net-mender who lost her fingers to a reef-cutter’s debt. My father was a bell—that is to say, he rang when the Admiralty pulled his string. I learned to read the lantern codes before I learned my letters, and I learned that every light is a lie waiting to be told.

The Admiralty runs this place like a pirate charter with a budget—and oh, how they love pretending it’s legitimate. They control the beacons that lure ships onto reefs, the booms that shatter hulls for salvage rights, and the tariffs that fund their champagne baths. But here’s the secret the commodores don’t want you to know: the lanterns are afraid of the dark too.

I’ve seen a Mirror-Keeper weep when her reflection showed her a daughter she’d sold for a pilot’s license. I’ve watched a Privateer Captain kiss a silver coin before a raid—the same coin her mother kissed before she never came back. This is not a city of heroes. It is a city of people who learned that the sea is cheaper than mercy.

If you want to move cargo through the straits without becoming fish food, get a convoy letter stamped with the Admiral’s seal. Or get smart. Or get gone.

— Captain Mira “the Lucky,” Sidhi exile, pirate by convenience

What do people eat for breakfast? Salt fish porridge—dried cod soaked overnight, boiled with hardtack and a splash of lamp oil. The oil is for calories. The fish is for memory. Every Kahfagian child learns to taste the salt and know which coast their grandmother came from.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count ships by their lanterns. Three lanterns means a Kahfagian merchant—safe. Four means an Oshiiran grain barge—neutral. One, low in the water, means a privateer. Run.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their hands. Dock-workers have rope-burned palms. Merchants have ink-stained fingers. Privateers have no fingernails—they bite them off in storms.

What is the most common crime? False manifests. Every third ship carries cargo that doesn’t appear on its papers. The Admiralty knows. The Admiralty takes a cut. The Admiralty calls it a ”tariff surcharge.“

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Mirror-Keepers are not born—they are made. And the making requires a death. A sister, a daughter, a lover. Someone who drowned so the keeper could see through the fog.

Where do people go when they die? The tide takes them. Kahfagia has no cemeteries above ground. The drowned are weighted with chain and dropped in the deep channel. Their families light candles in paper boats and float them out at dusk. If the candle reaches the horizon, the soul is forgiven. If it sinks, the debt is not yet paid.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer your bell. Every Kahfagian carries a small brass bell on a leather cord—the sound of their name. Offering it to the wronged party means ”I give you my voice. Speak for me.“ Refusing to return the bell after an apology is a declaration of blood feud.

What is the most important festival? The Beacon Lighting (midwinter). Every lighthouse keeper lights a new lamp from the Admiral’s flame. If your beacon catches first, your year will be lucky. If it sputters, you will owe a death to the sea.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Admiral’s lantern code has a ninth signal—never used, never spoken, burned into every keeper’s memory. It means “Kill the witnesses. Sink the evidence. The sea will hide the rest.”

Quick Reference

Tagline: Every mirror is a blade, every beacon a promise. The sea obeys when we move the lights — but the spiders weave in the mirror-towers.

Admiral of Lantern-Law (Elite): “Every mirror is a blade, every beacon a promise. The sea obeys when we move the lights — and woe to the fool who rows against them. Yet I have seen the reflection move before the hand that holds it. The tides are not the only ones with secrets.”

Genre / Mood: Creole thalassocracy, signal-and-lane politics, eldritch tide, secret infiltration.

Starting Location: The Lantern Pier admiralty chapel, where votive ships hang from the ceiling and brine drips from the icon of a drowned saint. A sea-notary unlocks a salt-stained reliquary and produces a chart with gilt edges. “Fetch the contraband bell from the corsair galleass Santa Dolorosa before moonrise — or the harbor names you accomplices. And watch for silk threads on the bollards. The Spider’s children are everywhere.”

Kahfagia is the Creole child of the Vilikari and the deep-sea traders — a thalassocracy where reckonings meet tide-law. The Admiralty of Lantern-Law rules not by armies but by light, yet the real power flows through a tangled web of oligarchic families, each with their own spies, their own private navies, and their own seat on the Shadow Council. Above them all floats a central Admiralty fleet, charged with protecting the beacon monopoly — and with watching the watchers.

But the oligarchs are not the only players. Unseen, the Lethai-ar of Inaea have been infiltrating the mirror-towers for decades. A factor’s trusted secretary, a harbor master’s silent wife, a pilot’s apprentice with too-still eyes — they weave their webs not for coin, but for control. They do not seek to destroy Kahfagia. They seek to own it, strand by strand, until the entire thalassocracy hums to the rhythm of the Spider’s mercy.

What you notice first: The constant chime of bell-buoys. The smell of salt, tar, and burnt oil from a thousand lanterns. The way everyone — admiral to dockhand — glances at the tide timer before speaking. And the faint, almost invisible silk threads tied around the bollards of the wealthiest piers.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never argue with a Mirror-Keeper about where the channel lies. If they say the safe water moved, it moved. Your chart is already kindling. And if the Mirror-Keeper smiles too widely, check the back of their neck for a spider’s brand.

Regional Mood: The Lantern’s Vigil & The Spider’s Web

Power is visible here — literally. Every night, a thousand lamps burn along the channels, each a statement of control. To dim a light is to cede ground. To move a beacon is to declare war. Commodores sit in their mirror-towers, watching reflections, while tide-timers tick down to the next auction, the next inspection, the next quiet burial of a reputation.

But beneath the visible power, another war is fought in whispers. The Lethai-ar agents of Inaea have spent a generation infiltrating the oligarchic families. A young heir who suddenly displays uncanny negotiating skill may not be talented — they may be woven. A Mirror-Keeper whose beacons never fail may be receiving guidance from a source that does not appear on any chart. The Spider’s web is patient. It does not break the thalassocracy; it guides it.

The Oligarchic Houses (Shadow Council)

The true rulers of Kahfagia are not the Admiralty alone, but the Shadow Council of seven oligarchic families. Each controls a monopoly (spice, salt, silk, timber, pitch, shipbuilding, or intelligence). Each maintains its own private navy and network of spies. Each vies for the empty seat left by the family that the Lethai-ar have already woven into silence.

  • House al-Kassam (Spice): Oldest, proudest, most vulnerable. The Lethai-ar have already replaced the family steward.

  • House Carrasco (Salt): Ruthless pragmatists; they suspect infiltration but cannot prove it.

  • House Mirat (Silk): Secret allies of the Lethai-ar — they trade webs for influence.

  • House del Puerto (Shipbuilding): Neutral; they build for anyone who pays.

  • House Henares (Timber): Deeply weighed down by Admiralty claims; the Lethai-ar are slowly gathering leverage.

  • House Ira ("Miscellaneous"): Neutral; Sells arcane goods, artifacts, weapons, and contraband to the highest bidder.

  • Naval Intelligence: The Admiralty’s own spy network — and already compromised.

  • The Empty Chair: Once held by House Morat, now vacant. The other houses whisper that the Morats were not destroyed — they were rewoven.

Secret Faction: The Silk Vigil (Lethai-ar Infiltrators)

Agents of Inaea, the Spider’s children have spent a generation worming into Kahfagia’s elite. They do not steal or sabotage — they guide. A decision that benefits House Carrasco is subtly nudged toward the Lethai-ar’s long-term goal: a unified thalassocracy that answers not to the Admiralty, but to the Web.

Signs of Infiltration (d6)

When a character suspects a Lethai-ar presence, roll or choose a sign:

  1. A factor’s handwriting becomes unnaturally precise, the loops forming faint eight-shapes.

  2. A Mirror-Keeper’s reflections show the same face in every tower — a woman with chestnut-brown skin and eyes like polished jet.

  3. A ship that should have foundered instead limps to port, its crew speaking of a gray-robed passenger who vanished before docking.

  4. Silk threads appear on the rigging of a warship; the threads are too strong to break with bare hands.

  5. A merchant’s ledger contains entries in disappearing ink; heat reveals names of Lethai-ar contacts.

  6. The Veiled Harpist’s music is heard from a room that has no windows.

Named Agents of the Silk Vigil

These Lethai-ar agents may appear in any Kahfagian campaign.

  • The Veiled Harpist (Leader): No one knows her face. She plays only in mirror-lit rooms. Her music leaves threads of silk in the listeners’ hair. She has woven three oligarchs into silence.

  • Sefu the Clerk: A quiet man in the Admiralty’s archive. He adds clauses to treaties that favor House Mirat. He has not spoken above a whisper in seven years.

  • Liana of the Gray Shawl: A pilot’s apprentice with too-still eyes. She knows every reef and every patrol route. She has never been lost—only redirected.

Mechanical Hook: Once per session, a PC may roll Wits + Lore (DV 4) to notice a silken thread in an official’s collar or a spider’s brand on a dockhand’s wrist. On success, they identify a Lethai-ar agent. On failure, the agent becomes aware of their scrutiny — and the Web tightens.

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = leverage. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Diamonds are codified outcomes (papers/rights/priority) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo sea & signal motifs — beacons that migrate, tides that remember, law that flows like water. If a King or Ace of Spades appears, also echo web motifs — silk threads on a bollard, a reflection that holds too long, the faint smell of spiced tea (the Lethai-ar are near).

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Pilot’s Mirror: Kahfagia’s strength is not its fleet but its monopoly on light and lane. To post a beacon is to redraw the map. Any character with a Mirror Warrant may permanently move a beacon or buoy (GM sets a 4-segment timer). For the duration of that timer, all navigation rolls on that lane are at +1 Position for the party and –1 Position for rivals.

  • Lantern-Law Privilege: While flying an Admiralty flag or carrying a valid Lantern License, your ship may pass through chain-booms and customs points at +1 Position. The first time in a session you invoke this privilege, the GM gains 1 SB (the Admiralty is watching).

  • Oligarchic Standing (Leverage Track): Instead of tracking coin weights, track Standing with the great families (al-Kassam, Carrasco, Mirat, etc.). Standing 1–3 gives you access to private docks, early auction warnings, and off-the-books pilots. Standing 4+ lets you call in a family to block a rival’s safe-conduct or raise a false alarm on their ship.

  • The Web’s Patience (Secret Timer [6]): Tracks the Lethai-ar infiltration of the Shadow Council. The timer ticks when the party helps a Lethai-ar agent, exposes a rival house’s secrets, or ignores a silken thread. At 3, the Harpist’s name appears on a dockmaster’s manifest. At 6, the Empty Chair is filled — and the new house head bows to a gray-robed stranger. The party may reduce the timer by exposing an agent or by warning an oligarch (requires spending 2 Standing).

  • Western Reach Rumors: Once per port visit, a character may roll Wits + Lore (DV 3) to hear a rumor from a captain returned from beyond the mirror. On success, the GM reveals one of the twelve rumors (see below) as a foreshadowing hook or a clue for an ongoing arc.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Lantern Bearer — The Traveler. A light that never goes out; a promise that never sinks.

    • The Mirror-King — The Witness. Reflections that remember every lie, every promise, every cargo.

    • The Tide Father — Khemesh. The pressure beneath the keel; the weight that the sea holds at ebb.

    • The Spider’s Daughter — Inaea (secret). The web that binds the oligarchs, strand by patient strand.

    • The Unlit — Ikasha. The ninth lamp that is never lit; the safe harbor that exists only in rumor.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • Saint Alvaro of the Lantern (The Traveler): The Admiralty’s mirror-towers double as wayshrines for the Saint of Lost Channels. Sailors who light a candle before a voyage and whisper a destination to the mirror claim the tide will not turn against them before they reach it. The Mirror-Keepers charge a fee for this service — payable in salt, not silver.

  • The Silken Coup (Inaea): The Lethai-ar call their infiltration the Genteel Harvest. They do not conquer; they adopt. An oligarch who accepts a gray-robed advisor finds that their children are betrothed to shadow-kin, their records are copied in disappearing ink, and their mirror-tower shows only the face they wish to see — not the one that watches them sleep.

  • The Tide-Sworn Compact (The Admiral of Sealed Tides): A small but influential order of notaries based in Kassamira Port. They adjudicate disputes over beacon rights, cargo claims, and pilotage fees. Their judgments are always delivered in triplicate, and they accept no bribes — only sworn promises. Those who lie before a Covenant judge find that their next three voyages are plagued by contrary winds.

  • The Hospitallers of the Salted Veil (The Bitter Root): A hospice on Deadman’s Quay is run by gray-robed healers of the Order. They ask no payment, but they never forget a face. It is said that if you accept their care, the sea remembers your name — and the sea never forgets. Smugglers leave small offerings of fresh water on the pier to stay in the Order’s good graces.

  • The Ninth Lantern Omen (d6): In any lantern-lit scene, the ninth lamp lit in a room reveals a threshold. The wise stop at eight. Those who light the ninth are said to see a figure standing on the water outside the window — waiting.

    1. The figure is a drowned sailor you once knew. It speaks your name in a voice like breaking ice.

    2. The lantern gutters and relights itself, burning green. The next ship to leave port will not return.

    3. A reflection in the water shows a version of you that made a different choice. It is still alive. It is still waiting.

    4. The flame casts no shadow. The Hollow has accepted your offering. You will not be asked to pay — tonight.

    5. The lamp’s oil turns to salt water. The figure is the Admiral of Red Shoal, and she offers a choice: a safe passage, or a secret.

    6. The ninth lamp is not lit by your hand. Someone else lit it. They are standing behind you, and they have a question.

In Kahfagia, power flows through control of the sea lanes themselves — and through the silken threads that bind the oligarchs to the Spider’s will. Tagline: The flame remembers. The marble cracks. The law forgets — but the statues watch.

Grand Magistrate (Elite): “The law is written in marble and witnessed by the flame. Every judgment echoes in the stones — but the stones have grown quiet. The patricians do not listen. They have forgotten what the flame tastes like. They seal their windows with silk and call it governance.”

Genre / Mood: Imperial decay, bureaucratic horror, memory as weight, Renaissance decadence — a city walled off from its own rot, where the wealthy dance while the poor cough ash.

Starting Location: The Coin-House floor, marble cracked with soot lines, where votive candles gutter beside melted icons. A factor with smoke-stained fingers offers a folded note. “The Lame King moves tonight. The Basilica burns with him. The Amber Palace hasn’t answered a summons in a month. Once the heart of the Utaran Empire, Ecktoria has worn its laurels into chains. Three centuries of sack, fire, and slow decline have not stripped its marble forums nor its gold-sealed coin-houses — but each day, more of its weight slips into the hands of mercenaries and whispers. The Black Banners march on its purse, avenging old Ykrul wounds by proxy. Processions still crown its stairways, yet ash lines every step. The city burns not only with memory, but with a grief that refuses to name itself.

Yet the true sickness is not in the streets. It is in the Amber Palace, where the old patrician families have walled themselves away. They sit on laurels preserved from the empire, drinking wine that costs a village’s harvest, whispering deals with merchants who have never seen a loom. Maelstraeus, the demon of commerce, has threaded their accounts with invisible tolls; every contract they sign forgets a clause. Livaea, the demon of desire, has wrapped their hearts in silk; they marry for influence, divorce for scandal, and love only the reflection of themselves in gilded mirrors — reflections that no longer blink. The common folk call them the Unburdened — not because they carry no weight, but because they have forgotten what weight feels like.

The Vigil — once the empire’s secret police, now a fractured ghost of an institution — fights a silent war over the dream of restoration. One faction, the Censer-Knights, serves the Everflame and the Synod, hunting heresy and latent magic with brass censers and bells that ring at dawn. Another, the Unburnt, answers only to the old patrician houses, ensuring that no whisper of rebellion reaches the Amber Palace. A third, the Red Vigil, has sold itself to Maelstraeus, auditing not coin but memory — they collect the names of those who have forgotten what they owe. They do not fight each other openly. They meet in the hypogeum of the Arena, under the gaze of statues that weep marble tears. The statues are not decorative. They are the city’s witnesses.

Beyond the capital’s walls, the province is a patchwork of struggling towns, abandoned fortresses, and grainlands whose harvests are disputed by rival tax-collectors. The Everflame Orthodoxy preaches unity, but each sermon is a coded signal for a political camp. The statues watch. The fire judges. And in the sewers, the Vigil’s informers listen for the name of the next person to vanish — not into death, but into the margins of a record no one will ever read.

What you notice first: The smell of old incense and fresh bread — and beneath it, the faint sweetness of rot. The way crowds part for a procession — and close again for a knife. The marble steps, worn smooth by centuries of petitioners. The way the wealthy never step on the cracks. The cracks are where the petitions of the poor are wedged. The cracks are where the Hollow reads.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never refuse a toast in a coin-house. The tallies are the true judges, and a declined cup is a declined trust. You will not borrow again. But more than that: never accept a gift from a patrician without naming its price aloud. Maelstraeus and Livaea both listen, and a gift accepted in silence is a contract written in water — it will drown you eventually.

Regional Mood: The Flame That Remembers — And The Dancers Who Forgot

Here, the past does not fade — it is polished into monuments. Every statue, every obelisk, every triumphal arch is a legal precedent carved in stone. But precedent is not justice. Precedent is memory, and memory is a weight that the city carries like a cracked keel. Ecktoria is not one city but three: the marble forums of the old patricians (where the Unburdened hold masques that last for weeks, their faces painted with ash from the Everflame), the soot-stained alleys of the mercenary camps (where Black Banner captains drink with Vigil informers), and the basilicas where the Everflame’s priests balance piety against profit (and sometimes sell indulgences for the sin of being born poor). The Grand Magistrate presides over a skeleton empire, but the real power moves in coin-houses, grain rolls, and the whispered judgments of the Censor’s Hall — and in the dreams of patricians who no longer remember the scent of rain.

The Vigil does not patrol the streets. It permeates them. Every tavern has an informer; every bathhouse has a spy; every marriage is witnessed by someone who reports to a faction of the Vigil. The Censer-Knights hunt unlicensed magic; the Unburnt hunt sedition; the Red Vigil hunts what was forgotten. They are not allies. They are not enemies. They are three creditors fighting over the same soul — and the soul is the city’s.

The patricians — the Old Blood — no longer govern. They perform governance. A session of the Parlement might see two senators debate the price of grain while their slaves hold silver trays of honeyed figs. A decree might be passed that doubles the salt tax, then rescinded an hour later because a duchess sneezed. They are not cruel. They are distracted. Livaea has taught them to crave the next pleasure before they have finished the current one. Maelstraeus has taught them that every transaction is a game, and every game is winnable if you have enough coin — but they have forgotten that the house always wins. The house is the Hollow.

The Everflame still burns in the basilicas, but its light has grown dim. The High Priest speaks of unity, but his sermons are interrupted by coughing from the pews — not from illness, but from the ash that falls like snow. The common folk whisper that the flame no longer judges truth; it only records it, filing confessions in a vault that no one ever opens. The Vigil has a key. The patricians have a copy. The poor have nothing but their names, which the Censor’s Hall writes in ink that fades when you look away.

The Everflame — Faith of Ash and Incense

The state religion of Ecktoria is a syncretic faith that venerates the Eternal Flame as the divine principle, but its power flows through three great Orders, each devoted to a different Patron. The Basilica of the Unquenched Flame is the heart of the city: a cavernous space of gold mosaics, weeping saints, and the perpetual smell of beeswax and myrrh. Mass is held at dawn and dusk, the congregation coughing through the ash that falls from the censers. The priests sell indulgences in the side chapels — a silver coin for the remission of a small sin, a gold coin for a large one, a horse for a murder. The choir sings in Utaran, a language most parishioners no longer understand, but the melody is beautiful enough to make them weep — and to make the statues turn their heads.

  • Order of the Oath of Flame & Light (Orthodox) — The public face of the church. They lead processions, bless marriages, and anoint the dying. Their rites are those of vows, radiance, and protection. They are the ones who light the great candles at dawn. Many are sincere; most are tired. The tired ones are the ones the Vigil watches.

  • Order of the Inquisitor Prime (Prosecutorial) — The Censer-Knights. They hunt untutored magic, root out demonic cults, and enforce the purity of the flame. Their rites are those of judgment, purification, and burning censure. They carry brass censers that smoke with bitter herbs, and bells that ring once for a warning, thrice for a raid. They are feared, but also respected — for they are the only ones who still take the flame seriously. And the flame takes them seriously in return.

  • Order of the Oath of Mercy and Grace (Hospitalier) — The healers and confessors. They run hospices, tend to the sick, and hear the confessions of the dying. Their rites are those of healing, forgiveness, and the spiral of care. They are often at odds with the Inquisitor Prime — mercy can look like leniency. The common folk love them; the patricians ignore them. The Vigil watches both.

The Everflame is both beautiful and ugly. Its gold leaf covers the same marble where petitioners once scratched their names. Its hymns are sung by choirboys whose families sold them for a loaf of bread. Its relics — a nail from the True Cross, a tear of Saint Lucia — draw pilgrims from across the empire, but the priests charge admission, and the poor sleep on the steps. The flame itself is a wonder: a pillar of blue-white fire that never gutters, tended by the High Priest alone. It is said to show the truth to any who stare into it long enough — and to show the same truth to the Vigil, who watch from the shadows. The truth is always the same: you have forgotten something.

The Three Ecktorias: Faction Spectrum

Ecktoria is not a single polity but a battlefield of three competing visions. Most citizens and factions fall somewhere between these poles, shifting allegiance as the grain price changes. Superimposed on them are the whispers of Maelstraeus and Livaea, who have no faction — only those who owe.

  • Old Blood (Patrician Traditionalists) – The ancient senatorial families who remember when Ecktoria ruled the world. They worship precedent, hoard marble, and see mercenaries as necessary filth. Their power is in law, lineage, and the silent networks of the Censor’s Hall. Timers: Patrician Patience [4], Blood Price [6]. Patron whispers: Livaea — they marry for influence, betray for pleasure, and mistake cruelty for wit. They mistake silence for consent.

  • Black Purse (Mercenary & Trade Leagues) – The new power: condotta brokers, coin-house factors, and mercenary captains who have bought what birth could not give. They care about supply lines, not bloodlines. Their loyalty is to the next contract, and their god is the tally — but the tally has no god. Timers: Contract Spiral [4], Payday Fever [6]. Patron whispers: Maelstraeus — they measure profit in margins, margins in suffering, and suffering in silver. They do not measure the weight of marble.

  • The Everflame (Church & Inquisition) – The priests, inquisitors, and relic-keepers who claim that the Eternal Flame is the only true sovereign. They offer legitimacy to whoever pays the tithe — and withdraw it from whoever forgets. Their power is in pulpits, processions, and the fear of excommunication. Timers: Heresy Index [4], Sanctuary Violation [6]. Patron whispers: Neither — the Everflame remembers, but the Vigil has learned to filter its truths. The flame’s truth is ash; the Vigil’s truth is silence.

Superimposed on these are the Ykrul creditors (who hold Ecktoria’s weight like a leash), the Black Banners (who hold its streets like a lease), and the Vigil (who hold its secrets like a knife — and sometimes use it). A wise politician learns to balance all three — and keeps a boat ready on the Dolmis.

The Vigil — Three Faces of the Secret Police

The Vigil is not one order but three, each claiming the mantle of the old imperial secret police. They do not cooperate. They do not war. They audit each other. They also audit the dead.

  • The Censer-Knights (Orthodox, Everflame) — Hunters of untutored magic. They carry brass censers that smoke with bitter herbs and bells that ring once for a warning, thrice for a raid. They answer to the Synod and the High Priest, but their true loyalty is to the precedent — the law that magic must be licensed, indexed, and taxed. A Censer-Knight raid is heralded by a single, low bell. They are the most feared and the most public. They are also the most forgetful. Timers: Candle-Smoke Sweep [4], Heretic Index [6].

  • The Unburnt (Patrician) — Bodyguards and spies of the Amber Palace. They wear red sashes and carry no visible weapons. Their job is to ensure that no whisper of rebellion, no bread riot, no unpaid toll ever reaches a patrician’s ear. They are experts at redaction — not killing witnesses, but making them forget. They have forgotten their own names. Timers: Palace Secrets [4], Redacted Memory [6].

  • The Red Vigil (Maelstraeus’s Own) — Collectors and enforcers. They serve no noble, no church, no magistrate — only the record. They audit the wealthy for the wealthy, finding hidden obligations, unpaid tithes, and compound tolls that have grown teeth. A Red Vigil agent never demands coin. They demand names. They also demand silence — the silence of the forgotten. Timers: Toll Due [4], Roll of Names [6].

The Vigil’s three commanders meet once a month in the hypogeum of the Arena, under the gaze of statues that weep marble tears. No record is kept of these meetings. No one admits they happen. The common folk say that if you see a Vigil agent, it is already too late — but they cannot agree on which Vigil. The statues know. The statues do not tell.

Not all at once—slowly, branch by branch, forest by forest. The Blackwood turned gray, then brittle, then silent. The farmers said it was a curse. The duke said it was blight. Nobody said it was guilt. Nobody had to. Guilt is the smell of mildew on old parchment. Guilt is the way a village watches you pass without opening its gates. Guilt is the empty chair at the feast, the unmarked grave behind the chapel, the child who will not meet your eyes because their parents told them not to speak to strangers.

The truth—the one we do not speak—is that we could have saved the Blackwood. We saw the first trees wither. We saw the sap run black. We did nothing. We told ourselves it would pass. We told ourselves the duke would fix it. We told ourselves the forest was not our responsibility. And then the forest died, and the silence that followed was our answer.

The Curse of Acasia is not a spell. It is a memory. The land remembers what we did—and what we failed to do. Every broken milestone on the old Imperial Road is a forgotten promise. Every abandoned vineyard is a harvest we chose not to plant. Every bridge that leads back to the same shore is the road we refused to take.

Never sign a contract on a bridge. The river is a witness, and it never forgets a signature. But more than that: the bridge knows when you are lying. Not to the other party. To yourself.

We are the Curse now. We carry it in our silence, in our empty fields, in the way we turn away when someone asks where the children went. Acasia is not a broken province. It is a province that broke itself, one small refusal at a time, until there was nothing left to break but the people who stayed.

And still we stay. Because leaving would mean admitting we were wrong. And that is the one thing guilt cannot bear.

— Corvin, charcoal-burner of the Blackwood, who no longer names the trees because there are too few left to name

What do people eat for breakfast? Hardtack soaked in broth—if you have broth. If not, hardtack and hope. The granaries have been empty for three winters. The merchants sell grain at famine prices. The duke says the roads are too dangerous for caravans. The farmers say the duke owns the caravans.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To name the dead. Every Acasian child has a list of ancestors they repeat at bedtime. The list gets shorter each generation. Not because children forget. Because the dead are forgetting themselves.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they have a shadow. The Curse takes shadows first. A man without a shadow is not cursed—he is already gone. His body just hasn’t stopped moving yet.

The roads were straight, the bridges paid, and the Curse was just a farmer’s whisper.

Then the empire rotted from within. When the last Utaran emperor died and the provinces broke away, Acasia lingered for another hundred years — a ghost province whose governors still stamped Utaran seals, whose courts still cited Utaran law, whose coins still bore the emperor’s face. No one came from the west to collect the taxes. No one sent legions to enforce the peace. The Acasians kept the forms, because they had never learned to keep anything else. They had no native king, no unifying faith, no common tongue beyond the legionary’s Utaran. They were the empire’s children, and when the empire died, they became orphans.

For a century they clung to the ruins. Then the plagues came. Then the famines. Then the Curse — a slow rot of roads that led nowhere, bridges that looped back on themselves, villages that woke to find their neighbors’ houses standing empty. The old imperial surveys became maps of places that no longer existed. The grain blights turned the storehouses to dust. The free companies, once hired to keep the peace, became the only law.

That was two hundred years ago.

Now Acasia is a wound that will not close. It sits between Ecktoria to the west and Vhasia to the east, claimed by both, ruled by neither. Petty kings raise their banners in burned keeps. Free Companies trade loyalty like grain, swapping colors mid-march. The Margravine of the Broken March keeps a tally of feuds, binding bloodlines to her feast days. The Lame King travels on a throne carried by debtors, making law by pointing. And the Curse — the nameless thing that makes crossroads turn back on themselves — is not a myth. It is the weight of broken promises, and Acasia is drowning in them.

South, on the marshy islands of the Amaranthine coast, the city of Silkstrand squats on reclaimed silt, trading in silk, silver, and secrets. The Acasians call it “the Sink” — a place where the Curse thins, but where law is measured in coin, not custom. Few Acasians go there. Fewer return unchanged.

What you notice first: The smell of mildew on old parchment and wet ash. The way every milestone has been recarved — first in Utaran, then in a half-dozen failed scripts. The silence of a village that heard you coming and chose not to open its gates.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never sign a contract on a bridge. The river is a witness, and it never forgets a signature. The Empire’s last governor signed a treaty on the Pont-du-Tithe. No one knows what it said. The bridge remembers.

Regional Mood: Famine, Ambition, and the Ghost of Empire

Here, the past is not a memory — it is a geometry. The Utaran Empire left behind roads that lead to nowhere, laws that contradict each other, and a hunger that no harvest can fill. Acasia’s petty kings hold court in burned keeps, their crowns forged from iron nails pulled from famine coffins. The Margravine keeps a tally of feuds, binding bloodlines to her feast days. Free Companies trade loyalty like grain, swapping colors mid-march. And beneath it all, the Curse waits. It is not a monster. It is a wrong turn: the bridge that leads back to the same shore, the milestone that faces the wrong direction, the bell that tolls for no funeral. In Acasia, the land itself has become a broken promise, and the fine print is written in mildew.

The Curse is not ancient. It is a legacy. Some say it began with the last Imperial Survey, when the surveyors tried to map every bridge, every ford, every toll—and in the measuring, broke something. Others say it was the plagues, when the dead were buried in unmarked pits and the priests forgot the proper rites. The hedge-witches say it was the empire’s own fault: they ruled Acasia for centuries but never taught the Acasians to rule themselves. When the empire left, the land had no king, no law, no memory. The Curse filled the gap.

Now the Margravine and the Lame King fight over the scraps, while free companies sell their swords to whichever claimant pays first. The hedge-witches tie red thread around bridges and leave offerings of salt at crossroads, hoping to keep the Curse from spreading. The villagers pray to saints the Empire forgot. And the roads — the old imperial roads — still march west toward a capital that no longer exists. The capital does not answer. But the roads keep marching.

The Curse of Acasia (Campaign Timer)

The Curse is a slow accumulation of broken promises and unpaid tolls. Track it with a visible timer:

Curse Awakening [10] – Advances when:

  • A contract is broken on a bridge (+1)

  • A character speaks the ninth word in a negotiation (+1)

  • The party ignores a road omen (GM spends 1 SB to tick +1)

  • A free company flips allegiance mid-contract (+2)

At 4 segments: Navigation rolls in Acasia gain +1 DV (roads shift). At 8 segments: The Curse Stirrs complication triggers once per session (roll on the table below). At 10 segments: The Curse manifests as a physical threat — a bridge eats a village, a road swallows a patrol, or the ghost of the last emperor rides.

Curse Stirrs (d10) When the timer reaches 8, roll 1d10 for an immediate complication:

  1. A milestone points the wrong way. Navigation DV +2 for the scene.

  2. A bridge you crossed earlier now loops back to where you started. Lose 1 segment of progress.

  3. A toll collector’s ghost demands payment in a currency that no longer exists. Pay 1 Boon or mark 1 Fatigue.

  4. A child with no shadow offers directions. If you follow, you arrive at your destination — but you forget why you came.

  5. The Margravine’s seal cracks. One of your Diamonds (writ, charter, token) becomes a forgery until you re-witness it.

  6. The Hollow’s breath freezes the ford. Crossing requires a Desperate roll; on a Miss, you lose 1 Supply (drowned).

  7. A ghostly legionary patrol marches past at midnight. If you watch them, you gain +1 die on the next roll against imperial constructs. If you speak to them, they demand a password that died with the empire.

  8. The bridge you are standing on remembers a name you have never spoken. It whispers it in your ear. Tick Curse Awakening +1; you may also learn a secret about the bridge’s history.

  9. A tax collector’s ghost appears with a ledger. It demands payment on a debt that is older than your grandfather. Pay 1 Boon or lose a memory.

  10. The crossroads shift. Three roads become one; the path you choose leads to a place you have already been. Lose 1 segment of progress and roll again.

Two crowns. One kingdom. A river so wide it might as well be a border—and oh, how the crowns love pretending it’s not.

I am a Road Warden. I have served under three different banners on this same stretch of road, and the only constant is that someone always wants to collect tolls. What do people eat for breakfast? Dark bread and watered wine—the wine cuts the dust from the road. The nobility eat spiced eggs and fresh cheese, but they eat behind closed doors. To eat well in public is to invite envy. Envy is a dagger.

What does a child learn before they can walk? Which colors not to wear. Black and gold are the Northern Crown’s colors. Red and silver are the Southern Parlement’s. A child who wears the wrong color to the wrong village may not return home.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Which side of the road they walk on. The north side is the Crown’s. The south side is the Parlement’s. The middle is no man’s land—literally. Bandits, spies, and messengers use the middle. So do ghosts.

What is the most common crime? Raiding across the Belworth. The river is the border, the border is a suggestion, and the suggestion is enforced by armed men on both sides. The crime is not in raiding. The crime is in being caught on the wrong bank at dawn.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Sun-court was not burned. It was bankrupted. The Fenwoods did not sack Lence—they bought it. Every judge, every clerk, every guard captain had a price. The Fenwoods paid it. The Vhasians are still paying interest.

Where do people go when they die? To the crypts beneath the Sun-court—if they can afford a plaque. The poor are buried in unmarked graves along the Belworth floodplain. The river floods every spring. The dead wash downstream, into the land of the other crown. Neither side claims them.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a chevauchée—a raiding party that burns a village in your name. The apology is not in the burning. The apology is in the promise that the village will be rebuilt with your own hands. If you burn a village and do not rebuild, you have not apologized. You have started a feud.

What is the most important festival? The Sun-return (spring equinox). Both crowns claim the sun as their symbol. On this day, both hold processions. The processions are identical. The participants do not acknowledge each other. The sun does not care.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Queen-Mother has no claim to the regency. Her son is not the Dauphin. The real Dauphin died in childbirth. The Queen-Mother’s daughter was stillborn. The child on the throne is a foundling, bought from a Tulkani caravan. The Tulkani know. The Tulkani are still waiting to be paid.

Vhasia is a realm of broken coronations and split loyalties, still bleeding from a wound a decade old. Once a proud province of the Utaran empire, the unified Sun Court shattered like its heraldic sunburst when the Fenwoods of Viterra and their allies crossed the Belworth, sacked the capital of Lence, and cast down the old Blood line. Duke Braedyn Fenwood—uncle of Veralyn, now the Warrior Queen of Viterra—led the assault, his ironshod cavalry grinding the Sun King’s army into the mud of the Ford of Ashes. They burned Lence until the mirrors melted and the very stones wept. They left the throne empty—not destroyed, but open. And they left a wound that has not healed.

But the wound runs deeper than smoke and stone. Forty years ago, during the Province Wars, King Wymund of Viterra sought alliance with Vhasia through marriage—taking Elianor Everblood (née Longblood) as his queen. She bore him two daughters, his heir Valdais Longblood, and a second son, Wyllmot, who was made heir to the Viterran throne. When Wymund died, Elianor married his son Baldvyn II, becoming queen of Viterra again and binding the two realms in blood—but also in rumor. The jealousies between these unions ignited the provincial wars that weakened both realms, culminating in Wyllmot’s clash with Valdais at Adria—a victory stained by the suspicion that brother had fought brother for a throne built on doubtful bloodlines.

When the Fenwoods came, Elianor was in her western duchy of Ethor, having married into the Vhasian royal Bloods line—a final attempt to stitch the realm back together. Her nephew had been made heir to the Sun Court on condition of that marriage, a settlement that the sack of Lence tore apart utterly. Now, Elianor rules from Ethor, her alliances woven from the threads of three marriages and the quiet terror that none of her sons may be truly “hers” by the blood that matters. She is not mourning. She is waiting—for proof, for a sign, for the moment when the fractured sun might finally cast a single shadow.

The memory of the sack haunts every negotiation. The Queen-Mother, Elianor Everblood, lost her third husband—the old Sun King—and her cousins and sisters in the flames. Her second son, Wyllmot, King of Viterra and Duke of Aberiel, died fighting rebels in Viterra, leaving his crown to his daughter, Veralyn—who is now the Warrior Queen, who was the Fenwoods’ ward, and who was there when they burned Lence. Elianor rules from a riverside keep, weaving alliances and hiding a secret—a Dauphin, perhaps her grand-nephew, perhaps a changeling, who may be the key to restoring the Sun Court. Her eldest son, Valdais Longblood, Duke of Adria in the west, claims the throne by blood and steel, but Elianor does not trust him. She has outlived three husbands and two sons. She is not mourning. She is waiting.

The Fenwood sack was not just a military defeat; it was a betrayal of chivalry itself. The knights of Vhasia had sworn to protect the realm with the Round of Five Virtues: Courage, Courtesy, Mercy, Prowess, Truth. At Lence, the Fenwoods showed no mercy. The knights still argue about whether chivalry died there or whether it was already a corpse, dressed in armour, a thin veneer over the cruelty of the world. The Cantor Alain sings the Planctus Armatus each spring, and the congregation weeps for Sir Corin, who unbuckled his gorget to hear a thief’s voice and was undone by the mercy he offered. Sir Corin died at Lence. He was trying to save a child. The child is now the Dauphin—or so the Queen-Mother claims—and perhaps the living proof that Elianor’s bloodline, however tangled, still holds the true sun.

What you notice first: The smell of old smoke and fresh ink. The way every noble’s seal has a hairline crack—like the fault lines in old marriage alliances. The constant, nervous laughter of courtiers who remember whose head rolled last season—and whose mother married which king. The way the knights touch their gorgets when someone speaks of mercy, remembering Sir Corin’s unbuckling at Lence—and the queen who watched it all from Ethor, wondering if the mercy she’d been shown was ever real.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never carry a coin that bears only one sunburst. The realm has three mints, and the wrong metal on the wrong road is treason. Also, never speak the name “Fenwood” without naming Queen Veralyn first. The wound is still raw. The Queen-Mother’s spies are everywhere—and they have long memories. And never forget: the first treason was not in the field, but in the nursery, when a queen wondered if the son in her cradle was truly the king’s.

Here, the past is a weapon and the future is a weight—not of silver, but of memory. The Fenwoods came from Viterra across the Belworth with fire and iron, broke the Sun King’s army at the Ford of Ashes, and burned Lence until the mirrors melted. Duke Braedyn Fenwood led them; his daughter Veralyn—now the Warrior Queen of Viterra—was her father’s attendant and witness to the sack, a fact that complicates every border negotiation. So they fight each other instead.

The Ninth Taboo Manifestation

In any Vhasian court, the ninth citation of precedent voids all previous. The wise stop at eight and settle. Those who cite the ninth are said to hear a faint laughter from the marble walls—and their cases are never heard again. The laughter is the dead king’s. He was the ninth king of his line. He died at Lence. He is still laughing—because the ninth citation is the only one that names the Fenwoods as the true heirs. No one has ever cited it. No one dares.

Also, the ninth cup poured at a feast is for the Hollow. The wise pour eight and leave the ninth empty. Those who pour the ninth see a pale figure standing at the high table—waiting to collect a weight. The figure is the dead king, checking his oath-stick.

The Province Wars (Forty Years Ago)

Before the Fenwood sack, there was the Province Wars—a generation of low-intensity raiding between the cantons that left the old crown weakened and the knights bitter. The peace that ended the Province Wars was a treaty of exhaustion, not reconciliation. It was during those wars that the dynastic tangles began: King Wymund of Viterra married Elianor Everblood to seal the peace, only for her to later wed his son Baldvyn II after Wymund’s death—a union that sparked the very conflicts it was meant to prevent. The veterans of the Province Wars are now the old men who watch the new claimants with weary eyes. They remember when mercy meant something. They also remember when it cost everything—and they whisper that the first casualty of those wars was trust in the marriage beds of queens.

The Players of the Great Game

The succession crisis has four major factions, each with a claim, a secret, and a price—each shadowed by the legacy of dual marriages and uncertain parentage.

  • Queen-Mother Elianor Everblood (née Longblood): Widow of three kings—Wymund of Viterra, his son Baldvyn II, and the last Sun King. Mother of Valdais and the late Wyllmot. She rules the western canton of Ethor. Her price is a guarantee that her grand-nephew (the Dauphin) will not be killed. Her secret: she is not sure if Valdais is truly her son by Wymund—or if he was conceived during the turbulent shift between husbands. She acts as if he is of the Blood, because certainty would destroy what remains of the Longblood line—and because the Dauphin may be her only hope for an heir without doubt.

  • Duke Valdais Longblood of Adria: The western claimant. Nephew—or half-brother, depending on the rumor—to the last Sun King. A tournament champion with a flawless record and a suspicious habit of opponents falling ill before jousts. His claim rests on blood and steel. His secret: his mother, Elianor, does not trust him—and he suspects why. He was not at Lence. He wishes he had been—so he could have died with honor instead of living with the question: “Whose son am I, truly?”

  • The Two Crowns (Rival Dukes of the South and East): Backed by the southern parlements and the eastern march lords who face the Belworth. Their claim rests on election and expediency. Their secret: they have been known to accept Viterran gold—but never the Fenwood seal. They whisper that Veralyn is not her father’s daughter. They are wrong. They are also afraid—afraid that if the Viterran line is false, then the Vhasian claim via Elianor’s marriages is equally doubtful, and the realm has no true sun left to claim.

  • The Last Dauphin (The False/Real Heir): A child of nine years, hidden in the Queen-Mother’s keep. His legitimacy is a matter of faith. His existence is the only thing keeping Valdais from marching on his mother’s castle. His secret: even Elianor does not know if he is truly her grand-nephew—but she has dreamed of Sir Corin, who died saving a child at Lence. The child in the dream has the Dauphin’s face—and perhaps, just perhaps, the unbroken line of Elianor’s blood, however it flowed.

The Cantor Alain and the Planctus Armatus

No figure embodies Vhasia’s tragedy more than Alain, the Iron Bard. A cantor who survived the sack of Lence as a boy—rescued by Sir Corin himself—Alain carries a cracked ring and a voice that carries the weight of the dead. Each dusk, he sings the Planctus Armatus (the Lament for the Armoured), a song that recounts Corin’s unbuckling and the mercy that killed him. The song has eight verses. The ninth is silence—the space where the Hollow listens. The fourth verse, oft-whispered, speaks of “the queen who wore two crowns, and the son who knew not which father’s blood he carried.”

On a failure, they mark 1 Fatigue—the weight of remembering. If they recite the fourth verse specifically, they may instead gain +1 Effect on a roll involving inheritance or legitimacy—but mark 1 Corruption, as the doubt it stirs is a poison to certainty.

(Château/Cathedral/Forest)

Wayside shrine with pilgrims tacking petitions to a scorched sun disc; vine-terrace clos above a millstream with napping watchmen. Spades — Places

  1. Wayside Shrine – Pilgrims tack petitions to a scorched sun disc; each parchment curls in different winds. The petitions are never answered. The shrine is a mailbox for the dead—especially those who fell at Lence. The dead do not write back. They wait.

  2. Vine-Terrace – Clos above a millstream; watchmen nap, halberds traded for bottles. The best vintage is called “Crownbreaker.” It tastes like smoke and regret. The year of the sack was a particularly good harvest. The wine remembers.

  3. Bastide Square – Neat as a checkerboard; merchants pay homage to different “hours” of the Sun. The square has a gallows. It rotates to face whichever claimant lost the last duel. The wood is still green—from the forest where the Fenwoods camped before Lence.

  4. Royal Forest – Antler posts mark laws no king remembers; poachers still hang. The forest’s deer have grown bold. They wear crowns of shed antlers. Some say they are the ghosts of the old nobility—or the knights who unbuckled their gorgets too late.

  5. Pont-du-Tithe – Tollbridge flanked by chapels and crossbows; chains blessed by rival bishops. The chains were forged from melted sunbursts. They ring in different keys. The Fenwood army crossed here from the east. The bridge still groans when Viterrans approach. The groans are prayers.

  6. Great Fair – Outside a chartered town; counting sheds ring like bells, rumors louder still. The fair has a Court of Miracles where counterfeiters sell to informers. The best forgery is a sunburst coin that rings true until you test its weight. The weight of a lie is memory.

  7. Salt Pans – Windpumps on an estuary; gulls own the law, smugglers own the night. The salt is gray. It tastes of old tears and older weights. The smugglers carry more than salt—they carry the Queen-Mother’s letters. The letters smell of Lence.

  8. Siege-Château – Hoardings patched, cistern low, banners cut into ragged suns. The banner’s ragged edges are from a Fenwood sword. The owner still limps. He lost his son at Lence. He fights for Valdais but he does not love him. He loves the ghost of his son.

  9. Cathedral Yard – Cranes creak, stones fly, scaffolds bristle with rival masons’ marks. The mason’s marks are clan signs. A scratched-out mark means a dead foreman. One mason’s mark is a sunburst with a crack—the mark of the Queen-Mother’s architect. He was at Lence. He built the pyres.

  10. Parlement Hall – Hung with faded tapestries; sunbursts spliced and restitched by claimants. The tapestries show battles that never happened—and some that shouldn’t have. One tapestry shows the sack of Lence from the Fenwood perspective. The Queen-Mother has ordered it burned three times. It keeps reappearing. The thread is from the shrouds of the dead.

  11. Queen’s Causeway – Mile-long oath paved in quarried stone; half-sunk each spring. The sunken half is a graveyard for vows. The stones are slippery with old lies. The Queen-Mother walks here at dawn. She speaks to the dead. The dead answer in the voice of the Dauphin.

  12. Sun Palace (Lence) – Shuttered mirrors, brambled gardens; courtiers playing at kingship. The mirrors were covered after the sack. No one wants to see what they show now—a reflection of a kingdom that no longer exists. The courtiers pretend the king still lives. The servants know better. The mirrors know best.

  13. King’s High Road – Milestones chipped of crowns; yet every army still marches it. The road is paved with the bones of tax collectors. They are not missed. The Fenwood army marched here from the Belworth. Their footprints are still visible—in the stone and in the memory of every orphan.

Hearts — People & Factions

  1. Road Warden – Dented badge, honest horn; ignored by both crowns but knows the roads. His horn is cracked. It still sounds—but the note is always half a step flat. He was at Lence. He does not speak of it. The horn does.

  2. Vintner Syndic – Counts saints’ days as grace periods, vintages as treaties; wine as diplomacy. He keeps a bottle of Crownbreaker in every cellar. He knows which vintage each duke prefers. He also knows which duke’s wine is poisoned. He does not ask. The poison is memory.

  3. Abbess-Chatelaine – Rules a fortress-convent with iron rule and sharper scripture. She has never drawn a blade. The scaffold has, on her orders. She sheltered refugees after the sack. The Queen-Mother owes her a weight. The Queen-Mother does not forget weights—or the smell of burning wool.

  4. Routier Captain – Scarred and mercenary; prefers ransoms to loyalty; coin as conscience. He switches banners as often as his shirt. His loyalty is to the paychest—and to his mother. He fought for the Fenwoods at Lence. He still has nightmares. He pays for them in silver.

  5. Duchy Constable – Splendid armour, empty coffers, pride swollen as a banner; glory over gold. His armour was his grandfather’s. It still has the dent from a Fenwood axe. He serves Valdais because Valdais pays—but he served the old king first. He cannot forgive himself for surviving Lence. The dent weeps rust.

  6. Cantor Alain – The Iron Bard, bearer of the Cracked Ring; his songs are the only honest history. Feathers grow from his temples. His shadow moves a half-step behind him. He sings the names of the dead every dusk. The dead listen. And sometimes, they sing back.

  7. Salt-Farmer – Gabelle lessee with cudgels, writs, and a river-chart no crown has; tax as tyranny. His tally is written in salt. One drop of water and the obligations vanish—along with the evidence. He is the Queen-Mother’s man. He does not say so aloud. His salt tastes of ash.

  8. Trouvère – A scandalous lay about the last Sun King brings knives to tavern doors. He sings the truth. That is why he sleeps in a different inn every night. He knows who really ordered the burning of Lence. He will not say. He values his tongue. The truth is a currency he spends in silence.

  9. Marshal-Exile – The Sun Court’s hammer; now wielded by whoever pays in silver. He lost the Battle of Ashes. He still sees it in his dreams and his nightmares. He was the last man to see the old king alive. The king’s last words were a curse on the Fenwood name. The curse is still unpaid.

  10. Heretic Perfect – Preaches peace while tucking hidden daggers into parchment margins. His sermon is a code. The psalms tell you which bridge to cross and which to burn. He is the Queen-Mother’s spy. He is also Valdais’s spy. He is also his own spy. His sermons are the only honest prayers left.

  11. Queen-Mother – Riverside keep in the west; weaves alliances as if they were dowries. She has outlived three husbands and two sons. She is not mourning. She is waiting. Her grand-nephew—real or false—will sit on the Sun Court. She will not live to see it. She has made her peace with that. The peace tastes of Lence.

  12. Two Crowns – Rival dukes, each “crowned” in rival cathedrals, neither whole. One wears a crown of iron. The other wears a crown of words. Both rust. Neither was at Lence. Neither speaks of it. They pay others to speak for them. And sometimes, they whisper that if the Viterran line is false, then the Vhasian claim via Elianor’s marriages is equally doubtful.

  13. Last Dauphin – A rumor with a birthmark; armies march for whispers alone. He may be a baker’s son. The armies don’t care. They need a sun to follow. He has never seen the sun. He knows three languages, the lineage of every noble house, and the taste of every poison. He does not know if he is a prince or a puppet. He does not ask. He dreams every night of a knight with an unbuckled gorget.

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = leverage. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Diamonds are codified outcomes (charters/patents/rights) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo fractured sun motifs—light that splinters, oaths that crack like glass, smoke that smells of Lence, and a child who may be a prince or a puppet. Also echo the weight of memory: a ghost knight, a mirror that shows the sack, a bell that rings for the dead.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Fractured Seals: When brandishing a writ, badge, or charter tied to a Sun Court claimant, step Position up one step for the scene’s next interaction at a gate, court, or checkpoint. Each use advances a Legitimacy [4] timer linked to the claimant; when it fills, rivals challenge the authority on sight. The seal’s crack widens with each challenge. Eventually, it will break—and the broken seal will smell of Lence.

  • Parlement Games: If a scene touches petitions, guild disputes, or feudal appeals, the first Diamond played becomes a policy lever: treat it as two Diamonds toward swaying a decision or rewriting a custom. Afterward, start or advance a Whispered Precedent timer; when it fills, backlash spreads as other courts cite the case against you. The Queen-Mother’s clerks are masters of this. They have never lost—because they have never forgotten the price of losing. The price is Lence.

  • Gabelle Knife-Edge: At bridges, fairs, or salt depots, convert Diamonds tied to tolls, leases, or safe-conducts into immediate passage or tariff relief for the company (no roll). Each conversion ticks a Resentment [4] timer for the locale; when it completes, expect boycotts, ambush rumors, or an audit by rival officials. The salt farmers remember Lence. They do not forget. The salt in their tears is gray.

  • Vhasian Chivalry & the Round of Five Virtues: The knights of the western cantons (Ethor, Adria) follow the Round of Five Virtues: Courage, Courtesy, Mercy, Prowess, Truth. In a formal joust or a legal duel, the GM may award Victory by Virtue rather than by blood: the combatant with the higher Virtue score wins the dispute, even if they lost the physical exchange. This is the law of the tilt and the law of the court. But at Lence, the Fenwoods showed no mercy. The knights still argue about whether chivalry died there—or whether it was already a corpse, dressed in armour, a thin veneer over savagery. And sometimes, they whisper that the first crack in the sun came not from the Fenwoods’ swords, but from the marriage beds where queens bore sons to two kings—and the sons who never knew which father’s blood they carried.

  • The Armor Closes / The Song Escapes (Chivalric Timers): The Cantor Alain, bearer of the Cracked Ring, sings a warning at every tourney:

    • Armor Closes [6] advances when knights choose protection over mercy, when gorgets are buckled tight, when chivalry becomes siege mentality. When full, the cantons close their borders and the raids become wars. The Fenwoods taught Vhasia that mercy is a weakness. The knights have not forgotten. They wear their gorgets to bed.

    • Song Escapes [8] advances when a Cantor sings the truth, when mercy is shown, when armour is unbuckled. When full, a moment of genuine connection occurs—though it may be fatal. The Cantor Alain has been trying to fill this timer for ten years. He is still trying. He sings every dusk. The dead listen—and sometimes they answer.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • Seint Michel, the Judge of the Scar (Mykkiel): The law that does not bend; the judge who cannot be bought. His shrines are at tourney fields and courtrooms. Knights swear oaths on his scale before the joust.

    • Seint Isabeau, Who Slumbers Amongst the Lilies (Ikasha): The dream of setting aside the crown; the mercy of forgetting duty. Her groves demand that armour be unbuckled at the threshold.

    • Seint Evroul of the Cairn (Mor’iraath): The oath that outlasts the oath-taker; the blood-price paid in stone. The barrow-kings demand tribute in stories, not gold.

    • The Cantor’s Road (The Traveler): The road that belongs to no lord; the path that finds its own way. Cantors are immune to chivalric protection—but subject to its violence.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • Seint Michel, the Judge of the Scar (Mykkiel): The Order of the Gilded Scale maintains the Chivalric Roll—a record of every oath sworn and broken in the cantons.   • Seint Isabeau, Who Slumbers Amongst the Lilies (Ikasha): In the Ethorian marches, knights whisper prayers to Seint Isabeau when they long to set aside their steel. Her shrine is a hawthorn grove where armour must be left at the gate. Pilgrims who enter bare-skinned and speak a true regret find that their next wound heals in a single night—but they dream of a woman in white who asks, “Will you remember how to be touched?” Those who return to the grove a second time are never seen again. The knights who survived Lence do not go near the grove. They are afraid of what they might forget—or what they might remember.

  • Seint Evroul of the Cairn (Mor’iraath): In the high passes of the Ethorian marches, a rusted knight in an unopened helm haunts the old barrows. He will witness one blow; if it lands, the foe is Marked by the Cairn—any spirit may locate them for a year. If it misses, the blow turns inward. The Marshal-Exile carries a scar from such a bargain; he does not speak of it. He named the Fenwoods. The blow missed. He still does not understand why. The Cairn weeps rust.

They ask no payment, but they record every name in a scroll that never dries. The Restorationists suspect they are spies for the Queen-Mother; the Two Crowns accuse them of erasing obligations. Sister Oria, the hospice’s custodian, is also the keeper of the Lantern-Rites House on the Belworth—neutral ground where even claimants must leave their blades at the door. The scroll has a page for every person who died at Lence. The page is still being written. The ink is still wet. The ink is made from ash and tears.

In Vhasia, power flows through the remnants of imperial authority, fractured legitimacy, and the careful manipulation of memory and mercy. Former Utaran provincial structures still provide a framework for governance, but competing claimants to the Sun Court have created a complex web of overlapping jurisdictions and conflicting loyalties. The true power lies with those who can navigate this legal maze—and who understand that the only currency that matters is the memory of Lence.

But beneath the charters and the writs runs a deeper current: the wound that will not close. The Fenwood sack was not just a military defeat; it was a wound to the soul of Vhasia, proof that chivalry was only ever a thin veneer. Every negotiation, every duel, every sealed document carries the weight of that loss. The Queen-Mother plays a long game, her Dauphin a card she may never play. Valdais wants the throne his father could not hold. The Two Crowns will accept any solution that leaves them richer. And the common folk—the farmers, the salt-panners, the road wardens—just want the raiding to stop.

They will wait. They have waited ten years. They can wait longer.

For the GM: Patronage in Vhasia revolves around legitimacy, legal documents, and the ability to command respect from institutions that no longer have a clear central authority. Rewards often take the form of charters, writs, and official positions that can be leveraged into greater influence. To emphasize the post-sack grief and the fragile peace:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (seals, badges, writs) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided—especially if they bear the old king’s seal or the Fenwood mark.

  • Let rival claimants issue conflicting authorities, forcing players to choose whose favor matters more—and whose memory of Lence they are willing to invoke.

  • Use the parlements, cathedrals, and châteaux as arenas for social contests, where knowledge of law and custom determines success—and where the mere mention of the Fenwood name can shift the tide.

  • Introduce the Lence’s Shadow mechanic: whenever a scene involves a legal dispute, a military manoeuvre, or a negotiation with a noble who lost family in the sack, the GM may spend 1 SB to invoke the memory of the burning. The Position worsens by one step, and the losing party may gain the Haunted condition.

  • Use the Armor Closes and Song Escapes timers as a visible campaign front. The party’s actions—acts of mercy or cruelty—tick one or the other. When the Armor Closes, the cantons become fortresses; when the Song Escapes, a moment of genuine connection may change everything—or kill everyone.

In Vhasia, your papers are your power, and your power determines whether you rule or are ruled. But your memory of Lence—and how you use it—determines whether anyone will follow you.

The Vacant Throne & Campaign Timers (For longform political campaigns)

  • Vacant Throne Timer [10]: Advances when a claimant gains a major advantage (battle, noble defection, foreign alliance). Retreats when the party brokers a truce or exposes a weakness. When full, a claimant takes Lence and declares themselves king. The Queen-Mother will not live to see it. She will die the day the sun rises whole again.

  • Armor Closes [6]: Advances when knights choose protection over mercy, when gorgets are buckled tight, when chivalry becomes siege mentality. When full, the cantons close their borders and the raids become wars. The Fenwoods taught Vhasia that mercy is a weakness. The knights have not forgotten. They sharpen their gorgets.

  • Song Escapes [8]: Advances when a Cantor sings the truth, when mercy is shown, when armour is unbuckled. When full, a moment of genuine connection occurs—though it may be fatal. The Cantor Alain has been trying to fill this timer for ten years. He is still trying. He sings every dusk. The dead listen—and sometimes they answer.

  • Claimant’s Coup (per claimant): A 6-timer that advances when that claimant wins a Parlement vote, a joust, or a skirmish. When full, they march on Lence. The Dauphin—real or false—watches from the Queen-Mother’s keep. He does not know if he should cheer or weep.

  • Lence’s Reckoning [8]: Tracks the party’s engagement with the memory of the sack. Advances when they aid a survivor, recover a relic, or speak the truth about what happened. When full, a survivor reveals a secret: the old king’s last words, the location of a hidden treasure, or the true parentage of the Dauphin. The secret will change the succession. It will also change the party. Some secrets are heavier than crowns.

The Succession Campaign Structure

A Vhasian succession campaign follows three phases. The Dauphin’s legitimacy, determined above, shapes every encounter.

Phase 1: The Investigation (Sessions 1–3)

The party is hired (by the Queen-Mother, by Valdais, or by a neutral party) to determine the truth of the Dauphin’s claim. Key scenes:

  • The Queen-Mother’s Keep: Elianor grants an audience. She speaks of the Dauphin but does not show him. She tests the party’s loyalty with small favors. Her spies watch.

  • The Cantor’s Testimony: Alain sings the Planctus Armatus. If the party listens carefully, the fourth verse hints at the Queen-Mother’s doubt. If they press him, he may reveal the cracked ring—and the name of the child Sir Corin died to save.

  • The Valdais Gambit: Valdais offers the party a counter-offer: find proof that the Dauphin is false, and he will grant lands, titles, and his eternal gratitude. He is desperate. He does not know the truth himself.

  • The Two Crowns’ Proposal: The rival dukes offer a simpler bargain: support their election, and the Dauphin’s legitimacy becomes irrelevant. They will pay in silver and pardons.

Phase 2: The Confrontation (Sessions 4–6)

The party has gathered evidence. Now they must decide:

  • The Dauphin’s Reveal: The child is brought before the party. He is nine years old. He knows the taste of every poison. He has never seen the sun. He asks: “Am I real?”

  • The Trial of Blood: A formal inquiry is called at the Cathedral Yard. Claimants present evidence. The party may testify. The dead of Lence are watching.

  • The Forged Charter: Someone has produced a document that purports to prove the Dauphin’s legitimacy—or his falsehood. The document is a forgery. Who made it? The party must decide whether to expose it or use it.

  • The Queen-Mother’s Confession: Elianor speaks privately to the party. She reveals the truth (as determined by the d6 roll). She asks them to keep the secret—or to act on it.

Phase 3: The Resolution (Sessions 7–8)

The succession is decided. The party’s actions determine the outcome:

  • The Vacant Throne Fills: One claimant takes Lence. The Queen-Mother may live or die. The Dauphin may rule, be exiled, or be killed.

  • Armor Closes or Song Escapes: The party’s choices have pushed one of the chivalric timers to full. The cantons either seal their borders or open their hearts. The realm changes.

  • Lence’s Reckoning: The memory of the sack is either honored or buried. A survivor reveals a final secret—the old king’s last words, the location of a hidden treasure, or the true name of the child who was never meant to be found.

GM Guidance: Running the Succession

  • Let the dice decide: The Dauphin’s legitimacy is random each campaign. This prevents players from assuming they know the outcome. The mystery is real.

  • The Queen-Mother’s knowledge: If the Dauphin is false, Elianor may know or not know. Her behavior shifts accordingly. If she knows, she is a pragmatist; if she does not, she is a desperate grandmother.

  • Valdais’s desperation: Valdais does not know the truth. He suspects his mother is hiding something, but he cannot prove it. He will believe whatever the party tells him—if they seem convincing.

  • The Cantor’s role: Alain can reveal the truth, but only if the party earns his trust. He carries the cracked ring, and he knows the name of the child Sir Corin died to save. He may not know the child is the Dauphin. He may know and have chosen silence.

  • The Hollow’s interest: The Ninth Taboo is a constant presence. If the party cites the ninth citation, the dead king’s laughter may reveal a secret they were not meant to hear. The secret is always about the succession.

Terrestrial Patrons: Myrmis and Canray

Two lesser, regional patrons haunt Vhasian court life without ever being invoked by name in polite company.

Myrmis answers prayers in kind, not in mercy — a hound given for a wish, a warrior-daughter for three gentle ones. Her worshippers are rare, hidden, and disproportionately Renard by blood; her sites were burned during the Seven Week Uprising, and her surviving faithful read every death at court as a payment coming due. A PC who takes Myrmis as a patron should expect exactly what they ask for, priced however the GM finds most interesting.

Canray, called Kon’reh in the old Aeler tongue, is not a god but a discipline — the board game, the ledger-logic, and the patience the Fenwoods carried out of exile. Courtiers who “play Canray” are not gaming; they are negotiating in a shared vocabulary of clauses and forfeits that outsiders mistake for small talk. A PC who takes Canray as a patron (treat as an unusual but valid Terrestrial Patron) trades raw power for the ability to always, eventually, collect — at the cost of never being allowed to act purely on impulse without narrative consequence.

What do people eat for breakfast? Barley porridge with a drizzle of honey—if you own bees. If not, porridge with salt. Salt is the currency of the poor. Honey is the currency of the hopeful.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To recite their lineage back three generations. Every Viterran child knows their grandparents’ names, their grandparents’ trades, and their grandparents’ debts. A child who cannot recite their lineage is a child without standing. Standing is everything.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they carry a seal ring. A seal ring means they have the right to make contracts in their own name. A stranger without a seal ring is either a servant or a criminal. In Viterra, the difference is a matter of paperwork.

What is the most common crime? Boundary disputes. A hedge moved three feet east. A milestone replaced under cover of darkness. A ford declared ”private“ when it has been public for two centuries. The courts are clogged with such cases. The lawyers are rich. The hedges are patient.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Queen is not a warrior. She has never drawn a sword. Her reputation was forged by a propaganda campaign run by her spymaster. The spymaster is dead. His successor is more discreet.

Where do people go when they die? To the family plot—if they have a family. The landless poor are buried in parish cemeteries, their headstones marked with a single word: ”Anonymous.“ The ghosts of the anonymous wander the hedgerows at dusk. They do not remember their names. They remember their debts.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You petition the hedge-court for a ”writ of contrition.“ The writ requires you to state your wrong aloud, in public, while holding a handful of earth from the wronged party’s land. If the earth crumbles, you are forgiven. If it holds, your apology is not accepted. You may try again next season.

What is the most important festival? The Opening of the Rolls (autumn). Every county sends its tax records to the capital. The procession is a week long. The documents are carried by children, so that the next generation may learn the weight of obligation.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Codex of Precedents has a missing page. It was torn out fifty years ago, during a trial for treason. The page contained the precedent for executing a monarch. The Queen knows. The Justiciars know. The page is hidden in a lead-lined box beneath the hedge-court at Thornwood. The hedge-court is haunted. The ghost knows where the key is.

(Reeve/Guild/Knight/Legal Advocate)

Fen reeve with tally-rod speaking for dike guilds; river-carter syndic moving grain faster than rumor; justices of the peace settling disputes with careful pen—and a wary eye for Vhasian poison in the ink.

Using the Viterra Deck

Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = leverage. Highest rank sets the main Timer : 2–5 → 4 segments, 6–10 → 6 segments, J/Q/K → 8 segments, A → 10 segments.

Diamonds are codified outcomes (writs, charters, priority) that change position rather than call for a roll.

If any Ace appears, echo hedge-law motifs—boundaries blur like old scars, charters contradict like guilty consciences, precedent splinters like shaky oaths, and the careful web of legal authority shows its living cracks. Legal warfare escalates to physical conflict not as failure, but as the only language left when words run dry.

Viterra Mechanics

Core Timers
Border-Lace [4]

Tracks competing legal claims in a locale. Starts when contradictory papers are invoked. Ticks when:

  • A second (or third) valid writ is presented for the same right (+1).

  • A player steps up Position using Hedge-Law Momentum (+1).

  • A Vhasian or local authority disputes a crown document (+1).

At 4: The dispute escalates immediately to a riot, duel, or jurisdictional void. All social rolls in the locale suffer +1 DV until a ruling is imposed by force or decree.

Succession’s Shadow [6]

Tracks the erosion of Queen Veralyn’s legitimacy from the ghosts of Baldwyn Brightstide’s death and the lingering resentment of the northern earls and eastern barons. Ticks when:

  • A document bearing Baldwyn’s seal surfaces (+1).

  • A northern earl or eastern baron publicly defies the crown (+1).

  • A legal duel is lost by the Queen’s champion (+1).

  • The GM spends 2 SB to introduce a witness, forgery, or rumor tied to the crisis (+1).

At 3: Viterran officials and allies suffer –1 die to social rolls (they doubt the throne’s stability). At 6: A serious claimant emerges—perhaps a northern earl with a forged charter, or a Vhasian pretender promising to restore the old border. The Queen must win a legal duel or face a general levy against her authority.

Queen’s Peace [Front]

Once per session, if the party resolves a cross-border dispute in the Queen’s favor without bloodshed, advance this front. If they escalate violence or validate a Vhasian claim, tick Succession’s Shadow instead.

Status & Environment
Hedge-Law Momentum

When a valid writ, charter, or warrant is presented in the proper parish with dry ink and unbroken seal, step Position up one category on social or logistical actions in that locale for the scene. The first contradiction starts the Border-Lace [4] timer.

Beacon & Bell Chain

If two adjacent beacons or brotherhood bells are under your influence and lit for the correct reason, convert one Club draw into a travel delay for others while the party proceeds with improved Effect for movement and logistics. The signal draws scrutiny; the GM may bank 1 SB tied to local authority.

River-Right Window

At a ferry, tidework, or customs stair, play any Diamond tied to crossings or seals to open a short window of lawful passage: one convoy passes uninspected, or one cargo clears at priority. The window closes immediately if a storm, audit, or levy card is revealed.

Legal Duel Protocol

When legal disputes cannot be resolved through argument, parties may challenge each other to a duel. The winner’s position becomes legally binding. Requires proper sanction and seconds (see Duel Sanction Diamond). If either party lacks a sanction, the duel is treated as assault under crown law.

Vhasian Claims

Any Viterran noble with land across the Belworth may invoke a Ducal Warrant to attempt to collect rents or enforce judgments in Vhasia. Doing so starts a Border Snarl complication (or ticks Border-Lace), as Vhasian claimants are unlikely to cooperate. The Queen’s own Vhasian title (Duchess of Aberiel) adds a layer of irony—and suspicion.

The Succession’s Shadow

Any legal dispute touching on inheritance, legitimacy, or royal authority is automatically one step more volatile. The GM may spend 1 SB to introduce a witness, document, or rumor related to Baldwyn Brightstide’s death or the northern earls’ secret funding of King Wyllmot’s murder.

  3. Win two stones to secure a temporary Court Ruling (treat as a Diamond for one scene).

  4. Halric’s Interjection (2 SB): flip Precedent, but start Whispered Precedent [2] with the losing side.

  • Advantage: Vhasian Claim → Viterran Road (shortcuts), Ubral Pass → Vhasian Claim (concealment), Viterran Road → Ubral Pass (supplies).

The Queen’s Dial (Legitimacy ↔︎ Pragmatism)

Track the party’s footing between upholding the Queen’s authority and making pragmatic compromises.

  • Legitimacy High: +1 Position when invoking royal writs, crown law, or the Queen’s name. First compromise or pragmatic choice each session starts one step worse.

  • Pragmatism High: +1 Effect when brokering deals, accepting bribes, or negotiating with rivals. First appeal to royal authority each session starts one step worse.

  • Center the dial by resolving a dispute in a way that satisfies both the Queen’s law and local custom, or by winning a legal duel in the Queen’s name.

Lore Echoes

  • The Hedge-Seal (The Dike Covenant): Covenant judges in the eastern parishes adjudicate river tolls and inheritance. They carry iron scales and a copy of the Hedge-Law Tables—each page stained by a different flood. A ruling is binding in both Viterra and the Vhasian marches, but any party who lies before them forfeits their right to appeal. The Warrior Queen has granted them a charter; the Fenwood dukes consider them spies.

  • Saint Cuthbert of the Fen (The Traveler): A legendary reeve who walked the flooded dikes during the Great Storm, lighting every beacon by hand. His shrines are stiles and bridge posts; travelers who touch the iron ring and speak a destination find the road holds firm. The fen folk leave offerings of eels at his cairns.

  • The Cog-Cults of the Northern Hills (The Clockwork Monad): Secret fraternities of millers and surveyors near the Ubral border believe law is a machine that can be perfected. They meet in abandoned watermills, measuring boundaries with brass chains and reciting the Canons of Proportion. The Everflame calls them heretics; the Queen’s justiciars ignore them.

  • The Salt Sworn of the Eastern Coast (The Pale Shepherd): Along the Dolmis shore, a syncretic cult tends to the drowned. They build cairns of salt stone and leave no offerings—only the names of the lost, carved into driftwood. Thepyrgos is both realm and city, built on the bones of the drowned Astroegro Empire. The Astroegro were a human civilization of formidable magic, the ancient rivals of the Valewood’s Empire of Leaves. Their ruins litter the hills and swamps south of the city, and their legacy haunts every threshold. When the Empire of Leaves fell, Lethai-thora refugees—high elves who had served as archivists and lore-keepers—fled across the Black River and were granted sanctuary in Thepyrgos. Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Bell-Law: Bells are law made sound. Once per session, a Bellmark or tower bell summons the watch, a levy, or a decree. False rings stain a reputation; the Synod may inquire. The ninth ring is never false—it is always the Ninth.

  • Stair-Rights: Every stair is claimed, chartered, or under review. A token bypasses barricades and checkpoints; climbing the wrong stair without leave sparks a riot or a duel. The Unfinished Stair has no owner—only one who bears its mark.

  • The Unfinished Stair Timer [8]: Tracks the party’s entanglement with the hidden architecture of Thepyrgos.

    • 0-2: The stairs are hidden. The party has not yet attracted the Ninth’s attention.

    • 3-4: The stairs begin to appear. At night, the party sees an extra step where none should be. The Ninth’s Coterie begins to take notice.

    • 5-6: The stairs appear in daylight. A character who walks an Unfinished Stair may ask one question of the Ninth—but the Ninth will ask one in return. The party is marked. The Chain-Lanterns receive visions of their faces.

    • 7-8: A vault entrance opens. The Ninth’s Coterie is already there. The party must choose: enter the vault, seal it, or destroy it. The Unfinished Stair is now visible to all.

    Tick the timer when you use a hidden stair, ask a question the Synod cannot answer, or speak a true name of an Astroegro ruin. Reduce the timer by feeding a memory to the Ninth’s Coterie (lose a fact, a skill point, or a Bond) or by recording a truth in the Synod’s archives (gain the Witness’s Annotation).

  • The Offering at the Crossroads (Hedge Gift): Once per session, a Hyro character (or someone who has learned their customs) may leave a substitute at a crossroads—a written name, a lock of hair, a childhood memory. For the next scene, they are invisible to the Chain-Lanterns (cannot be tracked or identified by candle-smoke). The substitute becomes an Unfinished Proof that the GM may call in later (the Hollow accepted it; the Hollow will ask for more).

  • The Candle-Smoke Test (GM Tool): When the Chain-Lanterns test a village, the GM rolls 1d6. On a 1-2, they find a gifted child (the party must intervene or witness a tragedy). On a 3-4, they find a substitute (a false name, a hidden memory) and leave. On a 5-6, the village has already paid in silence—no raid occurs. The party can influence the roll by offering their own substitute (use a Boon to shift the result by 1).

  • The Astroegro Singing (Player Tool): A Hyro character can hum a fragment of an old empire song. The song has no words—only pressure. When hummed at a ruin, it may open a hidden door. When hummed at a Chain-Lantern, it may cause their lantern to flicker. Each use advances the Unfinished Stair timer by 1 (the past is waking).

    • Consequences (d6): Roll each time the song is used.

    • 1: The song attracts a Chain-Lantern patrol. They arrive in 1d4 scenes.

    • 2: A stone cracks nearby. A hidden door opens—or closes.

    • 3: A ghost of an Astroegro archivist appears. It will not speak, but it will point.

    • 4: The singer forgets the song. They cannot use it again until they learn a new fragment.

    • 5: The Hollow responds. The singer gains the Condition Heard by the Hollow (the GM may spend 1 SB to have a Oath-Keeper appear).

    • 6: The song is heard by the Ninth’s Coterie. They will offer a bargain within the day.

  • Lethai-thora Archivists: The high elf refugees have integrated into the city’s scholarly elite. They are the keepers of the deepest archives and the most secret keys. A character with Lethai-thora heritage (or who earns their trust) may gain access to the Archivists’ Mark—a brass key that opens one restricted door in the Library of Keys per session. However, the Archivists are not monolithic; some serve the Witness, others the Ninth. Choosing a side may alienate the other faction.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Unwritten Citation — The Witness. A precedent that exists only in the margin.

    • The Bell-Founder — Mykkiel. The law that rings true, even when it hurts.

    • The Abyssal Pressure — Khemesh. The pressure beneath the city; the pressure that the deep remembers.

    • The Silent Stair — The Ninth. The step that is never counted, the threshold that opens only once.

The Astroegro Vaults — Mini-Generators

Beneath the city, nine vaults. The Synod knows of three and has sealed them with Witness-branded wax. The Ninth’s Coterie has found a fourth. The other five are still buried, waiting for the right key—a true name, a specific death, a memory that has never been spoken.

Vault Contents (d6)

When the party opens a vault (or finds a way in), roll to determine what lies within.

  1. The Echo Forge — A chamber of black glass gears that still turn. The forge produces a single perfect bell every nine years. The current bell is half-formed and rings at a frequency that makes teeth ache. Weapon: A bell that, when rung, forces all who hear it to speak one true name (Resolve DV 4 to resist). Guardian: A ghost-smith who does not attack—it asks you to complete the bell. Cost: A year of your life to finish the forging.

  2. The Unwritten Archive — Shelves of blank stone tablets. The tablets are not blank—they are written in a script that only the dead can read. A ghost-archivist sits at a desk, writing the same number over and over. Weapon: A quill that writes in disappearing ink; the words become true for one hour, then vanish. Guardian: The archivist’s ghost will answer one question about the vault’s history, but will then ask for a number you have never written. Cost: You must forget how to read for one day.

  3. The Glass Orrery — A working model of the Astroegro cosmos, its gears carved from abyssal glass. It still ticks. The ticking changes when you lie. Weapon: A lens that shows the death of anyone you name—but only once per person. Guardian: A pressure-spirit that coils around the orrery; it will let you take the lens if you can answer its riddle. Cost: A dream of drowning (you will dream of the sea every night until you return).

  4. The Bone Gallery — A corridor lined with the remains of Astroegro archivists. Each skeleton wears a different style of bell-maker’s apron. The newest apron is still damp. Weapon: A finger bone that, when pointed at a door, opens it—once. Guardian: The archivist whose bone you take will ask for a story about a door you should not have opened. Cost: The archivist’s ghost follows you until you close a door you left open.

  5. The Counting Chamber — A room where the walls are covered in tally marks—eight scratches, a gap, eight scratches. The marks are not in sequence. A wright’s skeleton still holds a hammer. Weapon: A hammer that can drive a copper nail into any surface, sealing a threshold for one scene. Guardian: The wright will not attack—it will only count. If you interrupt the count, it becomes hostile. Cost: You must leave a tally of your own—a memory, a name, a debt—on the wall.

  6. The Sealed Heart — A chamber of black glass and fossilized bone, where the heat of the earth meets the cold of the void. Something coils in the dark—a child of Nidhoggr, a fragment of the Deep Drake’s attention. Weapon: A scale that, when touched, grants +2 dice to resist fear for one scene—but the scale grows warm each time. Guardian: The pressure that thinks. It does not attack; it invites you to stay. Cost: A memory of sunlight. You will forget what warmth feels like for one day.

Vault Guardian Quirks (d6)

In addition to their primary nature, each vault’s guardian has a quirk that defines how they can be negotiated with.

  1. Counting Obsession: The guardian will not attack unless you interrupt its count. It counts in eights. If you join the count correctly, it will give you a boon. If you miscount, it takes a memory.

  2. The Witness’s Seal: The guardian is bound by a Witness-branded wax seal. If you present a truth the Seal cannot refute, the guardian must let you pass. The truth must be witnessed by another living soul.

  3. Ninth’s Silence: The guardian only speaks in gaps—silences between words, spaces between steps. If you can answer a question in the silence, it will let you pass. If you speak over the silence, it attacks.

  4. Pressure Echo: The guardian is a pressure-spirit; it only attacks when you are afraid. If you can pass a Spirit + Endurance test (DV 4) and show no fear, it will let you pass. On a failure, it manifests and attacks (Cap 3).

  5. Archivist’s Grief: The guardian is the ghost of an archivist who failed to save a vault. It will let you pass if you can prove you have failed something important—a promise, a mission, a person. It will judge your failure. If it deems it worthy, you pass.

  6. The Hollow’s Claim: The guardian is Hollow-touched. It does not attack; it offers a bargain: one memory from each intruder, and the vault opens. Refusal means it takes a name instead.

Vault Costs (d6)

Every vault demands a cost to open or to take its treasures.

  1. Memory of Salt Water: You must forget a memory of the sea—a voyage, a ship, a drowning. You will remember that you forgot, but not what it was.

  2. Dream of Drowning: You will dream of drowning every night until you return to the vault. Each morning, mark 1 Fatigue.

  3. Confession of Hubris: You must speak aloud a time you were wrong and knew it, but refused to admit it. The confession must be witnessed.

  4. A True Name: You must give the vault a true name—yours, or someone else’s. The name will be added to the vault’s records.

  5. A Year of Life: You will age one year instantly. Gain 1 permanent Condition (e.g., gray hair, a limp, a tremor).

  6. A Bond Broken: You must sever a Bond—a relationship, a promise, a debt. The loss is permanent, though the wound may heal.

The Province — Mini-Generators

Beyond the city walls, the hinterlands of Thepyrgos are a haunted countryside of limestone hills, olive groves, and the Hyro people who still remember the Astroegro Empire. The Chain-Lanterns patrol the villages, and the Ninth’s Coterie moves through the ruins.

Village Situation (d6)

When the party enters a Hyro village, roll to determine its current state.

  1. Quieted Village: The Chain-Lanterns came last week. The gifted children are gone. The village is silent, and the elders will not speak to outsiders. A single child hides in the root cellar—too old to be taken, too young to understand why.

  2. Candle-Smoke Test Tomorrow: The hunters are coming at dawn. The village is in a panic. One family has a child who flinched at the last test. They need a substitute—a lock of hair, a false name, a memory to offer at the crossroads.

  3. The Ninth’s Coterie Visits: A gray-robed figure has been seen in the village. They offer protection from the hunters in exchange for stories—true names, forgotten memories, fragments of the Astroegro past. The village is divided: some trust them, others fear them.

  4. The Hollow’s Offering: A child was born last night with eyes the color of the sea. The elders say the Hollow has accepted a substitute—a memory of the Astroegro fall. The child will never have talent. The village is grateful, but the child’s mother is weeping.

  5. Ruin Singing: The stones outside the village have started humming at night. A Hyro elder believes an Astroegro vault is surfacing—the pressure below is rising. She needs help to listen, to count the stones, to find the door before the hunters hear the song.

  6. The Bear Cult: A group of villagers has taken to the hills, wearing cured skins and speaking in growls. They claim the only way to escape the hunters is to become the hunter. They are hunting Chain-Lantern patrols—and they are winning. The Archon has declared them outlaws, but the villagers leave offerings at their shrines.

Hyro Encounter (d6)

When meeting a Hyro villager, traveler, or hermit, roll for their disposition and need.

  1. Grieving Elder: Their child was taken in the last sweep. They will help the party if the party helps them find the child. They know of a hidden stair that leads to the Beacon Crown’s secret basement.

  2. Stone-Listener: A hermit who hears the pressure below. They can identify a thin place, a vault entrance, or a route that the hunters do not watch. They demand a story about a dream in return.

  3. Substitute Keeper: This villager sells false names and stolen memories to families who need to protect their children. They can provide a substitute for a candle-smoke test—but the Hollow will remember the debt.

  4. Chain-Lantern Informant: A villager who reports gifted children to the hunters in exchange for protection. They are despised by the village but have useful information about the hunters’ schedules. They are also deeply ashamed.

  5. Ruin Seeker: A young Hyro who believes the Astroegro vaults contain the key to defeating the hunters. They are reckless, know a few fragments of the old songs, and will follow the party into any ruin.

  6. Hollow-Touched: A villager who has given too many substitutes to the Hollow. They have begun to forget their own name. They speak in riddles and can see the Hollow’s attention on others. They are useful, but dangerous to be around.

Hunter Encounter (d6)

When the Chain-Lanterns or other Commissariat forces appear, roll for their objective and mood.

  1. Routine Sweep: The hunters are testing children with candle-smoke. They are bored, efficient, and will accept a bribe (1 Boon or 1 Supply) to skip a house. On a Miss, they notice the party’s arcane talent.

  2. Targeted Raid: The hunters have a warrant for a specific child. They will not be bribed. They will search every house. The party must hide the child, substitute a false name, or fight.

  3. Inquisitorial Audit: A Chain-Lantern Captain is auditing the local Commissary. They are looking for corruption, not talent. They may accept information about a corrupt officer in exchange for ignoring a minor talent.

  4. Ninth Pursuit: The hunters are not hunting talent—they are hunting the Ninth’s Coterie. A gray-robed figure has been seen in the village, and the Chain-Lanterns want to question them. They will detain anyone who looks suspicious.

  5. Desperate Hunt: The hunters have lost a prisoner—a gifted child who escaped. They are searching the village house by house. The child is hiding in a root cellar, and the hunters are getting closer.

  6. Pardon Offer: The Exarch has offered a pardon to any family that surrenders their gifted child voluntarily. The village is divided—some families are considering it. The party may need to intervene.

Hyro Discovery (d6)

When exploring a ruin, a village, or the hills, roll for something the Hyro have hidden or preserved.

  1. Fragment of the Old Song: A piece of the Astroegro empire’s music. Humming it can open one hidden door or cause a Chain-Lantern’s lantern to flicker. One use only.

  2. Substitute Cache: A hidden stash of false names, locks of hair, and written memories. Enough substitutes for three candle-smoke tests. Using them will not anger the Hollow—this time.

  3. Kurgan Offering: A stone marked with a Hyro offering. If you leave a memory here, the Hollow will accept it as a substitute for one child. The memory must be true and painful.

  4. Warden’s Log: A record of Chain-Lantern patrols, including schedules, informants, and the names of children taken. Valuable to the resistance—and dangerous to carry.

  5. Sealed Door: A hidden door in a hillside, marked with an Astroegro seal. Opening it requires a true name—or a memory of the empire. The Hyro have kept it sealed for generations.

  6. The Singing Stones: A circle of stones that hum at dusk. They are an Anchor site—a thin place where the pressure below is close. The Hyro leave offerings here. A character who offers a memory may ask one question of the Hollow.

  3. On a hit: cancel a Candle-Smoke Test or Proctors’ Gate tag for this scene. Consequence: start Bell Mark [2] or Rector’s Notice [2].

Scenario Starters (Plug-and-Play)

The Bright Child on the Exam Stair

Hook: A Bell-Runner begs you to get a latent child to campus before the dawn sweep. Complications: Candle-Smoke Test, Commissary Sweep, a jealous Street Thaum. Relief: Trade a Latency Seal for testimony; or ring a lawful Bellmark to create a corridor—accept Censure Murmurs [2].

The Black Index of Accusations

Hook: The College of Quiet wants proof that a Commissary inflates rolls to fill quotas. Complications: Synod Summons, Smuggler’s Ladder, Chain Jam. Relief: Publish via a Nomikon colloquy (gain Synod Indulgence); or submit it to the Archon (gain Archon’s Clemency but start Faculty Feud [4]).

The Golden Key and the Silent Stair

Hook: A dying Archivist gives you a Golden Key. “It opens the stair the hunters fear.” Truth: The stair leads to the Beacon Crown’s secret basement—a prison for children who passed the smoke test. Relief: Free the children and expose the Commissariat (gain Archon’s Clemency for the whole party, but start Exarch’s Claim at full); or seal the stair forever (gain Golden Key to use elsewhere, but the children are lost to memory).

The Ninth Vault

Hook: A fragment of an Astroegro weapon has been unearthed in a hill village. The Ninth’s Coterie and the Synod both want it. The village elder offers it to the party—if they can offer a substitute that satisfies the Hollow. Truth: The weapon is a bell that rings the true name of anyone you name. Its guardian is the ghost of the Astroegro archivist who sealed it. The ghost will not speak to anyone who has not climbed the Unfinished Stair. Relief: Climb the stair, speak the archivist’s true name, and take the bell—but the stair adds a segment to the Unfinished Stair Timer . Or destroy the bell with a memory of the Astroegro’s fall (lose a cherished memory permanently). Or give the bell to the Synod (gain a Synod Indulgence) or to the Ninth’s Coterie (gain the Ninth’s Annotation).

In Thepyrgos, power flows through elevation, education, and the precise application of law—but also through the hidden architecture of the Unfinished Stair. The Archon maintains authority through a complex web of guilds, synods, and civic institutions, but the real power lies with whoever can climb the stairs that should not exist. Social mobility is literally upward mobility, with stair-rights, bellmarks, and archive access serving as both currency and status symbols. But the highest status belongs to those who have seen the top of the Unfinished Stair—and remembered how to return.

Power flows through three overlapping hierarchies:

  1. Height: The higher you climb, the more authority you hold. The Archon lives at the peak. The poor live in the lower tiers. Every stair is a status symbol.

  2. Knowledge: The Library of Keys holds the city’s memory. Those who control the archives control the past—and the future.

  3. The Unfinished: The stairs that should not exist. Those who can climb them, who can ask the questions the Synod cannot answer, hold power beyond law.

Rewards are:

  • Keys (literal and metaphorical)

  • Stair-rights (permission to climb)

  • Bellmarks (authority made audible)

  • Archive access (knowledge as leverage)

  • A single climb of the Unfinished Stair (a question answered, a truth revealed, a vault opened)

Costs are:

  • Memory (forgotten facts, lost names)

  • Obligation to a faction (the Witness, the Ninth, the Synod, or the Commissariat)

  • Permanent marks (scars, brands, missing steps)

The Lethai-thora, as the city’s most respected archivists, hold a special kind of power: they know which shelves are watched, which keys are false, and which doors lead to the Ninth’s vaults. Some whisper that the Archon is secretly a Lethai-thora puppet; others say the real ruler is the ghost of an Astroegro emperor, still counting stairs in the deep.

For the GM: Patronage in Thepyrgos revolves around access—to towers, to knowledge, to the right to climb, and to the hidden stairs. Rewards often take the form of keys, passes, or legal exemptions that can be leveraged into greater influence. To emphasize the metaphysical war:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (tokens, keys, papers) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided.

  • Let rival guilds and the two Patrons (Witness and Ninth) issue conflicting rights, forcing players to choose whose favor matters more, and whose curse is worse.

  • Use the synods, towers, and staircases as arenas for social contests, where knowledge of law and custom determines success—but where the Ninth’s silence and the Witness’s memory can tip the scales.

  • Introduce the Unfinished Stair timer as a campaign tension; every time the party uses a hidden stair, they advance the timer and bring the Ninth’s attention closer.

In Thepyrgos, your ascent is your standing, and your standing determines whether you rise or fall—or whether you are simply forgotten.
