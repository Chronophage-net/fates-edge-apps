The Oasis Courts.

Water is not a right. It is a loan from the sand. The oasis remembers every drop drawn, every cup poured, every promise made at its edge. I do not judge the living. I judge the debt.

I am a Water-Clerk. My grandfather dug this well with a shovel and a prayer. My mother sealed the walls with clay from the dry river. I sit in the shade and measure every bucket. The sand does not forgive a miscount.

Water isn’t just law in Fhara—it’s a market, and oh, how the water-clerks love a good deal on aquifer access. They don’t just negotiate in three cups; they auction the right to the fourth cup. The Fhara are merchants, poets, and negotiators; they will bargain for an hour over a cup of coffee, and a day over a camel. But here’s the secret the well-judges don’t want you to know: the wells have ears. Every cup poured, every promise made at the edge, every drop drawn—the well-spirits are listening. And they have opinions. They favor the generous. They punish the greedy. And they never, ever forget a face.

The Fhara have no single king. Their confederacy is a web of marriage alliances, water-sharing treaties, and caravan tariffs. The desert is not empty—it is a ledger of dry wells, abandoned caravanserais, and the bones of those who could not share. A Fhara merchant can cross the waste with only a waterskin and a coffee pot, because every well has a keeper, every keeper a price, and every price can be negotiated. But the negotiation is a ritual. Three cups: the first is welcome, the second is bargaining, the third is a contract. Refuse the third, and you have broken an oath. The well-spirit will remember.

Never refuse a date offered by a well-keeper. The fruit is sacred to their well-spirits. Refusing it ends the conversation—and your access to water.

— Well-Judge Fatima al-Wadi, who carries a dipper made from a date-palm leaf that rots after each case

What do people eat for breakfast? Dates and thin coffee—the dates are from the well-keepers’ groves, the coffee is brewed with cardamom and camel milk. The milk is sour. The sourness is for endurance.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count cups. Fhara children are taught the eight cups of negotiation: Welcome, Bargain, Contract, Witness, Debt, Payment, Forgiveness, and the ninth—the cup that is never poured. The ninth cup is for the well-spirit. The well-spirit is always thirsty.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their water-skin. A stranger whose water-skin is full has not traveled far. A stranger whose water-skin is dry has traveled too far. A stranger who carries no water-skin is either a well-keeper or a fool. Well-keepers are honoured. Fools are buried.

What is the most common crime? Water theft—but water theft is not a crime, it is a declaration of feud. The well-spirit records every theft in the aquifer. The aquifer is the court. The judge is the well. The verdict is thirst.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The wells are not natural. They were carved by the first Fhara, who made a bargain with the sand. The bargain was for water. The price was memory. The Fhara remember everything. They cannot forget. That is the curse of the wells.

Where do people go when they die? To the oasis. The dead are buried in the date groves, their graves marked with a single stone. The stone is not carved. The dead’s name is spoken at sunrise. If the name is remembered, the dead rests. If it is forgotten, the dead walks.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a cup—the ninth cup, poured from your own water-skin. The cup is placed at the well’s edge. If the well-spirit drinks, you are forgiven. If the water evaporates, you are not. If the cup cracks, your debt is doubled.

What is the most important festival? The Filling of the Wells (spring). Every well is cleaned and blessed. The water-clerks recite the names of the year’s debtors. The debtors stand at the water’s edge. If their reflection wavers, they are forgiven. If it holds, they must pay.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The water-clerks’ ledger is a forgery. The original was lost in a sandstorm three generations ago. The clerks have been inventing water rights ever since. The wells do not care. The wells remember the original. The wells are waiting.

Quick Reference

Genre / Mood: Desert law, coffee ritual, water-as-community.

Starting Location: The shaded stone bench of a well-court, where the air is cool and the water is sweet. A Well-Judge dips a clay cup into the basin: “You have come for water, or for trade, or for a story. First, drink. Then tell me which promise you intend to keep today.”

The Fhara dwell on a great, sun-baked peninsula where scattered oases and dry riverbeds connect the coast to the interior. Their cities are built around wells and date groves, and their caravans are the arteries of the spice trade. Water is law – the well-keepers are judges, and the courthouse is the shade of a palm. The Fhara are merchants, poets, and negotiators; they will bargain for an hour over a cup of coffee, and a day over a camel. Their honor is measured in generosity, and their memory is measured in water-shares. Coffee is not a drink; it is a ritual. Three cups: the first is welcome, the second is negotiation, the third is a contract. Refuse the third, and you have broken faith.

What you notice first: The smell of roasting coffee and overripe dates. The way every well has a stone bench for the judge. The silence when the sun passes directly overhead – no one moves, no one speaks.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never refuse a date offered by a well-keeper. The fruit is sacred to their well-spirits. Refusing it ends the conversation – and your access to water.

Regional Mood: The Weight of Water

Power here is measured in well-depth, date groves, and the length of a coffee ritual. Each oasis is a sovereign court, ruled by a Sheikh and a Well-Judge. The Fhara have no single king; their confederacy is a web of marriage alliances, water-sharing treaties, and caravan tariffs. The desert is not empty – it is a record of dry wells, abandoned caravanserais, and the bones of those who could not share. A Fhara merchant can cross the waste with only a waterskin and a coffee pot, because every well has a keeper, every keeper a price, and every price can be negotiated – but the negotiation is a ritual of reciprocity, not of obligation.

The Antagonists: The Nerethi

From the deep desert comes the Nerethi. They are acentric, psionic, and distrusted by all. They do not believe in debt; to them, the concept is alien. They do not owe, they do not remember, and they do not forgive. They walk the dunes like shadows, their minds linked in a hive that rejects the Fhara’s water-ledger.

  • Philosophy: Memory is a chain. Debt is a shackle. The Nerethi burn both.

  • Mechanic: Nerethi agents can erase a Bond or Obligation from a character sheet, but the character loses the memory associated with it permanently.

  • Conflict: The Fhara see them as thieves of history. The Nerethi see the Fhara as slaves to the past.

The Lost Capital: Qalat al-Madina (The Sunken City)

Before the Kuvani and the Eastern Ykrul swept from the steppe, the Fhara had a single great city – Qalat al-Madina, the Mother of Wells. It stood where the peninsula’s largest aquifer surfaced, a ring of gardens and fountains, its walls white as salt. Merchants from Kahfagia and Thepyrgos paid the city’s Water-Tithe to cross the waste. Poets sang of its nine hundred wells, each with a different taste.

But Qalat al-Madina’s true secret was older than the city itself. Beneath its deepest well, a faction of scholars unearthed a vault of forbidden knowledge – fragments of a civilisation that had turned to ash twenty thousand years before the first Utaran legion marched. The knowledge was not magic; it was a reckoning. It spoke of a fire that could scour the world clean.

The three Omens – Inaea, Ikasha, and Isoka – appeared to the Eastern Ykrul and the Kuvani as whispers in the grass and cracks in the sky. They gave a single command: Bury it. Seal it. If it wakes, the world burns. The horse-lords rode against Qalat al-Madina, not for plunder, but to honor the Omens’ warning. They poisoned the wells, drowned the city in its own aquifer, and salted the earth. The curse they carved into the central well was not cruelty – it was a lock: “Let no Fhara ever drink here without paying a death.” The death was the knowledge, buried alive.

Now Qalat al-Madina is a sinkhole field, half-submerged in brackish water, its towers leaning into the mud. The ghosts of the drowned well-keepers rise at dusk, still trying to measure water that no longer exists – and to guard the vault that no one has found. The Kuvani and Ykrul have long since left, but the Omen-Whisper remains: in certain wells, at certain tides, a traveler may hear a voice speaking a single word: “Remember.”

What remains:

  • The Sunken Archive – a flooded chamber where clay tablets still float, their judgments still legally binding on the living.

  • The Nine Hundred Wells – now sinkholes, some connected to the underworld.

  • The Cursed Spring – a pool that rises every new moon; those who drink from it forget their names for a year and a day.

  • The Drowned Coin – a silver piece minted in the city’s last year; it is said to buy safe passage from any Fhara well-spirit, because it still bears the Mother of Well’s seal – and the memory of the Omens’ warning.

Adventure Hooks:

  • A Fhara sheikh wants to recover a water-tablet from the Sunken Archive. The tablet proves his clan never ceded the oasis – but the archive is haunted by the drowned judges who whisper of the forbidden vault.

  • The Kuvani are sending an expedition to the ruins to renew the curse. The Fhara need someone to sabotage them – or to learn why the Omens’ seal is cracking.

  • A ghost from Qalat al-Madina appears at a well-court, demanding justice for a murder committed three centuries ago. The accused is a modern Fhara noble, but the ghost’s testimony is legally binding under ancient water law. The ghost also whispers a fragment of the forbidden knowledge: a single number.

  • Nerethi Incursion: A Nerethi psion offers to erase the curse on Qalat al-Madina. The price: the party must forget why they came to the desert in the first place.

“I wrote what I should not have. Then I ran.”

The night the Kuvani and Eastern Ykrul rode against Qalat al-Madina, a young water-clerk named Samir al-Qalat sat in the deep archive, copying ancient tablets by lamplight. He was not a hero. He was the grandson of a well-keeper, and he had a bad habit of reading the tablets he was supposed to copy.

That evening, he unrolled a scroll sealed with a symbol he did not recognize – three interlocking rings, each a different shade of green. The ink was still wet. The scroll had been written that morning, by a hand that no longer existed. It was the Overseer’s fragment, smuggled out of a vault that should have stayed sealed.

Samir read eight lines. Then he stopped. He knew that if he read the ninth, he would never stop.

When the first torches appeared at the city’s edge, he wrapped the scroll in oilcloth, stuffed it into a clay water-jar, and ran. Behind him, the wells were poisoned. Ahead, the desert. He walked for nine days, drinking only the moisture he could lick from the clay.

On the ninth day, he stumbled into a Tulkani caravan circle. The Caravan Judge, an old woman with a cord that dragged on the ground, looked at the jar and said, “You have brought the ninth cup. We do not pour it. But we can carry it.”

Samir gave the scroll to the Tulkani. He did not ask for payment. He asked only that they keep it moving – that no single camp hold it for more than nine nights, no single wagon keep it sealed for more than a season. “The record is warm,” he said. “The ink is still wet. The Hollow reads over my shoulder.”

The Tulkani honoured his request. The fragment passed from wagon to wagon, from story to story, until it reached the Archivolt of Thepyrgos, where it was sealed in a lead-lined box, encased in karadasarythm, with a lock that requires eight witnesses to open. The ninth witness, as the Words of the Ninth say, is always the Hollow – and the Hollow is always available.

Samir al-Qalat died in the desert three years later, alone except for a single date-palm that grew from a seed he had carried from his mother’s grove. The tree still stands. Travelers who rest in its shade sometimes hear a whisper: “Count to eight. Then stop.”

GM Hook: The Tulkani still carry the memory of Samir’s scroll. A Caravan Judge may offer the party a single glimpse of a copy – but only if they promise to carry a message to the Sunken City: “The ink is still wet. Has the Overseer finished writing?”

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = leverage. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Diamonds are codified outcomes (tablets, writs, dippers) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo water-omens – a dry well that weeps, coffee that brews itself, a date that bleeds.

  • Three Cups, One Contract: Fhara negotiations follow a rigid ritual. First cup: welcome and genealogy. Second cup: bargaining and threats disguised as compliments. Third cup: sealing the deal. Refusing the third cup voids the negotiation and insults the host. Accepting the third cup without intending to honor the deal is a capital crime – the liar is tied to a date palm until the sun sets.

  • Water-Sharing Tablets: Water rights are recorded on clay tablets, stored in the Well-Judge’s archive. A tablet can be split, traded, or shared. The tablet is the well’s memory; break it, and the water becomes unclaimed – but the breaker becomes the new steward.

  • Well-Spirits (Kulub): Each well has a spirit, usually benign but watchful. Offerings of dates, coffee, or a true story keep it calm. A well that has witnessed a murder may turn bitter; a well that has been poisoned may turn sentient. A sentient well is a disaster – it follows those who wronged it.

  • The Drowned Coin and the Omen-Whisper: Silver pieces from Qalat al-Madina still circulate. A Drowned Coin can be used to invoke the Water-Right of the Lost Capital – one free, unquestioned draw from any well in Fhara territory. The well-spirit will remember, and it will ask for a story in return. But if you listen carefully while holding the coin, you may hear the Omens’ whisper: “Remember.”

The Narethi — The Unburied of the Deep Desert

“The sun remembers what the sand forgets. We remember both. That is our curse. That is our strength.”

Deep in the Deep Desert, where the Fharan oases give way to cracked salt flats and the bones of failed cities, dwell the Narethi. They are felid — cat-people, in the crude speech of lowland merchants — with tufted ears, slit-pupiled eyes, and a proud, slow way of moving. Their fur ranges from pale cream to deep rust. Their voices carry a harmonic resonance, two pitches at once, as if they are always speaking in duet with a shadow of themselves.

What you notice first: the way they never blink in unison. The scent of myrrh and old copper. The silence when they stop speaking — it is not empty; something is listening.

Where They Live

The Narethi have no cities. They dwell in the Sunken Courts — a network of subterranean chambers carved from ancient salt and basalt, hidden beneath the eastern Deep Desert. The entrance is a sinkhole that smells of ozone. Visitors are blindfolded and led through nine switchbacks before the tunnels open.

The Courts are lit by phosphorescent crystals that pulse faintly, as if breathing. The air is still and dry, scented with myrrh, old blood, and the sweet smell of mummification spices.

The Unburied

At the heart of the Sunken Courts lies the Unburied. Once a Narethi Sorcerer-King — the last of their ruling line — it faced annihilation. Something was coming. Something older than memory. In desperation, the Unburied reached into the Unsunken Well, a font of psionic energy with no known bottom, and pulled. It drew the well’s power into itself — and the thing that slept beneath the well with it.

The Unburied did not die. It transformed. Its body became crystalline — a statue of salt-crusted glass, fused into a throne of petrified wood. Its mind shattered into fragments, each one dreaming a different dream. And the thing that slept beneath the well is now trapped inside it, dreaming with it, their dreams entangled forever.

The Unsunken Well went dry. The Great Silence — the psychic backlash of the Unburied’s choice — killed most of the Narethi and turned the fertile basin into the Deep Desert. The survivors woke with no memory of who they had been.

The Unburied does not speak. It resonates. Its dreams leak into the world through crystals that grow from its body — shards that Narethi cut and polish, using them to commune with the dream.

The Two Dreams

The Unburied does not dream one dream. It dreams two — entangled, arguing, incomplete.

The Unbroken Way is the dream of calm. The Unburied’s regret — its wish that it had found another way. A world where control, discipline, and restraint could have saved its people without destroying them. Followers learn stillness, precision, and the open hand that redirects force rather than meeting it.

The Riven Path is the dream of rage. The Unburied’s fury — its desire to break the thing that forced its hand. A world where power is seized, chains are shattered, and oppression is answered with annihilation. Followers learn passion, liberation, and the closed fist that breaks what it cannot bend.

Both are true. Neither is complete. The Narethi argue about which dream the Unburied truly intends.

Crystals and Communion

Narethi who wish to draw on the Unburied’s power carry a dream crystal — a shard cut from the crystals that grow in the Unburied’s chamber. The crystal hums faintly. When held, it allows the Narethi to hear the dream.

A character with a dream crystal may:

  • Commune: Spend a scene in meditation to ask the GM one yes/no question about a recent event within the Deep Desert. The dream shows a fragment.

  • Draw Power: Once per session, spend 1 Fatigue to gain +1 die on a roll involving psionics, perception, or insight. The crystal grows warm.

  • Follow a Discipline: Narethi who dedicate themselves to one of the two dreams may learn Rites and Talents from the Unbroken Way or Riven Path Patrons (see Chapter 15). Their crystal is their Thiasos; their Codex is a chant passed down orally.

The crystal is not a focus — it is a witness. The Unburied knows when you use it. The Hollow watches.

The Hollow’s Interest

The Unburied broke the most important promise of all: it swore to protect its people, and it killed most of them instead. The Hollow has been collecting interest on that debt for centuries. Every time someone draws on the Unburied’s power, they dip into that debt. The Hollow notices. Use the power too often, and the Hollow will eventually come to collect.

Society and Structure

The Narethi are ruled by the Father of Resonance — an elder who interprets the dream. Her word is law because the dream has foreseen the consequence of disobedience. Below her are Chanters (priests and lore-keepers), Watchers (scouts who patrol the Deep Desert), and Silent Ones (Narethi who have taken vows of silence to better hear the dream).

Factions:

  • Keepers — Followers of the Unbroken Way. They maintain the crystals and the dream’s stability.

  • Breakers — Followers of the Riven Path. They believe the Unburied wants to wake, and that the Narethi should help it break free.

  • The Unbound — Narethi who have left the Courts. They are viewed with suspicion, but they are the only source of news from outside.

Relationship with the Fhara

At the boundary stones marking the edge of the Deep Desert, well-judges leave offerings of salt and dried dates twice a year. The Narethi take them. No one meets. The Fhara call the Narethi the Unremembered. It is not an insult. It is a warning.

The Father’s Secret

The Unburied is not a prisoner. It is a lock. Beneath it, deeper than the Courts, deeper than the salt, something else sleeps. The Unburied’s dream is the only thing keeping it asleep. If the Unburied ever stops dreaming — or if the two dreams ever reconcile — the thing beneath will remember what it was.

The Father does not know what that thing is. Only that the dream’s single word — “Remember” — is not a command to the Narethi. It is a command to the thing below.

The Diaspora of the Sealed Coast.

Theme & Atmosphere

Galanina is a limestone city on a promontory, its white walls streaked with salt and grief. The Ashaani occupied it for a generation, taking children to be hollowed into spirit vessels. The Covenant broke the siege, but the children never fully returned – some came back empty, their eyes the color of old brass. The Hollow watches from the Unnumbered Shore.

What you notice first: The smell of olive oil and sea salt. The soft ring of brass bells on every doorway. The way every Sidhi touches their lamp before speaking a child’s name. The silence when the green flame flickers.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never extinguish a green lamp. The flame remembers the child it was lit for. To kill the flame is to kill the memory – and the Hollow will ask why.

Additional Features

  • Lamp Oaths: Swearing a promise over a green lamp binds the speaker. If broken, the lamp gutters and the Hollow visits the oath-breaker within nine days. The oath-breaker may appease the Hollow by lighting a new lamp for an unnumbered child (costs 1 Supply).

The Roof of the Way.

The fire does not forget. It has witnessed every oath sworn in this valley, every treaty sealed with a dropped coal, every lie told in its light. I do not tend the flame. I listen to what it remembers. And it remembers everything.

I am a Caravan Scribe. My grandfather copied a tax ledger that had not been opened in three centuries. He found a mistake—a single miscounted bale of silk. That mistake started a war. We do not write for the living. We write for the dead who cannot correct their errors.

The Pereshi highlands are a vast, windswept plateau, cut by deep river valleys and crowned with snow-capped peaks. Ancient fire temples stand beside caravanserais; terraced vineyards climb the hillsides. The people are scholars, shepherds, and traders—proud, suspicious, and bound by a code of hospitality that demands three days of feasting for any guest, and three generations of vengeance for any insult. But here’s the secret the Flame-Keepers don’t want you to know: the fire in the temple is not just a flame. It is a witness. It has seen every oath sworn in this valley, every treaty sealed with a dropped coal, every lie told in its light. And it remembers everything. Including the Prince’s true name.

Once, the Forgotten Ashaani Prince conquered this land. He built garrisons, taxed the passes, and tried to burn every library. When he and his kingdom vanished into the sand—swallowed by the same curse that turned the desert to glass—the Pereshi rose. They threw down his garrisons, brick by brick, and sealed his name in a scroll they have never reopened. They have not forgotten. Every fire temple keeps a coal from the Prince’s own brazier—the ninth coal, never lit, a promise that his darkness will not return.

Never step on a fallen book. Written words carry the weight of law. To step on a book is to trample a contract, a genealogy, a verdict. The spirits of the words will haunt you.

— Flame-Keeper Zarifa, who has never been seen without a rose—fresh or dried

What do people eat for breakfast? Flatbread with honey and clotted cream—the honey is from highland bees, the cream is from goats that graze the temple steps. The goats are sacred. The goats are also delicious. The bread is baked in ovens heated with coals from the temple fire; families take turns carrying the embers home. A coal that goes out before dawn is a bad omen.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read a contract. Pereshi children are taught the eight clauses of the Covenant: Offer, Acceptance, Witness, Consideration, Term, Breach, Remedy, and the ninth—the silence where the unspoken terms live. The ninth clause is never written. The ninth clause is the one that kills you. Children also learn to check the ninth coal in the family shrine each morning. If it has grown warm overnight, they must wake the Flame-Keeper.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their book. Every Pereshi carries a ledger—their personal account of debts owed and collected. A stranger whose ledger is thick has many debts. A stranger whose ledger is thin has many creditors. A stranger with no ledger is a liar. Liars are not fed. But these days, the first thing many notice is whether the stranger casts a shadow. The Prince’s shadow has been seen walking alone.

What is the most common crime? Contract forgery—but forgery is not a crime, it is an art. The Pereshi courts are full of forged documents. The judges are forgers themselves. The law is a negotiation. The negotiation never ends. The newest crime is shadow-theft: someone steals another’s shadow to feed to the Prince. The victim becomes pale, listless, and cannot lie—but also cannot keep a secret.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Forgotten Prince did not vanish. He is buried beneath the largest fire temple, in a lead-lined coffin sealed with nine locks. The keys are held by the nine Flame-Keepers. The Flame-Keepers have never met. The Flame-Keepers do not know each other’s names. The Prince’s name is written on the inside of the coffin lid. No one has read it in nine centuries—but lately the lid has begun to sweat. A rust-colored liquid seeps from the seams.

Where do people go when they die? To the fire. The dead are cremated on the temple pyres. Their ashes are mixed with clay and pressed into bricks. The bricks are used to build new temples. The dead become walls. The walls witness. The walls do not forget—and they whisper the names of those who still live. Some walls have started to whisper a name that is not on any ledger.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a coal—a burning ember from the temple fire. The coal is placed in the wronged party’s hearth. If it stays lit, you are forgiven. If it smoulders, you are watched. If it goes out, you are cursed. The curse is the fire’s silence. The fire will not speak for you again. Some say that if the coal goes out, the Prince’s shadow will visit you that night. It asks only one question: ”Why should I not take your name?“

What is the most important festival? The Reading of the Ledgers (autumn). Every household brings its ledger to the temple. The ledgers are read aloud. The debts are tallied. The dead are named. The fire listens. The fire does not interrupt—but the ninth coal flickers when a lie is spoken. This year, the coal has not stopped flickering.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The ninth coal is not coal. It is a piece of the Forgotten Prince’s heart, petrified by the curse that swallowed his kingdom. The Flame-Keepers do not know. They think it is a relic. The heart is still warm. The heart is still beating. The heart is waiting to be reunited with the rest of him. And someone has been feeding it blood—a drop each night, from a different Flame-Keeper. No one knows who. No one wants to know who.

Regional Mood: The Weight of Ash and the Warmth of the Coal

Power here is measured in fire-shrine charters, caravan tolls, and the length of a family’s library. The petty lords of the highlands compete not through armies but through scribes who can produce the oldest charter, the most complete genealogy, the most beautifully illuminated reckoning. The Covenant has a strong presence in the sacred cities, but the Pereshi follow their own law: the word of a Flame-Keeper outweighs a judge’s gavel.

Travelers are expected to recite a verse when crossing a bridge—the water spirits demand it—and to present a dried sprig of feverfew before petitioning a lord. The high passes are measured in breath-counts, not miles, and the air is thin enough to make a foreigner’s head spin. But the thinnest air of all is in the sealed archive where the Prince’s name is buried. No one has opened it in nine generations. The lock has no key. The key is a memory no one wants to have—and lately, the lock has been weeping rust. The rust tastes of iron and old smoke.

The Unfired Crown: How the Pereshi Rule Themselves

The Pereshi do not kneel to a king. They kneel to fire, to ash, and to a name they erased. Each valley or fire-shrine city governs itself through a council of elders, a Flame-Keeper, and a war captain (if needed). There is no central treasury, no standing army, no code of laws beyond custom and the witness of the ninth coal. What unites them is not a crown but a memory of conquest: every Pereshi child learns the story of the Ash Prince, the burning of the great library at Khvarena, and the year the fires went out. They also learn the year the fires were relit—and the ritual that severed the Prince’s name from the world.

The Flame-Keepers of the nine largest shrines meet once every nine years at Mount Khvarena, the holiest peak. They do not elect a leader. They sit in a circle, each holding a coal from their own hearth. They speak of weather, of harvests, of bandit raids. They do not speak of the Prince—but each of them, before sleep, places a hand on the ninth coal that sits in a brass brazier at the center of the circle. If it is warm, they know the Shadow is stretching. If it is hot, they light a signal fire and send riders to every valley: The name stirs. Renew the seals.

No outsider has ever attended a meeting of the nine. The lowland merchants have tried to send spies; the spies never returned, and their names were erased from every reckoning. The Tulkani do not try; they leave a knotted cord at the edge of the shrine and wait for a reply. The reply is always a single burnt knot.

The Fear of the Lowlands and the Tulkani Unease

The lowland merchants fear the Pereshi. In the oasis cities, water-clerks whisper that every Pereshi carries a “name-coal”—a fragment that can lift a name from memory, unraveling every bond it once held. A merchant whose name is struck from the tallies is not bankrupt; he is forgotten. His wells dry up. His caravans lose their way. His children cannot remember his face. The lowland guilds settle their reckonings with the Pereshi with unusual haste, and they never, ever allow a Pereshi fire-keeper to light a lamp in their counting houses. Some say that the well of the lost oasis was cursed not by the desert nomads but by a Pereshi exile who wanted to make a trading post vanish.

The Tulkani have an uneasy relationship with the Pereshi. They share a border of silence. Both respect the ninth night, but for different reasons. The Tulkani say the Pereshi stole fire from the Dreamer (the sleeping goddess). The Pereshi say the Tulkani forgot how to build a hearth. They trade stories at the edge of the high passes, but never stay the night. A Tulkani who enters a Pereshi fire-shrine must leave a knot behind—and the Pereshi burn it. The smoke carries the dream to the sky, but the knot is gone, and the Tulkani cannot retie it. Some Tulkani elders whisper that the Pereshi know how to sever a name because they once tried to sever the Dreamer’s name—and failed. They do not say this aloud. The Hollow might be listening.

Everyone else underestimates the Pereshi. Lowland clerks call them “backward highlanders with no coin.” Armored knights sneer at their unpaved roads and unarmored sentinels. Grain factors pity them. The Pereshi prefer it this way. Let the lowlands send their reckoners; the passes are high, the cold is deep, and every seal of passage requires a verse that no foreigner can recite correctly. Those who try are turned back at the bridge—or find that their names have been quietly written in ash and left to the wind.

The Ash Prince—Heart as Coal, Name as Ash

Once, the Ash Prince—a conqueror from the southern magocracy, a man whose armies swept from the red desert to the eastern hills—turned his gaze to the Pereshi highlands. He burned the great library at Khvarena. He melted the shrine bells into arrowheads. He tried to erase the fire-faith, to replace it with the silence of the Veiled Ones. For nine years he ruled from a garrison in the lowlands, sending patrols to cut down any who still lit a lamp before dawn.

Then his empire cracked. The cursed gem that had given him power over fate began to devour him from within. His generals turned on each other. His shadow began to walk independently. He retreated to a fortress in the eastern passes, and the Pereshi followed—not with armies, but with priests.

The ritual took nine nights. They did not kill him. They unmade him. They spoke his true name into a lead scroll, sealed it with nine coals, and buried it beneath the largest fire-shrine. They cut his shadow from his body and pinned it to a stone throne (now lost in the red desert). And they took his heart—still beating, still warm—and placed it in a brass brazier. The ninth coal is not a relic. It is his petrified heart. Once a year, on the anniversary of his fall, it pulses. The Flame-Keepers feel it. They do not speak of it.

The Prince’s shadow still walks because the heart still dreams of conquest. The Pereshi do not fear him—they remember him. He is the Weight the Fire Lifted. But the weight grows heavier with time. Every broken name, every forgotten oath, every coal that cracks adds another shadow to the flame. If the ninth coal ever goes cold, the Prince will not return—he will never have been gone.

The party must either reseal him (by finding a new name, a new shadow, or a new coal) or bargain with him (he offers great power at the cost of a Pereshi valley). The valleys are not his to give. He offers them anyway. The ninth coal will be warm that night.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Fire-Shrines and the Ninth Coal: Every fire-shrine keeps a brazier with nine coals. The ninth coal is never lit—it is a promise to the Hollow and a ward against the Prince. Lighting it summons the Oath-Keeper or the Prince’s ghost. Flame-Keepers inherit the secret of which coal is the ninth; it changes with each keeper. The coal is warm to the touch. It should not be. Recently, all the ninth coals across the highlands have grown warm at the same time. They pulse in the same rhythm. The rhythm is not a heartbeat.

  • Verse Law: In Pereshi courts, a well-recited verse carries more weight than a witness. The old epics contain every legal precedent; a litigant who can quote the correct verse wins automatically. The problem is that the epics are incomplete—and the missing verses are hidden in the tombs of forgotten kings. The Prince tried to finish the epic with his own verses. They are still there, in the sealed archive, waiting to be burned—or to be sung. The singers who try to learn them lose their voices for a year.

  • Ash-Bound Tokens of Passage: A sealed way-token is a small clay disc stamped with ash and witness. The Pereshi keep no treasury of metal; the token itself holds the weight of the promise. False tokens are a grave transgression—the forger is forced to copy the entire epic by hand, blindfolded. The Prince’s reckoners were the first to suffer this fate. Their ghosts still copy, still blindfolded. The ink they use is rust-colored.

  • The Prince’s Seal (Forgotten): In the deepest vault of the archive, sealed in a lead box, is the Prince’s own signet ring. It is said to grant authority over any Pereshi—because it proves the Prince’s claim. No one has opened the box. The key was buried with the last emperor. The ring is still warm. Recently, the box has begun to hum. The hum is the same pitch as the ninth coal’s pulse.

  • Obligations of Naming: When the Pereshi reckon what is owed, they do not count metal. They ask for a “name”—the true name of an enemy, a forgotten ancestor, a secret that has never been spoken. A character who cannot yield it loses one Bond or one Asset (the name is erased from records and memory). The GM may offer a choice: yield a name you know, or lose one of yours. This is not trade. It is an unmaking.

  • Fire-Shrine Hospitality: The three-day feast rule is sacred. Breaking it marks the offender as anathema—all Pereshi refuse to speak to them, and the ninth coal in every hearth spits ash in their direction. Removing the mark requires a pilgrimage to Mount Khvarena and the offering of a true name to the ninth coal. The coal may accept or refuse. It usually refuses.

  • Fire-Reading (Kubuta mu Muriro): A Pereshi diviner can read the future in a flame. The ritual takes a few minutes and requires a brazier, a handful of ash, and a question. The diviner tosses the ash into the flame; the shape it makes before burning answers. A spiral means a journey, a straight line means a safe road, a broken circle means a broken promise. The ninth ash-flake always falls outside the brazier—it belongs to the Hollow.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Undying Flame (Oath of Flame and Light): The fire that witnessed the Prince’s defeat—and still burns in witness and in warning. The warning is the coal’s warmth.

    • The Keeper of Sealed Names (The Witness): The archivist who records what must never be spoken—including the Prince’s true name. She has started to forget. The forgetting is a mercy.

    • The Ninth Coal (The Ninth): The coal that was never lit; the promise that the Prince will not return. It is warm. It is warm. It is warm.

    • The Silent Smith’s Patron (Mykkiel): The law folded into steel; the covenant that cannot be broken—unless the Prince’s shadow crosses it. The shadow is crossing it now.

Among the Pereshi, power flows through fire, ink, and the weight of ancient witness. A lord’s authority rests on the age of his library; a flame-keeper’s influence on the purity of her fire. The petty lords are constantly forging genealogies, and the archivists are constantly exposing them. Power is not inherited—it is cited, and it is witnessed by the flame. But now a new power stirs: the Prince’s shadow, offering forgotten knowledge, impossible bargains, and the chance to rewrite history. Some Pereshi have begun to listen. The ninth coal is warm. The shadow is patient.

For the GM: Use the Prince’s Shadow Timer as the campaign’s ticking heart. Every session, describe how the ninth coal feels warmer, how a mirror in a lord’s hall has begun to show a second figure, how a scribe’s shadow now moves independently. Let the party decide whether to reseal the Prince, bargain with him, or—most dangerously—try to seize his power for themselves. The Pereshi are not a people waiting for a savior. That is how the Pereshi like it. But when the coal cracks, everyone will see—and it will be too late.

The Remount Steppe.

Horses, grass, and wind-songs—the Kuvani don’t just pay in stories; they auction the right to the next remount. They range across the eastern steppe, a vast grassland east of the Valewood. Their horsemanship and remount stations make them indispensable to any caravan that crosses the open plain. The Kuvani have no cities; their courts are circles of saddles, and their laws are sung. But here’s the secret the Wind-Singers don’t want you to know: the sky is not empty. Kuva, the sky father, sees everything—every oath sworn in the open, every horse born, every blade of grass bent by the wind. But Kuva does not speak. He listens. And he is patient. The Kuvani have no temples because the sky is their shrine. A horse is not a mount—it is a relative. Its name is a lineage stretching back to the first mare that Kuva breathed life into. A Kuvani who cannot name his horse’s ancestors is a Kuvani without a shadow. The steppe is not empty; it is full of ghosts: the ghost herd of the salt pan, the sky-road markers that hum at dusk, the Iron Corral where blades whisper the names of the dead.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Story Rite: Kuvani remount stations do not accept coin. To take a fresh horse, you must tell a story that the remount master has not heard. The story is judged by the horse—if the horse stamps its foot, the story is false. If it whinnies, the story is true. If it remains still, the story is boring—you walk. Kuva’s hawks watch; a hawk’s cry means the story is worthy of the sky.

  • Wind-Songs and Sky-Roads: The Kuvani navigate by the stars and the wind. A Wind-Singer can hum a melody that encodes the route to water, to grass, or to safety. The song changes with the season. Only the singer knows the current melody. Those who hear the song without permission are cursed—they will hear it in their dreams until they give a horse to the singer.

  • Grazing Compacts as Bond: A grazing compact (a sheepskin document sealed with horsehair) can be traded, sold, or used as proof of passage. A compact from a powerful clan is worth more than a chest of silver. Forging a compact is punishable by exile—the forger is left on the steppe without a horse. Kuva will not watch over them.

  • Kuva’s Witness (Oath Mechanic): Any oath sworn under open sky (no roof, no canopy) is witnessed by Kuva. Breaking such an oath imposes  − 1 die on all rolls until the oath is fulfilled or atoned for by a sacrifice: a horse, a memory, or a year of service to the wronged party. The GM may also tick a Kuva’s Attention [6] timer; when full, a sky-omen (drought, storm, ghost herd) strikes the oath-breaker.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • Kuva — The Eternal Blue Sky. The witness of all oaths, the father of horses, the wind that carries prayers.

    • The White Mare — The Traveler. The first horse, who showed the Kuvani the sky-road. Her hoofprints are the stars.

    • The Iron Hoof — Khemesh. The pressure beneath the steppe; the hunger that pulls horses down into the salt pan.

    • The Shadow Khagan — Malachai. A cursed king who sold his clan’s horses for immortality; he rides alone, and his horses have no shadows.

The Curse of the Ninth Night: Why We Wander

“The Spider spun the net, the Snake shed the truth, the Raven closed the sky. But the one who dreamed — she whispered through the cracks. ‘Take these threads,’ she said. ‘Tie them in eights. The ninth is for me. I will sleep, and you will carry my waking. Never stay nine nights in the same breath. The Hollow counts. The Hollow follows. If it catches you, give it a story — not silver, not blood. A story it has never heard. That is the toll. That is the mercy.’”

No Tulkani speaks openly of the Dreamer. The old ones say that before the first caravan, before the roads were named, three sisters watched the world burn and rebuilt it from ash. Two of them decided that mortals must never know the fire. The third — the one who dreamed of a different path — was sealed into a sleep that would last as long as the mountains. But before she closed her eyes, she taught a handful of herders and smiths how to knot her dreams into cord. “You will be my memory,” she told them. “And the Hollow will be my shadow. Keep moving, or it will ask you what I whispered.”

Thus the Tulkani became wanderers. They do not stay nine nights because on the ninth night, the Hollow catches up. It does not attack. It asks for a story. If the Tulkani can tell one that the Dreamer herself never heard, the Hollow retreats — until the next camp, the next ninth night. If they cannot, it takes a knot from the judge’s cord, and a piece of the Dreamer’s memory fades forever.

The Tulkani worship Kuva, the sky father, openly. They light no shrines to the Dreamer. But every circle has a Dream-Wagon — a painted wagon with no wheels, its door a curtain of knots. Inside, a single candle burns beside a bed of straw. The old ones say that if you listen at midnight, you can hear the Dreamer breathing. They also say that the Raven’s shadow falls across the wagon on the ninth night of every ninth season. No one has ever seen it and remained in the circle.

The Dreamer’s Purpose

The Dreamer is not a passive presence. She is the caravan’s guide, its conscience, and its burden. She dreams the path the wagons follow — not through prophecy, but through memory. The Tulkani believe that the Dreamer remembers every road they have ever walked, and that her dreams weave those memories into new paths. When the caravan is lost, they wait for her to dream a way forward. When they are in danger, they listen for her nightmares.

What the Dreamer Wants: The Dreamer is not content. She is waiting. The oldest Caravan Judges whisper that she is searching for something in her dreams — a story she heard before she was sealed, a name she cannot remember, a knot she never tied. She believes that if she finds it, she can wake. The Tulkani do not know if they want her to wake. They do not know what would happen if she did.

What Happens If She Wakes: The elders say that if the Dreamer wakes, she will remember everything — including the fire that the other two sisters sealed away. She will speak the name of the world’s first wound. And the Hollow will no longer be her shadow; it will be her voice. The world may end. Or it may begin again. No one knows which is more likely.

The Dreamer’s Agency: The Dreamer is not a passive symbol. She sometimes speaks through the Story-Sellers, sending them visions or fragments of dreams. She may choose a particular traveler to receive a dream, setting them on a quest she cannot complete herself. She is a quest-giver, a guide, and a potential antagonist — but never a straightforward one. The Tulkani protect her, but they also fear her. The Dream-Wagon is the most heavily guarded part of any circle, not because they fear attack, but because they fear what she might say if she wakes.

The Hollow — Nature and Purpose

According to the Tulkani, the Hollow is not a single entity. It is a class of beings — the shadows of forgotten promises, the ghosts of broken oaths, the accumulated weight of every story that was never told. The Tulkani do not distinguish between “the Hollow” as a singular and “the hollow” as a state of being. They are the same thing: the pressure of unfulfilled obligation.

What the Hollow Wants: The Hollow is hungry. It feeds on stories that are never told, promises that are never kept, knots that are never tied. It does not attack the Tulkani directly — it does not need to. It simply waits. On the ninth night, it asks for a story. If the Tulkani cannot provide one that satisfies it, it takes a knot from the judge’s cord. The knot is a story that will never be told. The Hollow consumes it, and for a time, it is satisfied. But it always returns.

Can the Hollow Be Defeated? The Tulkani do not believe the Hollow can be defeated. It is not an enemy; it is a condition. The Hollow is the price of the Dreamer’s gift. To defeat it would mean undoing the Dreamer’s teaching, and that would mean the Dreamer would forget how to dream. The Tulkani have chosen to live with the Hollow rather than live without the Dreamer.

What the Hollow Gives: The Hollow is not entirely negative. The Tulkani say that the Hollow’s attention keeps them honest. A liar cannot hide from the Hollow; a cheat cannot outrun it. The Hollow is the circle’s witness, the traveler’s conscience. It is the reason the Tulkani keep their promises — not because they are virtuous, but because they are watched.

The Tulkani and Other Cultures

The Tulkani are nomads in a world of settled peoples. Their relationships with other cultures are shaped by this tension.

The Aeler (Stone and Breath): The Aeler respect the Tulkani’s precision with knots, seeing them as a primitive but functional form of accounting. They sometimes hire Tulkani cord-makers to audit their ledgers, trusting the knots more than their own clerks. But the Aeler also distrust the Tulkani’s lack of permanence. A people without a mountain is a people without a foundation.

The Ykrul (Steppe and Hoof): The Ykrul see the Tulkani as cousins. Both are nomads; both follow the seasons; both measure wealth in stories and kinship rather than coin. Tulkani caravans are welcome in Ykrul camps, and Ykrul riders are welcome in Tulkani circles — as long as they respect the ninth night. The Ykrul have even adopted the knot metaphor in some regions, tying cords to mark the progress of a negotiation.

The Vhasians (Crown and Fracture): The Vhasian nobility distrusts the Tulkani. A people without a crown is a people without a place in the order. The Tulkani are useful as messengers and spies, but they are never trusted. Vhasian lords often try to “tame” Tulkani caravans by offering them permanent land — a suggestion that the Tulkani find deeply offensive. A Tulkani who accepts permanent land is a Tulkani who has forgotten the Dreamer.

The Viterrans (Hedge and Law): The Viterrans have a complex relationship with the Tulkani. On one hand, the Tulkani’s neutrality makes them useful as witnesses in boundary disputes. On the other hand, their refusal to accept written law undermines the hedge-courts. Viterran hedge-witches sometimes consult Tulkani Story-Sellers to resolve disputes that cannot be resolved by precedent — and the Story-Sellers always charge in stories, not coin.

The Lethai (Silk and Shedding): The Lethai respect the Tulkani’s connection to Ikasha. Both cultures understand that some debts must be carried, not paid. Tulkani Dream-Wagons are sometimes welcomed in Lethai groves, and Lethai silk-wardens have been known to trade weaving techniques for Tulkani story-cords. But the Lethai also find the Tulkani unsettling — a people who carry their memory outside themselves, in knots and cords, rather than in their own minds.

Tulkani Magic: The Knot-Weaving Tradition

The Tulkani do not separate magic from craft. A knotted cord is both a tool and a spell; a story is both a memory and a working. Tulkani magic is the art of tying and untying — binding promises, cutting debts, and weaving memory into form.

The Knot-Weaver (Witch Equivalent): A Tulkani knot-weaver is a witch who works with cord and thread. She can tie a knot to bind a promise, untie a knot to break a curse, or weave a cord to store a memory. Her tools are simple: horsehair, silk, and a pair of shears that have never cut anything living. She does not cast spells in the ordinary sense; she ties knots, and the knots remember.

Core Practices:

  • Memory-Cords: A cord braided with knots for every promise made and kept. The knot-weaver can read a memory-cord like a ledger, seeing every obligation the bearer has incurred. She can also add knots to a memory-cord, binding a new promise.

  • Dream-Cords: A cord braided from silk and hair, with nine knots — the ninth left loose. Sleeping on a dream-cord allows the bearer to ask Ikasha one question. The ninth knot tightens after use. When it tightens fully, Ikasha wakes.

  • Cut Cords: A cord cut with the knot-weaver’s shears severs a promise or a curse. The cut cord becomes a ghost — the memory of the broken promise. The ghost follows the oath-breaker until the promise is fulfilled or the ghost is tied back into a new cord.

  • Woven Wards: A woven cord hung around a wagon or a threshold keeps the Hollow at bay. The cord must be renewed each season; a new knot is added each time. After nine knots, it becomes a dream-cord — and the Hollow will notice.

The Knot-Weaver’s Price: Knot-weavers pay for their power in memory. Each knot they tie costs them a piece of their own recollection — a face, a name, a moment. They remember their trade, but they forget why they learned it. This is why Caravan Judges are old: they have tied so many knots that they no longer remember their own childhoods. They carry the memory of the circle instead.

Tulkani Cartomancy: Tulkani cartomancers are a variation on the knot-weaver tradition. The cards are a different kind of knot — a story told in symbols rather than thread. A cartomancer reads the cards by feeling for the tension between them, the way a weaver feels for the tension in a cord. The suits are the four strands of the world’s thread; the ranks are the knots that hold it together.

Regional Mood: The Measure of the Knot

Power here is measured in memory-cords, shared stories, and the number of nights camped on a lord’s land. The Tulkani have no king, no judges, no written law — only the Caravan Circle, where disputes are settled by storytelling. A liar is exposed not by evidence but by the knots on his cord: if a knot is tied in false witness, it will tighten until blood is drawn. The Dreamer’s cult is not a religion; it is a survival tactic — the dream inside the wagon, the whisper in the creaking wheel. The Tulkani give no permanent coin, but they answer in stories. A lord who demands silver will be told a story so boring that his crops fail. A lord who offers hospitality will be told a story so beautiful that his harvest doubles. The Tulkani do not keep accounts; they keep faith in reciprocity. What you give, you get back — maybe tomorrow, maybe in the next valley. But the circle remembers.

Additional Features: Itinerant Mechanics

  • The Ninth Night Rule: A Tulkani caravan may camp on a lord’s land for seven nights without payment. On the eighth night, the lord may ask his due — typically a story, a promise, or a small service. On the ninth night, the Hollow comes. No Tulkani stays nine nights in one place. A party camping with Tulkani must leave by the eighth dusk or face the Hollow’s attention. Mechanic: Each night the party camps in a Tulkani circle, mark a segment on a Ninth Night Timer [8]. At 7 segments, the lord (or local authority) arrives to ask his due. At 8 segments, the Hollow manifests. The party can leave before 8 to reset the timer.

  • Story as Kinship: Tulkani do not use coin. What passes between them are stories, promises, and memories. A good story can buy a meal, a horse, or safe passage. A bad story can start a feud. The GM should encourage players to tell brief in-character stories; the quality (or a Presence + Sway roll) determines its worth. On a success, the story is worth 1 Measure of value; on a critical success, 2. On a failure, the listener is offended — the party gains  − 1 Position on their next social roll in that circle. A player may also offer a truth (a secret, a confession, a memory) instead of a story; a truth is always worth 2 Measures but marks 1 Bond to the Dreamer.

  • Land Rent and Neutrality: Tulkani circles are neutral ground. No lord may collect taxes or enforce laws inside a circle, regardless of whose territory it sits on. However, the lord may ask his due at the circle’s edge, and the Tulkani will negotiate. A party inside a Tulkani circle is under the Caravan Judge’s protection — but if the party commits a crime, the judge may expel them to the Hollow.

  • Memory-Cords and Reciprocity: Every Tulkani carries a memory-cord — a braided strand of horsehair and silk, with knots tied for every promise made and kept. A Tulkani who breaks a promise must cut the corresponding knot; the cut cord becomes a ghost that follows the oath-breaker until the promise is fulfilled. Outsiders may borrow a Tulkani memory-cord; the knots then record their promises. The Tulkani see this as reciprocity: what is given returns, and the circle always balances.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Unwitnessed Weaver (Inaea): The knot that binds gently; the cord that remembers mercy.

    • The Cord-Cutter (Isoka): The clean break; the shedding of false promises.

    • The Dreamer Below (Ikasha): She who sleeps in the wagon; she who dreams the road.

The Ninth Night: A Story Timer

When the Hollow walks on the ninth night (or when the Ninth Night Timer reaches 8), it does not attack. It asks. The GM should select or roll on the table below to determine what story the Hollow demands. The party must then tell a story (in character, as a group) that satisfies the Hollow’s request. The GM judges success based on creativity, coherence, and whether the story fits the prompt.

d8 The Hollow Asks For…
1 A story about a door that was left open.
2 A tale of a promise kept when breaking it would have been easier.
3 A confession of a small cruelty you have never admitted.
4 A story that ends with the number eight, not nine.
5 A memory of a road you will never walk again.
6 A lie that became true because someone believed it.
7 A story about a gift you gave and never received thanks for.
8 A tale of a name you have forgotten — and why you forgot it.

If the party succeeds (the GM decides), the Hollow retreats, and the Ninth Night Timer resets to 0. If they fail, the Hollow takes a knot — one of the party’s active Boons, a point of Reputation, or a memory (erase a minor fact or a Bond for one session). The timer resets but the Hollow’s attention lingers (the GM gains 2 SB to spend on future supernatural complications).

Story Bonds: Promises and Stakes

The Tulkani treat stories as the most valued exchange. When dealing with Tulkani, the party may offer a Story Promise instead of coin or goods. A Story Promise is a verbal contract: the party agrees to tell a true story (on a future occasion) to the Tulkani elder or to a specific person the elder names. The promise is witnessed by the circle — a knot is tied on a cord.

  • Stakes: The Tulkani may ask for something held in trust — a minor secret, a lock of hair, or a written name — set against the promise. If the promise is broken, the stake is given to the Hollow (it vanishes, and the party gains  + 2 to the Hollow Attention timer).

  • Echo: A story promised but delayed echoes. Each season the promise goes unfulfilled, the GM may ask the party to tell a small story (a paragraph) to the table as a reminder. If they refuse, the Hollow’s attention ticks  + 1.

  • Forgiveness: A story promise can be forgiven if the party tells a story that makes the Caravan Judge weep — or laugh so hard she cuts the cord herself. This is a high-DC social roll (typically Presence + Performance vs. DV 5) and may require an actual in-character performance.

Mini-Generator: Stories the Tulkani Trade

When a Tulkani offers a story in exchange for information, goods, or passage, roll on this table to determine its nature and mechanical effect.

d6 Story Type & Effect
1 A Warning. The story reveals a hidden danger (the GM must tell the party about one upcoming hazard or ambush). The story is true — but the teller may have exaggerated. Effect: The party gains  + 1 Position to avoid that specific hazard.
2 A Route. The story describes a forgotten path, a smuggler’s trail, or a safe ford. Effect: For the next travel leg, the party may ignore one environmental penalty (difficult terrain, bad weather, etc.).
3 A Claim. The story recounts an old promise tied to the teller’s family. The party may choose to honor it (gain trust from the Tulkani but also a Mark — a minor promise that the GM can call in later) or ignore it (the Tulkani is offended;  − 1 Position on future social rolls with that circle).
4 A Secret. The story reveals a piece of gossip about a local lord, merchant, or rival faction. Effect: The party gains a one-use Secret Leverage —  + 2 dice on a single social roll against that person.
5 A Lament. The story is a tragedy — a death, a lost love, a failed quest. Those who listen properly gain  + 1 die to resist fear or despair for the next scene. The teller, however, becomes melancholy ( − 1 die on their next roll).
6 A Dream. The story is not quite true — it is a dream the Dreamer had. The party may ask the GM one yes/no question about the near future (the next scene or the next leg of travel). The answer is always cryptic, but never false.

Tulkani Knot-Weaving: A Witch’s Tradition

The Tulkani do not separate magic from craft. A knotted cord is both a tool and a spell; a story is both a memory and a working. The Knot-Weaver is the Tulkani equivalent of a witch — a practitioner who works with cord, thread, and the weight of promises.

Core Practices:

  • Memory-Cords: A cord braided with knots for every promise made and kept. The knot-weaver can read a memory-cord like a ledger, seeing every obligation the bearer has incurred. She can also add knots to a memory-cord, binding a new promise.

  • Dream-Cords: A cord braided from silk and hair, with nine knots — the ninth left loose. Sleeping on a dream-cord allows the bearer to ask Ikasha one question. The ninth knot tightens after use. When it tightens fully, Ikasha wakes.

  • Cut Cords: A cord cut with the knot-weaver’s shears severs a promise or a curse. The cut cord becomes a ghost — the memory of the broken promise. The ghost follows the oath-breaker until the promise is fulfilled or the ghost is tied back into a new cord.

  • Woven Wards: A woven cord hung around a wagon or a threshold keeps the Hollow at bay. The cord must be renewed each season; a new knot is added each time. After nine knots, it becomes a dream-cord — and the Hollow will notice.

  • Cartomancy: Tulkani cartomancers are a variation on the knot-weaver tradition. The cards are a different kind of knot — a story told in symbols rather than thread. A cartomancer reads the cards by feeling for the tension between them, the way a weaver feels for the tension in a cord. The suits are the four strands of the world’s thread; the ranks are the knots that hold it together.

The Knot-Weaver’s Price: Knot-weavers pay for their power in memory. Each knot they tie costs them a piece of their own recollection — a face, a name, a moment. They remember their trade, but they forget why they learned it. This is why Caravan Judges are old: they have tied so many knots that they no longer remember their own childhoods. They carry the memory of the circle instead.

  • Tulkani Memory-Cords and the Sidhi Freedman’s Seal: Sidhi merchants on the Brass Coast accept Tulkani memory-cords as proof of standing. A well-knotted cord can buy a ship. A cut cord can sink one. The Sidhi know that a Tulkani knot is worth more than a signature — and that the Hollow reads over their shoulder.

  • Ikasha and the Veiled Sisters (Ashaan): The Tulkani are the primary spreaders of Ikasha’s cult outside Ashaan. A Breath-less agent may be disguised as a Tulkani story-seller; a Tulkani caravan may be a mobile safe house. The Sisters have tried to infiltrate the circles three times. Aelaerem hedge-keepers sometimes hire Tulkani guides to reset the Hollow’s attention timers. The payment is always a story — and the story is always about a door that was left open.

A Tulkani judge may consult the Ukwe to verify a story’s truth. The Ukwe speaks in rustles. A single rustle means true. Two mean false. Three mean the story is not finished — and the Hollow is listening.

Among the Tulkani, power flows through knots, stories, and the memory of the circle. A Caravan Judge’s authority is recognized by every lord from the steppe to the coast — not because of armies, but because the Hollow respects the judge’s cord. The Tulkani have no temples, no kings, no borders. They have the ninth night, and that is enough.

For the GM: Power in Tulkani society is measured in knots tied, stories told, and nights counted. Patronage often takes the form of memory-beads, dream-cords, or safe-haven oaths — each holding the spirit of a promise. To emphasize the itinerant mechanics:

  • Require players to negotiate land rent with local lords, using stories or promises as their answer. A failed negotiation may lead to the Hollow.

  • Use the Ninth Night Timer as a tension mechanic: every night in a Tulkani camp advances the timer. At 8, the Hollow manifests unless the party leaves.

  • Let memory-cords be physical props — actual knotted strings that players can hold, tie, or cut. A cut cord is a dramatic moment.

  • Emphasize that storytelling is a skill: reward creative players with mechanical bonuses, and let failed stories have consequences — a bored lord may tighten his claim, an offended spirit may curse.

  • Treat Tulkani society as a web of reciprocity, not a reckoning of accounts. What you give, you get back — but the circle decides when.

In Tulkani society, the knot remembers everything — and the ninth night forgets nothing.
