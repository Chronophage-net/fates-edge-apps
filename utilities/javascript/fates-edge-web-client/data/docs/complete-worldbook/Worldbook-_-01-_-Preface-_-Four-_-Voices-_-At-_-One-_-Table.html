Preface: Four Voices at One Table

A note on the compilation

The Edgewalker: I collected these accounts for journeys that had to arrive. I have left the speakers’ names and occupations beside their statements because an account without a source is difficult to question. I have kept disagreements where I could not settle them. Mira, Ume, and Naru have written in the margins; I have answered where an answer seemed useful. None of us has walked every road described here.

The Ninth Words came to me as a copy attributed to Caravan Judge Sarnai, from Samir al-Qalat’s scroll. I did not open its reported resting place to verify that attribution. I use its repeated images to recognize questions worth asking. A resemblance is a lead. It is not proof that every well has drowned a city.

Four people were asked what this book is for. Each has walked its roads, paid its tolls, and returned with a different account of what makes a journey possible. Their accounts disagree about what is worth securing, as well as how to secure it.

The Edgewalker (Aeler, of the High King’s Office of External Opportunities)

“Find out what refusal costs. Then ask.”

I am an Aeler rogue. The hold has no place for me at its respectable tables; it still makes room for the wagons I bring home. The High King’s Office pays for opportunities and keeps my name off the public account. Today that means grain for mountains that cannot grow enough of it. A lord’s generosity is welcome. It is a poor basis for next winter’s ration. His successor may be less generous; his creditors may arrive first. I prefer an arrangement that survives his mood.

People call this cynicism. Very well. I have watched men praise sacrifice from the full end of the table. When someone tells you a duty is sacred, find out who performs it, who can excuse themselves, and who keeps the surplus. Sincere belief does not prevent an advantageous interpretation.

That does not mean everyone can be bought. A woman may refuse silver because she means her oath. Learn what the oath requires. A captain may accept ruin rather than disgrace his company. Learn who decides what counts as disgrace. If you mistake conviction for a price you have not yet offered, you will lose to someone you consider a fool.

At a river gate, a governor detained our grain under an inspection order. I spent the morning with his weigh-clerk. By noon I knew that his levy wagons used a bridge repaired on credit, that the bridge company’s payment was overdue, and that its lawful toll exemption expired that evening. I bought the claim at a discount. The company agreed to grant another month’s exemption if I countersigned it.

I put the exemption beside our inspection papers. I asked for our grain to be inspected first, at the posted fee. He could refuse. His levy could pay the ordinary toll. His treasury could settle the bridge account. I had made sure I could afford any of those answers.

He called me a carrion beetle. I waited while he signed. We were through before the dinner bell; by then he had sent his secretary to ask what else I held. I let the secretary wait until the wagons were beyond recall.

Nobody liked me more when I left. The grain moved. I also acquired a debtor who will remember my name long after the bridge is paid for. That belongs in the account. I will accept an enemy for a winter’s food; I will not pretend I have made a friend.

Read this book for dependencies: who needs a road open, whose word releases a cargo, how long each party can wait. Secure something they actually need, make a demand they can meet, and leave a way to comply without destroying the thing you came to obtain. I do not need to be welcome at the table. I need the people at it to have a reason to sign.

Mira of the Wandering Circle (Tulkani cartomancer)

“Leave room for an answer you did not bring with you.”

A ferryman once refused our silver and our letters of passage. His daughter had died that spring. He had kept her place at the landing, and our wagons were standing in it. We moved them. He took us across. I could not have prepared that bargain before meeting him; he had not thought of it as a bargain himself.

I read the cards to make room for such discoveries. A place has stories that its officials do not know, and a traveller may be offered a part in one. Sometimes a gift is an invitation to become a person who would give in return. I value that uncertain beginning. It can grow into a relationship neither party could have specified at the gate.

Draw the card. Read it aloud. Ask what it means, and let somebody else answer first. A refusal may contain a different road. Listen long enough to hear where it goes.

Ume “Eight-Knot” Nineterrace (Rota-Keeper of Yatsuno, the Eightfold Village)

“Put the replacement’s name beside the promise.”

In my valley the world is a board by the door with eight columns on it. One column tells us who walks the boundary. Another tells us who takes over if that person is ill. I have seen an agreement that satisfied every signatory fail because nobody had arranged for a tired woman to sleep.

A good bargain can bring us salt. Keeping the salt dry takes a roof, and keeping the roof sound takes work in a season when everyone has other work. I want arrangements that ordinary people can continue without an exceptional negotiator arriving every month. Give two people the key. Teach the apprentice. Leave enough in the store for a bad week.

When you use these pages, ask who maintains what you have won, and what happens when they cannot. My measure of success is a village that still manages the Tuesday after its rescuer leaves.

And eat before you talk. You have walked a long way and it is rude to do business hungry.

Naru of the Fourth Terrace (Still Point adept, of the Still Road)

“Know what you would let pass.”

I once carried a letter that would have restored my place in a household I had left. It was an excellent letter. Every time I approached a new town, I checked that it was dry. After a year I noticed that I was still choosing roads from which I could turn back to that house.

I burned it. I lost a real protection. The next winter was harder, and I will not call that hardship a hidden reward. But I had decided where I was going. Until then I had been preserving an answer to a question I no longer wished to ask.

Know what you want before you begin acquiring the means to obtain it. At a threshold, ask what you are willing to pay, what you will refuse, and whether passing still serves your purpose. You may keep the letter. You may use every claim you possess. Do it knowingly; the ability to win a negotiation is no obligation to enter it.

Use these voices as four ways into a place: the Edgewalker follows dependencies, Mira opens relationships, Ume builds continuity, and Naru examines purpose. Each can lead the company through a difficulty the others would approach differently. Let their priorities shape choices with consequences, rather than appointing one of them to deliver the book’s verdict.

— Set down at a weigh-house in Mbaro, under a lamp that flickers nine times before it steadies
