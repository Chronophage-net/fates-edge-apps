Preface: Four Voices at One Table

Four people were asked what this book is for. They were asked separately, which was a mercy. Each of them has walked the roads in it, paid its tolls, and come back with a different account of what the road actually is. They are printed here in full and in disagreement, because a gazetteer written in one voice is a gazetteer you can only use one way.

The Edgewalker (Aeler, of the High King’s Office of External Opportunities)

“The road is not a ledger. It is a butcher’s block.”

I am an Aeler Edgewalker. You call us mountain-dwellers who measure breath in silver bells and seal contracts in stone. How adorably naïve.

We don’t just measure breath—we own the lungs that draw it. When a Tulkani chieftain swears an oath on his firstborn’s life? That’s not honor—it’s collateral I’ve already hypothecated three times over. When a Veiled Sister binds a demon to a corpse to prop up her corpse-throne? That’s not terror—it’s a futures contract on soul-debt, and I’m the broker who sold the short position before the ritual even began.

You see the surface as “full of problems.” How quaint. Problems aren’t obstacles—they’re inventory.

This book isn’t a tourist’s guide. It’s a hit list with footnotes. I’ve marked the places where a well-placed bell doesn’t just open a pass—it starts a war I’ve already shorted the bond market on. Where a forged seal doesn’t just move a mountain—it triggers a landslide I’ve bought mineral rights to before the first crack appeared. Where a single true story doesn’t just buy a ship’s loyalty—it buys the captain’s silence while I sell his manifest to pirates.

You want to build journeys? Draw your cards. Set your timers. Spend your Story Beats like they mean something. But remember: every Diamond you earn is a tooth knocked out of someone else’s jaw. Every Debt you incur? I already bought it from your victim for half what you’ll pay—and I’m collecting interest in your marrow before you finish reading this sentence.

The road isn’t just a ledger. It’s a butcher’s block. Every gate isn’t a clause—it’s a throat waiting for the knife.

Mira of the Wandering Circle (Tulkani cartomancer)

“He is not wrong. He is only finished. That is a different thing.”

I have read for that man. Twice. He paid both times, in coin, promptly, and did not look at the cards once. That is the whole of him: a person who has decided in advance what every road will say, and who is therefore never surprised, and who is therefore never told anything.

The road is not a butcher’s block. It is a conversation, and he has stopped holding up his end. Every entry in the pages that follow is a thing somebody said—a bridge that remembers a toll, a hearth-aunt who will not let you refuse a cup, a boundary walked by a woman who has walked it forty years. He hears offers. I hear people, and they are talking to you, and they have been talking for a long time before you arrived.

Here is my instruction and it is shorter than his. Draw the card. Read it aloud. Then ask the table what it means, and let somebody who is not you answer first. The deck is not a script and it is not a hit list. It is a way of listening to what the world has already decided to tell you.

Ume “Eight-Knot” Nineterrace (Rota-Keeper of Yatsuno, the Eightfold Village)

“Somebody has to do the Tuesday. It is never him.”

Both of them are talking about the interesting parts. I would like to enter an objection on behalf of the dull ones.

He calls the world inventory. She calls it a conversation. In my valley the world is a board by the door with eight columns on it, and the terrible things happen in the weeks nobody wrote down. Every place in this book stands up because somebody is holding it up—walking the thread, hauling the salt, lighting the lamp at the correct hour, opening the crock to see whether the line has moved. The Edgewalker has never once asked who did that. He arrives after it is done and prices it.

So when you use these pages, ask the question neither of them will: who maintains this, and what happens in the week they don’t? That is where your adventures are. Not in the treasure. In the gap in the rota.

And eat before you talk. You have walked a long way and it is rude to do business hungry.

Naru of the Fourth Terrace (Still Point adept, of the Still Road)

“I have one question for the Aeler and he has never answered it.”

He is carrying the ledger. Not keeping it — carrying it, every day, up every road in this book, and he has told you at length how heavy it is and how clever he is for lifting it. The three of us have now spent four pages on what the road is. None of us has asked what he is holding, or whether he could set it down at the next milestone and walk on with his hands empty.

I am not asking you to be gentle. The road has real tolls and some of them are collected in blood; the Rota-Keeper is right that somebody must hold the world up, and the cartomancer is right that it is speaking. I am asking one thing only, and it is the thing my order asks at every threshold: when you reach the gate and find that it will cost you something to pass, notice what you are gripping, and whether you are gripping it because you need it or because you have never once opened your hand.

You may still pay the toll. Most of us do. But pay it knowing.

The four of them were not brought together afterward, and would not have agreed if they had been. Use whichever voice fits the table you are running. Most groups find they need all four before the campaign is out: the Edgewalker to price the job, Mira to hear what the place is saying, the Rota-Keeper to ask who has been holding it up, and the adept to ask what it is costing the person who pays.

— Set down at a weigh-house in Mbaro, under a lamp that flickers nine times before it steadies
