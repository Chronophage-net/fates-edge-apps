Oshiira

The Grain Covenant.

A short measure can unmake a house. I am not here to be liked.

I am a Grain Inspector. My stamp leaves a mark that shows under moonlight, and I have never yet had to explain one twice.

Oshiira is hundreds of tribes and city-states that agreed, in writing and a long time ago, that nobody inside the agreement would starve. That is the Covenant. Contribute in the fat years; be carried in the thin ones. It has held four centuries. I have stood in an Ecktorian counting-house and been asked, slowly, whether we have writing.

Mbaro is canals, weigh-houses, and the Granary Tower. Our millet fills northern holds at prices set here, which was fought for twice and paid for the second time in ships. The Vermilion Corps weighs loyalty as well as grain, and the Narrow Captains provoke the Admiralty now and again to remind Kahfagia who actually holds the straits.

And the Widow has not been seen in seven years. Understand the argument before you join it: the office has no heirs. It never has. A Widow is chosen out of the canal-mothers, marries the granaries, and holds for life. So the question in the weigh-houses is not whose daughter sits next. It is whether the Corps has been choosing in her name, and whether we still have the standing to tell them no. Four houses have answers. All four are argued out loud, by name, in public, by people who expect to be quoted and are.

Name the Widow before you raise the succession. Get that order wrong in front of me and you have told me what you think of us.

— Grain Inspector Adesuwa of the Ninth Weigh-House, who has refused a Vermilion warrant twice and expects to again

What do people eat for breakfast? Millet porridge and dried fish, and an argument about the fish. The millet is flood-plain grown and does not vary; the fish comes off whichever canal-barge made Mbaro that week, and households have strong and inherited opinions about the barges.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To handle a balance. A child gets a scale and one grain of millet, and by five can tell northern Sahel from southern riverland by weight alone. It is a party trick, mostly. What it teaches is that the measure is knowable, which is the whole basis of trusting anybody with your harvest.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their hands. Ink on the fingers means contracts signed; none at all means a person who has never been trusted with one, or has been trusted with the sort that go unwritten. Both are worth knowing before you sit down.

What is the most common crime? Short-weighting, and the elaborate professional culture that has grown up around catching it. The Corps audits the auditors, and this is not corruption — it is the design. Four centuries of people watching each other weigh things is why the Covenant still works.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? That the decrees coming out of the Granary Tower have been in the Corps’ phrasing for some years now. Everyone knows. Everyone also knows that a vacancy declared in a hurry is a vacancy the Corps fills, so nobody has yet been willing to be the one who says it in the open.

Where do people go when they die? Home to their district, under their own granary floor, so that the house they fed keeps standing on them. Mbaro is not sentimental about this and will not ship a body north for any price; foreign factors have offered, and the offers are still repeated as a joke.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You bring the correction yourself, to the weigh-house you wronged, and you stand there while it is entered and witnessed. The stamp is not the apology. Being seen to stand for it is.

What is the most important festival? The Reckoning, in autumn, when the year’s shortfalls are read out in the great square. It is not a shaming. Districts that came up short are named alongside the districts that will cover them, and the covering is read in the same breath and the same voice. That pairing is the point of the whole institution, and every Widow in living memory has insisted on reading it herself.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? That one of the four houses has a canal-mother’s testament, properly witnessed, naming the Corps’ authority void from the seventh year onward. It is genuine. It has been genuine for two years. The house holding it has not produced it, because producing it means governing afterward, and they are not yet sure they can.

Ashaan

The Veiled Throne.

The Veil is not a choice. It is survival. You will show your eyes and nothing more. Speak in thirds—offer two options and a hidden third. The ninth word is always a knife.

I am a Breath-less Agent. They say Ikasha sleeps. I say she is patient. The Sisters have ruled for generations; they believe the Veil is eternal. But the Veil is silk. Silk frays. And I am learning to bite through—one whisper at a time.

Ashaan is a nation that died long ago. The Veiled Sisters keep its corpse walking through spirit-binding, terror, and the endless consumption of their own people. The capital, Galanina, is a city of black marble and brass cages. The Red Desert, which now covers half the country, was once fertile land; the Sisters’ magic leeched the life from it. There is no recovery, only a slower dying. But here’s the secret the Sisters don’t want you to know: the Veil is cracking. The spirits they’ve bound for centuries are whispering to each other. And the Hollow has begun to notice.

The Esoiti control every ledger and every gate. The Black Hand hunts without uniform—but the Hand is human. They were once bakers, clerks, mothers, soldiers. Some are fanatics who chose the knife. Others are desperate souls who were given a choice: serve or vanish. They do not sleep well. Their hands shake when the Sisters” acolytes pass. And the Sisters—the Sisters are not human. They do not negotiate. They do not threaten. They state what will be. Their voices are the same as the wind over a mass grave. You cannot bribe a divine disaster. You can only survive its attention.

The Breath-less meet in root cellars, learning to read ledgers without leaving marks. We are not heroes. We are the ones who learned that a whisper can be louder than a scream—and that the Sisters fear silence more than they fear rebellion. Silence means the Veil is healing. Silence means the bound are forgetting. The Sisters cannot abide forgetting.

Never name the dead. The Sisters bind spirits to names. Speak a dead name aloud, and you may find yourself bound alongside them.

— Tamar, Breath-less cell leader, who has three names—each for a different jurisdiction

What do people eat for breakfast? Black bread and thin soup—the bread is made from grain grown on the edge of the Red Desert, the soup is flavoured with salt and memory. The memory is of green fields. The fields are gone. The bread is bitter.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To veil their face. Ashaan children are taught to cover their mouths when speaking, their eyes when listening, their ears when hearing secrets. A child who cannot veil is a child who will be taken by the Sisters. The Sisters take children. The children become spirits. The spirits serve the Sisters. The Sisters never stop taking.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their veil. Ashaan veils are coded—red for mourning, white for marriage, black for the Sisters. A stranger whose veil is gray is a Breath-less agent. A stranger whose veil is torn is a fugitive. A stranger with no veil is a fool. Fools are collected by the Black Hand. The Black Hand does not ask questions. The Black Hand was once a person. The Black Hand no longer remembers who.

What is the most common crime? Spirit-trading—but spirit-trading is not a crime, it is the economy. The Sisters bind spirits to brass cages. The cages are sold to the Esoiti. The Esoiti sell the spirits to foreign buyers. The foreign buyers do not know what they are buying. The Sisters do not care. The Sisters are not merchants. They are a natural disaster with a ledger.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? Everyone is afraid. The Black Hand fears the Esoiti’s audits. The Esoiti fears the Sisters“ gaze. The Sisters fear the Hollow’s patience. The baker fears the guard. The guard fears the Hand. The Hand fears the name on tomorrow’s list. The only person in Ashaan who is not afraid is the child who has not yet learned to read the ledger. That child will be afraid by next spring.

Where do people go when they die? To the brass cages. The dead are not buried. They are bound into spirit-lanterns and hung from the walls of Galanina. The lanterns glow green. The green is the light of the bound. The bound cannot speak. The bound cannot scream. The bound can only watch. Sometimes they weep. The tears evaporate into glass.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a name—a dead name, a forgotten name, a name the Sisters do not yet know. The Sisters bind the name to a brass cage. The cage is placed in the Hall of Silence. The Hall is full. The Hall is always full. The Hall is never silent. The cages whisper. The whispers are the names of those who were erased.

What is the most important festival? The Night of the Unbound (winter solstice). The spirits strain against their cages. The Sisters renew their bindings. The ceremony takes three days. The city is silent for three days. The silence is not prayer. The silence is fear. If you listen closely during the silence, you can hear the Hollow breathing.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The High Sister is not a sister. She is a spirit—the first one bound, nine hundred years ago. She bound herself to the throne. She cannot leave. She cannot die. She cannot stop binding. The other Sisters do not know. The other Sisters are her prisoners. The prisoners guard the jailer. The jailer does not sleep. Her veil is made of gold coins from a dozen dead empires. The coins do not clink. They weep when she speaks.

Ashaan — “The Veiled Throne”

Key Demographic: Human Capital: Ashaan (The City)

Ashaan is a setting of systemic cruelty: chattel slavery, state-sanctioned violence, child sacrifice (secret cults), and the erasure of identity. The Veiled Sisters rule through terror, the Black Hand erases dissent, and the Breath-less resistance struggles in shadows. These themes are presented as horror and injustice, never glamourised. Genre / Mood: Grimdark spirit-horror, bureaucratic terror, whispered resistance, creeping divine punishment.

Starting Location: A root cellar beneath a spice merchant’s shop, lit by a smuggled candle. A Breath-less agent presses a folded paper into your hand: “My brother argued with an Esoti about a misweighed sack of cardamom. His name was erased from the rolls by morning. Once, the Ashaani Empire stretched across the eastern Amaranthine rim — a river-fed dominion of sorcerer-kings, the 999 Immortals, and the great twin-river metropolis of Khasurah. Three centuries ago, an invasion of Oshiira shattered the empire. The Immortals were annihilated; plague, blockade, and northern mercenaries finished the ruin. Today, what remains is a rump city-state called Ashaan (or As’Ashaan), a fortified river-island north of the Khesai delta. The old lands — the Green Ribbon floodplains, the Great Desert where glass hums with ancient curses — are a patchwork of loyal towns, restless hill peoples, and the ruins of swallowed cities like Khasurah, which still breathes under dunes and salt crusts.

But the fall of Ashaan was not merely conquest. It was punishment. The priests whisper that the 999 Immortals committed a sin so profound that the gods turned their faces away — and the sand remembers what the living have forgotten. The Veil, the magical membrane that separates the living from the dead, is not a barrier. It is a wound that never healed. And the wound is spreading.

Neighbors and Rivals:

  • West – Oshiira: The grain empire that broke Ashaan’s back. The two nations maintain a cold peace, punctuated by Oshiiran grain barges that pay ruinous tariffs to pass the Brass Gate. Ashaani spies in Mbaro watch for any sign that the Widow might seek to finish what her ancestors started.

  • South – Taharka: The highland kingdom of the Monsoon Crown. Taharkan passes are a graveyard for Ashaani armies; the Veiled Sisters have never broken those mountains. Now Ashaani agents slip through disguised as spice merchants, seeking to weaken Taharka from within.

  • East – Galanina (Sidhi ancestral homeland) and the Pereshi Highlands: Galanina, the old eastern capital, lies under Ashaani control — but its Catacombs of the Unburied hold the bound spirits of Sidhi slaves, and the Breath-less dream of freeing them. Beyond, the Pereshi fire temples guard the secret of the Forgotten Prince, an Ashaani conqueror whose true name was severed. The Maeri want that name; the Pereshi will never give it.

The state is ruled by three masked beings known as the Maeri — the Veiled Sister (coins and winter), the Helmed Sister (steel and sickness), and the Masked Sister (red and black). No one knows what they are: demigods, archfey, lich-queens, or the last masks of the First Fey. They speak through the Esoti, a collegium of wizards who maintain the wards, govern the bureaucracy, and interpret the Maeri’s will. In secret, certain Esoti circles conduct child sacrifice to Khemesh, a sea-demon cult imported in the empire’s maritime days.

The Black Hand is the Maeri’s instrument: a fanatical network of bards and warlocks divided into three Aspects — Veiled (accountants, heralds, ruin by paper), Helmed (assassins and enforcers, ruin by blade), and Masked (courtesans, impresarios, ruin by desire). The Hand removes over-mighty nobles, disappears rival magi, and unpersons threats. Within it, ambition is a short road to an unmarked grave. Unlike the Sisters, the Hand is human — broken, desperate, or fanatical, but still capable of fear, doubt, and the occasional flicker of mercy.

Slavery is legal, widely practiced, and increasingly focused on wandered Sidhi lineages broken by the empire’s fall. Enslaved Sidhi bear blue marks on the forearm; illegal to remove without a manumission writ, but earth-wise midwives know ways to fade it. In the catacombs beneath Galanina — the old eastern capital — the dead are mapped like a city, and the spirits of the unburied are bound into brass cages.

Yet the Veil is cracking. In Galanina, devotion to the Angel Mykkiel (a solar of unbending Law and Justice) spreads. His zealots are incorruptible — an acid slowly eating the old order. And beneath the capital, the goddess Ikasha (She Who Sleeps) stirs in her sealed vault. Her followers, the Breath-less, meet in root cellars, learn to read records without leaving marks, and whisper that a name restored can weaken the Veil.

What you notice first: The smell of incense and rust. The way every doorway has a veil of brass chains. The silence when a Veiled Sister passes — even the spirits hold their breath. The brass tags on the dead — one for each Sister. And the green glass that glints in every shadow, fragments of a desert that was once a garden.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never name the dead. The Sisters bind spirits to names. Speak a dead name aloud, and you may find yourself bound alongside them. Also, never address a coin-veiled speaker at court unless you are ready to accept their price.

Regional Mood: The Weight of Damnation

Power here is measured in spirits bound, names erased, and the silence of witnesses. The Maeri rule through terror; the Esoti hold the records; the Black Hand hunts without uniform. The Breath-less whisper in cellars, learning to read records without leaving marks. Ashaan is not a place of open rebellion. It is a place of whispered warnings, vanished neighbors, and scrolls that rewrite themselves.

But beneath all of this runs the creeping. The Red Desert expands by inches each year, its green glass humming with a forgotten curse. The spirits bound in brass cages grow restless; their whispers are prayers to gods who no longer answer. The Veil, once a clean wound, has begun to fester. No one remembers what sin caused it. Perhaps no one ever knew. But everyone feels the weight — a pressure behind the eyes, a dryness in the throat, a certainty that something is coming.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Veil: The Maeri’s control is maintained through spirit-binding and terror. The Veil is a magical membrane that separates the living from the dead. When the Veil fails, spirits walk. Mechanically, the GM may spend 3 SB to trigger a Veil Failure scene: all spirits become visible, bound spirits may break free, and social rolls against the Maeri’s agents gain +1 Position. The Veil fails more often now. No one knows why.

  • The Veil’s Attention (Divine Threat Timer): Unlike mundane factions, the Veiled Sisters do not have a favor or debt track. They have a timer: Veil’s Attention [6]. It ticks when the party draws the Sisters’ gaze — by speaking a forbidden name, by freeing a slave, by resisting a Black Hand sweep, or simply by being in the wrong place at the wrong time. When the timer ticks, the GM may impose an omen: a mirror fogs, a bell rings in still air, a shadow falls where no light is cast. When the timer reaches 6, a Veiled Sister appears. She does not offer a bargain. She pronounces a judgment: a name to be erased, a memory to be taken, a life to be claimed. The party cannot fight her. They can only flee, or hide, or offer a substitution — a name they are willing to lose. The Sisters are not enemies. They are a natural disaster with a ledger.

  • The Breath-less (Ikasha’s Cult as Active Resistance): She Who Sleeps is not a patron of open rebellion. Her followers meet in root cellars, learn to read records without leaving marks, and whisper secrets under the cover of a cough. A character who whispers Ikasha’s name before sleeping gains +1 die to resist fear for the next day, but marks 1 Exposure (the Black Hand may notice). Their symbol is a broken brass chain. Aiding the Breath-less may earn a Breath-less Cipher (see Diamonds) but advances a Black Hand Interest timer.

  • The 999 Immortals: The failed invasion of Oshiira is commemorated in the Temple of the Golden Sepulcher, where the gem-eyed skulls of the 999 are interred. Each New Flood, magistrates process with gold-veiled reliquaries, praying not to repeat their hubris. The guardless helmed blades of the Immortals became trophies; many now hang at the hips of Oshiira’s greatest warriors. Some say the skulls whisper at night. They whisper a name that no one will speak.

  • Veil Etiquette: At court, a face fully veiled in coin signals “I speak as the treasury.” To address such a speaker is to accept their price. The ninth cup at elite tables is lifted without drinking — a silent remembrance of the 999 and a warning against hubris. Masktime (the hour before sunrise) is when contracts are sealed, lovers dismissed, and bribes counted; unmasked business at that hour is considered offensive. Those who break the ninth cup rule find that their next drink tastes of glass.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The Unbound Chain – Ikasha. She Who Sleeps; her dream is freedom. Her dream is leaking.

    • The Gilded Scale – Mykkiel. The Maeri’s twisted version of divine law; the zealots of Galanina. Their scales weigh not coin, but names.

    • The Drowned Prince – Khemesh. Pressure from the deep; the Red Desert was once his seabed. His cult practices child sacrifice in secret. The sacrifices are always the ninth child.

    • The Silvered Tongue – Malachai. Curses sold as blessings, whispered in the slave markets. The blessing always includes a ninth clause. No one reads it.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Keystone Bond (Relationship Mechanic): Each walking city has a Keystone — a single stone that bears the weight of the roof. The Keystone is also the physical anchor of the city’s ancestral spirit. A character who bonds with the Keystone (by spending a downtime scene in meditation at its base) gains:

    • Keystone Sense: Once per scene, the character can ask the Keystone one question about the city’s current state (“Is the eastern roller cracking?” “Is there a saboteur in the foundry?”). The Keystone answers truthfully, but only about the present.

    • Keystone Weight: The character gains a 4-segment Ancestor’s Attention timer. When it fills, the ancestor demands a service — usually a pilgrimage to a forgotten quarry or the retrieval of a lost roller.

  • The Roller-Pits (Hazard Mechanic): The city moves on rollers — massive wooden cylinders that must be greased and rotated. The roller-pits are dangerous, loud, and prone to sabotage.

    • Entering a roller-pit requires a Body + Athletics test (DV 4) to avoid being crushed. On a failure, the character takes 1 Harm.

    • Sabotaging a roller-pit (to stop the city or redirect it) requires a Wits + Craft test (DV 5). On a success, the city moves in the desired direction; on a failure, the roller jams and the city stops — and the ancestors are angry.

    • Repairing a jammed roller takes 3 actions. Each action requires a Body + Craft test (DV 4). On a failure, the roller shifts and the character takes 1 Fatigue.

  • The Migration Timer (Campaign Timer , size 10): Every walking city has a Migration Timer . It advances each day the city moves, and retreats when the city stops.

    • At 2: The city enters fertile land. The party gains +1 die to foraging and hunting.

    • At 4: The city passes a rival settlement. The party may trade or raid.

    • At 6: The city enters a narrow pass. All navigation rolls are Desperate.

    • At 8: The city approaches a sacred site. The ancestors grow restless — all rolls involving the Keystone gain +1 die, but failure ticks the Ancestor’s Attention timer by 2.

    • At 10: The city reaches its destination — the end of the migration. The ancestors are satisfied. The party may claim a Keystone Blessing (a permanent +1 die to all rolls involving stonework or negotiation with Ngomebe).

    The party can influence the Migration Timer by:

    • Scouting ahead (Wits + Lore) — success advances the timer by 1, failure ticks it back by 1.

    • Repairing rollers (Craft test) — success advances the timer by 2, failure advances it by 0 but ticks the Ancestor’s Attention.

    • Paying tribute (a Honey Jar, a story, or a Boon) — advances the timer by 1 without risk.

  • The Root-binder Heresy (Faction Mechanic): Root-binders are heretics who plant acorns in the roller-pits, hoping to stop the cities and let the forest reclaim the stone. They have a Sabotage Timer (size 6). Each time the party fails a Craft or Insight roll in a roller-pit, the Sabotage Timer ticks.

    • At 2: A roller cracks. The city loses 1 Speed (travel takes longer).

    • At 4: A roller-pit floods. The city must stop for repairs (lose 1 day).

    • At 6: A major collapse. The city cannot move until the party clears the pit (requires a Strength + Craft test, DV 5, and costs 1 day).

    Players can work with the Root-binders (gain information, sabotage rival cities) or against them (hunt them, protect the rollers). The Root-binders offer Green Blessings — a permanent +1 die to survival in the forest, but the forest’s attention tightens (the Hollow’s gaze falls more often).

  • The Mason-Kin Houses: The walking cities are not nations but extended families. Each House controls a keystone, a migration route, and the rights to carve. Houses feud over sledge-tracks, water rights, and the honor of the First City.

  • The Aeler Pact: The Aeler engineers of the southern ranges maintain the bearings and capstans that make the walking cities possible. The Ngomebe provide keystone hearts and stone-song. It is a partnership of equals, sealed in slate and iron.

  • Ancestral Cracks: Once per session, a character may press their ear to a keystone and roll Spirit + Lore (DV 3). On success, the ancestors whisper a warning or a clue (GM reveals a hidden complication or a shortcut). On a miss, the stone is silent — but the ancestors are listening.

  • Tokoloshe Pots: A character who carries a clay pot with a tokoloshe inside gains +1 die to detect sabotage or traps in the city. The tokoloshe sings when danger is near. However, if the pot breaks, the tokoloshe escapes and will haunt the carrier for a full season (all actions Desperate at night).

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The First Stone — The Sealed Gate. The keystone that never cracks; the oath that never breaks.

    • The Rain Queen — Raéyn. The bringer of storms and the drought; the mistress of the path.

    • The Lightning Bird — The Storm King. The shatterer of false keystones; the judge of masons.

    • The Ancestor’s Voice — The Witness. The whisper in the crack; the memory in the stone.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • Umbhemisi and the Salt Scar: The Cracking Wind is said to be the ghost of the First City, ground into dust by its own stubbornness. Some Ngomebe elders believe that if a city ever stops moving for nine generations, the Wind will reclaim it entirely. The Root-binders whisper that the Wind is not a curse but a teacher: it cracks only the stone that refuses to grow.

  • The Sledge-Wax Trade (Way of Silk): Sledge-wax is a valuable commodity on the caravan routes. Tulkani merchants trade it for story-knots; Fhara water-clerks use it to seal well-contracts. A missing shipment of sledge-wax could stop a city.

  • The Iron Mines of Vajrapur (Dhahara): The Aeler engineers of Ngomebe source their bearing-metal from the Dhaharan iron mines. A dispute between a Mason-Kin House and a Dhaharan raja could choke the supply of rollers.

  • The Hollow and the Still City (Eastern Realms): The Hollow is attracted to still places. A city that stops moving becomes a beacon. The Aelaerem whisper that the First City never stopped because it is running from something — and that something may be the Cracking Wind itself.

  • Tokoloshe and the Bitter Root: Daughters of the Cord sometimes capture tokoloshe and weave them into Hollowed children. The tokoloshe inside the child sings when a weight is overdue. The Ngomebe avoid Daughter-run hospices for this reason.

  • Sekogo’s Ukwe Roots: The Ukwe’s roots are said to extend beneath the southern savanna. A Ngomebe mason who dreams of roots cracking her keystone knows that a bond to the Speaking Tree has come due.

  • Aelinnel Bone-Spinners: The coastal gnomes who roll the dawn bones never explain why a failed roll sometimes sounds like a cracked keystone. The Ngomebe have learned to listen to both.

The Hook

The party arrives at the edge of a moving city — not one of the great metropolises, but a smaller Walker named Khomani’s Progress, named after the mason who carved its keystone three centuries ago. The city is dying.

Elder Morvath meets you at the sledge-track, his face gray with granite dust and exhaustion. “The city has not stopped in forty days. We cannot stop. The ancestors will not let us. But the rollers are cracking. The oxen are dying. And something is following us — something that walks in the dust and counts our breaths.”

He leads you to the keystone chamber. The great stone is warm. Too warm. A network of fine cracks spreads from its center like veins. The cracks are singing — a low, mournful hum in a language none of you recognize.

“The Ninth Roller is failing,” Morvath says. “It was carved by Khomani herself. It has never been replaced. If it fails, the city stops. If the city stops, the Hollow comes. Ancestor Appeasement

The ancestors are angry because the city’s last mason, a woman named Phendu, died without passing on the stone-song. Her ghost haunts the keystone chamber, singing the melody but forgetting the final note.

To appease the ancestors, the party must:

  1. Find Phendu’s apprentice — A one-eyed woman named Sola, now living as a Root-binder outcast. She knows the final note but refuses to sing it because the Mason-Kin exiled her.

  2. Convince Sola to return — Requires a Presence + Sway test (DV 6). On success, she returns and teaches the final note. On failure, she demands a price: the party must cut a promise to the Mason-Kin (lose one Keystone Charter or equivalent).

  3. Sing the full stone-song at the keystone — Requires a Spirit + Performance test (DV 5). On success, Ancestor Favor fills completely, and the keystone cracks heal. On failure, the ancestors are offended — Roller Integrity  − 2 immediately.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Ridge-Watcher Signals: A network of signal towers on every peak. A fire, a mirror, or a horn can send a message across the entire kingdom in a day. The Ridge-Watchers are neutral in Crown politics; they serve only the passes. A character who spends an action to light a signal fire may reduce the next weather hazard by 1 segment, but the fire also attracts mizimu (GM gains 1 SB).

  • Mchango of the Dhows (Mutual Aid): Along the coast, every captain contributes a fixed share to a rotating circle. A character who contributes to the mchango (by offering a share, repairing a dock, or telling a true story) gains a Community Favor token. Spend it to reroll a failed social roll with any coastal merchant or captain. Hoarding wealth without contributing, however, gives the GM 1 SB per session as the community closes ranks—and the mchango keeper may send a pepo to remind you.

  • Spirit Taboos (Mwiko): The old spirits have specific taboos. Breaking a taboo (counting to nine near a kichocheo’s baobab, refusing a second cup of coffee, wearing shoes in a shrine) draws their attention. The GM may then spend 2 SB to have a spirit appear and demand a story that ends with the number eight. A good story pacifies the spirit; a poor one curses the offender with Mwiko ( − 1 die on all rolls until they make an offering of honey and blood).

  • Horse Lineage as Fumbo (Riddle): Every highland horse belongs to a lineage that traces back to a legendary stallion—and each lineage has a fumbo, a riddle. Knowing a horse’s fumbo gives  + 1 die on social rolls with its owner. A character who can recite the fumbo of a horse from memory may treat the horse as a temporary ally (Cap 2) for one scene. Forging a lineage (inventing a false fumbo) is a capital crime—the Crown Council has executed three forgers in the last decade. Their ghosts guard the pass where they died, and their fumbo is unanswerable.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • Mzimu wa Kwanza (The First Spirit) – The Witness. The ancestor who remembers every oath, every betrayal, every cup of Tona refused.

    • Bwana wa Nyasi (Lord of the Grass) – The Traveler. The wind that guides the monsoon dhows and the rider lost in the high passes.

    • Shetani wa Tisa (The Spirit of Nine) – The Ninth. The presence that lives in the missing step, the uncounted wave, the breath after eight.

    • Bibi wa Kahawa (Grandmother of Coffee) – Inaea. The web of trade that binds coast to highlands, merchant to farmer, dhow to mule. Her fumbo is the answer to every broken promise.

Among the Taharka, power flows through passes, horse lineages, and the favor of the monsoon—and through the gada cycle, the eight-year rotation of political authority. For the GM: Power in Taharkan society is measured in altitude, access, and the answering of riddles. Patronage often takes the form of pass-stones, horse-bonds, or coffee cups—each tied to a specific valley, bloodline, or trade route. To emphasize the highland spirit-culture:

  • Tie rewards to physical tokens (carved stone, braided hair, clay cups) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided—and that may carry a fumbo of their own.

  • Let rival lords issue conflicting pass-rights, forcing players to choose whose path to follow—and whose mzimu to offend.

  • Use the rock-chapels, horse cemeteries, and monsoon wharves as arenas for social contests, where hospitality is law, a cup of coffee is a bond, and a pepo may be listening.

  • Introduce spirits as recurring obstacles—they are not enemies but old powers bound to balance, asking for stories rather than silver, and always asking: “Did you count to eight before you spoke?”

Quick Reference

Tagline: Small hands, old obligations. The hill remembers every footstep.

Genre / Mood: Pastoral highland folk, hospitality traps, mist-veiled pygmy clans.

Who They Are.

In the high passes of southern Taharka, where the air thins and the coffee terraces climb into the clouds, live the Aelaerem of the Southern Hills—a pygmy halfling people who were ancient in these mountains before the first Taharkan king raised his banner. They keep to the upper slopes, tending terraced groves of wild coffee, shepherding shaggy mountain goats, and weaving red thread through the antlers of the passes. Outsiders call them hill-kin or cloud-tenders; they call themselves the remembered, because every stone on every cairn has a name, and they are the ones who still speak them.

The Aelaerem have no king, no capital. Their courts are the circle of standing stones at a mountain spring; their law is the counting of cups and the tying of knots. They trade coffee, mountain herbs, and old tales with Taharkan caravans, but they do not trust the lowlands—and the lowlands have learned to respect the ninth cup.

What you notice first:

The smell of roasting coffee and damp moss. The way every threshold of their felt and stone huts has a red thread tied at eye level. The silence when a stranger approaches—and the sudden song when a child decides you are welcome.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never refuse a second cup of coffee. The first is welcome; the second is a test of honor; the third is a bond. Refuse the second, and you have insulted the house—and the house’s ancestors, who listen through the floorboards. And never, ever pour the ninth cup.

The Ninth Cup in the Highlands.

Among the Taharkan Aelaerem, the rite of the ninth cup is subtle: at any feast or negotiation, eight cups are poured. The ninth cup is left empty on the table, facing the door. It is for the Hollow—the presence that walks the high passes when the mist rolls down. If a stranger pours the ninth cup, the Hollow will drink, and the stranger will carry a burden. If the stranger drinks the ninth cup themselves, they have invited the Hollow to sit beside them. The Aelaerem will not intervene. The Hollow is patient.

Coffee & Knots.

Aelaerem coffee is famous across Taharka, but it is never sold for coin. It is bartered for stories, for promises, for red thread that will be tied around the wrist of one who carries an open promise. A bag of highland beans comes with a knot—a promise recorded in wool. The knot is cut when the promise is kept. Until then, the one bound can feel it tighten when they sleep.

Adventure Seed: The Ninth Cairn’s Promise

A Stone-Speaker has stopped speaking. The ninth cairn is weeping water that tastes of salt and old blood. The Coffee Wife says a promise was called in—a pledge made by a Taharkan prince to an Aelaerem chieftain, three generations ago. The prince’s descendant is in the party’s caravan, traveling in disguise. The Hollow walks the passes. The party must either:

  • Find the original promise (hidden in a cairn under a stone that has not been moved in a century—and guarded by a kichocheo that demands a story ending with eight).

  • Offer the weight in memory (a cherished recollection of a kindness, given to the Hollow at the ninth cairn—and the Hollow will also take the memory of your face).

  • Or convince the Aelaerem to forgive the promise—which requires a story that moves the stones themselves, and a cup of Baraka shared with the mizimu.

The reward is the Coffee Wife’s blessing: a single red thread that can tie any two willing parties into a binding promise, enforceable by the mountain itself—and a fumbo that answers itself.

Sekogo

The Green Council.

The jungle has an immune system. When a fever rises, we cut it out. When an oath is broken, we collect the price. We are not cruel. We are necessary. The tree remembers every unpaid debt, and we are its teeth.

I am a Honey-Gatherer. The bees know my name. They do not sting me because I sing to them the song of the first blossom. My grandmother taught me that the honey is not a gift; it is a loan. One day, the grove will ask for it back.

Sekogo lies between the Sahel grasslands and the southern plateau—a dry savannah that grows wetter as you travel east, until the baobabs give way to deep jungle and the air turns thick with the smell of damp earth and overripe fruit. Here, the Green Council rules through consensus, honey-orchards are sacred, and the old spirits still walk. Slavery is forbidden; the jungle itself would rise against any slaver who crossed its border. But here’s the secret the Council doesn’t want you to know: the jungle has a favorite. It is not the Conservators who protect the old pacts. It is not the Wakers who want to rouse the jungle with fever-mists and bloodier rites. It is the Ukwe—the Speaking Tree—that decides whose debts are collected and whose are forgiven. And the Ukwe does not answer to the Council.

The Leopard Society hunts those who break the deepest taboos. The Earth Society keeps the Calendar of Debt, a sacred list written on bark and buried at the Ukwe’s roots. The nganga work with minkisi—power objects inhabited by spirits—hammering nails into carved figures to release magic. Each nail is a new debt to the spirit within. Trickster spirits—children of Ananse, the spider-trickster—weave illusions and swap debts for laughter. And in the oldest groves, the Ninki Nanka—a serpent with a mirror-back—slithers between roots, its reflection showing the lies you have told.

— Hunter Okoro, Leopard Society mask-wearer, who never removes his mask

What do people eat for breakfast? Mashed yams and groundnut stew—the yams are grown in clearings that shift with the seasons, the groundnuts are harvested by monkey-tribes who speak the old tongue. The monkeys are not paid. The monkeys are remembered. Memory is currency.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To name the spirits. Sekogo children are taught the eight spirits of the grove: River, Tree, Stone, Rain, Fire, Wind, Honey, and the ninth—the spirit that has no name. The ninth spirit is the Ukwe’s shadow. The shadow watches. The shadow never forgives.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their mask. Every Sekogo wears a mask—carved wood, painted with the symbols of their lineage. A stranger whose mask is cracked has broken a taboo. A stranger whose mask is blank has no lineage. A stranger with no mask is not a person. The Leopard Society hunts the maskless.

What is the most common crime? Debt-forgetting—but debt-forgetting is not a crime, it is a sickness. The Ukwe keeps the Calendar of Debt. A name that is forgotten is a debt that is unpaid. The unpaid debt grows roots. The roots strangle the debtor’s house. The house collapses. The jungle reclaims it.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Green Council does not rule. The Ukwe rules. The Council is the Ukwe’s voice—eight speakers chosen by the tree’s roots. The ninth seat is empty. The ninth speaker was the tree itself. The tree does not speak. The tree waits.

Where do people go when they die? To the Ukwe’s roots. The dead are not buried. Their bodies are laid at the base of the Speaking Tree. The roots absorb them. The tree grows a new leaf for each soul. The leaves whisper the names of the dead. The whisper is the wind.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You hammer a nail into a nkisi—a power figure carved by the nganga. The nail represents your wrong. The spirit inside the nkisi accepts your apology. The nail rusts. When the nail is gone, your debt is paid. The nail never rusts.

What is the most important festival? The Honey Harvest (autumn). The bees are sung to sleep. The hives are opened. The honey is collected in copper bowls. The first bowl is offered to the Ukwe. The tree drinks. The tree does not thank.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Ukwe is not a tree. It is a prison—the binding of a spirit that tried to consume the jungle nine centuries ago. The spirit is still alive. The tree is its cage. The roots are its chains. The Green Council is its jailers. The jailers have forgotten what they guard.

Quick Reference

Tagline: The jungle remembers every promise. The mask is the law. The honey is a bond.

Leopard Society Hunter (Elite): “The jungle has a memory. When a fever rises, we cut it out. When an oath is broken, we restore the balance. We are not cruel. We are necessary. The tree remembers every deed, and we are its teeth.”

Honey-Gatherer (Commoner): “The bees know my name. They do not sting me because I sing to them the song of the first blossom. My grandmother taught me that the honey is not a gift; it is a trust. One day, the grove will need something from you, and you will remember the taste.”

Genre / Mood: Jungle folk horror, spirit diplomacy, mask-law.

Starting Location: A honey-orchard at the jungle’s edge, where the trees hum and the air is thick with bees. A masked figure in a leopard pelt steps from the shadows: “The Ukwe has asked for a name. A caravan master broke his oath to the grove. Find him before the roots do — or become his witness.”

Sekogo lies between the Sahel grasslands and the southern plateau — a dry savannah that grows wetter as you travel east, until the baobabs give way to deep jungle and the air turns thick with the smell of damp earth and overripe fruit. Here, the Green Council rules through consensus, honey-orchards are sacred, and the old spirits still walk. Slavery is forbidden; the jungle itself would rise against any slaver who crossed its border. But the Council is divided: Conservators want to protect the old pacts, while Wakers argue that the jungle has grown sluggish and needs to be roused with fever-mists, thorn-spirits, and older, bloodier rites. At the eastern edge lies the Crimson Valley, home to the Lethai-ar — chestnut-brown elves whose silk bridges span red canyons and whose web-law keeps both empires from using the valley as a shortcut to war.

Neighbors and the Unclaimed Ground:

  • North – Ameria and the Neutral Kingdoms: Beyond Sekogo’s northern savannah lies a patchwork of small, independent kingdoms — buffer states that pay tribute to neither Sekogo nor Kahfagia. Their courts are weak, their laws a creole of jungle custom and coastal tariff. Smugglers and exiles thrive there. The Leopard Society occasionally hunts across the border, but always with a local guide.

  • East – The Crimson Valley (Lethai-ar): The Ukwe’s roots extend into the valley’s red stone. The Lethai-ar silk-wardens respect the Speaking Tree, and the Green Council’s witnessed promises are sometimes recorded on a Lethai-ar cord. A bridge toll paid in Sekogo honey is honored on both sides of the gorge.

  • South and West – The Jungle’s Own: Sekogo’s borders are not lines but gradients. The deeper you go, the more the Ukwe’s roots thicken and the Leopard Society’s law becomes the only law.

What you notice first: The sound of bees — a constant, low drone that never stops, even at night. The way the trees lean toward you when you speak a secret. The smell of honey and rotting fruit, sweet and sharp at once.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never cut a marked tree. Each such tree is a living witness. The bark grows over the names of those who have broken harmony, but the wood remembers. Cut it, and the Ukwe will add your name to its memory.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Root

Power here is measured in witnessed promises, honey tithes, and the balance between jungle and council. The Leopard Society hunts those who break the deepest taboos — oath-breakers, slavers, outsiders who take more than they give. The Earth Society keeps the Calendar of Witness, a sacred list written on bark and buried at the Ukwe’s roots, preserving memories that must not be forgotten.

The spirits of Sekogo are consolidated into three kinds for play: Ancestral (bound to groves, speak in dead voices), Nature (guardians like crocodiles or baobabs, show rather than speak), and Vengeant (born from blood-promises, hunt until answered). The Hollow manifests here as the Mwitu, the forest that forgets where the village ends — and that forgetfulness is a hunger.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Three Societies: Sekogo is governed by three overlapping authorities: the Leopard Society (hunters who restore balance), the Earth Society (elders who keep the Calendar of Witness), and the nganga (spirit-doctors who work with minkisi). A single elder often belongs to two societies, and the tension between their roles is where drama lives.

  • The Ukwe (Speaking Tree): A living witness whose roots extend across Sekogo. Every broken promise, every forgotten harmony stains its leaves. It answers questions — but never with kindness, and never for free. Accepting its gift creates a reciprocal bond that the tree may call upon at any moment. The ninth root is never still — it searches for the name that was erased.

  • The Crimson Valley and Web-Law: The Lethai-ar guard the valley with fierce neutrality. Their skin-tattoos (Marks) record promises witnessed. When a Lethai-ar dies, their Marks are cut from the skin and buried at the Ukwe’s roots. The web-law is unwritten; each warden carries a different clause in memory. The ninth clause is always a secret known only to the First Weaver’s ghost.

  • Mask Law: In Sekogo, a mask is not a disguise — it is a role. Wearing the mask of a Leopard Society hunter, a Waker, or a spirit entitles you to speak for that office, but also binds you to its taboos. Breaking a mask’s taboo (e.g., a Leopard mask showing mercy to a slaver) causes the mask to crack and the wearer to lose 1 die on all social rolls until they atone. The ninth mask is the Hollow’s own — it has no face, and it never cracks because it was never whole.

  • Bark Promise-Cords (Mukanda): Promises are recorded on strips of bark called mukanda. Each strip is knotted; the knot tightens when the promise is strained, loosens when it is kept. A mukanda that rots means the bond is released; one that hardens into wood means the obligation has set. The ninth knot on a mukanda is never tied — it is left loose for the Hollow to judge.

  • Ananse’s Bargain (Trickster Mechanic): Once per session, a character may call upon Ananse’s Child to “renegotiate” any promise, oath, or contract. Roll Spirit + Lore (DV 4). On a success, the terms shift in your favor — but the GM gains 1 SB and may add a hidden clause that will trigger at the worst possible moment. On a miss, the burden doubles — and the ninth clause added will be the Hollow’s own terms.

  • The Mwitu (Hollow of the Forest): When the Ninth manifests in Sekogo, it is as the Mwitu — the forest that forgets where the village ends. It appears as a wall of gray mist between the trees, and it whispers: “Why are you still here?” Those who cannot answer lose their sense of direction (all navigation rolls at  − 1 die for one scene). The Mwitu can be appeased by leaving a mukanda at the edge of the mist — a promise to remember a forgotten name.

The Crimson Valley

Silk Bridges, Web-Law, and the Forest Above.

The web-law is not written down. It is woven — into every bridge, every strand, every knot. Make a promise here and the valley holds it exactly as you made it. Not more kindly. Not less.

I am a Mark-Cutter. Every line on my skin is something I witnessed. When I die they will cut the marks out of me and bury them at the Ukwe’s roots, and the tree will hold what I held. That is all that happens. People from outside always ask me what happens after, and I have stopped knowing how to explain that the question does not mean anything here.

The Crimson Valley is a jungle rift between Sekogo and Oshiira, a wound of red stone cut by a river that runs rust-colored after rain. Silk bridges span the gorges, spun from spider-sap and oath-hair, strong enough for a caravan and light enough to move in the monsoon. The Lethai-ar hold the valley floor and the bridges, and they hold them with a neutrality that outsiders consistently mistake for friendliness.

Understand the one principle and you will understand everything else: you get back what you put in. Exactly. A kindness is returned to the weight. An insult is returned to the weight. Shelter given is shelter owed and shelter repaid, and then nothing — no warmth left over, no grudge either, the strand cut clean and both parties standing where they started. The Lethai-ar are not generous and are not cruel, and both of those absences frighten visitors more than a threat would. There is no appealing to their better nature. It is not withheld. It is simply not a thing they have agreed to have.

Their skin-marks are the record: read publicly, by anyone, under the crimson moon. A Lethai-ar carrying a mark they cannot answer loses standing. A warden who cuts a bridge without cause is put out of the valley, and being put out is understood by everyone concerned to be the end of that person, though nobody says so and nobody stops them walking.

Never step onto a silk bridge without first naming a witness. The bridge holds what it was told to hold. If you go down, it will want to know who was supposed to be watching, and it will be a long time before it stops asking.

— Silk-Warden Anara, marked from wrist to shoulder, who has never once been owed anything

The Two Answers

A Lethai-ar swears once, in adulthood, and the oath answers back. You choose the oath; the oath chooses what you are. This is the valley’s own principle turned on its owners — you get back what you put in, including what you put into a promise — and no outsider has ever argued them out of it, chiefly because it keeps happening in front of witnesses.

Those who swear into something become Woven. They take after the canyon’s social spiders: hundreds to a web, no head to it, brood tended in common, no strand held above another. Woven are communal the way a hand is five fingers, not out of virtue but out of build. They feed each other’s children without being asked and without keeping count, because counting would imply something owed, and nothing is owed inside a web. They also know what the spiders know — that a web has a term, that colonies reach their size and come apart, and that every generation stands and watches one go. They weave anyway, and will tell you without much sadness that the coming apart is not the failure of the thing but the last part of it, and that a web which refused to end would have to start taking from its own.

Those who swear away become Shed: long-boned, cool-blooded, patient past the point where patience is a virtue. They can put on a face, and it is not a disguise but a growth; it takes months and it holds. And they shed, and not skin only — a Shed coming out of a shedding does not have the previous decade the way other people have decades. It is why they work so well in Kahfagia, and why the valley does not call them traitors: a Shed who has lost every memory of the errand goes on running it, because the oath is in the body and not the recollection. The Woven see them off with the rite they use for the dead, and mean by it what they mean at a funeral.

The tell is not what outsiders expect. A Shed cannot return a kindness at the weight — the severance took that — so every Shed learns a counterfeit return, practiced, smooth, and always a fraction generous. Old Kahfagian houses still test for it: offer a guest some small unearned courtesy and watch what comes back. A human over- or underpays and thinks nothing of it. A Woven returns it to the gram and is puzzled at being watched. A Shed returns it slightly too well, every time, and cannot stop, because the alternative is returning nothing at all.

There are no snakes in Theona. No Shed has ever worked there, none has been able to say why, and the Woven will not speculate in front of outsiders.

The Forest Above

The valley floor is Lethai-ar. The forest on the rim is not, and the difference is the most important fact in the region.

The Lethai-al hold the upper forest and do not admit anyone into it. This is not shyness, ignorance, or hostility in the way outsiders keep wanting it to be. It is a policy, arrived at by argument, minuted, and enforced with great competence by people who have thought about it for longer than Oshiira has existed. The boundary is marked — clearly, in a script that any Sekogo trader can read and most can read at a distance — and the marks are maintained. There is a procedure for requesting entry. It is genuine. It has been used successfully four times in nine hundred years, and the Lethai-al can tell you the names.

Cross without leave and you will be warned once, in your own language, by someone you did not see arrive and will not see leave. You will not be warned twice. What follows is not frenzy; it is fieldcraft, executed by foresters who know the ground and have no intention of meeting you in the open. Parties come back short and cannot say what happened, which is itself deliberate — the Lethai-al would much rather you left than died, and a survivor who cannot describe anything is worth more to them than a corpse.

Ask why, and any of them will tell you plainly, because it is not a secret. Every other Lethai people had a past and does not have it now. The Lethai-al watched that happen from the trees, more than once, and drew the conclusion that the people who kept anything were the people who let nobody in. They are not confident this is right. They are confident it is working.

Northward, where the valley rim meets the Oshiiran plateau, the forest is watched from the other side. The Spirit Wrestlers keep stone monasteries along the edge and walk the line in pairs, and the Lethai-al watch them do it and log each pass. What stands between the two is a mile of ground that Oshiira says is plateau and the Lethai-al say is forest, and the disagreement is conducted as a formal disputation — convened each dry season at a marked stone, argued in a third language so that neither side has the run of the idiom, recorded by both. No session has ever been won and neither delegation would know what to do if one were. People still do not come back from that mile. Oshiira enters those as misadventure and the Lethai-al enter them as a boundary matter concluded, and each sends the other a copy, and this has held four centuries, which is longer than either has managed with anything else they have tried.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge and fermented tea, the rice off terraces cut into the canyon wall, the tea aged in clay under the bridges. It is bitter, and the bitterness is the point — a valley proverb holds that anything sweet you did not work for is somebody else’s.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read a mark. There are eight shapes — Promise, Given, Witness, Returned, Refused, Cut, Released — and the ninth, which is the smooth place where a mark was taken out. Children are taught the eighth and ninth in the same lesson, and told the difference matters more than anything else they will learn.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their marks, and specifically whether anything is outstanding. A stranger carrying nothing unanswered is free to deal with. A stranger carrying something old is not refused — they are simply dealt with at that weight.

What is the most common crime? Cutting a bridge. It is treated as an act of war rather than theft, because a bridge is a promise between two shores and the shores did not agree to it ending. The wardens’ response is proportionate and immediate, and proportionate here means precisely proportionate.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? That the valley is shaped like something. The Lethai-ar do not discuss it with outsiders and are perfectly willing to discuss it with each other, at length, in the way people discuss weather they cannot change.

Where do people go when they die? Downriver, wrapped in silk, into the Ukwe’s reach. The tree takes them and keeps what they were holding. Nobody in the valley finds this melancholy and visitors reliably do, which the Lethai-ar find interesting and occasionally raise as a conversational topic.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You cut the mark out of yourself and offer it. Accepted, it passes to them and the matter is closed at that weight. Refused, it is buried, and a buried mark is not vengeance — it is a thing that stays exactly as heavy as it was, forever, and everyone can see where it is.

What is the most important festival? The Weaving, in spring: silk harvested from the canyon spiders, thread spun by the wardens, a whole bridge woven in one night. The bridge is named for the year and the year is named for a promise, and the promise offered is always the same one, which the wardens will not translate for visitors.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? That the river’s rust is not iron, that it has been running that color for nine centuries, and that the wardens have known the source the entire time and have made a deliberate collective decision to leave it alone. It is not ignorance and it is not cowardice. Several generations of them have argued it out and voted, and the vote is recorded, and anyone can read it.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Web-Law: The unwritten legal code of the Crimson Valley. Each warden carries a different clause in memory; together, they form a complete web. To cross a bridge, a traveler must answer a question about the web-law. The answer changes with each warden. On a successful roll (Wits + Lore, DV 3), you may bypass the challenge; on a failure, you must offer a promise instead – and the spider will witness it.

  • Marks (Skin-Tattoos): Each Lethai-ar’s Marks are a public record of promises witnessed. The Marks are cut from the skin after death and buried at the Ukwe’s roots. To read a living Lethai-ar’s Marks, one must know the phase of the crimson moon and the position of the valley’s spirits. A character who studies a Lethai-ar’s Marks for a scene may ask the GM one question about a promise that Lethai-ar has witnessed; the GM must answer truthfully – but the spider may also listen.

  • Spirit Ancestry: Every Lethai-ar can name the specific spirit (mountain, river, grove) where their ancestor left the spirit world to become mortal. That spirit is their witness; they owe it a promise at birth, and that promise is never fully paid. Once per journey, a Lethai-ar may invoke their spirit ancestor to gain +1 die on a roll related to that spirit’s domain (e.g., a river ancestor aids a crossing). The spider notes the favor.

  • Witness Economy: Instead of tracking debt, the Crimson Valley uses Witness as currency. A character who witnesses a promise gains 1 Witness token. Tokens can be spent to: gain +1 die on a roll involving that promise, force an oath-breaker to reroll a successful deception, or demand a favor from someone who witnessed the same promise. Witness tokens are lost when the promise is fulfilled or broken – and the spider watches the exchange.

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • The First Weaver (Inaea): The spider who taught the first knot; mercy is a strand, not a cut. Her web is the valley’s memory.

    • The Serpent Judge (Isoka): The fang that decides; a promise kept is a skin shed. She watches from the river stones.

    • The Crimson Witness (The Witness): The flame that sees through silk; no oath hidden from its light. The bridge glows when it passes.

    • The Bridge Father (The Traveler): The road that crosses the gorge; every crossing is a promise. He walks the strands at dusk.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • Crimson Silk and the Way of Silk: The silk of the Crimson Valley is prized across the continent for vow-cords, well-contracts, and bridge-building. The Tkanki spider-traders are the primary merchants; a single bolt can buy safe passage from Fhara to Dhahara. A Tkanki story-seller may accept crimson silk as payment for a truth – but the spider will ask for a copy.

  • The Ukwe’s Roots and Sekogo: The Ukwe’s roots extend into the Crimson Valley. The Lethai-ar bury their dead Marks at the riverbank root, creating a unique relationship with the Speaking Tree. The Ukwe knows every promise ever made in the valley. A Sekogo druid may ask the Ukwe to witness a new oath, binding it with the weight of the valley – and the spider’s thread.

  • Web-Law and the Covenant (Mykkiel): The Covenant judges respect the web-law as a valid legal system. A Lethai-ar truth-cord is admissible in a Covenant court. Some judges have learned to read Marks. A Covenant justiciar may offer to translate a web-law dispute into imperial law – but the translation always favors the spider.

  • The Unmarked and the Hollow (Eastern Realms): Lethai-ar who refuse all Marks become invisible to the web-law – and to the Hollow. The Hollow cannot track them. Some Aelaerem seek out the Unmarked to learn the secret of blank skin. The Unmarked charge a promise: “Do not seek me again.” The spider does not visit them.

Among the Lethai-ar, power flows through witnessed promises, silk strands, and the authority of the wardens. The web-law is unwritten, but every warden carries a piece of it. Marks are public, stories are visible, and the Ukwe remembers everything. The spider – Inaea – does not rule, but she watches. Her threads are the bonds between families, the knots that hold the valley together, and the silent witness to every oath.

For the GM: Power in the Crimson Valley is measured in witnessed oaths and the trust of the bridge. Patronage often takes the form of truth-cords, masks, or root shards – each tied to a specific promise. To emphasize this:

  • Tie rewards to objects that record or enforce promises (cords, masks, shards) that can be challenged, broken, or called in.

  • Let rival wardens issue conflicting interpretations of the web-law, forcing players to choose whose bridge to cross.

  • Use the bridges, Mark-cutter’s studio, and the Ukwe’s root as arenas for social contests, where a witnessed promise is a weapon and a broken strand is a death sentence.

  • Remember that the spider is not a god – she is a presence. She does not speak, but the valley hums with her attention. When a promise is broken, the threads tremble.

In the Crimson Valley, the bridge remembers every step – and the spider forgets no promise.
