For the GM: possible readings and ways to bring a fragment into play

In the compiler’s margins

These notes are outside the fragment. They offer ways to make a passage matter at the table; they do not decode it, resolve its contradictions, or establish a single account of the world. Give players the fragment or an excerpt without this commentary. Let its images recur before anyone offers an explanation.

The Edgewalker: I cannot certify the age of this copy. I can tell you that its omissions resemble practices recorded far apart. I would carry one passage to a keeper who had never read it and ask what work it describes. I would not supply my answer first.

This is a document the characters can encounter: a copied passage, a keeper’s gloss, or a disputed translation. Its images place the Hollow, the Ninth, and the costs of survival beside one another without making their relationship certain. Treat that account as something to investigate. Preserve established setting facts, but distinguish what the GM knows from what a witness or this text can demonstrate.

The Ledger beside the Compass

Use the fragment as a source of questions for cartomancy: a Place preserves a pattern; an Actor keeps, forgets, or contests it; Pressure threatens its continuation; Leverage permits a different response. This is an interpretive connection to the existing Compass, not a claim that Sarnai invented every regional tradition.

To prepare a scene, choose one passage below, then use the regional generator’s existing draw procedure and rank conversion. Connect the results to a local problem. State Position and Effect before a roll and use the relevant regional rules. Reading the Ledger does not itself generate SB, impose an obligation, or create a new magic ability.

A fragment at the table

  1. Give a usable clue. Show a short passage with a recognizable detail and a place or person the party can seek. Do not require them to solve the typography to find the adventure.
  2. Prepare independent evidence. Decide what can be learned from the place, a witness, and an object or record. Make essential access available through more than one approach.
  3. Ask what the party will do. A correct reading may reveal an option; it does not select that option for them.
  4. Keep three clocks at most. If needed, use a Situation clock for their effort, a Counter-Clock for its immediate danger, and one relevant Campaign front. Interpretation itself is not a reason to tick them.
  5. Return to the passage. After the outcome, let a marginal note acquire a different meaning. Reward investigation with usable knowledge, access, or an established reward; do not require a new currency.

Patterns survive their makers

An image to follow: The fragment describes refugees preserving patterns after the Crossing consumed their machines.

A possible reading: A repeated layout, route, or ritual can survive after its purpose is forgotten.

Use it: Give the party two examples and one difference they can inspect. A missing drainage channel might explain the failing wall; a corresponding omission in a ritual may remain unresolved.

The missing place as a seal

An image to follow: The missing step, unpoured cup, and unnamed name recur beside the language of a seal.

A possible reading: An absence may be deliberate maintenance rather than ignorance or error.

Use it: Put the absence in a physical place and name someone who preserves it. Offer a way to investigate before completing it. Use established local effects if the party interferes, not an automatic penalty for reading the passage.

Memory has a keeper

An image to follow: The fragment describes forgetting as protection and remembrance as a burden someone must carry.

A possible reading: Ask who benefits from forgetting, who is harmed, and who still knows.

Use it: Provide a witness, an object, and an independent record with overlapping details. A successful investigation reveals a useful fact; it need not certify the whole fragment.

What was displaced returns

An image to follow: The fragment speaks of the Hollow through accumulated pressure and the costs carried across the Crossing.

A possible reading: A benefit can move its cost onto someone out of sight. Obligation is one expression; neglected work, loss, and denied belonging can express it too.

Use it: Trace a visible problem to a human decision the party can affect. Repairing that harm matters even if no one can prove what the Hollow is. Keep existing rules for obligations and SB; the metaphor adds no universal charge.

Containment requires continuation

An image to follow: The fragment depicts a condition kept unfinished rather than a wall that can be built and forgotten.

A possible reading: A custom may preserve a function through ordinary repeated work.

Use it: Identify the keeper, the substitute, and the warning of failure. Let competent maintenance work. Where a local procedure calls for a clock, state its trigger and completion before advancing it.

Was it worth it?

An image to follow: The fragment ends with a question about the cost of survival.

A possible reading: A character can refuse a useful opportunity without receiving a secret compensating prize.

Use it: Offer an attainable benefit and an intelligible cost. Ask what the party wants to preserve. Resolve their chosen action through the normal rules; do not require the philosophically correct answer.

Omens without an answer key

The fragment’s spider, snake, and raven connect it to the setting’s Omens. A corresponding trace can draw attention to a scene: a cord repaired in a different weave, a shed skin where a seal stood, a bird repeating a witness’s cadence. The resemblance is evidence of a possible connection, not a command to reveal a divine agent. Apply named regional Omen and Ace effects as printed. A recurring image alone supplies no additional mechanical effect.

Worked scene: the unpoured cup

A copied line mentions a cup left unpoured. At a roadside well, a shelf has nine places and eight cups. The keeper says the empty place belongs to a household that left after a drought. An old water-share names that household; a repaired channel leads toward its abandoned garden. Those are three leads to a question: was its share withdrawn, preserved, or quietly diverted?

Decide the local facts before play. The party might find the descendants, inspect the channel, or seek witnesses to the allocation. Ordinary access to a public record needs no test. A dangerous descent or contested hearing uses the normal procedure. If the work becomes an extended challenge, Restore access [6] can face The channel dries [4]; an existing regional water front is the only possible third clock. Establish actual triggers. Restoring a fair share can solve the local problem without proving that the shelf helps contain the Hollow.

Mira: “Ask what they called the people who left. A household is more than its place on the shelf.” Ume: “And see whether the repaired channel can be kept clear.” Naru: “You may learn why they left and decide not to bring them back.” The Edgewalker: “All useful. I would secure their right to choose before announcing that we had found them.”
