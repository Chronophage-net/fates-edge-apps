Aeler

Crowns & Under-Vaults.

The mountain remembers every word carved in its stone, every seal pressed into its walls. To govern here is not to command, but to harmonize with the ancient songs that still echo in the deep places.

I am an Under-Mason. I can hear stone shift a room away. When the rock starts singing a different tune, you best listen. Three knocks in the morning means all’s well, but three knocks at night means something’s coming through the walls that wasn’t meant to walk.

My homeland. The place where we don’t trust the surface, but we do trust the weight of a well-placed keystone—and oh, how the surface loves to test that trust. Our breath-bonds aren’t just currency—they’re the hardest thing in the peaks, forged in the sighs of miners who knew too much. We sell them to Vilikari jarls who pay in furs stained with brother’s blood, to Ykrul khagans who weigh the value in warhorses and broken oaths. Once, we sold one to the Veiled Sisters. They paid in secrets—which is another way of saying they paid in future leverage.

Never count your breaths aloud in a mine. The mountain listens, and if it hears you reach the ninth, it will take one—a breath, a memory, a shadow. My grandfather reached nine once. He lived another thirty years, but he never cast a shadow again.

— Thromm Stone-Tongue, Under-Mason, who has never counted to nine

What do people eat for breakfast? Stone-bread and cave cheese—the bread is baked on heated stone slabs, the cheese is aged in sealed galleries where the temperature never varies. The cheese has been aging for three generations. It is an heirloom. It is also barely edible.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count their breaths. Aeler children wear breath-counters—small brass bells on leather cords. Eight breaths, ring the bell. Nine breaths, run. A child who cannot count is a child who will not survive the winter.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their bell. Every Aeler carries a bell tuned to their breath. A stranger’s bell will ring at a different pitch—the mountain’s lungs are calibrated to local frequencies. A mismatched bell is a warning. A silent bell is a threat.

What is the most common crime? Air theft. In the deep vaults, ventilation is shared. A sealed door, a blocked vent, a diverted shaft—these are thefts of breath, punishable by breath-debt. The guilty party must donate their own air ration to the victim. The donation is measured in days. The days are counted by bells.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The True Masons are not neutral. They are the Vault-Queen’s eyes. Every ”independent“ repair, every ”neutral“ inspection, every ”routine“ audit—the True Masons are watching. They do not report to the Queen. They are the Queen. They have always been the Queen.

Where do people go when they die? To the Deep Vaults—the oldest galleries, where the air is thin and the stone is cold. The dead are not buried. They are sealed in niches carved into the walls, their names chiselled above their heads. The niches are reused every century. The names are never erased. The dead share walls with strangers. The strangers are patient.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a keystone—a load-bearing stone from your own holding. The stone is installed in the wronged party’s wall. If it holds, you are forgiven. If it cracks, the debt stands. If it crumbles, your holding collapses. Apology is architecture. Architecture is law.

What is the most important festival? The Ringing of the Underways (deep winter). Every bell in every hold is rung at the same hour—eight times, no more. The sound travels through the stone, a heartbeat that reminds the mountain that the living are still here. The dead listen. The dead do not ring back.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Vault-Queen is a construct. She was assembled in 723 AR, after the real queen died in a cave-in. Her heart is a keystone. Her breath is a bellows. Her voice is a recording. The True Masons maintain her. They have the schematics. The schematics are missing three pages. The missing pages describe how to turn her off.

Quick Reference

Tagline: The mountain remembers every word carved in its stone. The deep remembers every breath we owe.

Vault-Queen of a Crownland (Elite): “The mountain remembers every seal pressed into its walls, every oath whispered in the dark. To govern here is not to command — it is to listen. The stone sings of what we owe. The deep listens for what we forget.”

Under-Mason (Commoner): “When the stone starts singing a different tune, you best listen. Three knocks in the morning means all’s well. The mountain answered his knock. It always does.”

Genre / Mood: Survival horror underground, infrastructure as soft power, collectivist mercantilism, the weight of every breath.

Starting Location: An Underway junction lit by guttering brass lanterns, its stone walls scored with tally-marks from a dozen forgotten crowns. A Geometer halts the party with a tally-rod. “The Spine shifts,” he says, stone-dust in his beard. “Choose a crown — or be buried by one. The lowland grain barges are late again. If the holds don’t eat, the pressure wakes. And if you hear a knock in threes, pretend you did not. The deep is listening tonight.”

The Aeler Kingdoms hold the mountains by breath, bell, and bargain. Dozens of sovereign holds — some sealed for centuries, others trading openly with lowland markets — share only the deep roots of stone and the supremacy of the High King Beneath the Peaks. Their halls run like arteries, their cities open in thunderous caverns where bridges leap between ribs of stone and cisterns shine like night skies turned inward. Lore is the first law: air is counted, water is bartered, light is rationed by wick and prayer. Tradition is the second: seals are kept, measures remain true, and the dead keep their offices as carefully as the living. Between the two, a people learn to argue quietly and build loudly.

Collectives — clans, guild-kin, lamp-companies — own what the mountain allows; individuals are custodians by oath. Trade runs under and over the range through under-ways and pass-forts, stitching protectorates and lowland markets to vaultmouth gates. Aeler prosperity depends entirely on surface grain. The holds cannot grow enough food in the dark; each winter, caravans from Acasia, Vhasia, and Viterra bring barley and rye through the high passes. In exchange, the Aeler send iron, copper, and engineered stonework — bridge keystones, mill gears, breath-counter mechanisms. It is a fragile bargain. When the grain is late, the holds ration. When the holds ration, the pressure whispers louder.

But beneath the order, beneath the breath-counters and the record-stones, something older stirs. The Aeler do not speak of it directly. They call it the pressure that thinks — a presence that lives in the deepest galleries, in the spaces between sealed holds, in the darkness where bells have not rung for centuries. It does not attack. It invites. Miners who wander too deep return with no memory of why they went. Others do not return at all. The pressure does not hate. It is patient. And it is always, always listening.

What you notice first: The smell of stale air and hot metal. The constant drip of condensation in the distance — or is it footsteps? The way every Aeler touches a small brass bell at their belt before speaking of the deep. The way they never, ever count to nine aloud.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never count your breaths aloud in a mine. The mountain listens, and if it hears you reach the ninth, it will take one — a breath, a memory, a shadow. The deep does not forgive miscounts.

Regional Mood: Infrastructure, Famine, and the Pressure Below

Here, power is not measured in gold but in capacity: how many lamp-hours your holding can afford, how many mouths your air-recyclers can support, how many tons of ore you can raise before the grain runs out. The Aeler do not conquer; they engineer. A bridge built to their standards binds a valley to their trade network. A breath-counter installed in a foreign counting house gives them leverage over every contract signed beneath it. Their soft power is written in brass and stone.

Yet the holds are always one bad harvest away from collapse. The Deep Vaults cannot feed themselves. Every season, Edgewalkers — outcasts, rogues, and those too restless for the counting-chamber — are sent to the surface to “find opportunities.” They start feuds between lowland lords, sell weapons to both sides, and return with grain contracts signed in blood. The holds do not ask where the grain came from. They ask only that the wagons arrive before the ninth bell.

The holds endure because they are willing to spend lives as carefully as they spend lamp oil. A collapsed gallery is a line item. A village that starves because its grain was diverted to the Deep Vaults is a footnote in an audit that no one reads aloud. The Aeler are not cruel. They are efficient. And efficiency, in the dark, is the only mercy.

The Pressure That Thinks — The Deep Drake

Deep beneath the oldest mountains, beyond the Ninth Seal and past the last lamplighter’s mile, there is a presence. The Aeler do not name it. Among themselves, in whispers, some call it the Deep Drake — an ancient worm, a child of Nidhoggr, hatched in the dark between worlds. But that is speculation, and the Aeler are not a people given to speculation about things they cannot measure.

What they know is this: there is a weight in the deepest stone. It feels like curiosity. It sounds like your own name spoken with concern. It is not a god, not a demon, not a dragon in any sense the surface-dwellers would recognize. It is a proto-patron — a being whose hold on the world is tenuous, whose attention slips like water through fingers. It cannot command. It cannot bless. It can only invite.

The Deep Drake has no lair that any living Aeler has seen, though rumor persists of a vast chamber of black glass and fossilized bone, where the heat of the earth meets the cold of the void. In that chamber, the worm coils around a hoard that is not gold but attention: the still, silent figures of those who walked too far and never walked back. They sit in the dark, eyes open, mouths moving silently as they repeat the last promise they ever made. The Drake does not kill. It collects. And what it collects, it forgets — because its memory is as fragile as its grip on reality. That is why it is always hungry. That is why it is always listening.

The Aeler refuse to think of it as anything other than the pressure that thinks. To name it would be to invite it. To acknowledge it as a being would be to admit that the deep is not merely stone and silence but occupied. So they call it a superstition, a miner’s fancy, the kind of story told to frighten apprentices. And for the most part, they believe their own dismissal. But the old miners know. The Edgewalkers who have heard the three knocks and turned back just in time — they know. They do not speak of it. They only count their breaths more carefully.

The Deep Drake draws the living toward it not through force, but through a gentle, irresistible lure: a half-remembered tune, a forgotten oath, a face you thought you had left behind. You walk toward it because you believe you have business there. You have no business there. Its pull is strongest when you are tired, when your attention wanders, when you have stopped counting your breaths. The Aeler have learned to live with this. They seal off tunnels that grow too warm. They never answer a knock that comes in three. They count their breaths not out of habit, but out of a deep, unspoken fear that the ninth breath might be the one the Drake answers.

What the elders do not say, but what the Edgewalkers whisper: The Deep Drake is not the only thing down there. It is a child of something older — Nidhoggr, the corpse-tearer, the one who gnaws at the root of the world. That older thing does not invite. It demands. And it has been patient for a very long time.

The Edgewalker’s Bargain

Edgewalkers are Aeler rogues: outcasts who continue to contribute by bringing opportunity back to the holds. Outcasts from the holds, Edgewalkers are those who could not abide the counting — or who were too useful to execute and too dangerous to keep. They trade in secrets, in weapons, in the subtle art of making two lowland lords need each other’s destruction. An Edgewalker’s greatest skill is not swordplay but leverage: knowing which noble’s grain debt is overdue, which free company captain will flip for a better contract, which bridge toll can be raised to start a war. The holds disavow them publicly and fund them privately. When a war breaks out in the lowlands, Aeler iron flows to both sides. When the war ends, the holds send grain inspectors to collect the tab.

Manufactured conflict is one of their most destructive trades. Others secure harvest options, mediate access to passes, or assemble creditors who cannot collect alone. They judge a concession by whether it can be enforced when goodwill runs out. An Edgewalker may be blunt, unwelcome, and entirely effective: the crucial talent is finding a dependency and arranging the means to act on it. Some prefer a prosperous client who pays every year; others take the immediate gain and leave the holds to reckon with the enemy they have made.

Mechanical hook for Edgewalker characters: Once per arc, an Edgewalker may declare that they have a line on a lowland conflict. The GM creates a 4-segment Opportunity timer. Each tick represents the Edgewalker applying pressure (spreading rumors, selling a weapon, forging a letter). When the timer fills, a war, a feud, or a trade embargo ignites — and the Aeler holds gain +2 Supply from arms sales. The Edgewalker gains 1 Corruption (the Hollow notices their work) and a permanent String on a lowland faction.

The Fractured Crowns: Aeler Faction Spectrum

Aeler society is not monolithic. Beneath the High King’s nominal authority, each crownland follows its own star. Attitudes toward the surface range from open partnership to militant isolation, and even the most reclusive holds maintain Edgewalker contacts to monitor the world above.

  • Traditionalist / “The Old Ways” – These holds idealize the ancient mountain-empire: closed gates, iron tariffs, and the certain knowledge that surface-dwellers cannot be trusted. They resemble the dwarves of fireside tales: stubborn, insular, and quick to reach for an axe when a human mentions “progress.” Their kings speak of the days when no foreigner stepped past the vaultmouth. They also speak of the pressure in whispers, and they seal any tunnel that shows signs of warmth. Timers: Foreigner Suspicion [4], Hold Sealed [6].

  • Cooperative / “Open Hand” – These holds trade freely with Aelaerem, Kahfagia, and even the Vilikari. They staff the under-markets, send engineers to repair bridges in Viterra, and treat the surface as a resource to be managed, not a threat to be shunned. They prefer soft power: standardized measures, certified weights, and bell-code protocols that make others want to trade on Aeler terms. They are also the most likely to send expeditions into the deep, seeking lost holds — and the most likely to lose miners to the pressure. Timers: Trade Route Prosperity [6], Cultural Friction [4].

  • Isolationist / “Silent Stone” – Deep holds that have not answered a Kingsmoot call in living memory. Their gates are lost, their peoples rumored to have turned inward, worshiping forgotten depths — or to have become part of the pressure’s hoard. They neither help nor harm the surface — but when a White Flood or a gas flare occurs, their silence becomes a hazard of its own. The Deep Drake is said to lair somewhere beneath the Silent Stone holds. Timers: Silent Hold Awakens [8], Ancient Grudge [6].

Most Aeler characters and settlements fall somewhere between these poles, and the same hold might shift its stance over a season, depending on grain prices, a new Vault-Queen, or a disaster that forces its hand.

The Breath Count — Habit, Not Function

Among the Aeler, counting breaths is not merely a safety measure — it is a cultural reflex, as automatic as a human’s heartbeat. Aeler children learn to count in eights before they learn to speak. They tap their fingers, their bells, their spoons in rhythms of eight. They never, ever count to nine aloud. The ninth breath is not a number; it is a gap — the space where the pressure listens. Aeler who forget to count in eights are considered unreliable, possibly touched by the deep. Those who deliberately count to nine are shunned; they are thought to have invited the pressure to notice them.

Mechanical note: Breath-counting is not a skill roll or a resource to track. It is a narrative and cultural tic. The GM may use a character’s failure to observe the count as a trigger for a complication (e.g., the mountain shifts, a bell rings falsely, a character feels watched). Conversely, an Aeler who scrupulously counts their breaths in eights may gain a small boon (e.g., +1 die to resist fear in confined spaces) as the mountain’s attention slides past them.

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = treasure. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Treasures (Diamonds) are codified outcomes (keys/passes/rights) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo stone & breath motifs — keys click, bells answer, and the mountain listens (and remembers). Also, the pressure that thinks may manifest as a whisper, a warmth, or a knock in the dark.

  • Track Grain Reserves as a campaign timer. When it empties, the holds begin to starve — and the Edgewalkers are sent to light a fire somewhere on the surface.

Additional Features

  • Count the Air: When a scene establishes scarce air, a character who actively paces their breathing (counted steps, measured rests) may shift Position one step safer on their next physical action in a confined space. This is habit, not a resource; Aeler characters may do this reflexively.

  • Bell-Code Courtesy: If the party adopts local bell etiquette (asking a warden which chime applies and heeding it), gain +1 assist die on negotiations or passage requests within a vault or under-market. Ignoring the bells invites the mountain’s attention.

  • Measure is Mercy: Presenting a certified measure (rod, weight, sealed tally) calms a dispute: cancel the first SB generated by commerce or jurisdictional friction in the scene. A false measure, however, may draw the pressure’s curiosity.

  • The Ninth Breath (Cultural Tic): An Aeler character never counts to nine aloud. If forced to do so (or if they deliberately break the taboo), they must test Spirit + Endurance (dv 3) or gain the Marked by the Pressure condition (the next environmental hazard automatically triggers a complication). This is not a mechanical resource; it is a narrative and cultural constraint.

  • Grain Reserves Timer [8]: Represents the holds’ food supply. Ticks when a surface caravan is delayed, a contract is broken, or an Edgewalker’s scheme fails. At 4 segments: rationing begins — all physical actions in the holds suffer –1 die. At 8 segments: famine. The Deep Drake’s attention sharpens. The party must find new grain — or feed the pressure something else.

In the deep places, survival depends on precision and tradition. The Aeler understand that stone does not forgive error — a misread seal can mean death, a miscalculated air measure can doom an expedition, and a broken bell-code can spark jurisdictional war. But beneath the law, beneath the tallies, there is the pressure. It does not hate. It does not judge. It simply thinks. And when it thinks of you, you will feel a warmth in the stone, a whisper in the dark, a knock that comes in threes. The wise pretend not to notice. The foolish walk toward it.

Thematic SB Spend Table

Minor Complications (1 SB)

  • Exposure: Your actions draw unwanted attention from vault wardens or lamplighter companies.

  • Noise: Sounds of your actions alert nearby under-guards or echo bats.

  • Trace: Evidence of your passage marks your route for trackers or assessors.

  • Delay: A brief but meaningful setback costs you time or favorable air.

  • Supply Strain: Mark +1 segment on a relevant resource timer.

Moderate Setbacks (2 SB)

  • Alarm Raised: Breath warden or vault warden becomes aware and begins responding.

  • Position Lost: You lose advantageous ground/cover/stealth due to air shift or seal change.

  • Foe Appears: A rival crown’s guard or gas wraith arrives on scene.

  • Gear Trouble: A piece of equipment becomes Compromised/Neglected.

  • Lock/Barrier: A simple obstacle now requires a test to overcome.

Serious Trouble (3 SB)

  • Reinforcements: Additional crown guards, true masons, or lamp companies arrive.

Bells, Salt, and Breath.

The bells here do more than mark time—they weave the very fabric of reality, keeping the mist at bay and the dead in their proper places. Each chime is a thread in the pattern that holds the world together.

I am a Bell-Warden. I refuse to write down the ninth note. It lives only in my memory—and that memory is fading.

The Mistlands are a protectorate of the Aeler in name only. The fog here doesn’t just hide the land; it hides the law, and oh, how the wraiths love that ambiguity. The bell-lines aren’t just defenses—they’re the only thing keeping the wraiths from dragging whole villages into the mist. The Aeler built them. The Aeler charge for them. The Aeler will leave when the money runs out. And when that happens, the fog will remember every single soul they abandoned.

Never answer a voice that calls your name from the fog. The mist collects names, and once it has yours, the ferryman will always know where to find you. I’ve seen men answer. I’ve watched them walk into the white, still talking, still smiling, until their voices came back from a direction they hadn’t gone.

— Warden Noah, who has seen the ninth bell rung once and will not speak of it

What do people eat for breakfast? Salt fish and sour bread—the fish is smoked over peat fires, the bread is leavened with fog-damp yeast. The yeast is caught in cloth traps hung from the bell-towers. The traps are emptied at dawn. The yeast is not alive. It is not dead either.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To ring a bell. Every Mistlands child is given a small bronze bell at birth. By age three, they can ring the eight notes of the ward-chime in their sleep. The ninth note is never taught. The ninth note is discovered. Discovery is fatal.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their salt-pouch. Every Mistlander carries a leather pouch of consecrated salt. A stranger without a pouch is either a fool or a wraith. A stranger whose pouch is empty is a fool who is about to become a wraith.

What is the most common crime? Ward-salt theft. The Aeler control the salt pans, and the salt is the only thing that keeps the dead from rising. A stolen pouch can buy a week’s protection. The price is a year’s debt to the Aeler. The debt is collected in labor. The labor is collecting salt. The salt is stolen. The cycle is perpetual.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Bell-Wardens are not chosen—they are volunteered. A village that loses a warden must send a replacement to the Aeler training holds. The holds are in the mountains. The mountains are cold. The trainees do not always return. The ones who do return do not speak of what they learned. Their bells ring at a different pitch.

Where do people go when they die? To the fog. Bodies are not buried in the Mistlands—the ground rejects them. Corpses are carried to the edge of the bell-line and left for the mist. The mist takes them. Sometimes, at dusk, you can see them walking the lanes. They do not speak. They do not need to. Their presence is a question.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You ring the Bell of Atonement—a cracked bronze bell that hangs in every village square. The bell rings once for each wrong confessed. If the bell rings true, you are forgiven. If it cracks further, you are not. If it shatters, you are banished to the fog. The banished never return. Their voices still answer from the white.

What is the most important festival? The Rehanging of the Lines (autumn). Every bell-line is taken down, cleaned, and rehung. The work takes three days. The villages are silent for three nights. The dead listen. The dead wait. The dead do not ring.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Legate of the Mists is a wraith. She died in 803 AR, during a bell-line failure. The Aeler rebuilt her from fog and memory. She does not know she is dead. She attends council meetings. She signs documents. Her signature fades after sunset. The documents are refiled every morning.

Mechanically, when you cross a threshold without a ward-salt or bell, roll Spirit + Endurance (DV 3). On a failure, the mist learns your name — gain the MARKED condition (the next spirit encounter starts at Desperate Position).

  • Salt as Promise: A handful of salt is more than warding — it is a vow. Spill it on plank or stone to swear safety. Once per session, a character may spend a handful of ward-salt to create a Salt Oath [4] timer. While the timer has segments, the oath-taker gains  + 1 die to resist fear or possession. Black Barrow Keep — The Ruin Above Payden’s Port

Looming over the eastern edge of Payden’s Port, Black Barrow Keep is a jagged wound in the skyline. Its basalt walls weep salt, and its broken towers lean into the fog like old men listening for a bell that never rings. The Aelinnel built it in a forgotten age—not as a fortress, but as a workshop. Here, they forged bell-automata, copper sentinels, and the first ward-lines that still keep the Direwood at bay. When the Mistlands became a Protectorate, the Aelinnel sealed the deep forges and retreated. The locals say the keep is haunted. The truth is worse: it is not haunted—it is occupied.

Below the keep, the catacombs stretch down through nine known levels. Each level was sealed by a different generation of wrights: Aeler engineers, Aelinnel bell-smiths, Linn shipwrights, and older crafts whose names are scrubbed from the records. The lowest level is said to contain a working that has been ticking since before the Hollow learned to count.

Accessing the Keep: The main gate is chained with a bell-lock that requires a copper key and the correct sequence of rings (eight, never nine). Side entrances include a collapsed ropeway from the salt pans, a flooded stair from the Ghost-Ferry Slip, and a smugglers’ tunnel that exits near the Gray Hospice. The Protectorate has officially sealed the keep, but wardens look the other way for a share of salvage—or a story about what moves in the deep.

Ykrul

Wolf Standards, Winter Camps.

The steppe remembers every oath sworn beneath its endless sky, and the wolf-standards carry the honor of clans that have outlasted empires. To lead here is to keep the old ways sharp as winter steel.

I am a Herd-Scout. I can read a trail by the way the grass bends—and a lie by the way a rider sits. The Ykrul don’t measure wealth in silver; we measure it in hoofbeats, in the snort of a warhorse at dawn, in the weight of a promise kept through winter.

We do not build monuments. We build relationships, each one a stone in an invisible cairn that stretches from the Dolmis to the Aberderrin. A camp is a weave of favors owed and honor satisfied. A feud is a slow equation: blood in, blood out, with interest compounded by winter.

The Khagan’s Court doesn’t rule from a throne—it rules from a saddle. Our ”laws“ are the Thirty-Six Sayings of the Road, and the thirty-seventh is the one you learn alone, on a night when the stars blink out and the wolf does not howl.

Never ride between two wolves at dusk. The space between them is a blind angle—a route with no exit, a debt with no payment. The wolves know this. They choose the gap deliberately.

— Sogata, Herd-Scout, who has never been lost but has been found many times

What do people eat for breakfast? Fermented mare’s milk and dried meat—the milk is drunk warm from the skin, the meat is sliced thin and dried on saddle-pommels. The meat carries the taste of horse sweat. The milk carries the taste of the steppe. Both are acquired. Both are necessary.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To sit a horse. Ykrul children are placed on a horse before they can stand. The horse is old, patient, and knows the child’s weight. A child who falls is a child who learns to hold tighter. A child who cannot ride is a child who cannot eat.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their braids. Ykrul warriors braid their hair with clan colors—red for the Wolf Clan, black for the Iron Horse, gray for the Salt Steppe. A stranger whose braids are cut has been shamed. A stranger whose braids are dyed black has killed a clan-mate. A stranger with no braids has no clan. A stranger with no clan is prey.

What is the most common crime? Horse theft. A stolen horse is a stolen relative. The price is blood—the thief’s or the owner’s. The courts are the council fires. The evidence is the horse’s memory. Horses remember faces. Horses remember smells. Horses do not lie.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Khagan is not chosen by blood. He is chosen by the horse. Every five years, the clan chiefs lead their best stallions to the sacred ring. The horse that steps forward first chooses the next Khagan. The horse has not stepped forward in twenty years. The council chooses by silence. The silence is growing loud.

Where do people go when they die? To the sky. Ykrul dead are placed on open platforms, wrapped in felt, and left for the wind and the birds. The bones are collected after a year and ground into powder. The powder is mixed with clay and fired into beads. The beads are worn by the dead’s descendants. The beads are warm. The warmth is memory.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a hostage string—a braided cord that ties your wrist to the wronged party’s. The cord is cut when the apology is accepted. If the cord is cut before the apology is finished, you are not forgiven. If the cord is never cut, you are bound. The binding is lifelong. The binding is called ”friend.“

What is the most important festival? The First Grass (spring equinox). The herds are released onto the new pasture. The riders race to the horizon and back. The winner names the summer camps. The loser tends the sick animals. The sick animals are always the loser’s. The loser always wins next year.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Sky-Speaker is a fraud. She cannot call storms. She cannot read omens. She cannot speak to the dead. She is a horse-witch who learned to read the clouds. The clouds are reliable. The Khagan does not know. The Khagan does not want to know.

What outsiders call “geometry” the Ykrul call the Count—the simple, bone-deep understanding that every route has a price, every camp has a weakness, and every alliance is only as strong as the remounts that sustain it. Ykrul Mechanics

Core Rules
Kon’reh — The Game of the Steppe

The Ykrul do not speak of “geometry.” They speak of Kon’reh, a dirt-board game of encirclement, escape, and blockade played with pebbles and bone. Every Ykrul child learns its lessons: count your exits before you enter; never commit to a single path; a screen of light pieces can trap a heavy king; the best move is the one your opponent thinks you have forgotten. In Ykrul culture, Kon’reh is not recreation—it is education. A war-council may be fought out on a Kon’reh board before a single arrow is nocked. A treaty may be argued as a series of moves and counter-moves. When a Ykrul says “the Count,” they mean the cold reckoning of advantage: remounts, routes, grain-days, and the price of a blade in winter.

In play: When a Ykrul NPC evaluates an offer or a threat, they are playing Kon’reh in their head. Social rolls against Ykrul suffer +1 DV unless the player can demonstrate a “route of escape” (an exit, a concession, or a hostage) that makes the deal survivable for both sides.

Hostage Strings [4]

Every oath is secured with a hostage string—a token kin, retainer, or sworn ally left in the other camp. If the deal is broken, the hostage’s fate becomes the story’s next beat. Mechanically: create a 4-segment Hostage timer for any major treaty. On breach, the timer becomes a countdown to vengeance. Ykrul take hostages as a matter of course; refusing to give one is treated as a declaration of bad faith.

Steppe Law

Travelers are bound by steppe law: hospitality lasts three nights, insults last three generations. The Keeper may demand 1 SB when either law is broken. The three nights are measured by the Camp-Mother’s fire; the three generations are measured by the Bone-Singer’s memory.

Sky Omens

When the sky shows strange signs—mock-suns, green fire, thunder without storm—the steppe itself speaks. Once per scene, players may treat an omen as a Diamond, shifting Position without a roll. The GM may bank 1 SB for future weather complications. Pragmatic Ykrul treat omens as weather-data misread by the fearful; even so, they do not ride against them.

Bowl and Board

Ykrul elders settle disputes not by shouting but by drawing two circles in the dirt: the Bowl for fairness, then the Board for routes and concessions. A successful Wits + Lore (or relevant skill) roll creates a Seasonal Concession String at a ford, harbor, or pass. On a miss, the Market Moot [4] timer advances as factions argue.

The Thirty-Seven Sayings of the Road (Excerpts)

The Ykrul teach strategy not in manuals but in proverbs—each a line of play from Kon’reh. A few of the most quoted:

Let the river freeze before you cross. — Do not rush. Let the opponent make the first move; your opening will be built on their impatience.

Borrow a corridor to trap a king. — When the enemy carves a path toward you, let it deepen. Then seal the mouth behind them.

Loot a burning house. — Strike only when the enemy is committed elsewhere.

Hide your dagger behind a smile. — Threaten one flank; strike the other.

The reed bends, the oak breaks. — Yield to overwhelming force, but do not break; preserve your core.

A distant banner casts no shadow. — A threat far away is no threat at all; control the center.

The guest who outstays his welcome steals the host’s peace. — Allow no force, however small, to remain within your lines uncontrolled.

Count exits, not victims. — Before you move, count the ways your foe can flee. If the number is greater than zero, you have not yet won.

Ykrul of the Eastern Steppe — The Iron Grass Clans

The wind is colder here, and so are the hearts. We do not forget an insult because we cannot afford to. The grass is thinner, the wolves are bolder, and the sky speaks only in threats. The western Ykrul trade in stories. We trade in teeth.

Eastern Khatun (Elite): “The westerners count their weights in silk and verses. We count ours in frost and iron. A broken oath here is not a story for the skald—it is a hole in the winter larder. We do not forgive because we cannot. The Hollow rides with us every night. We have no room for mercy, and even less for poetry. Kon’reh is still our teacher, but the board is frozen, and the pieces are bones.”

The Eastern Ykrul share blood with their Violet Steppe cousins, but generations on the harsher eastern ranges—where the Kuvani horse clans also roam—have carved them into a different shape. They are more isolationist, more pragmatic, and their honor code has less room for talk. They live among the Kuvani but do not intermarry; they trade when the snows allow, raid when the grass is thin, and speak a dialect that grinds like stones in a riverbed. Outsiders call them the Iron Grass Clans. They call themselves Those Who Remember Winter.

Eastern Ykrul Customs (d6)

When interacting with an Eastern Ykrul camp, roll for a local custom that may affect social rolls.

  1. No Left Hand: Offering anything with the left hand is an insult. All social rolls using Presence+Sway suffer –1 die unless you explicitly use your right hand.

  2. Fire First, Talk Later: Guests must warm their hands at the central fire before speaking. If you refuse, the host will be offended—the first request you make is automatically refused.

  3. The Empty Saddle: One saddle in the camp is always empty. It belongs to a rider who died last winter. If you sit in it, you must tell a story about a death you witnessed. If the story is true, the ghost may give you a warning (GM reveals one future hazard). If false, the ghost haunts you for a day (you gain the Haunted condition).

  4. No Naming the Dead: Eastern Ykrul do not speak the names of the deceased for one year after death. If you accidentally name a dead person, the clan demands a blood-price: 1 Supply or a lock of your hair.

  5. Salt Before Meat: A meal is not blessed until the host throws a pinch of salt into the fire. If you eat before the salt, you are considered an oath-breaker. The meal will not nourish you (you do not recover Fatigue from that rest).

  6. The Ninth Breath: When drinking fermented mare’s milk, you must pause after the eighth sip. If you take a ninth, you must immediately name an enemy. The clan will consider that enemy as their own for one season. The enemy may not appreciate this.

I am a Hearth-Mother. I have never lost a quarrel. My voice is soft, my memory longer than the Foedus Stone.

The Vilikari straddle the border like a drunkard on a fence—half empire, half steppe, all headache. Their foedus treaties aren’t just agreements—they’re a tragicomic mess, half carved in marble by emperors who forgot to pay the sculptors, half braided in string by chieftains who forgot which knot meant ”peace.“ And yet, somehow, the treaties hold. Not because of the marble. Not because of the string. Because both sides are too exhausted to start the argument over again.

Never invoke only one law. The mallus (oak-ring) and the basilica (marble bench) must both be honoured. Choose one, and the other will find you—usually at midnight, usually with a writ, usually with a sword.

— Hearth-Mother Vesna, who has fed three kings and forgotten none of their debts

What do people eat for breakfast? Oatmeal with dried berries—the berries are harvested from the border scrub, where the empire’s orchards meet the steppe’s wild brambles. The berries taste of both. The oatmeal tastes of neither. The oatmeal is filler. The berries are the point.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To speak both languages—Imperial Utaran and the steppe cant. A child who speaks only one is a child who will never cross the border. A child who crosses the border is a child who will come back with stories. A child with stories is a child who will be listened to. Listening is power.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Which side of the road they ride on. The empire’s roads are measured from the center. The steppe’s paths are measured from the edge. A stranger who rides in the center is an imperial. A stranger who rides at the edge is a nomad. A stranger who rides in the ditch is a spy. The ditch is not a road. The ditch is a message.

What is the most common crime? Treaty-splitting—selling the same rights to both sides. A Vilikari factor will promise river access to an imperial merchant and grazing rights to a Ykrul clan, then collect from both and disappear before the paperwork collides. The crime is not in the selling. The crime is in getting caught before you cross the border.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Foedus Stone is a copy. The original was stolen in 712 AR by a Vilikari trader who owed debts on both sides. She melted it down and sold the marble dust as ”treaty-strengthening powder.“ The powder was mostly chalk. The treaties are still holding. The traders are still selling.

Where do people go when they die? To the border. The Vilikari bury their dead at the edge—close enough to the empire for the priests to pray, close enough to the steppe for the shamans to chant. The dead are planted face-up, so they can see both skies. The skies are different colors. The dead do not complain.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You invoke the mallus—the oak-ring court. The wronged party sits inside the ring. The wrongdoer circles it three times, speaking their apology. On the third circuit, the elders judge. If the apology is accepted, the wrongdoer is admitted into the ring. If it is rejected, the ring closes. The wrongdoer is outside. Outside is the lawless land. The lawless land is where the wolves are.

What is the most important festival? The Renewal of the Foedus (summer solstice). The stone is washed with milk and honey. The milk is from a Ykrul mare. The honey is from an imperial apiary. The mixture is drunk by the elders. The elders speak the treaty aloud. The treaty is long. The elders are old. The ceremony takes all day.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Foedus Stone has a crack. The crack was caused by a hammer in 799 AR, during a dispute over horse tariffs. The stone was repaired with clay and prayer. The clay is drying. The prayer is fading. The crack is spreading. When the stone splits, the treaty splits with it. No one knows what happens then. No one wants to find out.

Tagline: Two laws, one people. The oak remembers; the marble writes. The stone is cracking.

Federate King Aldric (Elite): “We are the bridge between empire and wilderness, the marriage of law and custom. Our strength lies not in choosing one path but in walking both with equal surety. Genre / Mood: Early Holy Roman Empire on the steppe — legal dualism, hybrid identity, frontier pragmatism, forgotten treaties.

Starting Location: A longhouse quarter tucked into an old forum. Laurel garlands hang from broken columns. Smoke from a dozen hearths curls under cracked marble arches. A Hearth-Mother offers a cup of small beer and a piece of oatcake. “The Foedus Stone cracks,” she says. “The steppe listens. Which law will you speak when the Ykrul envoy arrives — oak or marble? The stone has a new crack. No one knows which promise it represents. Speak carefully. The stone is listening too.”

The Vilikari are forgotten. They do not choose between empire and steppe; they marry them, awkwardly, imperfectly, and with frequent arguments. Their halls are longhouses raised over ruined forums; their laws carved on both oak and marble; their loyalties braided like the war-maidens’ hair. They read the geometry of jurisdiction. They are the bulk of the non-elite population in Ecktoria—the legionaries’ descendants who never went home. A Vilikari blacksmith in Ecktoria might swear by the marble bench; his cousin two valleys over in Acasia still ties knots for the oak. This is not contradiction. It is survival.

But survival requires witnesses, and the witnesses are dying. The Foedus Stone, ancient guarantor of their autonomy, is cracking. Every season, another shard falls. The Ecktorian clerks have stopped renewing the oath; the Ykrul no longer send hostages. The Vilikari are becoming invisible to both sides. Their law still holds, but the witnesses are fading. The Elder of Elders speaks once a year, but fewer and fewer listen. The newest crack appeared last winter. It has no date. No one knows which promise it represents.

Some Vilikari elders still whisper the name Crom (or Krom) when they speak of the power that breaks chains.

On the coast, fishing villages double as smugglers’ coves, running fugitives, forbidden goods, and whispered messages. Their boats are fast, their pilots know every reef and patrol route, and their loyalty is to the cargo’s survival. They are the last people in the Amaranthine who still remember the old treaty’s third clause. They are not sure why it matters. They keep it anyway.

What you notice first: Woodsmoke and old parchment. The way everyone speaks two languages at once—switching mid-sentence. Longhouse roofs that use broken imperial tiles as shingles. The silence where there used to be oaths.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never invoke only one law. The mallus (oak-ring) and the basilica (marble bench) must both be honored. Choose one, and the other will find you. But now, neither may answer. The stone has forgotten which law applies—so it applies neither.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Crack

Here, the past is a weapon and the future is a weight — the weight of a stone that remembers every oath and every betrayal. The Vilikari live in the shadow of two legal systems that no longer trust each other, and in the growing silence of a witness that is slowly forgetting. Every negotiation is a tightrope between oak and marble; every handshake is a gamble that the other party will remember which law they swore by. The Foedus Stone is not a monument; it is a timer, and its cracks mark the hours until the Vilikari cease to exist as a recognized people. On the coast, the smugglers laugh at treaties and live by the tide; in the hill forts, the old legionaries drill with ghosts; in the longhouses, hearth-mothers pour beer for guests who no longer know why they came. The stone listens. The stone waits.

In Vilikari courts, the ninth witness called to a mallus (oak-ring) is always a ghost. The wise stop at eight and settle out of court. Those who call a ninth witness find that the ghost speaks the truth — but the truth is always the worst possible version, and the living witnesses remember it as their own. The stone’s cracks are full of these truths.

Vilikari Mechanics

Core Timers
Witness Fading Timer [8]

Tracks the erosion of Vilikari legal authority.

Ticks when:

  • The party defies a Vilikari judgment or brings a dispute to an outside court (+1).

  • The GM spends 2 SB to represent a forgotten oath (+1).

  • A foreign envoy (Ykrul, Ecktorian, etc.) publicly ignores a Vilikari ruling (+1).

At 4 segments: Any Vilikari NPC the party interacts with suffers –1 die to social rolls (they doubt their own authority). The stone’s cracks are visible to all.

At 8 segments: The Foedus Stone cracks completely. The Vilikari are no longer recognized as a neutral party. The Ykrul and Ecktorians begin fighting over their land. The party can reset the timer only by renewing the treaty—finding a witness older than the stone itself (a Lethai-al elder, a Trow, a ghost of the original signatory) and having them speak the oath aloud.

Two-Law Tension [4]

Whenever a jurisdictional conflict arises (mallus vs. basilica, oak vs. marble), players may step Position up by citing the Foedus Stone or an older precedent—but every use adds a tick to the Two-Law timer. When it fills, one side demands exclusive authority. The other side sharpens its knives.

If the Witness Fading Timer is at 4+, this timer ticks twice as fast.

Border Tension [Front]

Once per session, if a deal is struck with a foreign envoy or mixed-blood courtier, advance or start this front.

Market Knives [4]

Company Mechanics (Visible Tracks)

Every mercenary company (PC-led or NPC) tracks three timers:

  • Payday [4] – Missed compensation, bad terms, seized chests. On fill: a squad deserts (lose 1 Supplies) or a veteran sells secrets (GM gains 2 SB). The men whisper.

  • Supplies [4] – Weather, marches, sabotage. While Supplies ≥ 3, all risky maneuvers start Position –1 unless you bring a Diamond (scavenge, requisition). Hunger is a slow knife.

  • Cohesion [6] – Losses, betrayals, officer feuds. When Cohesion hits 1–2, the GM may convert the first 1 SB each scene into Desertion, Brawl, or Refusal. Trust is the first casualty.

Recovery: A successful Payline Drill scene clears 1 Payday. A Quartermaster Audit clears 1 Supplies. A Rally & Reform roll (Presence+Sway, DV 3) clears 1 Cohesion. Shared hardship binds faster than coin.

In the Black Banners, power flows through agreements, coin chests, and the weight of a reputation — but also through the sergeant who knows each man’s name, the cook who stretches the rations, the smith who works through the night. A Banner-Captain’s authority rests on the number of companies he commands; a Condotta Broker’s on the thickness of his roster. Yet loyalty is not bought — it is earned in the mud, at the latrine, across a shared fire.

For the GM: Power in the Black Banners is measured in payday timers, broker’s favors, and the length of a duel record — but also in the camp’s mood, the quartermaster’s ledger, and the stories the veterans tell. Patronage often takes the form of dual contracts, emergency requisitions, or the Bannerless Word – each tied to a specific company, broker, or share. To emphasize the mercenary realism:

  • Use the pay and supply timers as visible pressure. Let players negotiate, raid, or pledge future services to clear ticks.

  • Enforce Allegiances in Flux – every scene, loyalties may shift. The Keeper’s SB pool grows when the party breaks an agreement.

  • Let Honor vs. Pragmatism be a dial the party can slide through their actions. Center it by a costly act of mercy or a profitable betrayal.

  • Track Black Marks as a persistent reputation score. Three Black Marks and no condotta broker will write a clear agreement for you.

  • Remember the human element: the soldier who shares his last ration, the captain who stands in the line, the surgeon who weeps in private. These are the ties that outlast coin.

In the Black Banners, the camp sings when pay arrives — and falls silent when the coin chest is empty. But the camp also sings when a wounded friend survives, when the supply train arrives, when the snow melts and the road opens. The song is never just about coin.

Final GM Mantra (Black Banners Edition)

The camp sings when pay arrives — and falls silent when the chest is empty. Set DV low. Use Allegiances in Flux every scene. Let Payday, Supplies, and Cohesion be visible. Skerries & Storm-Oaths.

The sea keeps better counsel than any scribe, and the storm remembers every oath sworn upon its winds. To rule these shores is to dance between their moods and never miss a step.

I am a Net-Wife. I tie a knot for every storm I’ve survived. My belt has thirty.

North of the Aberderrin Sea, the Linns don’t just raid and trade—they do it with the fervour of the converted, swearing oaths to storm and sea that aren’t just words—they’re leverage with a supernatural clause. They pay in stories, not silver, because to them, a tale well-told is worth more than a chest of gold…especially when it’s true.

The jarls measure their wealth in ships. The skalds measure their wealth in verses. The dead measure their wealth in how many living remember their names. I measure mine in knots. Each knot is a promise I kept to the sea. The sea remembers. The sea does not forgive forgetting.

Never name a drowned person aloud while underway. The sea collects names, and if it hears you speaking one it doesn’t recognize, it may reach up to correct the ledger.

What do people eat for breakfast? Seal blubber and flatbread—the blubber is chewy, the bread is dense, and both are stored in the same barrel. The barrel is shared with the crew. The crew shares everything. The crew is family. Family is obligation.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To tie a knot. Linn children are given a length of rope at birth. By age five, they can tie the eight sea-knots: clew, clove, figure-eight, reef, sheet, stopper, timber, and the ninth—the one that slips when pulled. The ninth knot is for emergencies. The ninth knot is for funerals. The ninth knot is for when the sea asks for a life.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their breath. Linn sailors chew kelp to sweeten their breath—the kelp is harvested from sacred beds, blessed by the Volva. A stranger whose breath smells of salt has been at sea. A stranger whose breath smells of nothing has not. A stranger whose breath smells of copper has spoken a false oath. The sea knows. The sea will answer.

What is the most common crime? Oath-breaking—but oath-breaking is not a crime, it is a storm. The oath-breaker’s ship will founder. The oath-breaker’s nets will tear. The oath-breaker’s children will be born with webbed fingers. The punishment is not imposed by the jarl. The punishment is imposed by the sea. The sea is patient. The sea does not forget.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Sea-Queen is not a queen. She is a figurehead—literally. Her body is carved from the keel of a ship that sank in 612 AR. Her voice is a recording. Her throne is a navigation table. The jarls pretend. The jarls have always pretended. The pretending keeps the peace.

Where do people go when they die? To the waves. Linn dead are placed in boats and set adrift, weighted with stones. The stones are carved with the dead’s name. If the boat sinks before it reaches the horizon, the dead is accepted. If it reaches the horizon, the dead is rejected. If it returns to shore, the dead is cursed. The cursed wash up on beaches. The beaches are littered with their bones.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a storm-oath. You stand on a cliff during a gale and swear your apology to the wind. If the wind carries your words, you are forgiven. If the wind throws you into the sea, you are not. The sea does not give second chances.

What is the most important festival? The Launching of the Longships (spring thaw). The ships are dragged from their winter shelters and blessed with blood and mead. The blood is from a sacrificed goat. The mead is from the jarl’s own stores. The ships are named after ancestors. The ancestors are listening. The ancestors approve.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Volva of the Mist is a fraud. She cannot see the future. She reads the weather—the pressure, the wind, the taste of salt on the air. The sailors know. The sailors do not care. A weather-reader is more useful than a seer. The Volva knows this. The Volva uses this. The Volva is never wrong.

Genre / Mood: Norse coastal saga, oath-driven honor, horror of the unwitnessed death, the tyranny of seasons, the weight of wyrd.

Starting Location: A longship house lined with storm-charms and whale-bone runes, salt wind clawing at the doors. A Net-Wife binds a storm-token into a PC’s palm. “The Whale-Road sings,” she says. “But the Mistlands whistle back. North of the Aberderrin Sea, where fjords split the coast and skerries break the tide, dwell the Linn. They are traders and raiders in equal breath, their longships crowding the whale-road and river mouths. They descend the Ylolka like an axe-blade and cut into southern shores, or slip through the Thin Strip where the Valewood dares not touch salt. Yet even these storm-oath people dread the Mistlands and the Valewood, whose fog and trees have no need of ships or harbors to claim their due.

The Linn measure their lives in seasons, not years. There is a season for raiding (summer, when the seas are open), a season for planting (spring, when the frost breaks), a season for governance (autumn, when the ships are beached and the Thing meets), and a season for survival (winter, when the frost sets its mark). In winter, the fjords freeze, the longships are dragged into sheds, and the people gather around the longhall fires to settle debts, tell sagas, and wait for the sun to return. The winter debt is not a book — it is a set of carved oath-sticks, each notch a promise kept or broken, each knot a story still unfolding. When a Linn says a debt is “written in frost,” they mean it is unchangeable until the ice melts. And the ice, in the high north, never truly melts.

The gods of the Linn are not distant. Adar, the All-Father, walks the ridges in a gray cloak, his one eye watching for those who break oaths. He is a manifestation of the Oath of Flame and Light, but the Linn know him as the god of wisdom, death, and binding promises. Rorg, the Storm Father, rides the clouds, his hammer cracking the sky. He is a syncretic version of Xhak’thul (in the vein of Thor) — the patron of raiders and sailors, a thunder-god who laughs in the teeth of the gale. His cult is loud, proud, and dangerous — a Rorg devotee will swear a storm-oath by drinking seawater and shouting at the lightning. The Temple of Light calls him a demon. The Linn call him captain.

What you notice first: The smell of tar and salt fish. The creak of oars from unseen boats. The way everyone — jarl to thrall — touches a knotted cord before speaking of the sea. The oath-sticks hanging from every belt, each mark a weight carried.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never name a drowned person aloud while underway. The sea collects names, and if it hears you speaking one it doesn’t recognize, it may reach up to correct the debt. Also, never refuse a cup of winter mead — it is a test of your honor and your weight.

  2. Steersman — Tiller-hand never shakes, even when keel does. His left ear is scarred — he listened too long to a siren’s promise. His oath-stick is notched for every life he’s lost. [REPUTATION]

  4. Oar-Master — Sets beat that decides if home is today or never. He drums on a hollowed skull. The rhythm is a prayer to Rorg. The oar-crew’s debt is marked on his own arm — notches in the bone. [SOCIAL]

  5. Mist-Pilot — Ghosts boats through marsh lanes; reed-runner with secret paths. He never blinks in fog. Some say he traded his blink for safe passage. His memory-cord is knotted, not notched — the knots are spells. [STEALTH]

  9. Thing-Speaker — Makes war into lawsuit, lawsuit into lunch. He carries a water-timer that runs on meltwater. When it runs out, so does your patience. He interprets the winter debts for those who cannot read ice. [JUSTICE]

  10. Sea-Queen’s Hand — Envoy with seal-bag and sword-belt; queen’s will made manifest. He never draws his blade. The threat is enough. His oath-stick is branded with the queen’s mark — a seal that cannot be forged. [AUTHORITY]

  11. Sea-Queen — Crown of hammered silver, eyes for weather and law. She lost an eye to a storm — or to a spy. She won’t say which. She is the only one who can order the winter debts spoken. [AUTHORITY]

  12. High Jarl — First among raids, last to boast; paramount over strands. His beard is braided with silver from every ship he’s taken. His oath-stick is a whale’s rib — too long for anyone else to carry. [REPUTATION]

  13. Volva of Mist — Oath-reader whose nod stills surf; seer of tides and truth. She speaks once a year. The rest of the time, she listens to the fog. She reads the frost-bound oaths by touch — her fingers are scarred from counting. [LEGEND]

  2. Oar-Share — Claim two benches on a fast longship for a season. [TRAVEL] The oars are carved with your name. Row well, or they’ll be recarved. The oar itself is the promise.

  3. Pilot’s Token — Mist-runner guides through reed-maze safely. [TRAVEL] The token is a dried eel skin. Keep it dry. The pilot’s memory-cord is knotted in the same pattern.

  4. Wharf-Right — Dolmis waystation loading priority; first ashore. [TRAVEL] The harbormaster frowns but steps aside. Your name is now on a list.

  5. Salvage Claim — Flotsam on reef stretch becomes “inventory.” [TRADE] You must name each piece before touching it. The wrong word forfeits the claim. The promise of the salvage is bound to the tide line.

  6. Thing Ruling [Leverage: Authority] — Verdict in your favor that travels with you. [JUSTICE] The verdict is carved on a whale-rib. Carry it as a shield. The winter ice will not speak for the same dispute twice.

  7. Raid-Truce — Immunity in named bay for one market day. [TRAVEL] [SOCIAL] A white banner on your mast. No one will attack — openly. The promise of the truce is the banner rope. Cut it, and the truce ends.

  10. Escort Writ — Eight shields and wolf-banner from cape to cape. [TRAVEL] [COMBAT] The shields are painted with a wolf’s head. They die before you do. Their oath is their swords — notched for every oath kept.

  12. High Jarl’s Pennon — Levies open booms and mouths when it flies. [AUTHORITY] The pennon is torn from a captured ship. Fly it proudly — or else. The promise of the pennon is the ship’s mast.

  13. Storm-Oath Clause — Temporary exception to any sea-law, sealed. [AUTHORITY] [MAGIC] The clause is whispered, never written. The sea hears and obeys — once. Rorg will remember. The winter ice will not record it — the ice cannot hold a whisper.

Patronage & Power

Among the Linn, power flows through oaths, kinship, and the spoken word — and through the marks carved in ice. Jarls maintain authority through successful raids and wise judgments, while skalds wield influence through their ability to immortalize deeds in verse — or damn names through satire. The Sea-Queen holds sway over the coastlands, but her rule depends on the respect of the High Jarl and the Thing-speakers who interpret ancient laws. And beneath all of them lies the frost-bound memory — the carved word, not the written one.

For the GM: Power in Linn society is fluid and performance-based. Patronage often takes the form of foster-bonds, oar-shares, or raid-truces — each binding the recipient to service and reputation. To emphasize this:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (tokens, banners, verses) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided.

  • Let rival jarls issue conflicting claims, forcing players to choose whose favor matters more.

  • Use the Thing-Holm and harbor gatherings as arenas for social contests, where oaths are sworn and broken.

  • Introduce the Frost Mark as a recurring pressure. Every gift from a Linn comes with a weight. Players who ignore their marks may find themselves summoned to the Thing in winter.

  • Let the seasons change. A winter scene should feel different from a summer raid. Use the Season’s Turn to reinforce the cyclical nature of Linn life.

In Linn society, your word is your bond, and your reputation is your greatest treasure. But the frost-bound memory is patient. It waits for spring.

Temple Crusade Mini-Generators

Temple Pressure Events (d6)

Roll when the Northern Crusade advances or when the GM needs a story beat.

  1. Grain Fleet Arrives. Temple ships unload cheap southern grain. Prices drop, but the old shrine offerings are forgotten. Effect:  + 1 tick on Temple Pressure timer;  + 1d on social rolls with hungry villagers for one scene.

  2. Synod Court Opens. A Thepyrgosi judge hears the first case. Feuds may be settled with penance instead of blood-silver. Effect: Shrine-devout characters suffer  − 1 Position in this settlement until they prove their loyalty.

  3. Bell Tower Consecrated. A bronze bell rings nine times at dawn. The old shrine bells fall silent. Effect: Temple-leaning characters gain  + 1d on faith actions; every midnight, the GM may spend 1 SB to have the ninth bell attract a hungry ghost.

  4. Schoolmaster Arrives. Children learn to write Temple script. Old oral traditions are called “superstition.” Effect: The next generation will have  + 1 Temple Devotion; players may attempt a Synthesis Scene to save one shrine teaching.

  5. Bishop’s Progress. A high-ranking cleric tours the coast, consecrating altars. Refusal to host him marks you as an enemy of the Light. Effect: Begin a Bishop’s Favor [4] timer; if it fills, the bishop demands a public oath of loyalty.

  6. Iconoclasm. Zealots burn a seastack shrine or break a Thing’s oath-ring. The old patrons are furious. Effect: Shrine-devout characters gain a free Vengeance Oath String; weather and tide hazards become one step worse for 1d4 days.

Linn Reactions to the Temple (d12)

  1. Desperate Acceptance. “We ate their grain. We owe them.” Tag: Grateful–Sullen.

  2. Silent Resistance. Shrine offerings are left in secret; Temple priests are given cold hearths. Tag: Ice–Hospitality.

  4. Syncretic Curiosity. Elders ask the priest to bless a shrine. “Both fires warm the hall.” Opportunity: Earn a Synthesis Token.

  6. Conversion as Revenge. A family spurned by the old patrons embraces the Temple to spite them. Tag: Bitter–Righteous.

  7. Bread & Bells. The village accepts the Temple but keeps a hidden shrine to Hvı́tabjörn. Tag: Double–Altar.

  8. Heretic Burnings. Temple zealots execute a shrine-keeper. The hall is now Contested Ground.

  9. The Bishop’s Bastard. A priest’s secret child is now a Linn raider. String: Blood–Secret.

  10. Wave-Father’s Answer. A storm wrecks a Temple grain ship. Shrine-devout see a sign. Effect:  + 2 on a shrine-related roll this session.

  11. Oath-Ring Saved. A Temple judge rules that the old oath-ring is still lawful – a rare bridge-builder. Reward: Ambassador Status (one scene of neutral faith ground).

  12. The Silent Bell. The new tower’s bell cracks on the ninth toll. No one can explain it. Omen: GM gains 2 SB for fog- or ghost-related twists.

Temple Missions (d6)

Quests the Temple of Light might offer to a mixed or Temple-leaning crew.

  1. Recover a Stolen Relic. A shrine-devout crew has taken a fragment of the True Candle. Find it – by negotiation or force.

  2. Escort the Bishop. Take a southern prelate through a contested fjord. Protect him from “pagan” ambushes – or arrange one.

  3. Convert a Hall. A jarl sits on the fence. Persuade him (or his skald) that the Light is good for trade. Clock: Conversion [6].

  4. Burn the Shrine. A remote seastack shrine is a navigation hazard and a gathering point for resisters. The Temple offers silver for its destruction.

  5. Rescue a Synod Judge. He was taken hostage after a biased ruling. Infiltrate the clan’s winter hall and bring him out alive.

  6. Forgive a Blood-Debt. The Temple wants to end a feud by paying blood-silver from its own coffers. You must convince both sides to accept – a delicate parley.

Faith Tension Scenes (d6)

Quick scene starters for religious conflict.

  1. The Thinglet at the Hearth. Shrine-devout and Temple-leaning families argue over a broken betrothal. Position: Controlled → Desperate if you take sides openly.

  2. Midnight at the Bell Tower. Someone has silenced the ninth bell – by stuffing it with wool from a white bear’s pelt. Complication: Spiritual Conflict Condition begins.

  3. The Convert’s Shame. A Temple-leaning Linn begs you to smuggle her family heirloom (a shrine totem) out of the village before the bishop’s agents find it.

  4. Storm over the Synod Court. Vélki answers a shrine-devout’s prayer with a lightning strike on the Temple granary. GM: Spend 2 SB to have a priest accuse the party of witchcraft.

  5. The Wandering Skald. A singer arrives with a verse that claims the crew has a secret shrine oath. The verse is false – but now everyone believes it.

  6. Dual Oath at Dusk. A dying warrior asks to swear by both Hrævkn and the Light. The shrine-keeper and the Temple priest each demand you witness only their rite. Reward: A Synthesis Token if you honor both.

Syncretic Outcomes (d6)

When players successfully blend shrine and Temple practices, roll (or choose) a lasting effect.

  1. The Shared Cairn. A new shrine uses a Temple candle to light the old oath-ring. Effect: This site is now Mixed Quarter permanently; once per session, any character may reroll a failed faith-related roll here.

  2. Witness of Both. The crew is known as bridge-builders. Effect: Gain Ambassador Status (convert a faith-based Position penalty into a bonus once per session).

  3. The Ninth Bell Silent. The village agrees to ring only eight bells, leaving the ninth for the old patrons. Effect: Shrine-devout characters no longer suffer  − 1 Position in this village.

  4. The Skald’s Compromise. A Temple hymn is set to an old shrine tune. Effect: Any Audience tag that includes “Suspicious” downgrades to “Curious” in this region.

  5. Blood-Silver to the Poor. A feud is settled by paying wergild to the Temple’s poor fund – but the shrine witnesses the payment. Effect: The settlement counts as both a Thing Verdict and a Synod Absolution. The GM may not use this feud as a complication again.

  6. The Unbroken Spiral. A new rite is born: a white candle and a raven’s feather are offered together. Effect: The crew gains a permanent Synthesis Token that refreshes every season (instead of being one-use).

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Draugr Jarl (Unfinished Business): The Thing-Holm is haunted by a jarl who died before his oath was fulfilled. He rises when a storm-oath is broken, demanding a blood-price paid in stories. The Temple of Light has sent three missionaries to lay him to rest; all three drowned in calm water. His oath-stick is frozen into the ice of the Thing-Holm. If it ever thaws, he will walk again.

  • Adar’s Ravens (The Witness): Adar is said to have two ravens, Memory and Thought, who fly across the world each day and return to whisper in his ear. A Linn skald who prays to Adar may ask one question about a forgotten weight; the GM must answer truthfully, though cryptically. The price is a memory — the skald forgets something unimportant, but it may become important later.

  • Rorg’s Hammer (The Xhak’thul): The Storm Father’s hammer is said to be lodged in a cliff on the highest skerry. A character who touches the hammer and survives the lightning may invoke Rorg’s wrath once per session: all enemies within Near must test Spirit + Endurance (DV 4) or be Stunned for one exchange. The cost is that the character is marked by the storm — they will be struck by lightning at the worst possible moment (GM’s discretion).

  • The Tide-Washed Covenant (The Hospice of the Salted Anchor): A gray-robed healer runs a hospice on the edge of the Mistlands, treating wounded raiders and fog-mad sailors alike. She asks no payment, but she writes every name in a book that never dries. Linn captains leave offerings of fresh water before a voyage; those who refuse find their ship plagued by the drowned. The book is said to be a copy of the frost-bound oaths — but written in salt water, not ice.

  • The Ninth: In any longhouse, the ninth horn blown at a feast summons the Hollow. The wise stop at eight. Those who blow the ninth are said to see a pale figure standing at the high table — waiting to collect a weight. The figure is Adar, checking his oath-stick.
