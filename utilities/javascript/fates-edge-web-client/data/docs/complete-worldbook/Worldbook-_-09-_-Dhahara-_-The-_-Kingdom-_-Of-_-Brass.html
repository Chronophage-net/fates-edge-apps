Dhahara — The Kingdom of Brass

“The mountain weighs every breath, every ingot, every oath. Pay in iron, pay in silence, or pay in blood. And when the ninth lease cracks, even the elephants will remember who fed them during the famine.” — Dusana of the Raven Road, at the Viceroy’s Hall in Sarvistan

The Brass Coast

Shore of Silver and Spice.

”The tariff is the law. The law is the tariff. I do not care if your goods are stolen, blessed, or weeping. I care that you pay. The Admiralty does not rule this coast. The ledger rules. I am merely its pen.“

I am a spice merchant, grandson of a raja who watched the first Kahfagian customs house rise on our beach. The Admiralty calls me a subject. The Sidhi call me a partner. I call myself a son of the Brass Coast. My grandfather’s sword hangs above my desk. One day, I may need it again. The sword has nine notches. Each notch is a promise the Admiralty broke.

The Brass Coast is Dhahara’s southern rim: a humid, feverish coastline of palm groves, salt pans, and crowded port cities. The Kahfagian Admiralty administers it as a ”client province,“ but three powers bleed into each other here, and none can draw a clean breath. The Admiralty holds the ports and the tariff ledgers. The Sidhi diaspora runs the ropeways, the lighthouse courts, and the freedman’s seal. And the native Dhaharan rajas — the hill lords who have lived here since before any foreign flag flew — grow the pepper, mine the iron, and watch the coast from inland fortresses, waiting.

Here, a man can buy anything — a ship, a spice lot, a freedman’s seal — and sell anything except his own shadow. The Admiralty’s Maritime Court settles disputes with a judge’s gavel and a marine’s cutlass. The Sidhi lighthouse courts offer an alternative: justice by green flame, witnessed by the drowned. And the rajas enforce their own law in the hills, where the Admiralty’s writ does not run. But here’s the secret the Admiralty doesn”t want you to know: the ropeways that carry spice baskets from ship to warehouse are old, and the dead still pull their weight — not just Sidhi dead, but the drowned of every nation who could not pay their passage. The coast remembers them all. The ninth rope on every ropeway is always frayed. No one replaces it. That rope carries the debt.

Never board a ship that has a green lantern at the mast. It means the captain is under a death vow — and you are the payment. If the lantern burns blue, run. The blue flame is the Admiral’s oracle, and she does not make bargains.

— Raja Govind of the Kalinga foothills, who has never seen the sea and refuses to start

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice and fermented fish paste — the rice is grown in coastal paddies, the fish is caught by the lighthouse keepers“ children. The children are paid in copper. The copper is melted into seals. The seals are sold to whoever can afford them. The ninth child never takes payment. No one knows why.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read a lighthouse code. Brass Coast children are taught the eight lamp-flashes: Tariff, Inspection, Quarantine, Smuggler, Pirate, Rescue, Home, and the ninth — the flash that means ”the collector is coming.“ The ninth flash is never used. The ninth flash is a warning. The children learn it anyway.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their papers. The Admiralty requires a manifest for every shipment, a seal for every trader, a stamp for every dock. A stranger whose papers are in order is a merchant. A stranger whose papers are missing is a smuggler. A stranger whose papers are too perfect is a spy. The spies are Admiralty. The Admiralty spies on itself. The ninth page of every manifest is always blank. That is where the dead travel.

What is the most common crime? Tariff evasion — but tariff evasion is not a crime, it is a profession. The tariffs are set by the Admiralty. The Admiralty is corrupt. The evaders pay the Admiralty directly. The payment is called a ”consulting fee.“ The fee is tax-deductible. The ninth fee is always paid in salt, not silver. The salt comes from the Freedman’s Grave.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The lighthouse lamps are not fuelled by oil. They are fuelled by the fat of the drowned — whales, sailors, slaves who did not survive the crossing. The fat burns green. The green is the color of memory. The memory is the dead”s. The ninth lamp on every tower is never lit. It is reserved for the collector.

Where do people go when they die? To the sea. The dead are weighted with brass and dropped in the deep channel. The channel is called the Freedman’s Grave, but it holds rajas and pirates alike. The grave is unmarked. The grave is the entire coast. The ninth body dropped each year is never weighted. It floats. It always drifts toward the Admiral’s palace.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a tariff — a payment of silver, calculated by the wronged party’s estimate of their loss. The tariff is paid to the Maritime Court. The court takes a percentage. The percentage is the judge’s fee. The judge’s fee is negotiable. The ninth tariff ever collected was paid in a memory. The judge still remembers it. He wishes he did not.

What is the most important festival? The Burning of the Ledgers (new year). Every merchant’s ledger is brought to the lighthouse. The ledgers are read aloud. The debts are tallied. The ledgers are burned. The ash falls on the water. The water carries the debts to the sea. The sea does not forgive. The sea reschedules. The ninth ledger is never burned. It is kept in a brass box at the bottom of the Freedman’s Grave. It contains the Admiralty’s own debts.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The lighthouse lamps are not all green. One lamp burns blue — the Admiral’s personal beacon. The blue flame is not fat. It is a spirit — a Sidhi oracle who was bound to the lighthouse nine generations ago. She sees everything. She speaks to no one. The Admiral does not know she is still alive. Her ninth prophecy was never recorded. It said: “The collector will come by sea, and the ledger will burn from the bottom up.”

The Brass Coast — “The Shore of Silver and Spice”

Key Demographic: Humans Capital City:: Tamralipta

Quick Reference

Tagline: Three powers, one coast, and a memory written in salt. The wrong spark will burn the whole shore.

Raja Govind (Native Hill Lord): “My grandfather watched the Kahfagians raise their first customs house on our beach. My father paid their tolls in sweat. I will see them drown in their own silence — or I will drown trying. The Sidhi are guests. The Admiralty are thieves. The Brass Coast belongs to those who salt their own fish.”

Sidhi Freedman (Commoner): “My grandmother was born in Galanina. My mother was born in a slave ship. I was born in a lighthouse. The Admiralty calls me a subject. The Covenant calls me a freedman. But the rajas — they call me guest. And a guest who stays too long becomes a target.”

Genre / Mood: Mercantile noir, post-colonial pressure cooker, ghost-lit coast, three-way cold war.

Starting Location: A moonlit beach where three boats meet: a Kahfagian cutter, a Sidhi lighthouse skiff, and a Dhaharan spice dhow. A native pearl diver approaches you: “The Admiralty raised the salt tax again. The Sidhi say they will strike the ropeways. And my raja is sharpening his father’s sword. Pick a side — or the tide will pick for you.”

The Brass Coast is Dhahara’s southern rim: a humid, feverish coastline of palm groves, salt pans, and crowded port cities. Three powers bleed into each other here, and none can draw a clean breath.

The Admiralty (Kahfagian) holds the warm-weather ports and the toll schedules. Their warships patrol the shipping lanes, and their magistrates judge by maritime law. They call it a “client province.” The locals call it a noose. The Admiralty is not evil — it is hungry. Its thalassocratic empire needs new markets, raw materials, and a captive shoreline to fuel its oligarchs’ ever-growing appetites. The Brass Coast is a vein they intend to mine dry.

The Sidhi are a foreign diaspora — refugees from Galanina who fled the Ashaani slavers generations ago. They control the ropeways, the lighthouse courts, and the freedman’s seal. They are welcome nowhere and trusted less, but without them the spice trade stops. Their story is one of forced migration: driven from their homeland, they moved from place to place, unwelcome at every stop, until at last they reached this coast. Like the displaced of another age, they made a new life from salt water and stubborn memory. They resent the rajas for the cold shoulders and the whispers — “foreigners,” “drifters,” “they keep to themselves.” And they resent the Admiralty for trying to subjugate them all over again, trading chains of iron for chains of tolls. The Sidhi make the spice flow — and wherever flow is strong, hands reach to dam it.

The Native Dhaharan Rajas are the hill lords who have lived here since before the Admiralty’s first anchor. They grow the pepper, mine the iron, and salt the fish. They watch the coast from inland fortresses, and they remember when no foreign flag flew over their beaches. Some accept bribes. Some sharpen blades. All of them are waiting. They resent the Admiralty for the slow colonization — the tolls, the land seizures, the quiet erasure of their courts. But they also resent the Sidhi. Centuries have passed, yet the Sidhi remain “outsiders” in the rajas’ eyes — a people who keep to their own lighthouses, marry within their own circles, and speak a creole tongue that grates on the ear. The rajas envy the Sidhi’s quiet prosperity: the ropeways humming with trade, the seal that opens every port. They do not trust a people who have no homeland and yet have thrived. And that envy sharpens their knives.

The air smells of pepper, cinnamon, and the low tide of a thousand ships. Here, a man can buy anything — a ship, a spice lot, a freedman’s seal — and sell anything except his own shadow. But the price is never just silver. The Admiralty takes tolls. The Sidhi take stories. The rajas take blood.

What you notice first: The heat pressing down like a damp cloth. The way the sea is the same color as the sky, so the horizon disappears. The brass hooks on Sidhi belts, the brass scales on Admiralty desks, and the brass earrings in native chieftains’ ears — three metals, one coast.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never board a ship that has a green lantern at the mast. It means the captain is under a death vow — and you are the payment. Also, never refuse a cup of palm wine offered by a raja’s envoy. It is a test of honor, and the hills have long memories.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Toll, The Heat of the Hills

The Brass Coast is not a colony — it is a slow-burning argument. The Admiralty holds the ports, the Sidhi hold the ropeways, and the rajas hold the land. Each side believes the other two are temporary. Each side is wrong.

The Admiralty’s Powder Keg: The Kahfagian governor, Admiral Voss, rules from a whitewashed fort that was once a Sidhi stronghold. His marines drink coconut wine and mock the “lazy natives.” His clerks adjust tolls with the tide. But his ships are few, his garrison is underpaid, and the rajas have started hiring pirate dhows to intercept his grain convoys. The Admiralty needs new markets because its old ones are failing; the Brass Coast is meant to be the solution. Instead, it has become a wound that will not close.

The Sidhi’s Fragile Web: The Sidhi run the ropeways, the lighthouse courts, and the freedman’s seal. They are the only neutral party — and neutrality is a knife edge. A Sidhi strike would collapse the spice trade. A Sidhi betrayal would hand the coast to the Admiralty. And the rajas whisper that the Sidhi are just another foreign master, trading one chain for another. The Sidhi do not forget the long road that brought them here — the forced marches, the closed gates, the children buried in unnamed graves. They will not be pushed again. But the rajas’ envy and the Admiralty’s hunger leave them few roads out.

The Rajas’ Slow Burn: The native Dhaharan hill lords speak a dozen dialects and worship a hundred spirits. They do not agree on much — except that the coast belongs to them. Some, like Raja Govind of the Kalinga foothills, play the Admiralty and the Sidhi against each other. Others, like the Veiled Queen of the Mangrove Delta, have already sent envoys to the Ashaani Breath-less. And in the smoky kitchens of every hill fort, young warriors sharpen their grandmother’s steel and wait for the sign. They resent the Admiralty’s tolls, yes — but they also resent the Sidhi’s quiet prosperity. “They do not marry our daughters,” a raja’s son might say. “They do not pray to our gods. They are strangers who have grown rich on our land.” That resentment is a fire the Admiralty fans when it suits them.

The green flame of the Sidhi lighthouses, the brass scales of the Admiralty, and the iron swords of the rajas — the Brass Coast is a tripwire. Any spark could set it ablaze.

The Three Powers: Faction Timers

Use these timers to track the coast’s simmering tensions.

  • Admiralty Grip [6]: Ticks up when the party enforces Kahfagian tolls, sinks a pirate dhow, or exposes a Sidhi smuggler. At 4: Admiral Voss offers the party a Letter of Marque (legal piracy against rival powers). At 6: He declares martial law — all ports closed, all ships searched, all outsiders suspect.

  • Sidhi Ropeway Control [6]: Ticks up when the party helps a Sidhi lighthouse keeper, frees a Sidhi prisoner, or protects a Sidhi caravan. At 4: The Sidhi offer a Lighthouse Charter (safe harbour in any Sidhi port). At 6: They call a ropeway strike — trade halts, and every faction blames each other.

  • Raja’s Rebellion [8]: Ticks up when the party smuggles weapons to the hills, assassinates an Admiralty official, or reveals a Sidhi secret. At 4: A raja sends a secret envoy with a tiger’s claw (an invitation to an alliance). At 8: The hills rise — native war parties raid coastal towns, and the Admiralty responds with fire ships.

These timers are not mutually exclusive. A clever party might feed one to delay another — or light all three at once.

Spades — Places

Native sites are marked with ; Sidhi sites with *; Admiralty sites with .

  1. Tamralipta Harbour* — A forest of masts; the smell of tar and spices. The harbour is a maze of floating houses. The residents are all Sidhi. They never touch the shore — but they pay tolls to the Admiralty. [TRADE]

  2. Pepper Warehouse (Native-owned) — A long building with slatted walls; the air is sharp. The workers are freedmen earning passage for their families. Their eyes are red from pepper dust. They never blink. The owner is a raja’s cousin. [LABOR]

  3. Lighthouse of the Brass Flame* — A stone tower with a copper mirror. The light is green. It shows the faces of the drowned. The keeper knows their names — and some of them are still walking. [JUSTICE]

  4. Salt Pan of the Silent Widows — A flat expanse of white crust; women in black work the rakes. The women are all widows; their husbands were lost at sea — to storms, to slavers, to Admiralty press gangs. The salt is bitter. They sell it to remember. [COMMUNITY]

  5. Kahfagian Admiralty House — A white building with a veranda; a flag with a lantern. The flag is lowered at sunset. The Admiral is drunk by dawn. The clerks are sober — and watching. Native servants spit in the coffee. [AUTHORITY]

  6. Sidhi Court of the Lighthouse* — A circular chamber with a brass scale. The judge is blind; she hears truth in the echo of a coin. The coin is never returned. The rajas send observers, but they never speak. [JUSTICE]

  7. Palm Grove of the Elephants — A grove where temple elephants are bathed. The elephants are painted with turmeric. The paint is a prayer for safe passage — and for the foreigners to leave. [RITUAL]

  8. Coral Reef of the Sunken Ship (Neutral) — A reef just offshore; a mast breaks the surface. The ship was a slaver; the slaves escaped. They live in the reef now. They trade salvage for silence — and they remember which raja helped them. [LEGEND]

  9. Pepper Auction Floor (Native-owned) — A crowded hall; voices bid in claps. The auctioneer has a hammer of whalebone. It cracks when he lies. The cracks are getting louder. The Admiralty’s toll agents sit in a raised gallery. [TRADE]

  10. The Green Lantern Tavern (Sidhi)* — A low building with a green lantern. The lantern is never lit. The owner says it would attract the wrong kind of dead. The right kind drink here for free — and they remember every whispered plot. [SOCIAL]

  11. The Shipbreaker’s Yard (Admiralty-licensed) — A beach where old ships are dismantled. The workers are convicts; they wear chains that drag in the sand. They earn one link off each season. The chains are paid for by the rajas’ taxes. [LABOR]

  12. The Admiral’s Palace (Former Sidhi Fort) — A former Sidhi fort, now occupied by Kahfagian officers. The flag is flown upside down. The Admiral does not know the difference. The Sidhi do. They count the days. The rajas count the cannons. [AUTHORITY]

  13. The Undercliff (Smuggler’s Cove, native-controlled) — A smuggler’s cove accessible only at low tide. The entrance is a cave; the cave leads to a hidden harbour. The harbour is a city of thieves — and a union of freedmen. The rajas’ tax collectors meet here to plan. [LEGEND]

Heads — People & Factions

  1. Rani Kumar (Native Firebrand) — Raja Govind’s daughter, with a tiger coiled on her arm. “My father negotiates. I sharpen swords. The Admiralty will leave — or they will be buried here. There is no third way.” [POLITICS] [REBELLION]

  2. Sidhi Lighthouse Keeper* — Blind in one eye; carries a brass telescope. He can see the future through the lens. “Only the futures that end at sea,” he says. “The rest are private. I sell them to captains who need to avoid them — and to rajas who need to know when to strike.” [JUSTICE]

  3. Kahfagian Port-Master (Syndic Olena) — A thin woman with a brass hook for a hand. The hook is a stamp. She stamps every toll receipt. The stamp leaves a scar. She says it builds character. The scarred merchants say it builds grudges. [AUTHORITY]

  4. Pepper Factor (Native) — A thin woman with red-stained fingers and a black tongue. She is the raja’s eyes in the market. She can tell the quality of pepper by taste. She has never been wrong. She has also never been paid on time — by the Admiralty. [TRADE] [SPY]

  5. Shipwright Dara (Sidhi)* — A woman with a hammer and a jar of brass nails. She builds boats for freed slaves. Each boat is named after a dead family member. The names are never painted — only carved. The wood remembers. So do the rajas, who buy her boats for their own smugglers. [COMMUNITY]

  6. Admiral Voss — A gray-haired man with a glass eye. The glass eye is from a defeated pirate. The pirate still watches through it. Voss does not sleep well. The eye weeps salt water. He dreams of the rajas’ torches. [AUTHORITY]

  7. Raja Govind (Inland Hill Lord) — A young man in silk, come to the coast to sell iron. He has never seen the sea. He is afraid of it. He refuses to step on a boat. He negotiates from the shore, shouting through a brass horn. His daughter mocks him — but she will not leave his side. [POLITICS] [TRAGEDY]

  8. The Veiled Queen (Mangrove Delta) — A woman in a silver mask, ruler of the southern marshes. “I have spoken to the Admiralty’s spy, the Sidhi’s whisperer, and the Ashaani’s shadow. All of them will pay for my people’s freedom — or I will drown them all in the delta. The mask is not to hide my face. It is to hide my smile.” [MYSTERY] [MASTERMIND]

  9. Smuggler Captain (Neutral, works for highest bidder) — A woman with a parrot on her shoulder, a curved knife on her belt. The parrot is her navigator; it knows the reefs by sound. The parrot is blind. It has never been wrong. Her loyalty is to the cargo’s weight, not any flag. [STEALTH]

  10. The Black Bishop (Covenant Maritime Judge) — A judge who rules on maritime disputes. He wears a ring made from a ship’s nail. The ship was the first slaver he freed. The nail still has rust on it. He calls it his conscience. The Admiralty calls him a fool. The rajas call him a useful idiot. [JUSTICE]

  11. The Green Lantern’s Owner (Sidhi widow)* — A woman with no shadow. She sold her shadow for the tavern. The shadow now serves drinks. The customers do not notice. The shadow remembers every secret — and whispers them to the highest bidder. [MYSTERY]

  12. The Sea King of Kalinga (Pirate Lord, hired by rajas) — A pirate lord with a fleet of painted dhows. He has nine wives; each is from a different kingdom. The ninth is a spy. He knows. He keeps her for the gossip — and because she is the only one who knows the delta’s secret channels. [AUTHORITY] [PIRATE]

  13. The First Freedman (Legend, Sidhi)* — A slave who bought his freedom with a story. He now sits at the bottom of the Ninth Well. If you tell him a true story, he will tell you the location of a hidden lighthouse. The lighthouse is not on any map. It is where the rajas and the Sidhi first swore a secret pact. [LEGEND]

Clubs — Complications/Threats

  1. Pepper Dust Fire (Warehouse) — A spark ignites a native-owned warehouse; the air is flammable. [DISASTER] The workers are trapped. Save them or save the cargo. The fire was set by an Admiralty agent to frame the Sidhi. The rajas are watching.

  2. Admiralty Press Gang — Kahfagian marines grab bystanders for the fleet. [AUTHORITY] [COMBAT] The marines are drunk. They do not recognize rank. One of the bystanders is a raja’s son in disguise. The hills will demand blood if he is taken.

  3. Green Lantern Lit (Sidhi Tavern)* — Someone lights the forbidden lantern. The dead come. [DEATH] [FEAR] They are looking for the person who killed them. That person is a native tax collector who sold out his village. The crowd will tear him apart — or protect him. Choose.

  4. Shipbreaker’s Curse (Admiralty Yard) — A cursed ship in the yard breaks its chains. It wants a new victim — a ship’s captain. [DEATH] [COMBAT] The captain is an Admiralty loyalist. The ship was a slaver that carried native prisoners. The ghosts want his blood. Do you help him or let them take him?

  5. Toll Increase (Admiralty Decree) — The port-master doubles duties on all goods. The increase is a bribe. [AUTHORITY] [WEIGHT] The bribe is to the Black Bishop. The Bishop has already accepted. Your protest is treason. The native merchants are watching to see which side you choose.

  6. Coral Reef Piracy (Native-hired) — Pirates hide behind the reef; they attack at dusk. [COMBAT] [STEALTH] The pirates are hired by a raja to disrupt Admiralty supply ships. They will not harm Sidhi vessels — but they cannot always tell the difference.

  7. Elephant Rampage (Native Temple) — A temple elephant breaks free and runs through the market. [DISASTER] It is looking for its mahout, who was arrested by the Admiralty for smuggling. The mahout is innocent. The elephant will not hurt children. Admiralty marines are another matter.

  8. Smuggler’s Betrayal (Neutral) — A smuggler captain sells the party to the Admiralty. [SOCIAL] [TRAP] The betrayal was a test. The captain wants to see if you can escape. If you do, you are worthy of trust — and the rajas will contact you.

  9. Sea King’s Tax (Pirate Blockade, hired by rajas) — A Kalingan pirate fleet blockades the harbour. The tax is one ship. [AUTHORITY] [COMBAT] Choose which ship to give up. The Sea King will remember your choice. The crew will remember longer. The rajas are testing your loyalty.

  10. Drowned Priest’s Demand (Neutral) — The ghost appears during the day. He asks for a name. [DEATH] [SOCIAL] The name must be someone who owes a death — a slaver, a corrupt customs officer, a raja who sold his own people. If you cannot give one, he takes yours.

  11. Black Bishop’s Verdict (False) — The judge declares the party’s cargo forfeit. The verdict is false. [JUSTICE] [TRAP] The bishop is bribed. The briber is the Admiralty. Prove it, or lose everything. The real verdict is written in disappearing ink. The rajas’ spies are in the gallery.

  12. Sea King’s Ninth Wife (Spy, works for the Veiled Queen) — The spy reveals herself. She offers a deal: the Sea King’s treasure for safe passage. [POLITICS] [MAGIC] The treasure is a curse. The curse is a fragment of the Kalrantha amulet. The amulet whispers in your sleep. It tells you which faction will betray you first.

  13. Undercliff Collapse (Smuggler’s Cove) — A rockslide seals the smugglers’ cove. Inside are the party and a monster. [DEATH] [TRAP] The monster is a salt-crocodile. It is blind. It hunts by sound. The walls are listening — and so are the rajas’ spies, who will arrive at dawn to see who survived.

Diamonds — Rewards/Leverage

  1. Lighthouse Charter (Sidhi)* — The right to anchor in any Sidhi port without inspection. [TRAVEL] The key fits a lock on a sea-chest, not a harbour gate. The chest contains a map of smuggler’s coves — and the names of rajas who pay protection.

  2. Lighthouse Keeper’s Lens (Sidhi)* — Look through it to see the nearest slaver ship. [JUSTICE] The lens also shows the faces of the slaves on board. They look back. They do not blink. It also shows the brand on the slaver’s captain — and which raja sold them.

  3. Freedman’s Seal (Sidhi)* — Proof of emancipation. Glows when a slaver ship is near. [SOCIAL] It also glows when a Black Hand agent is near — and when a native informer is in the room. Trust the glow. It has never been wrong.

  4. Pepper Merchant’s Seal (Native) — A wax seal from a dead factor. Present it to sell pepper at double price. [TRADE] The factor’s ghost haunts the warehouse. He will ask for a story. The story must be about a bad deal — preferably one that cheated the Admiralty.

  5. Salt Widow’s Blessing (Native) — A bag of bitter salt. Throw it into the sea to calm a storm. [WEATHER] The salt is from the widows’ pans. They have blessed it with their tears. The storm will pause for one hour — long enough for a raja’s fleet to escape.

  6. Admiral’s Pardon (Kahfagian) — A signed document. Cancels one crime on the Brass Coast. [AUTHORITY] The Admiral signs with a glass eye. The signature is a lie. The pardon still works — but the eye watches you afterwards. It reports to the Admiralty’s spymaster.

  7. Sidhi Court Ruling (Sidhi)* — A written judgment. Win any maritime dispute in a Sidhi court. [JUSTICE] The ruling is valid only if witnessed by a lighthouse keeper. The keeper is Idris. He will witness for a story — and the story will reach the rajas within a day.

  8. Elephant’s Tooth (Native) — A tusk from a temple elephant. Present it to any temple for shelter. [SOCIAL] The elephant was executed for killing an Admiralty tax collector. The temple will not refuse. They will also not ask questions. They will offer you a cup of palm wine — and a message from the Veiled Queen.

  9. Shipbreaker’s Hook (Admiralty) — The hook of a dead shipbreaker. Use it to command the cursed ship in the yard. [MAGIC] The ship will obey one command — then it will try to drown you. The hook is warm. It remembers its owner’s last words: “Burn the toll ledgers.”

  10. Smuggler’s Map (Neutral) — A chart of hidden coves, tattooed on a dead sailor’s back. [TRAVEL] The skin is preserved in salt water. It must be kept wet or the map fades. The sailor’s ghost will guide you — for a fee. The fee is a promise to avenge him against the Admiralty.

  • The Three Faction Timers (see above). These are the campaign’s pressure gauge. Use them to determine which faction acts first when the party dithers.

  • The Sidhi Diaspora Network: Sidhi characters gain +1 die on social rolls with other Sidhi, but –1 die on social rolls with Admiralty officials. With native rajas, they are neutral (no modifier) — but the rajas will always treat them as “guests,” never as allies.

  • Native Hill Networks: Native characters gain +1 die on social rolls with rajas and hill folk, but –1 die on social rolls with Admiralty officials. With Sidhi, they are cautious (no modifier) — the Sidhi are useful, but they are still foreigners.

  • The Toll Timer [6] (renamed Admiralty Grip). Already covered above.

  • Green Flame Justice: Sidhi lighthouse courts operate on the principle of “witnessed truth.” Any party member may request a green flame judgment instead of a conventional trial. However, if the crime was committed against a native, the rajas will learn of it.

  • Ropeway Leverage: The ropeways are the arteries of the coast. Using this leverage ticks the Sidhi Ropeway Control timer by 1.

For the GM: Use the three faction timers as your campaign’s heartbeat. Every session, tick at least one timer based on the party’s actions (or inaction). Let the players see the powder keg growing. When a timer fills, something explodes — a rebellion, a strike, a massacre. The party’s job is not to prevent the explosion — it’s to survive it and choose who rises from the ashes.

Native Focus: The rajas are not victims — they are players. The Nine Kingdoms

The Peacock Throne and Its Rivals.

“The Peacock Throne is not a seat. It is a promise made by the first sun king: that the nine kingdoms would remain nine, never one, never fewer. I do not rule. I balance. A peacock’s tail has nine eyes. Each eye watches a different kingdom. When one closes, the throne weeps.”

I am a Temple Dancer. My dance is not for the king. It is for the monsoon. If my ankle bells ring true, the rains come. If they falter, the wells dry. They have never faltered. I do not sleep before a performance. I dream the rhythm. And I dream of the missing ninth.

Between the Satrapies plateau and the Brass Coast lies a fertile, contested lowland: the land of the Nine Kingdoms. This is the heart of Dhahara, where ancient dynasties still claim descent from the sun, and where the Peacock Throne of Ahimsa casts its shadow over half a dozen rival courts. Here, rivers are borders, elephants are war machines, and every harvest is a negotiation between kings who have never met but whose grandfathers swore blood oaths.

But here’s the secret the Maharaja doesn’t want you to know: the number “nine” is a lie. There have never been nine kingdoms. The number is a ritual — a prayer that the Hollow will not notice that one of the nine is always missing. The throne weeps because the missing kingdom is always the one that mattered. Its debt is still compounding.

The Covenant has a strong presence in the sacred cities; the Aeler have counting houses in the iron towns; the Sidhi sail up the rivers to trade. But the Nine Kingdoms belong to the Dhaharan — to their poets, their temple dancers, their bandit kings, and their queens who rule from behind silk screens. A king’s authority rests on his genealogy — how many generations back to the sun, how many treaties sealed, how many marriages made. But a king’s true power rests on how much of his family’s debt has been paid. The Peacock Throne of Ahimsa is the nominal center, but the other eight kings vie for influence, and the balance shifts with every monsoon.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge with mango pickle — the rice is from the royal granaries, the mangoes are from the king’s orchards. The king’s orchards are irrigated by the river. The river is contested. The contest is a negotiation. The negotiation is breakfast. The ninth grain of rice in every bowl is left uneaten. It is an offering to the missing kingdom.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To recite their genealogy. Nine Kingdoms children are taught the names of their ancestors back to the sun. A child who cannot recite is a child without a kingdom. A child without a kingdom is a servant. Servants are not fed. The ninth ancestor is never named. That is the gap where the Hollow sits.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their caste mark. The Nine Kingdoms have nine castes — priest, warrior, merchant, farmer, artisan, servant, outcast, foreigner, and the ninth — the caste that has no name. The ninth caste is the king’s spies. The spies are everywhere. The spies have no caste mark. Their faces are always the same. No one can describe them.

What is the most common crime? Tax evasion — but tax evasion is not a crime, it is a political statement. The king’s tax collectors are also the king’s spies. A merchant who evades taxes is a merchant who has something to hide. The king wants to know what. The king will find out. The ninth tax collector vanished last year. His shadow still walks the counting house.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Peacock Throne is empty. The Maharaja died seven years ago. His death was concealed by the courtiers, who rule in his name. The courtiers are the nine ministers. The ninth minister is a eunuch who controls the palace guards. The eunuch has the real power. He has no shadow. The guards do not notice.

Where do people go when they die? To the river. The dead are cremated on the ghats. Their ashes are scattered on the water. The water flows to the sea. The sea is the kingdom’s border. The border is a negotiation. The negotiation never ends. The ninth ghat is reserved for the missing kingdom. It has never been used. It is always warm.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a dance — a performance at the temple of the sun. The dance is judged by the priest. If the priest smiles, you are forgiven. If the priest weeps, you are not. If the priest is silent, you are cursed. The curse is the sun’s glare. The sun will not look away. The ninth dance ever performed was for a debt to the Hollow. The dancer is still dancing. No one watches.

What is the most important festival? The Coronation of the Monsoon (summer). The king offers a golden elephant to the rain clouds. The clouds accept or refuse. The clouds have refused for nine years. The king’s treasury is empty. The king’s patience is not. The ninth golden elephant was hollow. It was filled with ashes. The ashes belonged to the ninth king.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The golden elephant is hollow. It is filled with the ashes of the ninth king — the one who tried to unite the kingdoms and failed. His ghost rides the monsoon winds. His ghost is still trying. His ghost will never succeed. But every year, the clouds refuse because they are waiting for him to ask. He never does.

What do people eat for breakfast? Iron rations and tea—the rations are hardtack and dried meat, the tea is brewed from leaves that grow only above the snow line. The leaves are harvested by the Vent-Prior’s apprentices. The apprentices are chosen by lottery. The lottery is a death sentence.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read a pressure gauge. Himadri children are taught the eight pressures: Safe, Warning, Danger, Collapse, Flood, Fire, Seal, and the ninth—the pressure that means the mountain is breathing. The mountain’s breath is the miners’ death.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their bell. Every miner carries a breath-counter bell. A stranger whose bell rings too fast has been running. A stranger whose bell is silent is dead. A stranger whose bell rings the ninth note is a demon. The demons are sealed. The seals are cracking.

He is still alive. He has been counting his breaths for nine centuries. He has reached the ninth count. He is still counting.

The Himadri foothills are the highest inhabited place in the known world. Here, the Aelerian mountains scrape the sky, and the air is so thin that each breath is a measured gift. The native Aeler of these peaks — called the High Aeler or the Karak-Aeler (“Stone-Breath People”) — are not the mercantile dwarves of the lowland holds. They are severe, cold, and ancient, their faces carved by wind and frost, their voices rarely raised above a whisper. They do not trade in breath-promises for profit; they trade in survival. A Karak-Aeler’s word is iron not because of law, but because the mountain has no patience for lies.

The humans of the Himadri are equally remote: a scattering of monastic villages, goat-herding clans, and hidden fortress-temples that have never paid tribute to any king. Outsiders call them the High Folk, or simply the Mi-tsang — the Hidden People. They speak a language of tones and silences, and they venerate spirits that live in glaciers, hot springs, and the bones of extinct beasts. Their lamas (called Lopön) keep scrolls that predate the Utaran Empire, written in a script that no Aeler can read. They do not trust the lowlands, and the lowlands have learned not to cross their passes without an invitation.

The Karak-Aeler and the High Folk share an uneasy peace, brokered centuries ago when the first iron was traded for the secret of the high passes. Today, the dwarves mine the frozen mountains for ore and rare earths, while the humans tend the yak herds and the shrines that keep the mountain spirits from waking. The Covenant has a small mission in the highest valley, but its priests are always on the edge of frostbite and despair. The demon sealed in the Ninth Seal Mine — a fragment of Varash the Unmaker — is not a legend. It is a fact. The seal is cracking. And the mountain is listening.

What you notice first: The silence. Not the absence of sound, but the weight of it — as if the mountain itself is holding its breath. The way every Aeler touches a brass bell at their belt before speaking of distances. The way the High Folk never look directly at the peaks, only at the ground, as if the sky holds something they are not meant to see.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never count your breaths aloud in a mine or a high pass. The mountain listens, and if it hears you reach the ninth, it will take one — a breath, a memory, a shadow. The High Folk say that the shadow then walks the passes, counting.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Peak

Power here is measured in altitude, in the number of passes you know, and in the respect the mountain shows you. The Karak-Aeler maintain the iron mines and the toll-bridges, but they do not rule the High Folk — they coexist with them. The High Folk’s lamas negotiate on behalf of the mountain spirits, and the dwarves have learned to listen. The Covenant’s seal on the Ninth Seal Mine is maintained by a single, half-mad priest who has not seen the sun in forty years. The demon whispers promises of warmth, of gold, of the lowlands’ green fields. The miners cover their ears. The High Folk leave offerings of frozen butter tea at the seal’s entrance. So far, it has held.

Additional Features (High Altitude Mechanics)

  • Breath-Counting: In high passes (above 4,000 meters), characters must mark Fatigue for every hour of exertion. A character who deliberately paces their breathing (making a Spirit + Endurance test, DV 3) can reduce the Fatigue cost by 1. Counting aloud to nine in any context triggers a Mountain’s Attention roll (Spirit + Endurance, DV 4). On failure, the mountain takes something: 1 Fatigue, a minor memory (erase a fact), or a shadow (the character casts no reflection until they leave the high passes).

  • The Ninth Seal Timer [8]: The Covenant seal on the demon mine has a hidden timer that ticks when the demon whispers, when someone takes iron from the sealed tunnel, or when a breath-promise is broken nearby. When the timer fills, the seal cracks. The party can reset the timer by reinforcing the seal with a new Covenant rite (costs a memory) or by feeding the demon a story (costs a truth). The demon prefers stories about betrayal. It pays attention to those.

  • Mountain Offerings: The High Folk believe that the mountain must be fed with offerings of silence, memory, or warmth. A character who leaves an offering (a pinch of salt, a whispered secret, a lock of hair) at a shrine or a cairn may roll Spirit + Lore (DV 3). On success, the next weather hazard in that pass is downgraded (blizzard to snow, ice to frost). On failure, the offering is rejected — the character gains the Marked by the Mountain condition (the next environmental complication is automatically Desperate).

  • High Folk Taboos: The High Folk have many taboos: never point at a peak, never speak the name of a dead person in a pass, never refuse a cup of butter tea, never step over a prayer flag. Breaking a taboo in front of a High Folk witness causes the witness to fall silent and refuse to help. The GM may spend 1 SB to have a broken taboo attract the attention of a mountain spirit (a complication from the Clubs table).

  • Local Patrons (Syncretic Names):

    • Adur the Forge-Father — Oath of Flame and Light. The fire in the smelter, the heat that tempers iron and oaths.

    • Hemrir the Wanderer — The Sealed Gate. The keeper of thresholds; his shrines are bridge-posts and tunnel mouths.

    • The Breath-Eater — Khemesh. The pressure in the deep; the silence that follows a cave-in.

    • The Ice-Lama — The Pale Shepherd. The saint who froze to death; she guides travelers through whiteouts.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Ninth Seal and the Kalrantha Amulet (Dhahara): The demon in the sealed mine is a fragment of Varash the Unmaker, the same entity trapped in the Kalrantha amulet. The Veiled Sisters want to free this fragment to use against the Covenant. The Breath-less want to re-seal it permanently. The demon wants to be whole. It offers favors to anyone who brings it a shard of green glass.

  • Iron Trade and the Ngomebe Stone Men: The iron from the Himadri mines is shipped to Ngomebe to forge rollers for the walking cities. A disruption in the iron supply could stop a city mid-migration. The Ngomebe Mason-Kin have a permanent envoy at the Vent-Prior’s hall. The envoy is a woman who speaks only in stone-songs. She has never smiled.

  • Breath-Promises and the Covenant (Mykkiel): The Covenant recognizes Aeler breath-promises as legally binding, but only if witnessed by a judge. The Seal-Keeper is the only Covenant authority in the high passes. He has not witnessed a new promise in twenty years. He says the mountain remembers all obligations anyway.

  • The Silent Pass and the Hollow (Eastern Realms): The Silent Pass is a known threshold where the Hollow’s attention is thin. Aelaerem hedge-keepers leave offerings of salt at the pass entrance to keep the Oath-Keepers from following caravans. The salt must be from the pans of the Silent Widows. Any other salt will attract them. The Hedge-Keepers will not explain why. They say the salt knows.

In the Himadri foothills, power flows through breath, silence, and the weight of the mountain’s attention. The Karak-Aeler hold the mines and the forges, but they do not rule the High Folk — they coexist with them, bound by ancient compacts sealed in frozen iron. The High Folk’s lamas speak for the mountain spirits, and the spirits are not kind. The Covenant’s seal on the Ninth Seal Mine is a reminder of what happens when the mountain is not respected. The demon whispers. The mountain listens. And the passes remember every breath taken in them.

For the GM: Power in the Himadri is measured in altitude, in the number of breaths you can hold, and in the stories the mountain chooses to remember. Patronage often takes the form of breath-counters, iron ingots, or prayer flags — each tied to a specific pass, a specific spirit, a specific obligation. To emphasize the harsh, remote character:

  • Make breath-counting a constant tension: characters must manage their Fatigue carefully. The mountain punishes haste.

  • Let the Ninth Seal Timer be a visible campaign front. The party’s actions tick the timer; when it fills, the demon manifests. The demon does not fight — it negotiates. It offers warmth, iron, safe passage. It asks for small betrayals. The High Folk say that the demon’s bargains always leave a frost on the heart.

  • Use the High Folk taboos to create social tension. Breaking a taboo is not a crime — it is an insult to the mountain. The High Folk will not explain the taboos beforehand; they expect visitors to watch and learn. A character who makes an offering without being asked gains a small boon. A character who refuses an offering gains the mountain’s attention.

In the Himadri, the mountain forgets nothing — and the ninth breath is always waiting.
