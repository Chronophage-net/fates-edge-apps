At the table: A gathering with an empty seat

In Ayokha, a gathering is about to proceed without one community’s representative. The organizer wants a decision today; a family insists the absence means something is wrong. Let the party investigate, negotiate a delay, or argue that the missing community must still be heard.

Sihai

The Central Kingdom.

The Mandate of Heaven is not a right. It is a loan. The emperor borrows legitimacy from the ancestors, from the land, from the Hollow itself. When the loan is due, the empire falls. I do not serve the emperor. I serve the ledger that tracks the interest.

I am a Provincial Censor. My brush is heavier than any sword. A single stroke can erase a name, a family, a dynasty. The emperor reads my reports and trembles. He knows that I have written nine reports this year. The ninth is about him.

Sihai is the central kingdom, the seat of the Emperor and the Mandate of Heaven. Its canals carry grain from the south; its examination halls produce bureaucrats who govern by the brush. Power here is measured in ink, not iron. The Censorate watches everyone; the Salt Monopoly controls trade; the Lethai-thora keep the secrets that the Mandate prefers to forget. But here’s the secret the Emperor doesn’t want you to know: the Mandate is not a divine right. It is a loan. The ancestors are the creditors. The Hollow collects the interest. Every dynasty that has ever fallen did so because the interest came due and the emperor could not pay.

Beneath the mortal bureaucracy runs a deeper one: the Celestial Bureaucracy of gods, ghosts, and ancestors, where every official has a heavenly counterpart and every erased name becomes a hungry ghost. The Lethai-thora—the ancient archivists who taught the first emperors to write—still keep the hidden ledgers, the ones that record what the Censorate burns. A well-written couplet can appease a ghost; a forged seal can summon a demon. The Hollow’s ninth debt is the unpaid interest on the Mandate itself.

Never speak of succession before naming the Emperor. The Censorate has ears in every tea house. A misplaced word can erase your name from the rolls.

— Quiet Chancellor Wei, who has never smiled and whose seal is a laughing mouth

What do people eat for breakfast? Congee with preserved vegetables—the congee is rice boiled until it is memory, the vegetables are salted and aged in clay jars. The jars are sealed with the emperor’s own wax. The wax is a promise. The promise is that the vegetables will not poison you. The promise is sometimes broken.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To write their name. Sihai children are taught the eight strokes of the character “person”—the ninth stroke is the one that turns a name into a signature. A signature is a contract. A contract is a debt. A debt is a life.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their chop—the seal they carry to sign documents. A stranger whose chop is jade is a noble. A stranger whose chop is brass is a clerk. A stranger whose chop is wood is a merchant. A stranger with no chop is a ghost. Ghosts cannot sign contracts. Ghosts cannot be trusted.

What is the most common crime? Examination fraud—but fraud is not a crime, it is a family business. The examinations determine who governs. The questions are leaked in advance. The answers are memorized. The examiners are bribed. The system is a sieve. The sieve is called “merit.”

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Emperor is a figurehead. The real power is the Censorate—nine ministers who have never been seen together. Their names are not recorded. Their faces are not painted. Their orders are whispered. The Emperor signs what they tell him to sign. The Emperor has not refused in forty years.

Where do people go when they die? To the ancestral tablets. The dead are not buried. Their names are written on wooden tablets, placed in the family shrine. The tablets are fed with rice and tea. The dead eat the steam. The dead are never full.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a calligraphy—a scroll written in your own blood. The scroll is presented to the wronged party’s ancestral shrine. If the ancestors accept, you are forgiven. If the scroll bursts into flame, you are not. If the scroll writes itself, the ancestors are angry. The ancestors’ anger is a famine.

What is the most important festival? The Festival of the Ancestors (autumn). The tablets are cleaned and polished. The dead are invited to feast. The feast is laid out on the family altar. The food is removed after an hour. The dead have eaten the essence. The essence is memory.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Censorate’s ninth minister is a woman—the emperor’s own mother, who was supposed to have died in childbirth. She faked her death to escape the politics of the court. She has been running the Censorate from a hidden villa in the western hills. The emperor does not know. The emperor’s mother signs his death warrants. He signs them too.

Quick Reference

Tagline: The Mandate is a memory. The ancestors are listening. The Hollow collects what is forgotten.

Quiet Chancellor (Elite): “The Mandate of Heaven is not a right. It is a story we tell ourselves — that the emperor remembers the names of the dead. When the story falters, the empire falls. I do not serve the emperor. I serve the silence where the forgotten names should be.”

Genre / Mood: Celestial bureaucracy, ink-washed horror, examination hell, the weight of forgotten ancestors.

A well-written couplet can appease a ghost; a forged seal can summon a demon. The Hollow’s ninth weight is the silence at the heart of the Mandate itself. Every dynasty inherits its legitimacy; every silence is a weight.

What you notice first: The smell of ink and canal water, but also incense and grave-soil. The way officials stamp every document with a brass seal — and the way the seal leaves a faint watermark that looks like a face. The constant ticking of water-timers, but sometimes the ticking skips a beat, and the shadows pause to listen.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never speak of succession before naming the Emperor. The Censorate has ears in every tea house — and the ghosts have ears in every wall. A misplaced word can make a name vanish from the rolls, and from the memory of the ancestors.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Brush

Power here is measured in examination scores, salt licenses, and the thickness of a family’s archive. The Emperor rules, but the Censorate audits, the Salt Monopoly finances, and the Lethai-thora remember what the Censorate burns. A provincial governor’s authority rests on his examination rank; a merchant’s on his salt license. The dead are not gone — they are merely demoted. The Hungry Ghost Festival is the only time the Celestial Bureaucracy accepts appeals. The Hollow watches from the missing ninth step, the unpoured ninth cup, the unsaid ninth name.

Additional Features (Celestial Bureaucracy)

  • The Mandate Timer [8] (Shared Campaign Timer ): Tracks the stability of the dynasty. The party inherits 2 ticks at the start of any adventure set in Sihai (the inherited burden). The timer ticks when the emperor’s health fails, when a succession dispute arises, when a major corruption scandal is exposed, or when the party breaks an oath witnessed by an ancestor. When the timer reaches 4: the Censorate becomes more aggressive (all audits start at Desperate Position). When it reaches 8: the Mandate fails — the Hollow claims the throne, civil war erupts, and the party must choose a side or flee. Resetting the timer requires an Ancestor Rite at the Imperial Ancestral Temple (downtime, costs 1 Supply and a truthful confession) or a public execution of a corrupt official (costs 1 Boon and may create enemies).

  • The Two Ledgers (Ancestral Names Mechanic): Every character has two “names”: their Mortal Name (what the Censorate knows) and their Ancestral Name (recorded in the Celestial Ledger, kept by the Lethai-thora). The Ancestral Name cannot be erased by mortal means. Discovering a character’s Ancestral Name requires a Research Timer (size 6) in the Lethai-thora archives. Once known, the player may invoke the Ancestral Name once per session to gain  + 2 dice on any roll related to that ancestor’s domain (e.g., a farmer’s ancestor aids harvest, a soldier’s ancestor aids battle). However, invoking the Ancestral Name creates an Ancestral Weight (size 4 timer). When it fills, the ancestor demands a service — usually something the ancestor failed to do in life (e.g., “Apologize to my widow” or “Find my stolen seal”). Refusing the demand marks 1 Corruption and adds 2 ticks to the Mandate Timer .

  • Censorate Audit (Social Combat Mechanic): The Censorate can audit any character at any time. An audit is a social combat with three phases:

    1. Pleadings (Presence + Sway vs. Censor’s Lore). Success grants  + 1 die to the next phase.

    2. Evidence (Wits + Insight vs. DV 4). Success reveals a hidden burden or a forged document; failure gives the Censor a piece of evidence against you.

    3. Judgment (Endurance + Lore vs. DV 5). Success: the character is cleared. Failure: the Censorate erases a name — the character loses one Bond (that person no longer remembers them) or one Asset (it is seized), and the Mandate Timer ticks  + 1.

    Players can avoid an audit by: Bribing the Censor (costs 1 Honey Jar or 1 Boon), Presenting a forged document (requires a Forgery Timer , size 4), or Calling a ghost witness (requires a Spirit + Lore test, DV 4; the ghost cannot lie, but it will ask for a favor in return – usually a promise that creates a new Ancestral Weight).

  • Examination Hell (Dungeon Mechanic): The Forbidden Garden contains a subterranean examination hall where the ghosts of failed candidates still write their answers. The party can enter to find a lost Ancestral Name – but the hall is alive.

    • The hall has a Stress Timer (size 8). Each failed roll, each lie spoken, each broken taboo ticks the timer.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Duel Law: Disputes in Nihori are settled by duel to first blood. The loser yields a story; the winner returns a cup of sake. A duel can be witnessed by anyone; the witness’s word is law. Refusing a duel is an admission of guilt. In the shogunate courts, a duel verdict can overturn a written agreement.

  • Storm Season Timer [8]: Tracks the approach of the monsoon. When it fills, a typhoon strikes — all travel becomes Desperate for 2 legs. The timer ticks each day during storm season. The Shrine Maidens can delay it by one day for a suitable offering (a tide-charm, a memory, or a promise).

  • Kami Favor (Reputation Track): Instead of debt, track Kami Favor [6]. Gain favor by respecting shrines, winning duels honorably, and appeasing storm-spirits. Spend favor to ask a kami for a boon (see Kami Favor Boons table). At 0 favor, shrines are hostile — all social rolls at shrines are Desperate.

  • Oni Corruption Track [6]: Represents the influence of the Oni-Eyed’s cult and the Crater Oni. Tick when a character makes a bargain with an oni, drinks oni-brewed sake, or speaks the forbidden name. At 3 segments: the character’s shadow grows horns. At 6: the character becomes oni-touched — they gain  + 1 die to intimidation but suffer  − 1 die to all social rolls with shrine maidens and can never enter a shrine without being attacked.

  • Shogunate Faction Timers: Track the three daimyo’s schemes:

    • Southern Serpent’s Ambition [6] — advances when she bribes officials, builds her fleet, or sponsors pirate raids. When full, she declares open war on the Shogun.

    • Northern Crane’s Patience [6] — retreats when he is humiliated or his allies defect. When it reaches 0, he abandons the Shogun’s peace and marches south.

    • Oni-Eyed’s Ascension [8] — advances each time a character accepts an oni bargain or the party fails to stop a cult ritual. When full, the Crater Oni manifests in the material world.

  • Yokai and the Old Exchange: Unlike simple monsters, yokai in Nihori treat with travelers. They may offer bargains, demand tolls, or curse those who break taboos. Use the Yokai Encounters table to introduce them, and track any promises made to a yokai as a Yokai Promise timer [4]. When it fills, the yokai returns to call it in.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Rain’s Due (Shared Timer , size 8): Every broken promise to a river-spirit, every unpaid offering, every late monsoon gesture ticks the timer. The party inherits the God-King’s obligations at the start of any adventure set in Ayokha – they begin with 2 ticks already accrued (old burdens). When the timer reaches 4: the astrologer’s predictions become unreliable (roll twice for weather, take worse). When it reaches 8: a Oath-Keeper rises from the river and demands a story, a memory, or a season of service. Resetting the timer requires a Monsoon Rite (downtime, costs 1 XP or a story worth a season’s wages) or a sacrifice to the Drowned Nat (a memory or a magical item).

  • Monsoon Timer [6]: Tracks the approach of the rainy season. When it fills, the monsoon arrives — all river travel becomes Desperate for 2 legs. The timer ticks each day during the dry season. The astrologer can predict the exact day; a successful Wits + Lore test (DV 4) reveals the remaining ticks. The party can slow the timer by performing Rain Offerings (betel leaves, silk scraps, or stories) – each offering ticks the timer back by 1, but adds 1 tick to Rain’s Due.

  • River Court Justice: Disputes on the river are settled by a floating court. The judge is a Lethai-ar Vigil. The verdict is written on a clay tablet and thrown into the water. If the tablet floats, the verdict is true. If it sinks, the verdict is false — and the judge is drowned. A character may attempt to influence the verdict by offering a bribe (a Treasure or a story) before the tablet is thrown. If the bribe is a story, the GM gains 1 SB; if a Treasure, the judge’s verdict tilts in your favor automatically (no test).

  • Betel Leaf Hospitality: Offering a betel leaf is an oath of hospitality. Refusing is a grave insult. Accepting binds both parties to non-violence for the duration of the visit. Breaking this oath marks 1 Corruption and begins a River’s Grudge [4] timer. When the timer fills, a nat appears and demands an equal price (1 Harm, a memory, or a public apology).

  • Astrologer’s Scroll (Player Resource): A character who earns the astrologer’s trust (or steals his notes) may carry a copy of his calendar. The scroll has 3 pages. Each page can be used to:

    • Predict the Monsoon: Once per journey, ask the GM if the weather will turn against you in the next leg. The GM answers truthfully (yes/no).

    • Forge a Schedule: Once per session, treat a failed navigation roll as a success, but add 1 tick to Rain’s Due (the astrologer will notice the forgery eventually).

    • Blackmail an Official: Gain +1 Position on social rolls against any Ayokha official who relies on the monsoon schedule (harbor masters, customs officers, grain factors). Using this burns the page.

    Refilling the scroll requires a downtime action and a bribe to the astrologer (1 Boon or a story worth a season’s gossip).

  • The Ninth Terrace (Forbidden Zone, Mechanic): The terrace is flooded only once every nine years. The rice grown there is for the God-King alone – eating it is treason. But the rice is also the only thing that can cure the monsoon blight (a crop disease that ticks the Monsoon Timer by 2 each day). Entering the Ninth Terrace requires a Water-Key (a coral token, acquired from the astrologer or a nat). The terrace is guarded by Hungry Ghosts – the spirits of farmers who starved when the monsoon failed. They attack anyone whose Rain’s Due timer is 4 or higher. Harvesting the rice takes 3 actions. Each action triggers a ghost attack (Spirit + Endurance test DV 4 or take 1 Fatigue). Success yields a Ninth Terrace Rice grain (see Diamonds).

    • The Rain That Waits – Ikasha. She Who Sleeps beneath the flood; her dream is the monsoon’s hesitation.

    • The Unbroken Cord – Inaea. The river’s memory woven into silk; every oath is a knot.

In Ayokha, power flows through the monsoon, the river, and the weight of a witnessed oath. The God-King rules from his floating palace, but the astrologer sets the calendar, the Lethai-ar hold the records, and the spirit-mediums speak for the nats. No one rules alone — and everyone owes a tithe to the rain.

Aelaerem of the Eastern Realms

The same hearth, different responsibilities

At the table. The welcome can be sincere. Give meals, work, affection, and ordinary disagreements enough space that the uncanny does not consume the culture. Shared customs connect Aelaerem communities; they do not require identical campaigns.

A first situation. In Amedell, ask who belongs at a household’s table when a familiar guest returns changed. The Hollow’s Neighbors.

The Aelaerem of the eastern realms have adapted their hedge-magic to thresholds of a different kind: bamboo groves that whisper in the dark, temple stairs that count your heartbeats, and the silence after a storm bell that has no clapper. The Almanac of Ordinary Days (Eastern Aelaerem)

Their western cousins on the Dolmis inherited a defense and cannot explain it: eight cups, a ninth left for the empty chair, and no answer as to why. The eastern Aelaerem have gone the other way entirely. They will tell you what the thread and the bell and the bowl of salt are for, at length, and they will show you the boundary and where it has moved this year. They are not braver than their cousins and they are not less afraid. They have simply concluded that a thing you maintain is safer than a thing you avoid, and that a forgetfulness can be reminded on a schedule. Their villages keep the accounts of that maintenance the way a coastal town keeps a sea wall — with a rota, a budget, and a great deal of complaining about whose turn it is.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge with pickled vegetables—the rice is from the paddies, the vegetables are salted in clay pots buried at the boundary. The salt is blessed by the hedge-keeper. The vegetables are always a little bitter. The bitterness is a reminder.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To tie the eight boundary knots. The ninth knot is never tied—it is left loose for the Hollow to find. If a child ties the ninth knot, the Hollow will visit that night and ask for a name. The child’s name.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their shadow. A stranger whose shadow is too short has been touched by the Hollow. A stranger whose shadow moves independently is already lost. A stranger with no shadow is the Hollow itself, wearing a borrowed face. Do not invite them in.

What is the most common crime? Thread-theft—stealing a red thread from a boundary marker. The thread is not valuable, but cutting it breaks a promise. The Hollow becomes restless. The thief is marked: their shadow will shrink one inch each night until they return the thread.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Hollow is not a single thing. It is a place—the space between heartbeats, the pause before a sneeze, the silence after a bell. The Aelaerem do not guard against a monster. They guard against the gaps.

Where do people go when they die? To the salt. The dead are cremated, and their ashes are mixed with salt and scattered along the boundary. The salt draws the Hollow’s attention away from the living. The dead become the new boundary. They do not rest. They watch.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You leave a cup of sake and a lock of hair at the place where the wrong was done. The Hollow will witness. If the sake is gone by dawn and the hair is untouched, you are forgiven. If the hair is gone, the Hollow has taken your apology as a promise. You are now bound. Do not break that promise.

What is the most important festival? The Retting of the Threads (spring). All red threads on the boundary are taken down, washed in salt water, and re-tied. The village is silent for one night—no bells, no songs, no whispers. The Hollow listens. The Hollow remembers. If a thread is tied wrong, the Hollow will correct it. The correction is always a death.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The first red thread was not tied by an Aelaerem. It was tied by the Hollow itself, before it forgot what it was. The thread is still there, at the First Boundary. If it is untied, the Hollow will remember its true name. Then it will stop being hungry. It will become something else. No one knows what.

Regional Mood: The Chore That Holds the World Up

There are no lords here and no courts, only the rota. Standing is measured in weeks walked, threads re-tied, salt hauled up the terraces, and whether your handwriting on the board can be read by lamplight. A hedge-keeper’s authority is exactly as long as her stretch of boundary and not one pace further, and she will tell you so with some satisfaction. Outsiders read all of this as quaint. It is not quaint. It is a maintenance schedule for the edge of the world, kept by people who bake very good bread, and the reason they are so calm about the Hollow is that they know precisely how many mornings of neglect it takes before the calm stops being warranted.

They are, accordingly, the most welcoming people in the Eastern Realms and the least sentimental. A guest is fed, bathed, given the warm end of the room, and put on the rota by the second morning. Refusing the work is not rude. It is simply understood that you are the sort of person who will be somewhere else when the boundary drifts, and you will be fed anyway, and no one will trust you with a thread.

The Boundary’s Drift (Campaign Timer)

The Hollow does not attack. It forgets — and where it forgets, the boundary moves. Track the state of the valley’s maintenance with a visible timer:

Boundary Drift [8] — Advances when:

  • A week of the rota goes unwalked (+1)

  • A rice bowl is checked before dawn (+1)

  • A bell is rung unmuffled after dusk, or anyone whistles after dusk (+1)

  • Someone counts aloud past eight, anywhere in the valley (+2)

  • A red thread is cut, taken, or tied with the ninth knot (+2)

  • The GM spends 1 SB to let a small neglect compound (+1)

Crucially, and unlike the Hollow’s attention in the west, this timer runs backwards under work. See Work-Parties, below. The valley is not doomed; it is behind.

At 4 segments: The mirror-paddies stop reflecting the sky properly — they reflect the terraces as they were some other year. Cats will not cross the lanes. The rota-board’s oldest entries fade. At 8 segments: The boundary has moved. Something is inside it that was outside it yesterday. Roll on What Steps Through. Until the party or the village clears at least 3 segments, every night in the valley opens with a Story Beat spent on the uncanny.

Work-Parties (clearing the timer)

This is the region’s signature move and the reason to keep the timer on the table. Boundary work is dull, physical, communal, and it is the only thing that clears Drift.

  • Walk a week (clear 1). A full day spent re-tying one stretch: Body + Endurance DV 3, or Wits + Craft DV 3 if you are re-knotting rather than hauling. A Partial clears the segment but costs 1 Fatigue or 1 Supply.

  • Haul the salt (clear 1). Carry blessed salt up eight terraces. Body + Endurance DV 3. Followers with Cap 2+ can do this unaided; it is the classic job to hand a follower.

  • Keep the ledger (clear 1, once per season). An afternoon reconciling the rota-board against what was actually walked: Wits + Insight DV 4. On a Clean Success, also learn where the next drift will occur — ask the GM one true question about the boundary.

  • Stand the Retting (clear 3). The spring rite: every thread down, washed in salt water, re-tied in one silent night. This is a scene, not a roll — run it as a Silent Night [6] Timer where each segment is a stretch re-tied and every noise the party makes is a segment the GM takes back. Clearing it clears 3 Drift and is worth 2 XP to everyone who kept silent.

  • Feed the boundary (clear 1, costs). Set out the bowls yourself and do not look until dawn. No roll. Whoever set them out wakes with one memory gone soft at the edges — they can still name the thing, but not describe it. The GM gains 1 SB.

Escalation. A Tier I party clears Drift a segment at a time in one valley. A Tier III or IV party is asked to walk the First Boundary — eight days, eight prefectures, a rota nobody has kept in living memory — and that is a Long Rota [10] Timer with real teeth at every segment. The chore does not get easier. It gets longer, and there is more of the world behind it.

What Steps Through (d6)

When Boundary Drift reaches 8, roll 1d6:

  1. The Extra Stair. The shrine steps gain one. Anyone who climbs them arrives a little wrong: their shadow is short by a hand’s width until they descend backwards, counting nothing.

  2. The Guest Who Leaves No Steam. A traveller takes the bathhouse. Perfectly polite, correct in every courtesy, and the water does not fog around them. Turn them out and the Drift ticks +2. Let them stay and they will still be there in the spring, and so will their appetite.

  3. The Bowl That Was Checked. Every rice bowl in the valley is empty and clean, and no one set them out. Something has been keeping the rota. It will want the credit — and the seat at the board.

  4. The Village in the Water. The mirror-paddies show a Yatsuno with nine hamlets. People in it wave. If anyone waves back, that person’s name is now written on the ninth column of the rota-board in their own hand.

  5. The Borrowed Face. A villager comes home twice on the same evening. Both are warm. Both are hungry. Both know the rota. The village will not choose; that is not their way. They will feed both and wait, which is worse.

  6. The Unmuffled Bell. A bell rings once, at the wrong hour, with no one at the rope. Every promise made in the valley that day is now heard, and the Hollow keeps the accounts. All Obligation marked this session is doubled until someone confesses a lie at the bell tower.

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = treasure. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Diamonds here are almost never loot. They are things given by people who have decided about you: a week walked in your name, a place on the board, the warm end of the room. Hand them over in a scene, not in a pile.

  • If any A appears, the uncanny should be tidy. The Hollow in the east does not smash. It keeps the rota, straightens the bowls, and writes neatly in your column.

  • Track Boundary Drift [8] as a campaign pressure — and put the clearing conditions where the players can see them. A timer that only goes up teaches a table to despair; this one is a chore list, and the whole regional flavour is that the chore list works.

  • Let the cosy things be mechanically real. The bathhouse clears Fatigue. The persimmon eaves make a safe rest. The tea is genuinely good. A folk horror where the comfort is fake is a cheap one; here the comfort is true, and it is load-bearing, and that is what makes neglect frightening.

Additional Features

  • Take Your Turn: A character who has walked a week within the last season shifts one step safer on their first social action in any Aelaerem settlement in the Eastern Realms. Word travels along the rota faster than along the roads.

  • Eat Before You Talk: Accepting food before opening business cancels the first SB generated by negotiation in that scene. Refusing is not an insult and carries no penalty — but the household will note that you did not eat, and will remember it later, when someone is checking who is who.

  • Stop at Eight: Any character who deliberately breaks off a count at eight — of steps, coins, arrows, breaths — may re-roll one failed die on that action, once per scene. The habit is worth having and is exactly as easy to acquire as it sounds.

Nihori will not name the ninth wave, Ayokha pours the ninth cup for the drowned, and here nobody bathes ninth. A party who has met all three should be told, once, that these are one thing.

  • The Traveler: A gray pilgrim occasionally joins a work-party, carries more than his share, eats nothing, and is gone by the fourth terrace. Stretches he helped on do not drift for a year. Nobody has ever got his name and everyone has stopped trying.

Eastern Aelaerem Superstitions (burn for +1 die once)

  • Break Off at Eight: Stop a count aloud at eight, deliberately, where others hear you. Cancel one Boundary Drift tick, or take –1 die on your next Insight roll (your choice).

The eastern Aelaerem have no spells. They have a schedule. Their power lies in the fact that a thing which is maintained is a thing the Hollow cannot forget into its mouth — and maintenance, unlike courage, can be delegated, written down, argued about, and done again next week by somebody else. A red thread with eight knots is not magic; it is a statement that this line is attended to, and attention is the one thing the Hollow does not have. To be given a week on the board is to be told you are part of what holds the valley up. To lose it is not exile. It is simply that people stop counting you, and here, more than anywhere, a thing that is not counted is a gap.

Aelinnel of the Inland Sea

The Bell-Tenders.

The Hollow is not a place. It is a miscount. The ninth step that is never there, the ninth cup that is never poured, the ninth name that is never spoken. We do not fight the Hollow. We leave one empty chair, one unlit lamp, one uncounted stone. That is the price of sitting at the table.

I asked an Aelinnel bell-tender how she kept the tide-spirits from flooding her village. She tapped her temple. “I count them,” she said. “One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. Then I stop. They know that if I ever count nine, I am not counting them.”

The Aelinnel of the East do not hide from the spirits — they coexist. Small spirits inhabit every rock, tree, and wave. The Aelinnel have learned to negotiate with them using copper bells, precise counting, and occasional offerings of pickled plums. They are the engineers of shrines, the builders of floating bridges, and the keepers of the bell-lines that keep tide-spirits from straying. Some say Kuva, the sky, taught them the first counting song, and that the Hollow is simply the space where the count fails.

Unlike their western kin, who lean toward the Gift of the Mind, the Eastern Aelinnel practice a folk mathematics that blends logic and superstition. They believe every spirit has a number-name — a frequency that can be rung out of a bell, a sequence of steps that can be walked, a knot that can be tied. To offend a spirit is to miscount.

Never count to nine aloud near a shrine. The spirits count with you. When you reach nine, they will ask: “What comes after nine?” The answer is always the Hollow.

— Bell-Warden Tumble, who writes letters on copper wax

What you notice first: The sound of copper bells — not ringing in sequence, but chiming in overlapping waves. The way every bridge has a missing ninth plank. The smell of pickled plums and salt.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never count to nine aloud near a shrine. The spirits count with you. When you reach nine, they will ask: “What comes after nine?” The answer is always the Hollow.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Count

Power here is measured in bells rung, tides predicted, and the precision of a threshold. The Aelinnel have no kings or councils; their authority rests on the bell-wardens who maintain the shrines and the tide-counters who read the waves. A village that loses its bell-warden is a village that the tide-spirits will flood. A bridge without its ninth plank missing is a bridge that the Hollow will cross. The Aelinnel do not worship the spirits; they transact with them. An offering is not a prayer — it is a payment. A bell is not a musical instrument — it is a contract.

Starting Location

A floating shrine on the Inland Sea, where copper bells hang from every corner. A Bell-Warden rings a bell eight times, then stops: “The tide-spirit is restless. It wants a ninth bell. I will not give it. You — count the waves. Tell me how many break before the sun sets. If you miscount, the spirit will ask you for a story. Do not miscount.”

The Almanac of Ordinary Days (Aelinnel)

The Bell-Tenders have made deliberate incompleteness into a civil engineering discipline. One empty chair at the table, one lamp unlit on the row, one stone left uncounted in the wall — and every public work in the Inland Sea is built with a gap in it on purpose, signed off by an inspector whose entire office is verifying that the gap is still there. Western Aelinnel keep polite iron and a moonlit propriety they could not defend in argument. These ones have a form for it, a fee schedule, and an appeals process. Ask an inspector what happens if a gap is closed and you will get a straight, professional answer about structural risk that does not, on later reflection, explain anything at all.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge with pickled plums — the plums are fermented in salt brine and stored in copper-lidded barrels. The brine is never poured out; it is passed down through generations. The oldest batch is said to be nine generations old. No one has tasted it.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count the eight safe numbers. Children are taught to count pebbles, footsteps, and bell-rings — but always to stop at eight. The ninth is never spoken. A child who says “nine” aloud is sent to sit by the missing plank of the bridge until they forget they ever said it.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their fingers. Aelinnel tide-counters often have only eight fingers — the ninth was a miscount they paid for. A stranger with nine fingers has never made a mistake. A stranger with seven has paid a heavy debt. A stranger with no fingers is either a spirit or someone who counted to nine and lost everything.

What is the most common crime? Bell-theft — but not for the copper. A stolen bell can be rung at the wrong time, creating a miscount. The thief is not punished by law; they are given to the tide-spirit whose count was broken. The spirit decides the price.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The First Bell was not cast by an Aelinnel. It was found — already ringing, already counting. No one knows who made it. No one knows why it rang. The shards are scattered across the Inland Sea. When the ninth shard is found, the First Bell will ring again. The Hollow will hear it.

Where do people go when they die? To the bell-line. The dead are not buried. Their ashes are mixed with copper dust and cast into new bells. The bells are hung on the bell-lines. When the tide-spirits ring them, the dead speak. Mostly they say: “Count carefully.”

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a count — a public recitation of the wrong, spoken in eights. “I took the offering. I broke the bell. I counted to nine.” Then you wait. If a spirit rings a bell eight times, you are forgiven. If it rings nine times, you are cursed. If no bell rings, the Hollow will visit you at midnight.

What is the most important festival? The Retuning of the Bells (autumn). Every bell on the Inland Sea is struck once, in sequence, from the highest tide to the lowest. The sound travels for miles. The spirits listen. If a bell is out of tune, the spirits will correct it by morning. The correction is always a death — but the bell will ring true for another year.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The ninth bell was never meant to be cast. It was a mistake — a foundry worker poured one bell too many. They tried to hide it, but it rang once on its own. The tone was the Hollow’s name. The bell still exists. It is buried under a shrine on a small island. No one knows which island. Where do people go when they die? Into the tide-charms. The dead are not buried. Their names are carved onto shells and placed in oiled leather pouches. The living carry them. The dead walk with the living. When the pouch is lost, the dead become the Hollow.

The Lethai-al of Nihori once guarded a drowned kingdom — an archipelago that sank when a forbidden name was spoken. Their blood still carries the salt of that ancient catastrophe. Now they are wanderers in the oldest sense: masterless, landless, bound to no lord but fiercely bound to each other and to the storm spirits that swirl above the waves. They walk from shrine to shrine, from port to port, following the processional roads that circle the island. They do not farm. They do not build. They carry everything they own on their backs or in wooden carts. Their duels are not to the death — they are to the first draw of blood, after which the loser must join the procession and carry the winner’s burden for a season. Their courts are crossroads. Their temples are the road itself. The Eastern Ninth lives here: the Hollow does not hide under hills — it breathes in the salt foam. The ninth wave is never the largest; it is the one that remembers your name.

What you notice first: The smell of salt and incense. The way the Lethai-al never sit with their backs to the sea. The one rule that kills newcomers: Never walk under a torii gate without making an offering. The gate is a threshold. The road on the other side is not the same road you left.

Regional Mood: The Processional Road

Power here is measured in shrines visited, storms survived, and the number of dead ancestors carried as tide-charms. The Lethai-al have no king, no courts, no written law. Their authority is personal and processional: a Procession Elder’s word is law because she has walked the circuit more times than anyone else. Their guilds are not guilds — they are processional bands, each with its own rhythm, its own counting song, its own patron shrine. A band that walks too fast offends the spirits; one that walks too slow loses the tide. The Lethai-al do not believe in ownership of land; they believe in passage rights, storm oaths, and the memory of the drowned. Every crossroads is a court. Every storm is a messenger. What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their lamp. A Lethai-ar’s lamp is a public record of their witness. A stranger whose lamp burns green has kept every oath. Where do people go when they die? To the green flame. The dead are cremated, and their ashes are mixed with lamp oil. The oil is poured into the Vigil lanterns. The dead become the light. The light witnesses. The light does not lie.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a new cord — woven from your own hair, dyed with your own blood. The cord is tied between you and the wronged party. The Lethai-ar of Ayokha are the living bridges between mortals and the river spirits that carry the world’s promises. Their ancestors were river-folk that ascended to the realm of the fair folk, and they now walk the middle path, a step between bodies of water and the world of men. They keep lanterns that never go out, record every contract spoken on the monsoon wharves, and bind their vows with silken cords dyed in river mud. Unlike their northern kin who swore to distant deities, the Lethai-ar serve Benzaiten (aligned with The Witness) or Inari (aligned with The Traveler). Their vows are physical cords, braided from their own hair and dyed with river mud. When a vow is fulfilled, the cord is burned. When it is broken, the cord tightens around the oath-breaker’s wrist until blood is drawn (Harm 1). The Ayokhan taboo against the ninth cup is enforced by the Lethai-ar: the ninth cup is for the river spirits, and pouring it is an invitation to the Hollow.

What you notice first: The smell of river water and oiled silk. The way every Lethai-ar carries a small oil lamp, even at noon. The constant murmur of contracts being witnessed — a merchant’s promise, a captain’s oath, a lover’s vow.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never pour the ninth cup of tea or sake in a Lethai-ar’s presence. The ninth cup is for the witness. Pour it, and you have invited the river to judge you. The river is not merciful.

Regional Mood: The Weight of the Witness Cord

Power here is measured in lanterns kept lit, cords tied, and the number of oaths witnessed. The Lethai-ar have no army, no treasury, no territory. They have the river, and the river remembers everything. Every contract in Ayokha requires a Lethai-ar witness to be legally binding. The God-King’s astrologers may set the calendar, but the Lethai-ar keep the chronicle. Their neutrality is absolute: they will witness a vow between enemies as readily as between allies. The price of a witness cord is not silver — it is a promise to keep the river clean, to light a lamp at dusk, to speak no word that cannot be unsaid.

The Silent Ledgers.

We do not burn the dangerous scrolls. We bury them in plain sight, on shelves where no one thinks to look. The Emperor’s Censor once stood in our archive and asked for a tax ledger. We gave him one. He did not notice the shelf behind him held the true history of his own grandfather’s death. Silence is not emptiness. It is a door that opens only inward.

My grandmother tied this scroll with a silk cord. The cord has nine knots. The ninth knot is a secret she never told me. When I die, I will tie a tenth. Someone will untie it in a century. They will learn why the emperor’s concubine wept on the night of the lantern festival. Some secrets are too heavy for one lifetime.

The Lethai-thora were once courtiers of a celestial fey realm that spanned the Valewood and the eastern hills. When the old fey courts fell — when the Alder King withdrew and the Hazel Queen sealed her gates — many Lethai-thora fled east. They crossed the mountains with nothing but armfuls of scrolls and the memory of a civilisation that had forgotten how to die.

In the fertile river valleys of what would become Sihai, they found scattered agrarian tribes fighting over water and silence. The Lethai-thora did not conquer. They taught. They taught the tribes to write contracts instead of drawing blood, to measure grain instead of stealing it, to record debts instead of nursing grudges. Over generations, the tribes became a kingdom, the kingdom became an empire, and the empire became Sihai. The Lethai-thora became its memory.

Now they dwell in hidden pavilions, preserving ”forgotten ledgers“ that record what the Mandate of Heaven prefers to forget. Their knowledge is a burden: each secret they preserve slowly erases a piece of themselves. A Lethai-thora who guards a forbidden truth may find that she can no longer recall the color of her mother’s dress — a small price, she says, for keeping the ledger whole. They are no longer fey. They are no longer fully human. They are something in between: scribes of a story that has no ending, archivists of a past that never quite happened.

The Almanac of Ordinary Days (Lethai-thora)

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge with dried fish — the fish are smoked over fires fed with old scrolls. The smoke smells of forgotten contracts. The porridge is always lukewarm. The archives are never truly warm.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To read without moving their lips. A Lethai-thora child who whispers a secret aloud is sent to sit in the Chamber of the Ninth Scroll until they learn that silence is a shield. Some children never come back. They become scribes of the empty shelves.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their ink-stained fingers. A Lethai-thora has ink under every fingernail. A stranger whose fingers are clean has never copied a forbidden text. A stranger whose fingers are stained black has copied too many. A stranger whose fingers are stained red has copied a death warrant — their own.

What is the most common crime? Context key theft — stealing the carved stone that unlocks a scroll’s meaning. The thief is not punished by law. They are given a blank scroll and told to write their own confession. The scroll will fill itself if the thief lies. The ink is their own blood.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Ninth Scroll is not a scroll. It is a memory so dangerous that nine different keepers each sealed a fragment of it in their own minds. When the ninth keeper dies, the memory will be lost forever — or will escape.

Where do people go when they die? To the Well of Forgotten Names. The dead are not buried. Their bodies are placed in the well, and the well swallows them. The water turns black. The black is the ink of their unwritten secrets. The well is bottomless. It is never full.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You offer a silence — a period of total stillness, no breath, no heartbeat, no word. The silence is timed by a water timer. If you hold the silence for nine drops, you are forgiven. If you break it, you must speak the wrong aloud in the Chamber of the Ninth Scroll. The scroll will remember. The scroll does not forgive.

What is the most important festival? The Unrolling of the Ledgers (winter). Once a year, the keepers unroll a single forbidden scroll and read it aloud to each other — but they read it in a whisper, and only the walls hear. The walls remember. The walls do not judge. The festival ends with all nine keepers drinking ink from a silver cup. The ink is their own memory. The next year, they will have forgotten.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The emperor’s true lineage is recorded on a scroll that no living keeper has opened. The scroll is sealed with a cord tied by the first keeper. The cord has nine knots. The ninth knot is a lie — the emperor’s grandfather was not of the blood. The empire was founded by a concubine’s son. The scroll is warm. It is kept in the First Pavilion. The First Pavilion is invisible. Only those who have forgotten nine names can see it.

Lethai-thora — “The Silent Ledgers of Sihai”

Genre / Mood: Bureaucratic melancholy, hidden history, silence as power.

The Lethai-thora were once courtiers of a celestial fey realm that spanned the Valewood and the eastern hills. When the old fey courts fell — when the Alder King withdrew and the Hazel Queen sealed her gates — many Lethai-thora fled east. They crossed the mountains with nothing but armfuls of scrolls and the memory of a civilisation that had forgotten how to die.

In the fertile river valleys of what would become Sihai, they found scattered agrarian tribes fighting over water and silence. The Lethai-thora did not conquer. They taught. They taught the tribes to write contracts instead of drawing blood, to measure grain instead of stealing it, to record debts instead of nursing grudges. Over generations, the tribes became a kingdom, the kingdom became an empire, and the empire became Sihai. The Lethai-thora became its memory.

Now they dwell in hidden pavilions, preserving forgotten ledgers that record what the Mandate of Heaven prefers to forget. Their knowledge is a burden: each secret they preserve slowly erases a piece of themselves. An Lethai-thora who guards a forbidden truth may find that she can no longer recall the color of her mother’s dress — a small price, she says, for keeping the ledger whole. They are no longer fey. They are no longer fully human. They are something in between: scribes of a story that has no ending, archivists of a past that never quite happened.

What you notice first: The smell of old parchment and incense. The way scrolls are stacked with their spines facing the wall — so the words cannot see who reads them. The silence when a secret is spoken; even the insects stop chirping.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never touch a scroll without announcing your lineage. Even a false lineage is better than silence. The scrolls remember who opened them, and they will whisper your name to the Hollow.

The Fledging of the Empire (Origin Story)

When the Valewood’s fey courts crumbled, a caravan of Lethai-thora refugees crossed the eastern passes. Their scrolls were water-stained, their children hungry, their memories already fraying. They found a patchwork of warring clans along the Sihon River — farmers who fought over silt, herders who stole each other’s sheep, chieftains who could not agree on a name for the season.

The Lethai-thora did not offer swords. They offered ink.

They taught the clans to write their disputes on clay, to seal them with witnesses, to let the record speak instead of the spear. A generation later, the first emperor rose — a man who had learned his letters from a Lethai-thora tutor. He did not forget. He built his capital around the Lethai-thora archive, and he decreed that no law could be written without a copy placed in their care.

The Lethai-thora have been part of Sihai ever since — embedded so deeply that they can no longer imagine leaving, even if they wanted to. They are the empire’s memory, but also its conscience, its secret-keeper, and its silent judge. Some among them resent this entanglement; they dream of returning to the Valewood, of hearing the old songs again. But the Valewood has changed. The fey courts do not answer. And the Lethai-thora have become something they never intended: the architects of a mortal empire.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Silence as Sanctuary: Once per scene, when asked a direct question, a character may spend 1 Boon to answer with silence. Treat Position as Dominant for resisting interrogation — but the GM gains 1 Story Beat (the silence has weight). In Lethai-thora halls, a silence is worth more than a thousand words.

  • Context Keys: To read an old text or recall a forbidden history, you need three keys: place, season, and witness. Without all three, roll at disadvantage. A Lethai-thora may create a temporary Context Key by spending 1 Boon and describing a small ritual (lighting incense, touching a stone, speaking a name).

  • The Keeper’s Toll: When using a Lethai-thora Rite or consulting a hidden archive, mark 1 Fatigue. The knowledge costs something — a moment of warmth, a pleasant memory, the scent of rain. Some keepers have forgotten how to smile.
