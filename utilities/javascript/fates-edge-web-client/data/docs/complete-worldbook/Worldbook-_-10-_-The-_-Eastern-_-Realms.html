The Eastern Realms

“At one landing they asked who could vouch for me. At the next they asked who had witnessed my departure. At the third they asked why I had arrived in this month. I had crossed the water, not answered the same question three times.”

— Dusana of the Raven Road, at the Inland Sea

Sihai’s records, Nihori’s forms, and Ayokha’s calendars organize different relationships between a person and their community. Each can provide real protection and place someone in danger. The peoples who maintain thresholds, carry drowned names, or preserve forbidden texts have their own work and their own reasons for helping. Begin with the local problem rather than treating the East as one court with interchangeable customs.

Aeler communities belong to the mountains in which they live. The severe Himadri holds are not the origin of every eastern Aeler settlement. Foothill communities can work with human neighbors while keeping the character of their home range; neither that contact nor a trade in metal identifies everyone as an Edgewalker or Mason. The mountain chapters provide the particular regional context.

Sihai

The Central Kingdom.

Bring the second copy. A man may dispute your name; he should have to dispute two clerks as well.

— Quiet Chancellor Wei

Sihai’s canals carry grain into Jingdu, and its examination halls send officials out again. A household keeps birth registrations, postings, and marriage records where they can be reached during a flood. Being correctly recorded brings practical protections: a place at the examination desk, a recognized claim to water, relatives who can establish where an absent person belongs. The relief when a clerk finds a missing copy is genuine.

The Censorate can preserve those protections or turn them against a household. Quiet Chancellor Wei wants corruption exposed before it damages the Mandate. A provincial censor may have orders to erase a rebel lineage whose children have never left their village. Both use an office that people need. The clerk who helps recover a registration and the official who suppresses evidence need not agree about what service requires.

The Celestial Bureaucracy has offices corresponding to the mortal administration. Ancestors receive offerings at household shrines; hungry ghosts press claims that a living magistrate may have closed. The Kitchen God’s household report matters to people who also argue about salt prices and whether a cousin should sit the examinations again. A competent official can still enforce a harmful order. A forged document can protect a real person and make their next appeal harder.

The Lethai-thora pavilions keep histories the state would prefer unavailable. Their keepers helped shape Sihai’s archival traditions alongside human teachers and officials. Former pupils now hold offices that sometimes demand the surrender of collections they once studied. A family may owe its standing to a document the same office has ordered burned.

At a water-timer’s missed beat, a waiting room falls silent. Nobody points it out. When the clerk resumes reading names, several petitioners check that their own papers are still in their hands.

What do people eat for breakfast? Congee with preserved vegetables. A household sends a covered bowl to the relative waiting outside an office; somebody must keep their place while the food is eaten.

What does a child learn before they can walk? Their name and the names of the people who can vouch for them. Later comes the brush. A grandparent keeps an early attempt even when the child’s handwriting becomes much better.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Who introduces them and what their seal says. A traveler without papers may need witnesses or a replacement copy. They are a person with a problem, not automatically a criminal or ghost.

What is the most common crime? Examination fraud: purchased questions, substituted candidates, or a tutor’s unauthorized help. An honest examiner and a family that sold land for tuition can both have much to lose when an allegation is made.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A gap between postings draws attention. People quietly help a relative account for a missing year before submitting a petition, even when nothing unlawful happened during it.

Where do people go when they die? Families keep ancestral tablets and make offerings of rice and tea. The shrine sustains a relationship with the dead; it does not mean that a wooden tablet replaces every local practice for tending a body.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A written apology is presented where the injured household and its ancestors can receive it. A grave wrong may call for blood-written calligraphy. Restoring a damaged water right still requires action at the canal office.

What is the most important festival? The Festival of the Ancestors. Tablets are cleaned, a meal is prepared, and absent relatives are accounted for. The person serving usually knows which dish belonged to which elder.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? A suppressed copy contradicts an official’s account of a lineage erasure. The order may bear a valid seal. The investigation concerns who issued it, whom it actually names, and who widened its reach.

Opening situation.

An examination candidate has a valid admission slip, but his family name is missing from the hall’s register. Chen keeps the desk vacant while a clerk searches for a second copy. The examination begins at noon, and a relative’s petition is disappearing from another office.

For the GM.

Begin with the missing candidate or the flood-damaged prefectural registry, not both. Establish who altered or lost the record, which witnesses survive, and what another office can verify. Investigation, testimony, copying, and negotiated recognition should all be viable. Use existing Censorate pressures only when their triggers occur; a general audit is not automatically a succession crisis. Keep dangerous archival knowledge separate from routine paperwork, and do not explain the setting’s underlying metaphysics to characters.

Regional Situation

The Censorate needs a defensible record; the candidate’s household needs him recognized before noon. The Salt Monopoly can delay a copy traveling by canal, while a Lethai-thora keeper holds evidence that an authorized erasure has reached people it was never meant to name.

The Register: documentation establishes standing and belonging. Make the protection tangible before threatening it. Lost records, contradictory copies, and contested annotations create specific obstacles; this is not automatic immunity for a filed character or permission to make an unfiled one fail every action.

The ninth taboo: The ninth citation in the Celestial Ledger is left unwritten. Speaking it aloud erases the speaker from the record being cited and from the memory of those who heard. Copyists leave the space and move on; nobody treats filling it as a harmless correction.

Additional Features (Celestial Bureaucracy)

  • The Mandate Timer [8] (Shared Campaign Timer ): Tracks the stability of the dynasty. The party inherits 2 ticks at the start of any adventure set in Sihai (the inherited burden). The timer ticks when the emperor’s health fails, when a succession dispute arises, when a major corruption scandal is exposed, or when the party breaks an oath witnessed by an ancestor. When the timer reaches 4: the Censorate becomes more aggressive (all audits start at Desperate Position). When it reaches 8: the Mandate fails — the Hollow claims the throne, civil war erupts, and the party must choose a side or flee. Resetting the timer requires an Ancestor Rite at the Imperial Ancestral Temple (downtime, costs 1 Supply and a truthful confession) or a public execution of a corrupt official (costs 1 Boon and may create enemies).

  • The Two Ledgers (Ancestral Names Mechanic): Every character has two “names”: their Mortal Name (what the Censorate knows) and their Ancestral Name (recorded in the Celestial Ledger, kept by the Lethai-thora). The Ancestral Name cannot be erased by mortal means. Discovering a character’s Ancestral Name requires a Research Timer (size 6) in the Lethai-thora archives. Once known, the player may invoke the Ancestral Name once per session to gain +2+2 dice on any roll related to that ancestor’s domain (e.g., a farmer’s ancestor aids harvest, a soldier’s ancestor aids battle). However, invoking the Ancestral Name creates an Ancestral Weight (size 4 timer). When it fills, the ancestor demands a service — usually something the ancestor failed to do in life (e.g., “Apologize to my widow” or “Find my stolen seal”). Refusing the demand marks 1 Corruption and adds 2 ticks to the Mandate Timer .

  • Censorate Audit (Social Combat Mechanic): The Censorate can audit any character at any time. An audit is a social combat with three phases:

    1. Pleadings (Presence + Sway vs. Censor’s Lore). Success grants +1+1 die to the next phase.

    2. Evidence (Wits + Insight vs. DV 4). Success reveals a hidden burden or a forged document; failure gives the Censor a piece of evidence against you.

    3. Judgment (Endurance + Lore vs. DV 5). Success: the character is cleared. Failure: the Censorate erases a name — the character loses one Bond (that person no longer remembers them) or one Asset (it is seized), and the Mandate Timer ticks +1+1.

    Players can avoid an audit by: Bribing the Censor (costs 1 Honey Jar or 1 Boon), Presenting a forged document (requires a Forgery Timer , size 4), or Calling a ghost witness (requires a Spirit + Lore test, DV 4; the ghost cannot lie, but it will ask for a favor in return – usually a promise that creates a new Ancestral Weight).

  • Examination Hell (Dungeon Mechanic): The Forbidden Garden contains a subterranean examination hall where the ghosts of failed candidates still write their answers. The party can enter to find a lost Ancestral Name – but the hall is alive.

    • The hall has a Stress Timer (size 8). Each failed roll, each lie spoken, each broken taboo ticks the timer.

    • At 4: The hall begins to change. Doors lead to different corridors. Navigation becomes Desperate.

    • At 8: The Examiner Ghost appears — a skeleton in scholar’s robes. It asks one question. The party must answer in verse. If the verse is good, the ghost grants a boon (reveals one Ancestral Name). If poor, the ghost erases one of the party’s names — that character is forgotten by the living for 1d41\text{d}4 days (lose all Bonds temporarily, cannot be recognized). The Stress Timer resets after the encounter.

  • Paper Soldiers and Hungry Ghosts (Minion Types):

    • Paper Soldiers (Tier I, Cap 2): Origami constructs animated by Censorate magic. They have Harm 2, Body 2, Melee 1. They are Vulnerable to fire (double Harm) and to a successful Lore test (reveals their true name, causing them to crumple).

    • Hungry Ghosts (Tier II, Cap 3): Spirits of those whose names were erased. They have Harm 3, Spirit 3, and the ability Memory Drain (touch, target forgets a minor detail — a face, a password, a skill point for one scene). They cannot cross a line of salt or a properly witnessed oath-circle.

  • Water Rights as Ancestral Currency: In the canal system, water rights are traded like silver, but they are also offerings to the Dragon Kings of the Four Seas. A Canal Mother’s key is worth more than a chest of coins — it can also summon a rain spirit (once per season, calm a storm or flood a district). Forging a water right angers the Dragon King; the forger’s wells run dry for nine years (all Survival tests to find water suffer −2-2 dice).

  • Lethai-thora Archivists of the First Seal: The Lethai-thora are not merely librarians — they are the keepers of the original Mandate, written on tortoise shells and dragon bones. They do not serve the emperor; they serve the record that predates the empire. A Lethai-thora keeper can declare any document void by writing “void” in water over it. The water must be from the Well of Erased Names. Doing so costs 1 Obligation to the Lethai-thora and ticks the Mandate Timer +1+1 (the Censorate will investigate).

Nihori

The Isles of the Dawn Spirit.

They have agreed to first blood. Ask who will call the end. Ask it now, while both of them can answer.

— Shrine Maiden Hana

Nihori’s volcanic islands are linked by ferries, processional roads, and the reputations of people who cross between them. Smiths cool blades under waterfalls. Fishing households watch for the boats before starting supper. Shrine bells travel farther in the wind than a shouted warning. The drowned empire lies beneath those working shores, and its surviving names remain dangerous to speak.

Consequential acts require form. A duel must be declared, witnessed, and properly closed. A petition has an opening the recipient can recognize. The forms protect a household as well as the person acting in its name. A duelist may be angry and frightened while insisting that the witness stand in exactly the right place. Nobody needs to be fearless for the ceremony to matter.

Shogun Atsumori is alive. Four assassination attempts in seven years have left him wounded and watchful; a throat injury makes speech costly, and he communicates through his Chamberlain. He withholds the succession because naming an heir would make that person a target. Yoritomo’s Northern Crane, Sadako’s Southern Serpent, and the Oni-Eyed of the western ash country pursue different futures while attending carefully to the forms that could validate them.

The shrines have standing of their own. Hana must answer to the kami, to the households that use her shrine, and to people whose petitions she refuses. Oni cults offer access without the protections the shrines demand. The Kurenai-ha remains a recognized claim-token, not a substitute for every other person’s judgment.

Lethai-al processions move among groves, cliff shrines, and harbors, carrying tide-charms of the drowned. A band can know a crossing better than the lord who claims it. Its members have meals to prepare and carts to mend before they take anyone into the storm.

What do people eat for breakfast? Grilled fish and pickled vegetables, with rice where the household has it. A ferry departure decides whether breakfast is eaten at home or wrapped for the crossing.

What does a child learn before they can walk? When to stop speaking at a shrine and how to look to an older sailor before naming a wave. The tide lesson comes from somebody whose boat the child will eventually share.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? How they carry a blade, greet a host, or ask permission to cross. A missing weapon may say something about work or circumstance; it does not make a fisher, porter, or guest a coward.

What is the most common crime? An unauthorized duel or a challenge pressed outside its agreed terms. The question is often whether a witness accepted the opening, not which fighter used the better technique.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? Atsumori’s Chamberlain chooses what reaches an audience. Courtiers study the delay before an answer. The Shogun’s silence creates opportunities without making him dead or incapable of deciding.

Where do people go when they die? Some communities tend their dead in cliff caves overlooking the sea. Families bring offerings and repair access after storms. A Lethai-al procession may carry a drowned person’s tide-charm onward instead.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? The injured party names the form of redress. A matched duel is one recognized answer, especially among warriors; it is not the only possible response to every broken tool or insult.

What is the most important festival? The Calling of the Tides in spring. Shrine attendants sing the drowned names, and families listen from the shore. Names answered by the waves are remembered privately rather than repeated through the crowd.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? Evidence connects an assassination attempt to a household seeking the succession. A token may be planted; establish its route through witnesses before treating it as proof against a daimyo.

Opening situation.

At a clifftop duel, Hana has witnessed first blood and called the end. The winner lowers their weapon. The loser’s name continues to sound from a bell that no one is ringing, and the Shogun’s envoy orders everyone to remain until the contest can be closed.

For the GM.

For the duel opening, decide before play why the bell continues and which evidence can establish it. Interviewing witnesses, tending the loser, negotiating a stay, or investigating the bell can all help; do not require a second fight. Atsumori is living and wounded. A claim that he is a ghost is misinformation, distinct from the real Storm Emperor’s Ghost. Preserve Kurenai’s Claim and the blade’s existing mechanics; do not advance that succession timer for every unrelated breach of etiquette.

The Sona carries barges through Ayokha’s rice country to the Jade Coast. Step-pyramid temples rise above flooded ground; workshops hang dyed cloth where the next rain can be seen coming. Ayodharaya’s God-King receives the allegiance of port rulers and sea-lords whose cooperation gives his authority reach. Inland communities and river-junction courts have interests the palace cannot meet by issuing the same command to all of them.

Important acts have proper seasons. Marriage, mourning, litigation, and the founding of an enterprise are planned around a calendar consulted as seriously as the water level. Families find reassurance in doing a thing when it belongs. When someone acts out of season, even affectionate relatives may begin treating them with the careful gentleness owed to a person in danger.

The astrologer’s office maintains the calendar against observations of the rains. The astrologer has not seen the sun in thirty years; reports reach his candlelit workroom through other people’s hands. A late report, a purchased correction, or an act falsely backdated can matter as much as a cargo seized at the quay. People seek exceptions because weddings, hunger, and unfinished funerals do not always wait for a convenient month.

The nats of wells, fords, and bridges hold local claims. A spirit-medium knows which offering answers a particular demand and which household has already tried it. Lethai-ar Vigils witness the terms people actually speak. A merchant can accept a promise yet dispute when it was fulfilled. A witness who will not shade the date may be protecting the river while endangering a neighbor.

Ayokha’s ports are places where people adapt things deliberately: a ship’s rig for a different crossing, a foreign cloth for local rain, a borrowed prayer for a shrine whose spirit answers differently. Their work and judgment shape these exchanges. The kingdom has its own disagreements before any outsider arrives.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice congee with dried shrimp or river fish. A boat household eats when loading allows. Someone keeps the youngest child’s bowl above the splash from passing traffic.

What does a child learn before they can walk? Which step stays dry when the river rises and whom to ask before entering a boat. Reading the current and learning the household calendar continue long after the first lesson.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they know whose landing they have reached. An offering can acknowledge a nat, but the boat crew and the person repairing the steps also need to be asked.

What is the most common crime? A forged calendar consultation or a transaction entered under an earlier date. The signature can be genuine while the date is false, which makes the Vigil’s recollection inconvenient.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A respected household has acted outside its season. Neighbors bring food and make allowances before there is anything visible to mourn. The family must decide whether accepting that kindness makes matters worse.

Where do people go when they die? Many families cremate their dead and return the ashes to the river. Mediums attend to the passage among nats and ancestors; a missed observance can leave the living with work they must finish.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A medium may bring the apology before the local nat, with a Vigil witnessing any new promise. Calming the water does not replace returning a stolen boat or repairing the bank that was damaged.

What is the most important festival? The Welcoming of the Monsoon. The God-King stands in the rain, the bell is heard across the water, and households prepare for the work the flood will bring. A late monsoon affects a celebration people genuinely wanted.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? A calendar correction was sold before the observation that supposedly justified it. Copies may survive with a foreign buyer, a season-broker, and a household that trusted the original date.

The household needs an answer before the couple’s boat leaves the wharf, and the medium reports that an upstream promise remains unfulfilled.

For the GM.

Distinguish a prediction that proved wrong, a record that was falsified, and a spirit’s unfulfilled terms. They are different causes and need different remedies. For the marriage opening, establish who changed the calendar and why before play. Seeking an exception, fulfilling the upstream promise, protecting the couple, or exposing a purchased correction can all matter. Keep the existing campaign timer for its stated triggers rather than using it to punish every delay. Treat dynastic and supernatural secrets as discoveries, not introductions delivered by every boatman.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • Rain’s Due (Shared Timer , size 8): Every broken promise to a river-spirit, every unpaid offering, every late monsoon gesture ticks the timer. The party inherits the God-King’s obligations at the start of any adventure set in Ayokha – they begin with 2 ticks already accrued (old burdens). When the timer reaches 4: the astrologer’s predictions become unreliable (roll twice for weather, take worse). When it reaches 8: a Oath-Keeper rises from the river and demands a story, a memory, or a season of service. Resetting the timer requires a Monsoon Rite (downtime, costs 1 XP or a story worth a season’s wages) or a sacrifice to the Drowned Nat (a memory or a magical item).

  • Monsoon Timer [6]: Tracks the approach of the rainy season. When it fills, the monsoon arrives — all river travel becomes Desperate for 2 legs. The timer ticks each day during the dry season. The astrologer can predict the exact day; a successful Wits + Lore test (DV 4) reveals the remaining ticks. The party can slow the timer by performing Rain Offerings (betel leaves, silk scraps, or stories) – each offering ticks the timer back by 1, but adds 1 tick to Rain’s Due.

  • River Court Justice: Disputes on the river are settled by a floating court. The judge is a Lethai-ar Vigil. The verdict is written on a clay tablet and thrown into the water. If the tablet floats, the verdict is true. If it sinks, the verdict is false — and the judge is drowned. A character may attempt to influence the verdict by offering a bribe (a Treasure or a story) before the tablet is thrown. If the bribe is a story, the GM gains 1 SB; if a Treasure, the judge’s verdict tilts in your favor automatically (no test).

  • Betel Leaf Hospitality: Offering a betel leaf is an oath of hospitality. Refusing is a grave insult. Accepting binds both parties to non-violence for the duration of the visit. Breaking this oath marks 1 Corruption and begins a River’s Grudge [4] timer. When the timer fills, a nat appears and demands an equal price (1 Harm, a memory, or a public apology).

  • Astrologer’s Scroll (Player Resource): A character who earns the astrologer’s trust (or steals his notes) may carry a copy of his calendar. The scroll has 3 pages. Each page can be used to:

    • Predict the Monsoon: Once per journey, ask the GM if the weather will turn against you in the next leg. The GM answers truthfully (yes/no).

    • Forge a Schedule: Once per session, treat a failed navigation roll as a success, but add 1 tick to Rain’s Due (the astrologer will notice the forgery eventually).

    • Blackmail an Official: Gain +1 Position on social rolls against any Ayokha official who relies on the monsoon schedule (harbor masters, customs officers, grain factors). Using this burns the page.

    Refilling the scroll requires a downtime action and a bribe to the astrologer (1 Boon or a story worth a season’s gossip).

  • The Ninth Terrace (Forbidden Zone, Mechanic): The terrace is flooded only once every nine years. The rice grown there is for the God-King alone – eating it is treason. But the rice is also the only thing that can cure the monsoon blight (a crop disease that ticks the Monsoon Timer by 2 each day). Entering the Ninth Terrace requires a Water-Key (a coral token, acquired from the astrologer or a nat). The terrace is guarded by Hungry Ghosts – the spirits of farmers who starved when the monsoon failed. They attack anyone whose Rain’s Due timer is 4 or higher. Harvesting the rice takes 3 actions. Each action triggers a ghost attack (Spirit + Endurance test DV 4 or take 1 Fatigue). Success yields a Ninth Terrace Rice grain (see Diamonds).

Aelaerem of the Eastern Realms

Rice, Salt, and the Ninth Step.

Ume walked it last week. Tomo signed for this week. Ask Tomo where the mud on his boots came from before you give Ume the bucket again.

— A thread-binder at Yatsuno

At Yatsuno, the Eightfold Village, boundary work is part of keeping a household. Red threads must be checked, salt carried, rice bowls set out at dusk and left alone until dawn. A person who knows the work will explain it over tea and expect a visitor staying another morning to help. The welcome is sincere. So is the irritation when the same person misses their turn again.

The eastern Aelaerem have made maintenance explicit where many western cousins rely on inherited courtesy. Their rota can be checked against the ground. A re-tied thread, a washed bell-cloth, and a properly kept stretch of boundary make an observable difference. Knowing the procedure does not mean knowing the ultimate nature of what it keeps out.

Hedge-keepers may live alone beside a difficult crossing or among families on the terraces. A solitary gatekeeper relies on the family bringing rice up from below; the family relies on the gatekeeper being there when the lane changes. A household can be warm and generous without volunteering to carry another household’s neglected duties indefinitely.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge, salted plum, and pickled vegetables. A host has opinions about the crock they opened for you and expects you to try it before discussing the boundary.

What does a child learn before they can walk? The eight boundary knots, with a loose end left alone. An older hand unties a mistaken ninth knot without turning the lesson into a public humiliation.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they interrupt someone counting or step over a thread without asking. A visitor can learn. A visitor determined to demonstrate that the precautions are foolish will not be trusted with the bell.

What is the most common crime? Thread-theft and a turn on the rota signed for but not walked. Missing thread leaves physical evidence; a false signature may not. Both can put another household’s stretch at risk.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? Someone has quietly done a neighbor’s work twice. The village needs the boundary kept, but it also needs the same few people not to carry it indefinitely.

Where do people go when they die? Their ashes are mixed with salt and scattered along the boundary. Families know the stretch where an elder was laid and continue tending it after another person takes the work.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A cup of sake and a lock of hair may be left where the wrong occurred. The wronged household also expects the thread returned, the shift covered, or the false entry corrected.

What is the most important festival? The Retting of the Threads in spring. Threads are washed and re-tied through a silent night. Food, clean cloth, and people willing to relieve tired hands have to be arranged beforehand.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? A respected keeper has concealed weeks of missed work. The reason may be illness, favoritism, or an attempt to protect a relative. Find what went unwalked before assigning blame.

Opening situation.

The east thread came loose in a storm. Two people have signed the rota for the same morning, but neither can describe the newly fallen tree beside it. A work-party must check the boundary before dusk while the households settle who was supposed to go.

For the GM.

Retain the existing Boundary Drift and Work-Parties procedures. Neglect can be repaired; do not replace their reversible pressure with an inevitable catastrophe. Make villagers capable of doing the work. The party can contribute labor, investigate an omission, or cover for someone with a genuine competing responsibility.

Entanglements

Sihai – The Central Kingdom’s censors would very much like the boundary surveyed, numbered, and taxed. Every generation a surveyor arrives with a measuring chain. Every generation the chain comes back one link short, and no one can say which link.

Nihori – The storm-isles keep the same taboo in a different dialect: they will not speak of the ninth wave, the Aelaerem will not walk the ninth step. Nihori duellists find the hedge-keepers superstitious. Hedge-keepers find duellists loud.

Ayokha – The monsoon moves the boundary a little every year, which is why the Retting is a spring rite and not an autumn one. Ayokhan river-factors buy indigo and salted plum here and pay in seed-rice.

Lethai-thora of Sihai – The Silent Ledgers and the boundary rota borrow each other’s bookkeeping and each other’s silences. A Lethai-thora clerk will audit a boundary ledger for free, for the pleasure of it.

Aelinnel of the Inland Sea – Gnomish tide-keepers and Aelaerem thread-binders trade methods at the equinox and argue about them for the rest of the year.

Quick Reference

Tagline: The boundary is a chore. Do the chore.

Rota-Keeper (Elite): “You want to know what keeps the Hollow out? Not courage. Not a sword. A rota. Ume has the north thread in the first week, Tomo in the second, and if Tomo is drunk again then Ume has it twice and Tomo owes her two crocks of pickle. Write it down. Read it back. That is the whole of it. The terrible things happen in the weeks nobody wrote down.”

Persimmon-Wife (Commoner): “Sit. The tea is already made — I heard you on the road, you walk heavy. Eat the plum, it is salted, it will do you good. No, do not count the stairs on the way up. I know they are lovely. I know you want to. Talk to me instead and you will not notice how many there were, and that is the point of me talking.”

Genre / Mood: Eastern folk horror in a farmhouse kitchen. Terrace fields at dusk, persimmons drying on strings, an indigo vat breathing under a blanket, and one lamp that must be lit at the correct hour or the whole valley will be wrong by morning.

Starting Location: A boundary-house at the top of the terraces, warm and low-beamed, smelling of charcoal, wet straw, and salted plum. A rota-board hangs by the door: eight columns of neat brushwork with one week rubbed out and re-written twice. An old thread-binder pours tea without asking, and sets a second cup down on the floor beside the hearth — not for you. “The east thread came loose in the storm,” she says, “and nobody has walked it since. It is a morning’s work. It is always only a morning’s work. That is why it never gets done.”

Yatsuno is beautiful, and the beauty is the defence. The terraces are cut in eight steps because eight is a finished number; the mirror-paddies are kept still because a rippled reflection is a door left ajar; the persimmons hang in strings of eight under every eave because a thing that is counted is a thing the Hollow cannot forget into its mouth. Every cosy detail in this valley is load-bearing. The quilt is warm and it is also a boundary. The pickle crock is delicious and it is also a boundary. The bathhouse is a pleasure and it is also a boundary, and there is a reason nobody bathes ninth.

What the western Aelaerem do by avoidance, the eastern Aelaerem do by maintenance. They will explain the whole system to you over tea, at length, with diagrams, and then they will hand you a bucket, because you are here and the north thread is not going to walk itself. The horror is not that the Hollow is coming. The horror is that it is already here, in the gaps, patient as damp, and the only thing between it and the village is whether somebody remembered to do the Water.

What you notice first: How clean everything is, and how deliberate. Red thread at eye height on every gate, eight knots, always eight. Rice bowls set out at dusk along the terrace edge and nobody looking at them. Bells with cloth tied over the clapper. And the silence at exactly the hour when a village this pretty ought to be singing.

The one rule that kills newcomers: Never count the steps between your door and the crossroads. The Hollow counts with you. If you reach nine, it will be waiting at the ninth step. It will ask, “Where are you going?” Do not answer.

Regional Mood: The Chore That Holds the World Up

There are no lords here and no courts, only the rota. Standing is measured in weeks walked, threads re-tied, salt hauled up the terraces, and whether your handwriting on the board can be read by lamplight. A hedge-keeper’s authority is exactly as long as her stretch of boundary and not one pace further, and she will tell you so with some satisfaction. Outsiders read all of this as quaint. It is not quaint. It is a maintenance schedule for the edge of the world, kept by people who bake very good bread, and the reason they are so calm about the Hollow is that they know precisely how many mornings of neglect it takes before the calm stops being warranted.

They are, accordingly, the most welcoming people in the Eastern Realms and the least sentimental. A guest is fed, bathed, given the warm end of the room, and put on the rota by the second morning. Refusing the work is not rude. It is simply understood that you are the sort of person who will be somewhere else when the boundary drifts, and you will be fed anyway, and no one will trust you with a thread.

The Boundary’s Drift (Campaign Timer)

The Hollow does not attack. It forgets — and where it forgets, the boundary moves. Track the state of the valley’s maintenance with a visible timer:

Boundary Drift [8] — Advances when:

  • A week of the rota goes unwalked (+1)

  • A rice bowl is checked before dawn (+1)

  • A bell is rung unmuffled after dusk, or anyone whistles after dusk (+1)

  • Someone counts aloud past eight, anywhere in the valley (+2)

  • A red thread is cut, taken, or tied with the ninth knot (+2)

  • The GM spends 1 SB to let a small neglect compound (+1)

Crucially, and unlike the Hollow’s attention in the west, this timer runs backwards under work. See Work-Parties, below. The valley is not doomed; it is behind.

At 4 segments: The mirror-paddies stop reflecting the sky properly — they reflect the terraces as they were some other year. Cats will not cross the lanes. The rota-board’s oldest entries fade. At 8 segments: The boundary has moved. Something is inside it that was outside it yesterday. Roll on What Steps Through. Until the party or the village clears at least 3 segments, every night in the valley opens with a Story Beat spent on the uncanny.

Work-Parties (clearing the timer)

This is the region’s signature move and the reason to keep the timer on the table. Boundary work is dull, physical, communal, and it is the only thing that clears Drift.

  • Walk a week (clear 1). A full day spent re-tying one stretch: Body + Endurance DV 3, or Wits + Craft DV 3 if you are re-knotting rather than hauling. A Partial clears the segment but costs 1 Fatigue or 1 Supply.

  • Haul the salt (clear 1). Carry blessed salt up eight terraces. Body + Endurance DV 3. Followers with Cap 2+ can do this unaided; it is the classic job to hand a follower.

  • Keep the ledger (clear 1, once per season). An afternoon reconciling the rota-board against what was actually walked: Wits + Insight DV 4. On a Clean Success, also learn where the next drift will occur — ask the GM one true question about the boundary.

  • Stand the Retting (clear 3). The spring rite: every thread down, washed in salt water, re-tied in one silent night. This is a scene, not a roll — run it as a Silent Night [6] Timer where each segment is a stretch re-tied and every noise the party makes is a segment the GM takes back. Clearing it clears 3 Drift and is worth 2 XP to everyone who kept silent.

  • Feed the boundary (clear 1, costs). Set out the bowls yourself and do not look until dawn. No roll. Whoever set them out wakes with one memory gone soft at the edges — they can still name the thing, but not describe it. The GM gains 1 SB.

Escalation. A Tier I party clears Drift a segment at a time in one valley. A Tier III or IV party is asked to walk the First Boundary — eight days, eight prefectures, a rota nobody has kept in living memory — and that is a Long Rota [10] Timer with real teeth at every segment. The chore does not get easier. It gets longer, and there is more of the world behind it.

What Steps Through (d6)

When Boundary Drift reaches 8, roll 1d6:

  1. The Extra Stair. The shrine steps gain one. Anyone who climbs them arrives a little wrong: their shadow is short by a hand’s width until they descend backwards, counting nothing.

  2. The Guest Who Leaves No Steam. A traveller takes the bathhouse. Perfectly polite, correct in every courtesy, and the water does not fog around them. Turn them out and the Drift ticks +2. Let them stay and they will still be there in the spring, and so will their appetite.

  3. The Bowl That Was Checked. Every rice bowl in the valley is empty and clean, and no one set them out. Something has been keeping the rota. It will want the credit — and the seat at the board.

  4. The Village in the Water. The mirror-paddies show a Yatsuno with nine hamlets. People in it wave. If anyone waves back, that person’s name is now written on the ninth column of the rota-board in their own hand.

  5. The Borrowed Face. A villager comes home twice on the same evening. Both are warm. Both are hungry. Both know the rota. The village will not choose; that is not their way. They will feed both and wait, which is worse.

  6. The Unmuffled Bell. A bell rings once, at the wrong hour, with no one at the rope. Every promise made in the valley that day is now heard, and the Hollow keeps the accounts. All Obligation marked this session is doubled until someone confesses a lie at the bell tower.

Breaches of the Rota (d6)

When a PC skips their week, cuts a thread, counts aloud, or whistles after dusk, roll 1d6:

d6 Consequence
1 Your name is written on the board in a hand not your own, in the week you missed. You now owe that week.
2 The pickle crocks buried on your stretch are opened and found sweet. Sweet is wrong. The village will be polite about it and will not eat from your table for a month (–1 Position socially in the valley).
3 A red thread ties itself around your wrist with eight knots. It tightens when you are about to lie. Cutting it ticks Boundary Drift +2; wearing it out takes a season.
4 Your shadow is a hand’s width short and shortens another hand each night you do not make the week up. At four, animals will not come near you (+1 DV on anything involving beasts or trust).
5 The hot spring runs cold on your side of the bathhouse. This is read, correctly, as a judgement. Gain the Condition Unwelcome at the Warm End.
6 The rota-keeper gives you the east thread — the long one, the one nobody wants — and the whole village knows. Start a Owed Work [6] Timer. Nothing bad happens when it fills. They simply stop asking you, and stop counting you, and that is how people become gaps.

Spades — Places

  1. The Eight Terraces [FARM] [ORDER]
    Eight steps of paddy, cut so the water falls from one to the next without a sound.
    There were nine, once. The lowest is not spoken of and is not planted.
    Mechanical hook: Working a day here restores 1 Fatigue and clears 1 Boundary Drift. Anyone who walks down onto the unplanted ninth takes a Wits + Insight DV 3 test to come back up the way they went; on a Miss they arrive at the top having lost an hour they cannot account for.

  2. The Mirror-Paddy [WATER] [OMEN]
    Held still all season; the village shaves and mends its collars by it.
    A ripple is a door left ajar. That is why the children are not allowed to throw stones, and why they do.
    Mechanical hook: A Wits + Insight DV 3 read of the surface answers one question about something happening elsewhere in the valley right now. If Boundary Drift is 4+, the reflection answers about a different year instead, and the GM gains 1 SB.

  3. Persimmon Eaves [HEARTH] [COUNT]
    Fruit hung in strings of eight under every roof-edge, drying orange against gray wood.
    A string of nine appeared on the smith’s house the winter he died. Nobody hung it.
    Mechanical hook: Sleeping under the eaves is a safe rest — no night SB is spent on the sleepers. Counting the strings aloud ticks Boundary Drift +2 and the counter dreams of the ninth fruit.

  4. The Indigo Shed [CRAFT] [SECRET]
    Vats kept warm under quilts, breathing like something asleep; the dyer talks to them daily.
    The vat is alive in the ordinary way. It is also the only ink the boundary ledger will accept.
    Mechanical hook: A Wits + Craft DV 3 gets you a bolt of true indigo, which counts as a Minor Asset: writing in it makes any record legible after any drowning, burning, or forgetting. Fail and you have spoiled a vat the village needs, which is a social problem for a season.

  5. The Bathhouse at Warm Spring [REST] [TABOO]
    Cedar, steam, a bench worn smooth, and a strict and unexplained order of bathing.
    Eight bathe. The ninth waits. There is always someone willing to be the ninth, and there is always a reason they were not, later.
    Mechanical hook: Bathing clears all Fatigue and 1 Condition. Bathing ninth clears everything, including one thing you wanted to keep: mark 1 Identity Strain and the GM gains 2 SB.

  6. Thousand-Step Shrine [SACRED] [COUNT]
    A stair up through cedar to a shrine no bigger than a pantry.
    The steps count your heartbeats and give you the number at the top. It is always slightly wrong. Slightly.
    Mechanical hook: Climbing without counting (Spirit + Endurance DV 3) earns a true omen: one honest answer about what the Hollow wants here and now. Anyone who counts arrives with the wrong number in their head and takes –1 die on Insight until they sleep.

  7. The Whispering Grove [BAMBOO] [DANGER]
    Bamboo so dense the paths are decided for you; it speaks on nights with no wind.
    The gaps between the stalks are the same width as a door.
    Mechanical hook: Navigation here is Desperate unless someone keeps up steady talk (Presence + Performance DV 2 to hold the group’s attention). On a Miss, the party emerges in the grove’s own reckoning of the valley — everything correct except one building that should not be there.

  8. The Charcoal Kilns [INDUSTRY] [SMOKE]
    Earth mounds smouldering for eight days on the ridge, watched in shifts.
    The watcher who falls asleep wakes to find the fire banked and the tools cleaned. Nobody admits to it.
    Mechanical hook: A watch shift is a slow scene with excellent talk; any one character may clear 1 Identity Strain by telling something true. Whoever is watching when the mound is opened gets first pick of the charcoal — worth 1 Boon of trade in any eastern market.

  9. The Pickle Ground [BOUNDARY] [FOOD]
    Crocks buried in rows along the boundary line itself, salt-heavy and bitter.
    The bitterness is a reminder. Sweetness in a boundary crock means the line has moved under it.
    Mechanical hook: Opening a crock is a free, reliable read on the boundary’s true position: bitter is sound, sour is drifting, sweet means the line has already moved and the GM sets Boundary Drift to at least 6.

  10. The Rota-House [LAW] [RECORD]
    A single low room, a board of eight columns, and three centuries of ledgers on the shelf.
    Every gap in the ledgers matches a year the valley does not discuss.
    Mechanical hook: A Wits + Lore DV 4 search of the ledgers finds the record of a previous drift and how it was closed — a Clue, and a method that will work again. On a Miss you find a year that has been walked by someone whose handwriting appears nowhere else, in any ledger, ever.

  11. The Boundary-House [SHELTER] [WATCH]
    The last roof before the line: one hearth, one lamp, one bed always made for whoever is walking.
    The lamp is lit at the correct hour by whoever is nearest. There is no list of who is nearest. It has never once gone unlit.
    Mechanical hook: Guest-right here is absolute and free. In exchange, the guest lights the lamp at dusk. Forgetting ticks Boundary Drift +1 and the house will be cold and correct and slightly further from the path next time you come.

  12. The Salt Road [DEAD] [PROCESSION]
    The switchback up which the dead are carried, ash mixed with salt, to be scattered on the line.
    The dead become the boundary. They do not rest. They watch. This is considered a good outcome.
    Mechanical hook: Carrying a stranger’s ashes up (Body + Endurance DV 3, a full day) earns the valley’s real trust: one favour, honoured without argument, from any hedge-keeper on the eastern boundary. On the way up you may ask the ashes one question at the fourth terrace.

  13. The First Boundary [ANCIENT] [LAW]
    A post at a crossroads two days out, older than the terraces, with one thread on it.
    The first red thread was not tied by an Aelaerem. It was tied by the Hollow, before it forgot what it was.
    Mechanical hook: The thread cannot be re-tied, only inspected. A Spirit + Arcana DV 5 reading confirms the eight knots are tied from the inside. Untying it is not a roll and not a scene; it is a campaign, and the GM should say so out loud.

  14. The Ninth Hamlet [CURSE] [PLACE]
    Yatsuno has eight. On certain evenings there is a lane you have not walked, and lamps at the end of it.
    Everyone there is kind. The tea is already poured. The rota-board has your week on it.
    Mechanical hook: Entering is easy and pleasant. Leaving costs what you would not give up: Spirit + Endurance DV 5, and on anything but a Clean Success you leave one relationship behind — an NPC who knew you no longer does. The hamlet keeps whatever it is given and is genuinely grateful.

Hearts — People & Factions

  1. The Boundary-Cook [HEARTH] [FEEDING]
    Feeds the walkers, the guests, and the bowls, in that order, and never asks who the bowls are for.
    She sets a second cup on the floor at every meal. She has done it since she was six. She will not discuss it.
    What they carry: She knows every person in the valley by how they take their tea, which means she knows immediately when one of them has been replaced.
    What they ask: That you eat before you talk. She is not being warm. She is checking whether you eat.

  2. The Rota-Keeper [LAW] [RECORD]
    Writes the board, reads it back, and holds the whole valley together with a brush and a grudge-free memory.
    Her authority ends where her stretch ends and she is proud of that. Offer her more and she will be offended.
    What they carry: Three centuries of ledgers and the ability to find the one year you need in an afternoon.
    What they ask: A week. Any week. She will take payment in nothing else.

  3. The Bell-Warden [WATCH] [FEAR]
    Rings once at dawn, muffles at once, and does not sleep well.
    He heard an unmuffled bell as a boy and has been deaf in one ear and careful in the other ever since.
    What they carry: He can tell, by the way a sound dies in the valley, whether the boundary has moved — days before the crocks turn sweet.
    What they ask: Someone to take the tower for one night so he can sleep on a floor with people on it.

  4. The Thread-Binder of the East Stretch [BOUNDARY] [SOLITUDE]
    Walks the long thread nobody wants, alone, in all weathers, and prefers it.
    She counts under her breath as she ties and stops at eight with a small physical effort you can see.
    What they carry: She has walked the east stretch for forty years and knows every place the line has ever been — a map that exists nowhere else.
    What they ask: Company for exactly one day, and then no more, and she will be curt about it because she means it.

  5. The Salt-Hauler [DEAD] [LABOUR]
    Carries the ashes up and the salt with them, and talks to both on the way.
    He says the dead answer on the fourth terrace and not before. He is not joking and does not enjoy being asked.
    What they carry: He knows which stretches of boundary are held by which dead, and whose relatives are therefore obliged to whom.
    What they ask: Help with the load. It is very heavy. That is all.

  6. The Sihai Surveyor [EMPIRE] [MEASURE]
    Seventh of his line to measure this boundary, with a chain that is one link short.
    His father’s chain was also one link short. So was his grandfather’s. He has stopped writing that part down.
    What they carry: Imperial authority, real money, and six generations of quietly falsified surveys he would rather nobody read.
    What they ask: One honest measurement. He has wanted it his whole career and is frightened of getting it.

  7. The Indigo Dyer [CRAFT] [PATIENCE]
    Keeps four vats alive under quilts and talks to them the way other people talk to dogs.
    A vat that goes quiet is dying. A vat that answers is a different problem.
    What they carry: The only ink the ledgers accept, and the knowledge of what makes it work, which is not a secret so much as a discipline.
    What they ask: That you keep the shed warm for eight days while he is away. It is the most trust anyone here will place in you this early.

  8. The Work-Party [COMMUNITY] [PRESSURE]
    Nine or ten neighbours with tools, rice balls, and opinions, walking a stretch together.
    They are cheerful, loud, and the safest thing in the valley, which is exactly why they are loud.
    What they carry: Numbers, tools, gossip, and the fastest way to clear Boundary Drift — treat them as a Follower group with Cap 4 for boundary labour only.
    What they ask: That you keep up and take your turn carrying. Both are noticed.

  9. The Guest Who Leaves No Steam [HOLLOW] [COURTESY]
    Perfectly polite, correct in every courtesy, and the bathwater does not fog around them.
    They know the rota. They have offered to take a week. The village is thinking about it.
    What they carry: Genuine good manners, and a name that no one in the valley can afterwards recall being told.
    What they ask: A place on the board. They are asking properly, which is the horror of it.

  10. The Hedge-Keepers’ Circle [FACTION] [MUTUAL AID]
    Every thread-binder on the eastern boundary, meeting twice a year to argue about method.
    They have no leader and no rules except that the boundary is walked, and they will feud for decades over knot spacing.
    What they carry: Collective knowledge of eight hundred li of boundary, and the ability to summon two hundred people with tools in four days.
    What they ask: That you take a side in the knot-spacing argument. There is no neutral position. Choose and be committed.

  11. The Retting Chorus [RITE] [SILENCE]
    The eight who lead the spring rite: one per hamlet, chosen by who walked the most weeks.
    They do not speak from dusk to dawn on the night, and for a week after they speak very little and very carefully.
    What they carry: The right to re-tie the First Boundary’s neighbours, and the memory of every Retting they have stood.
    What they ask: That you either stand silent with them or leave the valley for the night. There is no third option and no offence taken either way.

  12. The Woman Who Came Home Twice [HOLLOW] [FAMILY]
    Both of her are warm. Both are hungry. Both know the rota and both are correct about it.
    The village has fed both for three weeks and has not chosen. That is not indecision. That is the method.
    What they carry: Between them, one whole life, and neither will say which half.
    What they ask: That you do not make the village choose. Whoever forces it will be blamed, correctly, for what follows.

  13. The Rota-Keeper’s Predecessor [MEMORY] [AUTHORITY]
    The old keeper, retired thirty years, who still walks a week she was never assigned.
    Her handwriting is in ledgers from before she was born. She has read them. She has stopped mentioning it.
    What they carry: The only living account of the last time the boundary moved and what it cost to move it back.
    What they ask: That someone reads the year she will not read aloud, and tells her whether her hand is really in it.

  14. The Hollow, Keeping the Rota [HOLLOW] [DREAD]
    The bowls have been set out and cleared for a month by something that wants the work.
    It is doing the job properly. That is not a relief. A thing that maintains the boundary decides where the boundary is.
    What they carry: Perfect, patient competence, and the beginnings of a claim.
    What they ask: A column on the board with a name in it. It does not have a name. It would like one of yours, and it will say please.

(Neglect/Weather/Uncanny)

Rain that unpicks a knot a night; a week nobody wrote down; a lane that is one house longer than it was.

Clubs — Complications/Threats

  1. The Week Nobody Wrote Down [NEGLECT] [QUIET]
    A column on the board is blank and everyone assumed it was somebody else’s.
    Nothing happened that week. Nothing at all. That is the complication.
    Mechanical hook: Tick Boundary Drift +1 for each such week the party fails to notice. A Wits + Insight DV 3 audit of the board finds them; nobody in the valley will find them unprompted, because everyone is sure.

  2. Rain Unpicks the Knots [WEATHER] [WORK]
    Three days of monsoon rain and half the eastern threads are hanging loose.
    The knots do not fail in a storm. They fail two days after, in the sun, when nobody is worried.
    Mechanical hook: A full stretch must be re-walked in one day or Boundary Drift +2. This is a straightforward Body + Endurance DV 3 for everyone who helps, and a genuine problem for a party who consider themselves too important for it.

  3. Somebody Counted [TABOO] [PANIC]
    A child, a guest, or a drunk said nine out loud in the lane.
    The village does not punish it. The village goes very quiet and starts checking things, which is worse to watch.
    Mechanical hook: Tick Boundary Drift +2. Until it is cleared, the ninth of anything the party counts is wrong: the ninth arrow, the ninth step, the ninth coin. The GM should apply this once, memorably, at the worst moment.

  4. Sweet in the Crock [OMEN] [MOVED]
    A boundary pickle crock is opened and the contents are sweet.
    Bitter is sound. Sour is drifting. Sweet means the line has already moved and you are standing on the wrong side of the question.
    Mechanical hook: Boundary Drift is immediately at least 6. The party’s current location may now be outside the boundary; the GM should not announce which.

  5. The Lamp Went Unlit [NEGLECT] [DOOR]
    Nobody was nearest to the boundary-house at dusk. It had never happened before.
    The house is not damaged. It is simply further from the path than it was, and it will be further again tomorrow.
    Mechanical hook: The boundary-house cannot be reached again until someone spends a night on the line without it (Spirit + Endurance DV 4, and the GM gains 1 SB on anything but a Clean Success).

  6. The Surveyor’s Chain [EMPIRE] [PRESSURE]
    Sihai wants the boundary in the register, and a register is a fixed thing.
    The valley’s whole method depends on the line being allowed to move. A survey is not a nuisance. It is a threat.
    Mechanical hook: Start a Registered [6] Timer. If it fills, the boundary is legally fixed where it is not, and every subsequent Work-Party clears one fewer segment until the register is corrected — which is a matter for clerks, bribery, and Lethai-thora auditors.

  7. A Bell at the Wrong Hour [SOUND] [ACCOUNT]
    One ring, unmuffled, with nobody at the rope.
    Every promise made in the valley today has been heard, and something is keeping the accounts.
    Mechanical hook: All Obligation marked this session is doubled. It can be undone only by a public, specific confession of a lie at the bell tower, in front of people who will remember it.

  8. Whistling After Dusk [TABOO] [INVITATION]
    Somebody in the party is cheerful and does not know the rule.
    Whistling is calling. The Hollow does not know it was not being called.

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = treasure. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 →\rightarrow 4, 6–10 →\rightarrow 6, J/Q/K →\rightarrow 8, A →\rightarrow 10).

  • Diamonds here are almost never loot. They are things given by people who have decided about you: a week walked in your name, a place on the board, the warm end of the room. Hand them over in a scene, not in a pile.

  • If any A appears, the uncanny should be tidy. The Hollow in the east does not smash. It keeps the rota, straightens the bowls, and writes neatly in your column.

  • Track Boundary Drift [8] as a campaign pressure — and put the clearing conditions where the players can see them. A timer that only goes up teaches a table to despair; this one is a chore list, and the whole regional flavour is that the chore list works.

  • Let the cosy things be mechanically real. The bathhouse clears Fatigue. The persimmon eaves make a safe rest. The tea is genuinely good. A folk horror where the comfort is fake is a cheap one; here the comfort is true, and it is load-bearing, and that is what makes neglect frightening.

Additional Features

  • Take Your Turn: A character who has walked a week within the last season shifts one step safer on their first social action in any Aelaerem settlement in the Eastern Realms. Word travels along the rota faster than along the roads.

  • Eat Before You Talk: Accepting food before opening business cancels the first SB generated by negotiation in that scene. Refusing is not an insult and carries no penalty — but the household will note that you did not eat, and will remember it later, when someone is checking who is who.

  • Stop at Eight: Any character who deliberately breaks off a count at eight — of steps, coins, arrows, breaths — may re-roll one failed die on that action, once per scene. The habit is worth having and is exactly as easy to acquire as it sounds.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

Nihori will not name the ninth wave, Ayokha pours the ninth cup for the drowned, and here nobody bathes ninth. A party who has met all three should be told, once, that these are one thing.

  • The Traveler: A gray pilgrim occasionally joins a work-party, carries more than his share, eats nothing, and is gone by the fourth terrace. Stretches he helped on do not drift for a year. Nobody has ever got his name and everyone has stopped trying.

The eastern Aelaerem have no spells. They have a schedule. Their power lies in the fact that a thing which is maintained is a thing the Hollow cannot forget into its mouth — and maintenance, unlike courage, can be delegated, written down, argued about, and done again next week by somebody else. A red thread with eight knots is not magic; it is a statement that this line is attended to, and attention is the one thing the Hollow does not have. To be given a week on the board is to be told you are part of what holds the valley up. To lose it is not exile. It is simply that people stop counting you, and here, more than anywhere, a thing that is not counted is a gap.

Aelinnel of the Inland Sea

The Bell-Tenders.

The bridge is finished. The gap is part of the bridge. Put the plank down and come look at the drawing.

— Bell-Warden Tumble

The Bell-Tenders build floating shrines, maintain bridges, and negotiate with the spirits that share the Inland Sea. Their counting songs and copper bells are precise instruments. Deliberate gaps appear in public works: an unlit lamp, an empty place, the missing ninth plank. Builders must leave them as carefully as they fit the surrounding timber.

This is a learned discipline. A tide-counter records the conditions of an observation; a bell-warden checks whether a replacement carries the right note. A maker can appeal an inspection and still refuse to open an unsafe crossing. Practical disagreements concern who checked the tide, who authorized a repair, and what the spirit was actually promised.

The procedure is more explicit than the inherited proprieties of many western Aelinnel. That does not make its deepest causes plain. An inspector can explain how to preserve a gap without explaining why completing the pattern lets something cross. Keep the working knowledge useful and the remaining uncertainty intact.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge and pickled plums. Good brine is kept and reused. A jar borrowed for a work-party usually returns with an argument about whose household makes the better batch.

What does a child learn before they can walk? When a ritual count must stop and how to keep their place when a bell sounds from another shore. Ordinary quantities still have to be counted; children are taught the difference in practice.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they wait for the bell before stepping onto the landing. A traveler who asks for the crossing procedure gets an answer, usually more detailed than they expected.

What is the most common crime? Bell-theft, or replacing a bell without recording the change. Copper has a market value, but a stolen bell sounded at the wrong shrine can do damage far beyond it.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A new piece of public work has passed inspection under conditions nobody tested. People who signed it can disagree over whether closing it now would admit negligence or prevent it.

Where do people go when they die? Ashes may be mixed with copper dust and cast into a bell. Relatives know where that bell hangs and attend when it must be retuned or taken down.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? The wrong is recited publicly in the prescribed count. The harmed party can name the repair still needed. A spirit’s response does not rebuild a landing by itself.

What is the most important festival? The Retuning of the Bells in autumn. Work is scheduled from high tide to low tide. A household may supply food to the next crew because its own crossing depends on their finishing.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? An inspector approved a complete pattern after receiving a contradictory set of plans. The original drawing survives. Whether the substitution was careless or deliberate changes whom the community can trust.

Lethai-al of Nihori

The Storm-Touched Wanderers.

We leave after Yuki has dried the pouches. The tide will turn again. The names will not carve themselves a second time.

— Procession Elder Kenji

The Lethai-al of Nihori follow processional circuits through island groves, ports, and cliff shrines. Bands carry tide-charms of the drowned, wrapped against weather and entrusted to people who know their care. The loss of their kingdom travels with them as surviving names and broken accounts. It has not left them with a complete explanation of what was lost.

Their ties to the land concern passage and return rather than a lord’s claim to a field. Morikage is a regional gathering place, and roadside groves and shrines sustain a circuit people expect to walk again. Painted carts, winter charm-vaults, and camps require repair. A moving community still cooks, raises children, and makes things it can carry.

An elder’s standing comes from repeated circuits and the people willing to follow. A route may be familiar yet closed by a daimyo, a landslide, or a shrine’s unresolved demand. Bands can disagree about a departure without ceasing to belong to one another. Their first-blood duels can settle a burden to be carried for a season; they do not make every disagreement a fight.

A tide-charm can quiet a storm long enough for a crossing. That usefulness does not make an ancestor’s name expendable equipment. A traveler asking the band for protection is asking particular people to take responsibility for a name they carry.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice balls and smoked fish on the road. At a camp with time and shelter, they cook. Someone must fetch water and mend the wrapping before the next departure.

What does a child learn before they can walk? How to recognize the person carrying the family’s charms, and when to be quiet at a gate. The wave-count is learned beside someone who can interrupt it.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? How they treat a tired companion and whether they leave room for the carts. Feet tell how far a person has walked; they do not settle whether that person deserves a meal.

What is the most common crime? Charm-theft. Recovering the shell matters as much as finding the thief. A dispute over who should carry a recovered name may continue after the theft is resolved.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A band has missed a shrine but still claims to have completed its circuit. Someone who stayed behind to tend an injured companion knows which observance was omitted.

Where do people go when they die? Names of the dead are carved onto shells and carried in oiled pouches. Families entrust their care to a living bearer. Losing a pouch creates a search and a responsibility, not proof that every lost ancestor has become the same spirit.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A crossroads circuit and a public admission may be required. Carrying another person’s burden for a season makes the redress visible during the journey.

What is the most important festival? The Procession of the Ninth Wave in autumn. Bands come to the shore with the names of those drowned that year. Getting the elderly, the carts, and the charms there is part of the observance.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? An elder has substituted a newly carved charm for one lost on the road. The family believes its ancestor still travels in the old shell. Finding the original may be possible; concealing the search serves someone.

Opening situation.

A storm has closed the usual crossing. Kenji needs the band through before the circuit’s next observance; Yuki cannot find one tide-charm after the carts were moved. A fishing household offers shelter, while a lord’s messenger insists that the road remain clear for his retinue.

For the GM.

Give the band practical resources and choices. The party can recover a charm, secure shelter, repair a cart, or negotiate passage. Do not require abandoning all possessions to join people whose carts and charms are central to their lives. Treat complete accounts of the drowned kingdom’s fall as interested traditions, not a solved history handed to every visitor.

Lethai-ar of Ayokha

The River-Bound Oath-Keepers.

I heard you promise delivery before the rain. I did not hear you promise that the river would remain where it was.

— Vigil Lady Suyin

At the monsoon wharves, Lethai-ar Vigils attend promises made before goods or people depart. Silken witness cords, braided with hair and dyed with river mud, hold those terms in a form a household can keep. A fulfilled vow releases its cord to the fire. A broken one tightens. The magic makes a breach consequential; the people present still need to understand what was promised.

Nakhon Phanom is a regional center of this river-bound life. Witnessing is a practice sustained by families, cord-makers, and attendants who keep the lamps. The Vigils will witness agreements between enemies. That exactness does not make them detached from the harm an agreement can cause, or willing to supply a false date because a neighbor asks kindly.

River shrines associate Benzaiten with the Witness and Inari with the Traveler. Offerings are made before a departure and after a vow is fulfilled; the witness and the traveler may be the same person on different mornings. Accounts of river-folk and older fae courts survive beside the work; neither provides a complete recovered past for all Lethai-ar.

Lady Suyin has cut nine witness cords, the ninth her own. That is her history, not the rule for every Vigil. A lantern at the landing may disclose a lie without deciding whether a speaker misunderstood, which calendar governs a delivery, or how a promised crossing can be repaired.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge and river fish, with bones returned to the water. A witness working the early landing eats when another can keep the lamp attended.

What does a child learn before they can walk? The witness knots and how to repeat someone else’s words without improving them. The difference between a promise and a hope takes longer to learn.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whom they bring to witness their account. A lamp matters, but a traveler whose lamp has gone out may need oil and an explanation rather than immediate banishment.

What is the most common crime? Cord-forging: passing a false cord as the work of a recognized Vigil. Investigators need the maker, the dye, and the people who claim to have heard the oath.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A promise can be perfectly witnessed and still be harsh. The witnesses must decide how to help those living under it without making their own account false.

Where do people go when they die? Their ashes may be mixed with lamp oil and used in Vigil lanterns. Families remember which lamp carries whom. A working light remains someone’s relative as well as part of a hearing.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A new cord can bind an undertaking to repair the wrong. Its words must be spoken and understood; the injured party’s acceptance matters alongside the rite.

What is the most important festival? The Reckoning of the Cords in spring. Broken and cut cords are brought out for the burning barge. A cord that will not burn brings its unfinished terms back before the living.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? A royal cord displayed in public does not match the cord the original witness tied. Establish whose work it is and when it was substituted. The discrepancy is evidence to investigate, not a complete account of Suyin’s motives.

Opening situation.

A captain promised delivery before the first monsoon rain. The cargo reached its named landing after an upstream storm but before rain fell at the port. The cord has tightened. Suyin needs the original wording, the dated observations, and both parties present before anyone proposes cutting it.

For the GM.

Establish the exact promise and the facts of its breach before play. Preserve the cord’s printed tightening and Harm rules; do not add universal mind-reading or automatic answers to every dispute. Testimony, a renewed agreement, restitution, and addressing the relevant nat can all be useful. Keep Suyin’s personal history separate from unverified accusations about the God-King’s cord.

Lethai-thora of Sihai

The Silent Ledgers.

The copy you need is here. The account of how it came here is not. Please do not write them as though they were the same answer.

— Keeper Lian, writing on water

The Lethai-thora pavilions hold texts that an imperial office may deny, destroy, or demand back. Linshu’s keepers copy carefully, preserve context keys, and attend to the small material distinctions that make a record usable. A decree without its witness, a map without its date, and a true quotation detached from its occasion can each mislead without containing a false word.

Refugees came east carrying scrolls from older fae courts. The first emperor studied with a Lethai-thora tutor, and archival traditions grew through those contacts. Human households, teachers, and officials made institutions of their own around and alongside the pavilions. Partnership, patronage, and conflict followed. A human school may prize the connection while disputing a pavilion’s right to retain copies its founder entrusted to the keepers.

The records they preserve are more complete than their own past. Keeping certain forbidden truths takes personal memories: the color of a mother’s dress, a familiar preference, a face once loved. The keeper retains the guarded knowledge and may fail to recognize the thing sacrificed. Colleagues learn to notice what a person no longer asks for.

Their reluctance to publish is deliberate. A keeper can answer a practical question fully while declining the conclusion a visitor wants attached to it. Some compare observations in private and leave the public explanation unwritten. This makes them careful witnesses, not omniscient arbiters who secretly know every answer. Keeper Lian has not spoken aloud in twenty years; her written testimony still has a recipient and a purpose.

What do people eat for breakfast? Rice porridge and dried fish. Cooking fires stay away from the collection. A keeper checks a bundle of discarded paper before releasing it to the kitchen; apprentices complain about how long this takes.

What does a child learn before they can walk? How to handle paper with clean, dry hands and wait while another person finishes reading. Reading without moving the lips follows. A child who mouths a line is brought back to the beginning by someone who remembers learning it.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Whether they keep the place marker and ask before moving a context key. Ink stains can show work; clean hands can show that somebody washed before entering.

What is the most common crime? Context-key theft. A text may remain physically present while becoming useless to its authorized readers. Following the key can require interviewing someone who has forgotten why they took it.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? A colleague no longer recognizes a keepsake everyone remembers them carrying. The guarded text is intact. The people around its keeper must decide what to say about the missing memory.

Where do people go when they die? The Well of Forgotten Names is part of the pavilions’ funerary practice. The living arrange the transfer of a keeper’s texts and duties; the dead person’s name is not a convenient replacement for the evidence they left.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? A witnessed silence may accompany the apology. Returning a context key, correcting a misattribution, or releasing a withheld copy gives that observance a practical meaning.

What is the most important festival? The Unrolling of the Ledgers in winter. Keepers gather over a forbidden text whose reading has consequences for them. Who attends, who records it, and who remembers the last reading all matter.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? A sealed genealogy contradicts the lineage an imperial household presents. Its custody and meaning must be established before publication. Possessing it does not answer whether every person named understood the deception.

Opening situation.

The Quiet Chancellor requests a scroll whose catalog entry survives but whose context key is missing. Lian can show the copy and identify its recent readers. One reader remembers the text exactly and no longer recognizes the person who brought them to the pavilion.

For the GM.

Make the requested fact investigable. Establish what the context key unlocks, who last held it, and what evidence can travel without the original. Guarded knowledge may cost an ordinary personal memory under the existing rules; do not disclose an ultimate cosmological explanation as a reward. Preserve gaps in the Lethai past. No surviving origin account should conveniently restore the whole lost civilization.

  • Trap Springs: A prepared danger activates with full effect.

  • Authority Arrival: High Keeper, Quiet Chancellor, or Ghost of the Ninth Scroll intervenes.

  • Scene Shift: The environment changes dramatically—archive floods/well overflows/first pavilion materialises.

  • Patron Omen: Divine/arcane forces take notice—omen appears/blessing lost/curse manifests.

  • Narrative Pivot: The story takes an unexpected turn that reframes objectives.

Region-Specific SB Options

  • Lethai-thora (Silence Law): Lamps flicker without breeze, scrolls shuffle their own order, a whisper is heard from a sealed jar.

  • Lethai-thora (Memory Law): A keeper forgets your face, a memory-thread glows, you recall a fact you never learned.

  • Lethai-thora (Context Law): The season changes inexplicably, a stone context key warms, a witness appears who was not there.

Pursuit Ladder (Near / Mid / Far)

  • Advance/Withdraw (Wits + Stealth): On a hit, shift one band. On a strong hit, also impose Silent Step.

  • Cut the Memory (Presence + Sway): On a hit, freeze range for one exchange; if a memory-thread is in your possession, +1 die.

  • Weather the Interrogation (Endurance + Spirit): On a hit in Censorate’s Interrogation, you pick who is questioned; on a miss, the GM does.

Boss Seeds (Ink & Silence)

  • The Scroll-Eater (Nine Forgeries) — Phases: Scrolls Burned →\rightarrow Orchestrated Erasure →\rightarrow Archive Coup. Cracks: recover a true scroll, expose a false context key, survive a night in the well.

  • The Forgotten God (Memory Eater) — Phases: Voices from the Shrine →\rightarrow Procession of Blank Scrolls →\rightarrow Ink-Baptism of a Keeper. Cracks: relic memory-thread, survivor testimony, the first pavilion opening its doors.

Among the Lethai-thora, power flows through silence, ink, and the weight of a forgotten secret. A Keeper’s authority rests on how many secrets she has buried and how many she has forgotten. The archive is the fortress; the silence is the shield. Yet they are also entangled with the empire they helped create — the Censorate watches them, the Emperor needs them, and the common people fear them. The Lethai-thora can no longer leave Sihai, even if they wished. Their fate is bound to the Mandate of Heaven.

For the GM: Power in Lethai-thora society is measured in scrolls sealed, context keys held, and the length of a pause. Patronage often takes the form of forbidden scrolls, context stones, or memory-threads — each tied to a specific secret, archive, or keeper. To emphasize the entangled relationship with Sihai:

  • Use the Silence as Sanctuary mechanic to create tense social scenes where a pause is a weapon.

  • Require Context Keys to unlock ancient lore — the party must gather place, season, and witness before a scroll can be read.

  • Let the Keeper’s Toll be a meaningful cost: fatigue, lost memories, or forgotten faces.

  • Remind players that the Lethai-thora are not outsiders; they are the empire’s conscience. A Lethai-thora NPC may hold a secret about a PC’s family, or about the true cause of a war, or about a crime the emperor himself committed.

In Lethai-thora society, the ink forgets nothing — and the silence remembers everything.

Aeler of the Eastern Ranges

The mountains have more than one manner.

An Aeler from the Wenkar Hills can spend an afternoon over tea with someone whom a Kazurai household would already have put to work clearing snow. Both can recognize the other’s stone-cutting. Neither would mistake the other’s home for their own.

Aeler live throughout the eastern mountains and hills. Their communities carry the character of those landscapes into their houses, their arguments, and the welcome they offer a stranger. The Himadri’s severity belongs to its immense heights. It does not describe every wooded ridge, warm escarpment, or volcanic island beyond them. Within a range, a closed mountain hold and a busy foothill settlement may retain a recognizable kinship while disagreeing about how much company to keep.

Under every range lie older workings, forgotten kingdoms, and undervaults. A living hold may occupy only a few inhabited levels above a much greater depth. Elsewhere the old entrance has become a spring, a terrace wall, or a place children are told not to shelter from the rain. The people living above it are descendants and neighbors, not necessarily custodians who can answer every question about what lies below.

The names here are those used on regional roads and maps. They name landscapes, not seven kingdoms or seven kinds of Aeler.

Sihai: dividing ridges and wooded hills

The Qianvar Mountains — the Middle Rampart

A long, wooded barrier crosses Sihai’s interior from west to east. Its northern approaches and southern valleys receive different weather, and a pass can bring a traveler into another season’s work before nightfall. The Qianvar stand between settled countries; their Aeler have lived with arrivals from both sides for generations.

There is breadth to their manner. A host may listen through a long disagreement, ask for another pot, and finally give a refusal that leaves no uncertainty. Their hillholds open onto courtyards with heavy lintels and shaded galleries. Foothill families share market streets with human neighbors; the upper gates may admit those same neighbors only on particular business. Openness at the table need not imply access to the hold.

Buried roads pass beneath later roads. In one valley a bridge abutment contains the roof of an older gatehouse, itself built above an abandoned royal stair. A flood exposes the stair while two communities are arguing about reopening the bridge. The quickest way across leads through a kingdom neither can name with confidence.

The Yundrath Ranges — the Folded Mountains

Southwestern Sihai rises into parallel ridges and deep river gorges, east of the Himadri’s high country. Snow can remain above a valley where fruit is ripening. A neighboring settlement is visible across the river and still several days away by a safe crossing. These are separate ranges folded close together, not another name for the Himadri.

Yundrath Aeler often appear reserved until they have established where a visitor stands with them. Once the welcome is given, it can be expansive: cousins arriving from another gallery, bowls passed along a crowded bench, someone insisting that the guest remain until the river falls. Narrow approaches open into surprisingly spacious homes. Their songs can travel farther than the path between the singers.

The undervaults change level as well as direction. A gallery abandoned high on one gorge face may once have been the lower approach to a kingdom whose greater halls have fallen away. A family’s returning relative has found a passage across the gorge, but its far door opens into an occupied house. The inhabitants recognize the workmanship and deny that there has ever been a door there.

The Wenkar Hills — the Rain-Green Steps

In southeastern Sihai, forested uplands descend through red-stone gorges and lower tea hills toward the coast. Paths wind around wet rock and old gardens. The Wenkar name covers the hills and their higher wooded ridges; travelers who dismiss the country as gentle usually do so before attempting a shortcut.

Wenkar Aeler favor sheltered courts, rounded stone thresholds, and things improved by long use. Their conversation can be leisurely and their hospitality elaborate. An unanswered question may wait through the preparation of several cups without anyone considering it forgotten. Weathered patience is not weakness. A household can refuse the same request for a century without raising its voice.

Mixed foothill neighborhoods are well established here. Potters, tea growers, stoneworkers, and their families need one another without sharing every custom. Under a kiln yard, rebuilding reveals chambers from several vanished reigns. The present owner needs a working kiln; an Aeler neighbor recognizes the lowest doorway from a household carving and asks that it remain closed. Neither can identify the people who last used it.

Nihori: snow and fire

The Kazurai Ridges — the White Spine

The high spine of Nihori’s central island rises in sharp ridges above wooded valleys. Snow closes the upper approaches for long stretches; the thaw sends water hard down narrow channels. The Kazurai share the islands’ nearness to the sea without resembling their warm harbors.

Their Aeler tend toward a spare, deliberate bearing. A well-kept doorway, a mended sleeve, and an invitation made without ceremony can carry considerable regard. The first evening after a pass reopens may be loud enough to startle a visitor who met the same family in winter. Their restraint has seasons. It is not the Himadri’s more relentless austerity.

Foothill communities keep close ties with shrine settlements and human valley households. A snowbound relative is somebody’s immediate concern, whatever lies between their families. Below the inhabited halls, older kingdoms are separated by collapsed galleries and buried slopes. When meltwater enters a sealed stair, one household wants the old channel restored and another fears what restoring it will uncover. The village downstream needs an answer before the next thaw.

The Akazun Mountains — the Ember Crowns

In Nihori’s southwestern island country, the Akazun rise around craters, wooded slopes, and hot springs. They are one named group within the wider Fire-Mountains, not a replacement name for every volcano in the isles. Some summits have changed within the memory of the families living beneath them.

Akazun Aeler can be forthright and demonstrative, with a warmth that visitors from stricter holds find disconcerting. A house can be rebuilt while remaining the same house: an old lintel is carried to the new doorway, a rescued hearthstone laid where children will sit. Permanence belongs to what people keep doing together as much as to the wall. They can defend a tradition fiercely and replace its damaged vessel without considering that a contradiction.

Aeler and human families share bath settlements, kiln towns, and the work of returning after ashfall. This does not give every family the same appetite for risk. Older halls lie between layers of ash and sealed rock; their dates do not form a single story of destruction. After a vent changes, warmth reaches a long-cold undervault. Voices are heard below the bathhouse. The families disagree about whether to shut the baths or send someone to listen.

Ayokha: upland basins and the long wet ridge

The Thanek Uplands — the High Basins

North of Ayokha’s main river country, broad uplands hold limestone ridges, enclosed valleys, and lakes. Roads leave an open basin and climb into country hidden from it. The sky seems wide until the next valley closes around the traveler. This is a landscape of adjoining communities whose horizons overlap imperfectly.

Thanek Aeler often combine an easy public welcome with a firm sense of household boundaries. A visitor may eat well, be introduced to everyone at the market, and discover that nobody has offered an invitation beyond the outer court. Their homes make room around the stone: covered verandas, planted terraces, and courtyards where several generations can disagree without leaving the shade.

Some hillholds keep their distance from human settlements; others have shared lakeside work and caravan stops for generations. Neither arrangement speaks for the whole upland. Beneath the basins, older kingdoms persist in dry chambers beyond flooded approaches. A falling lake reveals a royal landing below the present one. Fishers want access to the newly exposed water, while a nearby hold recognizes the landing as the entrance to a kingdom its own records had placed elsewhere.

The Tavarun Range — the Long Rainwall

A forested range runs along the western side of Ayokha’s river country and continues south into the peninsula. Granite and limestone rise through wet forest; short streams can make long journeys difficult. The Tavarun divide approaches and weather without forming a single, neatly administered frontier.

Tavarun Aeler tend to reveal themselves by degrees. From the path a settlement may show little more than a roof and a dark entrance. Within are successive sheltered spaces, stone walls softened by timber, and conversation that becomes less guarded as a guest is brought farther inside. Their reserve can coexist with an unhurried, durable attachment to neighbors. They do not need a summit above the clouds to possess gravity.

Foothill settlements include families who prefer human company to the restrictions of an upper hold, as well as families displaced from deeper workings. They remain settled communities; a porter, brewer, or parent does not become an Edgewalker or Mason by living there. Roots and water enter the undervaults beneath successive buried capitals. An old drain begins carrying carved fragments into a human village’s spring. The nearest hold can repair the channel, but the workers want to know why its inner face bears their grandparents’ marks.

Using the ranges

Choose a community’s home range before deciding how open it is to outsiders. Then give its inhabitants room to differ. A sociable Kazurai household may retain its spare manners; a Wenkar hold can be secluded without becoming severe in the Himadri manner. These tendencies concern regional voice and imagery. They grant no automatic ability and dictate no individual personality.

The situations above are local adventure seeds. Their evidence establishes a problem, not a required answer about the origin of all Aeler. Decide what lies beneath the chosen settlement before play, provide ways to discover it, and let the people above it have interests beyond opening the door. Shared craft can make cooperation possible without requiring one crown, one catastrophe, or continuous passages beneath the entire East.
