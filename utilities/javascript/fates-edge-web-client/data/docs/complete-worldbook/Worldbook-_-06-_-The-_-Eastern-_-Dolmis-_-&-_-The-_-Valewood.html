What do people eat for breakfast? Oatcakes and heather ale—the oats are grown on terraced hillsides, the heather is harvested from the high moors. The ale is brewed in secret, by women who have forgotten more recipes than the men will ever learn. The men do not ask. The men are wise.

What does a child learn before they can walk? To count to eight. Theona children are taught numbers on their fingers—eight fingers, two thumbs. The thumbs are not counted. The thumbs are for pointing. Pointing is for warnings. Warnings are for the ninth thing.

What is the first thing you notice about a stranger? Their shadow. Theona is a land of mists and twilight. A stranger whose shadow is too short has been touched by the Host. A stranger whose shadow is too long is running from them. A stranger with no shadow is already taken. Do not speak to them. Do not meet their eyes. Do not offer them salt.

What is the most common crime? Breaking the ninth taboo—but breaking the taboo is not a crime, it is an invitation. The Host rides for those who pour the ninth cup, speak the ninth name, or take the ninth step. The Host does not arrest. The Host takes. The taken are never found. Their empty cups remain on the table. The cups are warm for three days.

What does no one talk about but everyone knows? The Green Host are not ghosts. They are the living—Theonans who broke the taboo and were taken. They ride with the King in Green, who was once a chieftain of the northern isle. He broke the ninth vow. He was not taken. He was crowned. His crown is made of thorns. His throne is a barrow. His court is the mist.

Where do people go when they die? To the barrows. Theona’s dead are buried in long, low mounds that line the eastern cliffs. The mounds are planted with gorse and broom. The gorse blooms yellow in spring. The yellow is for mourning. The broom is for memory. The dead are not mourned. The dead are remembered. Remembering is enough.

How do you apologize for a serious wrong? You walk the Ninth Mile—a stretch of road that circles the central isle. The road is nine miles long. You must walk it at night, alone, with no light. If you reach the end by dawn, you are forgiven. If you do not, the Host will find you. The Host is always looking. The Host is always waiting.

What is the most important festival? The Feast of the Eighth Cup (autumn equinox). Every household pours eight cups of mead—one for each of the living generations. The ninth cup is poured onto the ground. The ground drinks. The ancestors listen. The ancestors do not answer.

What is the secret that could destroy a powerful person? The Three-Isles King is a changeling. The real king died in 798 AR, drowned in a peat bog while hunting. The changeling was placed by the Lady Beneath as part of a bargain. The bargain was for peace between the isles. The peace has held. The changeling has aged. The changeling’s reflection does not.

The Gray Liturgy: A Syncretic Faith of Theona

Deep in the mist-shrouded valleys and along the tide-cut stairs of Theona, a different faith endures—one that does not flee the Ninth but approaches it with ritual, witness, and the patient weight of carried secrets. The Gray Liturgy is not a foreign import. It is the old faith that was nearly erased, pushed to the fens, the barrows, the places where the ninth step is already half-taken. Its practitioners are not priests. They are keeners (who sing the dead across), well-walkers (who tend the threshold-wells), and taboo-breakers (who have taken the ninth step and returned—changed, but alive).

Where Theona’s folk practice avoids the Ninth, the Gray Liturgy engages it—ritually, carefully, with witnesses and salt and the Shepherd’s lantern. They are not heretics. They are the ones who remember what the Ninth actually is: not a demon, not a curse, but a door that was left unlocked. The Gray Liturgy carries the key.

The Saints of the Threshold (Syncretic Patrons)

The Gray Liturgy teaches that before the True Light came to Éire, there were the Naomh na Tairsı́—the Saints of the Threshold. They were not gods. They were the first to cross from death to life and return. They guide the living, carry the dead, and keep the doors between worlds from swinging shut. The Ninth (the Caillte) is not a demon—it is a saint who forgot his own name. He cannot guide because he cannot be called. The Liturgy’s highest calling is to remember him.

Saint Aodhán the Herdsman (The Pale Shepherd)

Aodhán was a herdsman who lost his flock in a blizzard. He searched for three days. On the third night, he saw a light moving through the snow—a lantern carried by a figure in gray. He followed the light to a cave. Inside, his flock was waiting, warm and unharmed. The figure in gray said: “You have found them. Now you will guide those who cannot find their own way.” Aodhán became the first Tréadaı́. His lantern is the Shepherd’s Lamp, carried by Gray Walkers. His feast day is the winter solstice.

Symbol: A lantern with a single gray flame.

Invocation: “Aodhán, who found what was lost, guide this soul to the green hills. The snow is deep. The night is long. But your lamp burns.”

Saint Lasairı́ocht of the Bells (The Confessor Beneath the Bell)

Lasairı́ocht was a nun who tended the dying in a plague village. She heard so many confessions that she began to carry the weight of the dead—their sins, their secrets, their unfinished business. One night, she rang the chapel bell at midnight. The bell cracked. The weight lifted from her shoulders and fell into the earth. A voice from the bell said: “You have carried enough. Now you will be the one who carries for others.” Lasairı́ocht is the patron of confessors. Her bell is the Clog na Faoistine (Bell of Confession). Her feast day is the first of November—the day after Samhain, when the dead are closest.

Symbol: A cracked iron bell.

Invocation: “Lasairı́ocht, who rang the bell at midnight, take this burden from my shoulders. Let the earth drink what I cannot swallow. Let the bell ring when I am empty.”

Saint Ciarán of Balance (The Unbroken Way)

Ciarán was a monk who spent seven years standing in a river, meditating, while the water flowed around him. He never moved. He never fell. When pilgrims asked how he remained still, he said: “I do not resist the water. I become it. The water flows through me. I am not still—I am the stillness between drops.” Ciarán is the patron of Well-Walkers. His meditation is taught to all who fear death. His feast day is the spring equinox, when day and night are equal.

Symbol: A standing stone with a spiral carved into it.

Invocation: “Ciarán, who stood in the river, steady my breath. The water flows around me, not through me. I am the stone. I am the still point.”

Saint Fı́onntan of the Book (The Witness)

Fı́onntan was a scribe who copied every manuscript in his monastery. When the Vikings burned the library, he saved nothing—because he had memorised every word. He spent the rest of his life dictating what he had lost. He never forgave himself for not saving the books. But he never lied about what he remembered. Fı́onntan is the patron of Truth-Tellers. His ledger is not a book of judgment—it is a book of testimony. He records what happened, not what should have happened. His feast day is the autumn equinox.

Symbol: An open book with a single gray thread marking a page.

Invocation: “Fı́onntan, who remembered what was burned, witness this crossing. Write the name in your ledger. Do not judge. Do not forgive. Only record. The truth is enough.”

An Caillte (The Lost One — The Ninth)

An Caillte was once a saint like the others. But he forgot his own name. He wandered into the fog and never returned. Now he is the Dorcha—the Darkness. He is not evil. He is forgotten. The Gray Liturgy teaches that if someone can remember his true name, he will return and the Ninth threshold will become a door instead of a wound. But no one remembers. And every year, on the night of the dark moon, he knocks. The Gray Liturgy does not open the door. They wait. They listen. They hope.

Symbol: An empty circle. A threshold with no door.

Invocation: “An Caillte, whose name is lost, we do not call you. We cannot call you. But if you remember yourself, knock twice. We will open the door.”

Note: The Liturgy does not invoke An Caillte in the same way as the other saints. He is not called upon—he is listened for. Taboo-Breakers are those who have heard him knock and lived. They are the only ones permitted to approach the Ninth threshold.

Regional Mood: Three Greens, No Ninth

Here, peace is a ritual, and every word sharpens the blade that seals it. Theona is claimed by Viterra but ruled by moots, well-keepers, and the old faith. The Viterran sheriffs count heads for the tax-roll; the Brehon judges measure truth by the witness of cattle and the memory of old words. The Viterran bishops raise hymns to the White Flame; the well-keepers leave milk and butter at the thorn crosses. The Everflame Inquisitors count sins in the silence between prayers. Every hedge is a boundary, every bridge a negotiation, every ninth step a door the Green Host might open. And in the mist-shrouded valleys, the Gray Liturgy keeps its candles lit and its bells waiting.

Linn dragon-prows cut through the fog for raiding; Ashaani slavers hunt the coves in stolen coracles; pirates from Zakov and the Vilikari shore trade stolen goods for kelp and salt. Sidhi sailors and Aelinnel factors run cargo up the tide-stairs, and Aelaerem hedge-wives barter red thread for hawthorn passes. To the west, across the narrow sound, the Viterran crown sharpens its taxes and its nooses. Through it all, the Ninth Taboo threads every handshake, every toast, every whispered prayer. To break it is to invite the Green Host—and the Host does not relent. To break Viterran law is to invite the sheriff’s noose. To speak the silence is to invite the Everflame’s brand. The Theona have learned to break neither—and to bend all three. The Gray Liturgy has learned to listen for the knock.

Starting Location: A Moot Hill under rain, where vows are carved into standing stone and blackthorn knives gleam beside the ogham pillar. Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = leverage. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Diamonds are codified outcomes (blessings/tokens/rights) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • The “No Ninth” custom: whenever a 9 appears in your seed, add an omission to the scene (a missing step, an unsaid name, an unseen guest). If the 9 is a Diamond, you may break the taboo once—but someone will come to collect later.

  • If any A appears, echo green and count motifs—hawthorn that whispers, bells that miscount, promises that take the long way home. If the A is a Heart, a Confessor may appear. If the A is a Spade, a bell-shrine. If the A is a Club, the Green Host rides. If the A is a Diamond, the Gray Liturgy offers a thread.

Additional Features (Cultural Mechanics)

  • The Silent Count: Players and GMs are encouraged to omit a detail (a name, a step, a tally) when the Ninth appears, echoing Theona’s taboo. This is how the islanders evade Viterran law: they simply do not say what the law requires. The law cannot count what is not spoken. Mechanical Effect: If a player speaks the ninth name/step/item in a scene, tick the Green Host Rising timer by 1. If they omit it successfully, gain 1 Boon. If they tie a gray thread instead of speaking, gain 2 Boons and do not tick the timer.

  • Green Host’s Omen: Any dream sequence or omen may be framed as the ride of the Green Host—hoofbeats across the sky, wind through the moors, sea spray tasting of salt and blood. The Viterran garrison has learned to ignore the omens. The garrison is understrength. The Everflame Inquisitors document them as evidence of demonic activity. The Gray Liturgy interprets them as messages from the dead.

  • Bride-Peace Feasts: Weddings, truces, and funerals blur; sharing a feast can end a feud for a season—or doom a guest if the ninth cup is poured. The Viterran crown has outlawed these feasts. The feasts continue. The crown’s officers attend. They never pour the ninth cup. The Abbess sends clerics to bless the cups. The cups break. The Confessor pours the ninth cup into a cracked bell. The bell rings. The feast is blessed.

  • The Gray Thread: A character may tie a gray thread around their wrist as a symbol of a burden carried or a promise to a Confessor. The thread cannot be cut by the same hand that tied it. Once per session, the wearer may transfer a Condition caused by guilt, fear, or despair to a willing Confessor NPC, clearing the Condition. The Confessor gains 1 Fatigue. The thread must be burned at a bell-shrine within a week, or the Condition returns.

  • The Viterran Plantation Timer [8]: Tracks the Viterran crown’s efforts to colonize the isles. Advances when the party carries the king’s salt (guides a patrol, enforces a writ, speaks against a moot) or when a Viterran sheriff successfully imposes common law. Retreats when the party supports a moot, hides a fugitive, or exposes a crown official’s corruption. When full, a new plantation is declared: a Viterran town is built, lands are confiscated, and a new sheriff is appointed. The Green Host’s shadow grows. The Confessor’s bell rings. The dead watch.

  • The Everflame Orthodoxy Timer [6]: Tracks the Abbess of Whiterick’s influence. Advances when the party aids the Inquisition, purges “heresy,” or accepts Everflame gold. Retreats when the party exposes Everflame manipulation, protects native rites, or turns Everflame allies against the Abbess. When full, the Inquisition establishes a permanent tribunal; native rites become illegal under penalty of burning. The Gray Liturgy goes underground. The bells fall silent.

  • Brehon Law vs. Common Law: In any legal dispute, the party may choose to invoke Brehon law (custom, precedent, witness) or Viterran common law (statute, seal, written evidence). Brehon law grants +1 die if the party can produce a respected elder or a witnessed oral history; Viterran law grants +1 die if the party has a writ or a seal. Choosing one over the other shifts the Viterran Plantation timer by 1 segment toward or away from the crown. Choosing to ignore both (relying on Taboo) shifts the Green Host timer. Choosing to invoke the Gray Liturgy (a gray thread and a witnessed silence) shifts none—but the Confessor will remember.

Theona Superstitions (burn for +1 die once)

  • Bread to the Bog: Toss a crust into the mist before crossing a causeway. Ignore the first Ground-Mist penalty.

  • Knot the Eighth: Tie a single knot in a ribbon before a moot. Cancel Spoken Geas or take –1 die on your next oath (your choice).

  • Name to the Well: Whisper a dead enemy’s name into Saint’s Well. Gain +1 Effect against his kin for the scene, but the Matron will remember your name. The Abbess will also remember.

  • Thread to the Confessor: Tie a gray thread around your wrist before a dangerous journey. Once, when you would take a Condition from fear or despair, you may transfer it to a Confessor. The Confessor will carry it. The thread will turn black. Burn it at a bell-shrine.

In Theona, power flows through ancient custom, sacred spaces, and the careful observance of taboo. The Three Greens maintain their independence through a web of moots, well-keepers, and geas-keepers who ensure the old laws are honored. The King and Matron rule by consensus and tradition, while the unseen Lady Beneath influences events through dreams and whispers. Against them stands the Viterran crown—a power of sheriffs, bishops, and undertakers who seek to replace Brehon law with common law, hawthorn with church stone, and the Green Host with the White Flame. And watching from Whiterick Abbey, the Everflame Abbess schemes to preserve power by pitting Viterra, the native factions, and raiders against each other.

In the mist, a quieter power waits. The Gray Liturgy does not scheme. It rings. It carries. It listens. Its Confessors hold the weight of the dead. Its Gray Walkers guide the dying across. Its Well-Walkers descend into the Ninth and return. It offers no gold, no land, no writs. It offers only a gray thread and a cracked bell. For those who have lost too much, that is enough.

For the GM: Patronage in Theona revolves around blessings, exemptions, and the delicate balance of keeping or breaking taboos. Rewards often take the form of tokens, rights, or divine favor that can be woven into greater standing. To emphasize the colonial conflict:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (ribbons, tokens, blessings) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided by crown officials.

  • Let the Viterran crown, the Green Host, and the Everflame Abbess grant conflicting protections—players must choose whose favor matters more, and whose curse is worse.

  • Use the moots, wells, and causeways as arenas for social contests, where knowledge of Brehon law (and its deliberate omissions) determines success against Viterran common law and Everflame orthodoxy.

  • The Viterran Plantation Timer and Everflame Orthodoxy Timer are visible campaign fronts. Players can accelerate or delay them, but once they fill, the isles change forever—new towns, new sheriffs, new taboos, new inquisitors.

  • For parties drawn to the Gray Liturgy, add a third timer: The Unringing Bell [8]. Advances when the party breaks a taboo without offering a gray thread, ignores a deathbed confession, or fails to carry a burden. Retreats when the party ties a gray thread, rings a bell-shrine, or helps a Confessor. When full, the Gray Liturgy withdraws its protection. The Confessors will not ring for you. The dead will not listen.

In Theona, your word is your weave, and your weave determines whether you walk the green path or invite the Host—or the sheriff’s noose—or the Abbess’s brand—or the Confessor’s silence.

Zakov is the pirate-city of the Dolmis coast, built on the drowned bones of an Astroegro port city that sank beneath the waves when that ancient empire fell. The old stone ruins — shattered columns, flooded plazas, sealed vaults — lie only a few fathoms down, and at low tide you can see their roofs breaking the surface like the spines of dead leviathans. The city’s streets are built on the rubble, its piers driven into the wreckage, and its deepest cellars open into sunken chambers where the water is warm and still. The Astroegro are gone, but their secrets remain: crystalline shards that hum with a terrible patience, sealed doors that weep brine, and the slow, creeping corruption that the locals call the Reaping.

Zakov’s streets are piers, its courts are taverns, and its rulers are whoever the Syndicates fear most this season. The Salt Prince claims the throne of driftwood and extortion, but the real power ebbs and flows with the tide — and the tide remembers everything. Here, every manifest is a lie, every harbor a doorstep to a dozen conspiracies, and every fog-light a lure or a warning. The city breathes with the sea: at low tide, secrets exposed; at flood, the drowned walk the Sunken Quarter and knock on doors that should be locked. Zakov is not a place you visit. Above the water, three powers circle. Naval Intelligence watches from the Ember, a Kahfagian cutter permanently anchored in the smuggler’s gate, waiting for the Syndicates to make a mistake. Patron Alaric funds the Oligarchs’ artifact hunts from a counting-house in the Crimson Docks, a man whose glass eye records everything it sees. And the Salt-Bitten, a cult that worships the Reaping’s hunger, grows in the sewers and the sunken cellars, fed by the Ordo Artificum’s scholars and the Oligarchs’ greed.

Beneath it all, the Reaping. Centuries ago, the first Salt Prince — then a common adventurer — dove into a submerged Astroegro vault off the coast. What he found there no one knows for certain. Some say it was a living crystal, a fragment of a god that fell into the sea. Others whisper it is the father of Khemesh, the first hunger. Dreams curdle into obsession. Generosity rots into expectation. The city’s famous “everyone owes everyone” is not a culture; it is a symptom. The Salt Prince keeps the Reaping’s source sealed beneath his palace, but the seal is cracking, and the crystal grows.

The Reaping — A Campaign Timer

The Reaping is not a monster to be fought. It is a pressure, a corruption, a slow unmaking of the soul. Track the party’s entanglement with it using a Reaping’s Corrosion timer [8]. Tick the timer when:

  • A character accepts a bargain from the Salt Prince or a Syndicate factor.

  • A character uses a Serpent’s Mark (see Diamonds).

  • A character fails a Spirit + Endurance test while in Zakov (drowning dreams, crystalline whispers).

  • The party lingers in the Sunken Quarter or the Serpent’s Spine for more than a scene.

  • The party handles an Astroegro artifact without protective measures.

  • The party accepts aid from Naval Intelligence, the Oligarchs, or the Salt-Bitten.

At 4 segments: the character gains the Crystalline Whisper condition (you hear the Reaping’s voice in quiet moments; –1 die to resist fear). Their shadow begins to shimmer faintly. At 8 segments: the character becomes Harvested. Their shadow detaches and walks into the sea, and they owe the Reaping a name (a true name of someone they love, or their own). They may still act, but they are marked. The GM may spend 2 SB to have a Collector — a crystalline spirit in the shape of a drowned sailor — appear and demand payment. To clear the timer, the party must:

  • Offer the Reaping something it values more than a soul: a memory of a crime no one witnessed, a true name from the Astroegro era (found in the sunken vaults), or a shard of its own prison (a heist into the Salt Prince’s vault).

  • Perform a deep-dive ritual at the Serpent’s Spine, cutting a crystalline growth from the reef and replacing it with a lock of hair from a willing volunteer.

  • Deliver an Astroegro artifact to the Ordo Artificum for “containment” — though this rarely ends well for the artifact.

The city’s lifeblood is the narrow sea-lanes between the Dolmis and the Amaranthine. Zakov’s pirates and smugglers must bribe, bluff, or blast past both to reach the rich southern waters. The Linns of the northern fjords sometimes portage their longships south from the Aberderrin into the Dolmis, trading amber and walrus ivory for Zakov’s more questionable cargoes — and leaving behind storm-oaths that the local Syndicates are careful not to break. The Kahfagian presence is the newest and most dangerous: Naval Intelligence’s Ember is a permanent fixture, and the Oligarchs’ factors are everywhere. The Syndicates have learned to tread carefully around the Gray Lady and her agents.

For the GM: Patronage in Zakov revolves around smuggling rights, safe harbors, and the ability to provide protection from both legal and illegal authorities. To the north, the Valewood breathes; to the south, the downs of Amedell where the Aelaerem tie red threads to keep the Hollow at bay. The Green Gate sometimes opens onto halfling barrows – a fact neither people speaks of aloud, because speaking aloud gives the gate permission to move.

No single crown rules the gnomish lands. Dozens of courts, seasonal pacts, and garden societies weave a shifting lattice of loyalty. Some Aelinnel keep the old fae treaties, speaking in two voices and never carrying naked iron; others barter in the Green Market with memories instead of coin, treating courtesy as a kind of lockpicking. Still others vanish into the deep woods, listening to stag-roads where the trees whisper gossip about the living. The gnomish heart is divided – and that division is the only reason the fae haven’t eaten them whole.

Craft and courtesy govern every traffic between mortal keeps and the courts under the hill. Gnomish stone-singers hum bridges into alignment, their melodies keeping the Green Gate at bay. Forester-wardens hammer copper nails into thresholds because iron, they say, startles the truth. In markets under living roofs, a sentence can be folded like a napkin, and a memory can be wrapped for travel – but never opened before the second sunrise. Paths sometimes shorten for those who keep good count, and lengthen for those who sneer at patterns. The fae are never rude – but they are never, ever safe.

Field Procedures (Use At the Table)

  • Two-Voice Talk: When parley stalls, the GM asks: “What was said? What was meant?” If players state both, cancel the first social SB this scene; if they refuse, the next bargain demands collateral (memory/name). The collateral is always returned – but never in the same condition.

  • Count or Be Counted: At any fae-touched crossing, a player who audibly counts in a fitting sequence (steps, heartbeats, breaths) shifts Position one step safer for that crossing. Boasting or mocking numbers adds a free “Geas Catches” SB. The geas is always about a door. The door is always behind you.

  • Copper Courtesy Check: Producing copper/brass tools in a ritual space removes the first Fae Offense tag this scene; drawing iron instead escalates the next Complication by +1 Cap. The escalation is always polite. The result is never.

The Hollow’s Attention (Campaign Timer )

The Hollow is not a monster. It is a forgetfulness that lives in thresholds, miscounts, and broken guest-right. Track its attention with a visible timer:

Hollow Attention [8] – Advances when:

  • A character refuses an offered cup (+1)

  • A promise is broken on a threshold (+1)

  • The ninth cup is poured (+2)

  • A guest-right is violated (GM spends 1 SB to tick +1)

  • A child points at an empty chair (the Hollow is already listening; +0, but the GM gains 1 SB)

At 4 segments: Lanterns flicker without wind. Dogs whine at nothing. The Hollow is watching. At 8 segments: The Hollow manifests. Roll on the Hollow Walks table. The party must offer a story, a memory, or a name to appease it.

Hollow Walks (d6)

When the Hollow Attention timer reaches 8, roll 1d6 for the manifestation:

  1. A pale figure stands at the edge of the circle. It asks for a name. Give a name (living or dead) and it leaves. Refuse, and it takes your shadow (–1 die on stealth for a week).

  2. The ninth cup pours itself. Whoever drinks from it forgets their greatest joy (lose 1 XP, refundable by a quest).

  3. A red thread appears tied around every PC’s wrist. It tightens when you lie. Cutting it costs 1 Corruption.

  4. The geese fall silent. The Hollow is inside the circle. It demands a story that ends with a death. If the story is true, it leaves. If false, it takes a memory.

  5. The Moot Oak bleeds sap. The Hollow speaks through it: “One of you carries a promise unpaid. Settle it now.” The one named must offer 1 Boon or 1 Supply. Refusal ticks the timer again immediately.

  6. The empty chair is occupied by a figure in gray. It does not speak. It eats. If you serve it food, it leaves. If you ignore it, it eats your luck (all rolls are Desperate for the next scene).

Quick use notes

  • Draw until you have all four suits: Spade = place, Heart = actor, Club = pressure, Diamond = treasure. Highest rank sets the main Timer (2–5 → 4, 6–10 → 6, J/Q/K → 8, A → 10).

  • Treasures (Diamonds) are codified outcomes (blessings/tokens/host-rights) that change position rather than call for a roll.

  • If any A appears, echo Hearth & Hollow motifs—quiet bells, red thread, watch-geese, footsteps under the soil—so the dread feels woven through the hedges.

  • Track the Hollow Attention [8] timer as a campaign-wide pressure. Use the Hollow Walks table when it reaches 8.

Additional Features

  • Bread & Lantern: Presenting a guest-loaf and a lit lantern at a threshold shifts your next social action one step safer while you remain a guest of that hearth. The lantern must be lit from the host’s fire.

  • Neighborly Courtesies: When you keep local taboos (count “eight-and-one,” turn village stones inward, leave butter at cup-marks), cancel the first SB generated by rites or negotiations in that scene.

  • Listen at the Stile: A quiet pause at a stile, barrow, or hedge-tunnel yields a true omen—ask one short question about what the Hollow wants here and now; the answer arrives as a sign, a sound, or a small animal.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Sealed Gate: Some Aelaerem thresholds are marked with a nail from the Sealed Gate’s original shrines. A door with such a nail can be barred against any spirit for one night—but the nail must be removed before dawn, or the door will never open again.

  • The Savage Heart: In the deepest hollows, the Hollow is said to wear a bear’s shape during the winter famine. Hedge-keepers leave raw meat at the boundary, not as an offering but as a treaty: “Eat this, not our children.”

  • The Traveler: Lantern-wardens sometimes find a gray pilgrim waiting at a stile at dusk. The pilgrim asks for directions to a village that no longer exists. Those who answer truthfully find that the next road they walk is half as long.

  • The Clockwork Monad: Aeler engineers tried to map the hedge-tunnel lanes using breath-bonds and brass chains. The surveyors reported that the lanes would not hold still. The maps were burned. The Aelaerem still smile when asked about it.

  • Ikasha, She Who Sleeps: The Hollow is sometimes called Ikasha’s shadow—the part of her that did not fall asleep. Aelaerem children are taught never to whisper the name of the dreamer before bed, lest the Hollow mistake them for a messenger.

  • The Ninth: On the ninth night of a feast, the empty chair is left for the Hollow. If a guest sits in it, the Hollow will take their place at the table. The feast will continue, but the guest will never be full again.

Aelaerem Superstitions (burn for +1 die once)

  • Bread to the Barrow: Crumble a crust at a cup-mark stone. Ignore the first Hive-Swarm penalty.

  • Knot the Ribbon: Tie a single knot in a red thread at a threshold. Cancel Out-of-Season Mumming or take –1 die on your next festival roll (your choice).

  • Name to the Hollow: Whisper a dead person’s name into an empty chair. Gain +1 Effect when invoking guest-right this scene, but mark Obligation to the Pale Shepherd.

The Aelaerem weave their power through careful observance and small courtesies. Their magic lies not in grand gestures but in the precise attention to thresholds, seasons, and the proper ordering of things. Red thread binds more than wounds—it ties names to places, promises to people, and the living to the Hollow that walks beneath their feet. To offend a hedge-witch is to invite the Neighbors’ attention; to earn an elder’s blessing is to walk with the earth’s favor.

Lore Echoes (Cross-Expansion Hooks)

  • The Genesis Vats (The Clockwork Monad): Deep beneath the Amber Ziggurat lie the empire’s failed laboratories. Here, the Lethai ancients combined elf and beast, hoping to create the perfect guardian — to match the Astroegro’s engines of war. The vats still hum, and the beast-kin who survived do not speak of what they saw. The Shardwrights say the Monad’s pressure is still optimizing the old designs, and that one day the vats will open again. When they do, the soft place will breathe deeper.

  • The Parliament of Claw and Fur (The Savage Heart): The beast-kin are not savages. They hold a moot once every season, deep in a clearing that no star-road touches. Their laws are carved in scent and scar, and their judgments are final. The Savage Heart speaks through their eldest, but it does not command—it suggests. Outsiders who attend the moot must bring a true name of a foe and leave it on the speaking stone. Those who lie are given fur and four legs, and are hunted at the next moon. The beast-kin do not speak of the vats. The Savage Heart remembers.

  • Saint Oren of the Unfinished Star (The Ninth): A Lethai-ar astronomer who calculated the exact angle at which the empire’s star-roads would collapse. He was erased from the imperial records, but his ghost still walks the Ivory Observatory, tracing equations in starlight. Those who find him may ask one question about the forest’s hidden geometry. The answer is always a single number, and that number binds you. He was trying to close the door. He failed.

  • The Gray Pilgrims (The Traveler): A wandering order of Lethai-ar who maintain the old way-cords. They wear gray cloaks woven from mist and speak only in riddles. It is said that if you give a Gray Pilgrim a lock of your hair, they will knot it into your way-cord, and you will never be lost in the Valewood again—but you will also never find a place you truly wish to leave. They know the location of the sealed doors. They will not speak of it.

  • The Daughters of the Cord (The Bitter Root): A renegade Sister hides among the moss-scribes of the Rooted Amphitheater, trying to untie her own cord from the Hazel Queen’s design. She claims the queen’s hospitality is a cage, and that the forest’s memory is a trap. The Hazel Queen has not corrected her; she only smiles, and the roots grow tighter each night. The Sister whispers that the queen came through the door. No one knows if she means the door to the vats or the other door.

  • The Ninth: In any Valewood scene, the ninth leaf to fall from a tree will land pointing to a hidden threshold. The wise catch it—or pretend they didn’t see. Those who follow the leaf’s direction find a place that was not there a moment ago, and something old waits inside. The threshold may lead to a sealed laboratory, a fae court, or a clearing where the air smells of jasmine. It is always warm.

In the Valewood, power flows through ancient magic, forgotten oaths, and the delicate balance between guest and host. The Lethai-ar maintain influence through their knowledge of paths and ruins, while the fae courts wield power through hospitality and geas. Beast-kin serve as guardians of the deeper mysteries, and the echoes of the empire still patrol their eternal rounds, enforcing laws written in starlight and stone.

But beneath it all, the sealed doors wait. The fae courts are not the masters of the forest — they are its children, born from the soft place that pressed through when the empire’s experiments tore the fabric. The Lethai-thora who survived burned their records and sealed their guilt in vaults of amber and iron. The beast-kin carry the memory in their bones. The monsters are the ones who remember too clearly.

For the GM: Patronage in the Valewood revolves around hospitality, knowledge, and the careful navigation of ancient laws. Rewards often take the form of charms, keys, or safe passage that can be leveraged into greater influence. To emphasize the hidden horror:

  • Tie rewards to visible symbols (tokens, mirrors, cords) that can be challenged, stolen, or voided — and that may carry a whiff of the vats.

  • Let rival courts issue conflicting protections, forcing players to choose whose favor matters more — and which sealed door they are willing to approach.

  • Use the ruins, groves, and star-roads as arenas for social contests, where knowledge of ancient customs determines success — and where a misstep may lead to a place that was sealed for a reason.

  • Introduce the Genesis Vats Aftermath as a recurring tension. The deeper the party goes, the more they risk waking something that should sleep.

In the Valewood, your word is your world, and your world determines whether you walk as a guest or a ghost. But the ghosts are not the only things that walk. Something came through the door. It is still here.

The Lethai-al (Wood Elves)

Beneath the courtly titles and the star-road records, the true wood elves endure. They call themselves the Lethai-al—those who remain when the tower falls. They do not walk the high roads; they mend them from below, speaking to roots instead of stars. Their elders are oaks, their children are saplings, and their grievances outlast stone.

Where the Lethai-thora sought perfection and the courts seek dominion, the Lethai-al seek only reciprocity. They keep no scrolls and sign no contracts, yet nothing is given without expectation. A meal shared is a shield-pledge. A path shown is a future claimed. To them, the forest’s question is not spoken but lived: What was taken, and what will be returned? Outsiders rarely recognize the weight of these moments until the trees lean in.

The Lethai-al do not attend the parliaments of beast-kin or the audiences of Hazel. When they must speak with outsiders, they do so through intermediaries—moss-scribes, owl-sisters, or the rare gray pilgrim—so that the words are never truly theirs, and the obligation, if any, remains unrooted. They remember the sealed doors better than the archivists, for they were the ones who grew the thorns over them.
