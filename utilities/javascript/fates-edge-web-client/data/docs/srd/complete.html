Quickstart

The Core Loop in Brief

  1. Declare intent and approach. What do you do and how?

  2. GM sets Position and Difficulty (DV).

    • Position: Dominant (advantage), Controlled (balanced), Desperate (high risk).

    • DV: 2 (routine) to 5+ (extreme). Default DV = 3.

  3. Roll dice. Pool = Attribute + Skill (d10s). Each 6–9 = 1 success; each 10 = 2 successes.

  4. Count Story Beats (SB). Each die showing a 1 gives the GM 1 SB.

  5. Compare successes to DV. Use the outcome matrix.

Boons (Player Resource)

  • Earn 1 Boon on a Partial, 2 Boons on a Miss.

  • Maximum 5 Boons; trim to 2 at scene end.

  • Spend 1 Boon to: re-roll a single die, activate an Asset, or improve Position by one step.

  • During downtime, convert 2 Boons → 1 XP (max 2 XP per session).

Position and Effect

  • Effect (reward): Limited, Standard, Great.

  • Spending 1 Boon before a roll improves Position by one step.

  • The GM may spend SB to worsen Position.

Fatigue and Harm

  • Fatigue track = Body. Each Fatigue point worsens Position by one step. At Desperate, each Fatigue gives -1 die.

  • Harm levels: 1 (-1 die on related actions), 2 (-1 die on most actions), 3 (incapacitated).

  • Armor converts Harm to Fatigue. Minimum 1 Fatigue per hit.

  • Taking Harm clears all existing Fatigue (the shock of injury resets exhaustion).

Core Principles

Safety Tools: Lines, Veils & X-Card

Before play, establish how the table handles sensitive content. Fate’s Edge uses three standard tools.

X-Card. Any player (including the GM) may draw an X on a card or simply say “X” when a moment makes them uncomfortable. When the X-Card is shown, the described content is removed from the fiction — no questions, no explanations, no judgments. The scene is retconned or redirected immediately.

Lines. A Line is a hard boundary: content that will not appear in the game at all. Examples: “No sexual violence,” “No harm to children,” “No body horror.” Before the campaign or a session, players name their Lines. The GM and group honor them completely.

Veils. A Veil is a soft boundary: content that can happen off screen or fade to black. The event is acknowledged but not described in detail. Example: “A romantic scene can happen, but fade to black before it becomes explicit.” Veils allow the story to acknowledge mature themes without forcing players to roleplay through them.

Player-Managed Modules

Players track their character-facing resources to reduce GM overhead and keep play flowing:

  • Obligation (per Patron, marked when Rites are invoked/pushed).
  • Corruption Timer (for Cantors, marked when pushing Songs or GM spends SB on occult actions).
  • Leash (for Summoners, marked when spirits take harm, act against nature, split focus, or cross wards).
  • Asset States (Symbol/Item condition: Maintained, Neglected, or Compromised).

GMs retain all world-facing elements: Timers, NPCs, faction moves, Patron intrusions, and narrative consequences. Why this matters: when players manage their own modules, the GM spends less time bookkeeping and more time driving the story. Always verify player tracking at scene start/end.

Design Note. This rule prevents “GM as auditor” syndrome. If a player forgets to track a module, the GM does not correct it mid-scene — instead, note it for downtime resolution. Trust fosters faster play.

What Is Fate’s Edge?

Fate’s Edge is a narrative-first tabletop roleplaying system where every action carries weight, every choice has consequence, and even failure feeds the story. Dice are not merely success meters — they are instruments of fate, weaving opportunity with risk.

“The road remembers. Every broken wheel leaves a mark and every lit lamp had someone behind it. The only question is which of those you mean to be.”

The Central Question

What are you willing to risk, and what are you willing to surrender, to reshape the world around you?

Tone of Play

  • Cinematic: Scenes breathe; tense exchanges resolve in moments.
  • Consequential: A whispered secret can topple a kingdom; a missed roll can spare a life.
  • Collaborative: The GM sets stakes; the players drive the fiction.

Key Concepts

Narrative Time

Time is measured by story weight, not by timers:

  • A Moment: a heartbeat, a strike, a whispered word.
  • Some Time: a few minutes — a skirmish, a careful lockpick, a short negotiation.
  • Significant Time: hours — travel between locations, a ritual, an interrogation.
  • Days: marches across countryside, training a cadre, recovery from serious harm.

Story Beats (SB)

Every time a player rolls a 1 on a die, the GM gains a Story Beat. SB are narrative currency — spend them to introduce complications, escalate tension, shift Position, or advance timers.

Affinity

Each culture grants an Affinity — a narrative edge or metaphysical bond that makes certain actions more reliable (e.g., Aeler stone-sense, Aelaerem hearth-luck). Affinities are described in the full cultural guides.

Note on Stacking. If your ancestral gift (from your people) and your cultural affinity provide the same benefit (e.g., both improve Position), they do not stack. You may use one per scene. Choose which applies when you need it.

Prestige Abilities

High-level talents unlocked by mastering cultural or arcane arts. They are narrative milestones as much as mechanical upgrades.

On-Screen vs. Off-Screen

  • On-Screen: followers, allies, companions who stand beside you in a scene.
  • Off-Screen: assets, titles, informants, safe houses — resources that act when you spend Boons or during downtime.

Boons Overview

Boons are narrative tokens earned primarily by failing rolls with meaningful consequences. They represent momentum, luck, or a second wind.

Earning Boons:

  • Partial (0 < successes < DV): Gain 1 Boon.
  • Miss (successes = 0): Gain 2 Boons (provided the roll was meaningful and generated at least one SB).
  • Bond-driven action: Once per bond per session, when you act meaningfully on a Mutual Bond and provide an Intricate Description, gain 1 Boon.
  • GM award: For creative solutions, sacrifices, or outstanding roleplay.

Spending Boons:

  • Re-roll a single die after seeing the pool.
  • Activate an on-screen Asset.
  • Improve Position by one step before a roll.
  • Power certain Rites or abilities as described in their text.
  • During downtime, convert 2 Boons → 1 XP (once per session; max 2 XP per session via conversion).

Limits:

  • Maximum 5 Boons held at any time.
  • At the end of each scene, reduce held Boons to a maximum of 2 (excess are lost).
  • At most 2 Boons from failures per character per scene.
  • Repetition rule: the same action with the same stakes in the same scene cannot award another Boon.

GM Guidelines for Timers and SB

To keep the game flowing and avoid overwhelming the table:

  • Timer Limit: Maintain at most three active timers per scene. Combine redundant timers or treat additional timers as off-screen campaign timers (ticked between scenes as a result of scene outcomes).
  • SB Banking: Try to keep banked SB (unspent at scene end) under Tier + 1 (e.g., Tier I: max 2 SB, Tier II: max 3 SB). Unspent SB beyond this may carry over (GM discretion, max 4).
  • Terminology: Use “Success with SB” instead of “Success with SB” for clarity — it emphasizes the narrative opportunity rather than a penalty.

Fail Forward: Every Roll Matters

  • A Clean Success advances the fiction without added friction.
  • A Success with SB achieves the goal but introduces a complication (GM spends SB).
  • A Partial makes meaningful progress and gives the player a Boon.
  • A Miss fails to achieve the goal, gives the player 2 Boons, and gives the GM SB to escalate.

Selling the Action

When a player provides an intricate description that reveals stakes, risks, or character investment, the GM may grant a mechanical edge — improving Position by one step, granting +1 die, or banking a Story Beat. This is a reward for engagement, not a tax for shy players. A simple ‘I attack’ is always valid.

No roll should result in “nothing happens.” Every outcome changes the situation.

What Are Story Beats, Really? Story Beats (SB) are the game’s permission to make things interesting. Every time a player rolls a 1, they are inviting the world to push back. SB are not punishments — they are narrative fuel generated by player dice, telling you: “Make this interesting.” Spend them to introduce complications, escalate threats, or reveal hidden dangers. Do not hoard SB for the climax — the climax is made of the SB you spent along the way. A scene without SB is a scene where players are in control; a scene with SB is where the world pushes back. They are your primary pacing tool.

Can I Roll Fewer Dice? No!

Never reduce a player’s dice pool. A player who asks to roll fewer dice to avoid Story Beats misunderstands the system. In Fate’s Edge, a 1 is not a failure — it is narrative fuel. Reducing dice pools breaks the math: Boons become scarce, SB dry up, and the player’s investment is undermined.

If you want tension, use Position, DV, Timers, and SB — never reduce the player’s dice pool. They earned those dice. Let them roll them.

Core Mechanic

The Resolution Loop

Every action that carries risk and uncertainty follows these five steps.

  1. Declare intent and approach. The player states what they want to accomplish and how (Attribute + Skill).
  2. GM sets Position and Difficulty (DV).
    • Position: Dominant (advantage), Controlled (normal), Desperate (high risk).
    • DV: 2 (routine) to 5+ (extreme). Default DV = 3.
  3. Roll dice. Pool = Attribute + Skill (d10s). Count successes:
    • 6–9 = 1 success.
    • 10 = 2 successes.
  4. Count Story Beats (SB). Each die showing a 1 gives the GM 1 SB.
  5. Apply the Outcome Matrix.

Critical Success

Rolling a 10 (counting as 2 successes) creates additional benefits:

  • One 10: Choose one – gain a free Boon, improve Position by one step, or add a narrative flourish.
  • Two 10s: Choose two benefits or one powerful effect (e.g., ignore armor + gain tactical advantage).
  • Three+ 10s: Legendary success – resolve the conflict dramatically and advance a secondary timer or create a significant story development.

Note: 10s are never re-rolled (even in Desperate position). Critical effects apply only if the roll succeeds (Successes ≥ DV).

Difficulty Values (DV)

DVSituation
2Routine, low stakes, nearly guaranteed.
3Default – pressured or minor opposition.
4Hard – hostile conditions, active resistance.
5Extreme – multiple constraints, dramatic gambles.

The ladder ends at 5. There is no DV 6. A task harder than Extreme is not a bigger number — it is a clock, a worse Position, or a flat no. Raising the DV past 5 only moves the roll toward automatic failure, which is the one outcome the Outcome Matrix already has a row for and does not need a difficulty to produce.

Position & Effect

Position (Risk)

  • Dominant: Re-roll one failure (die ≤ 5).
  • Controlled: No re-rolls.
  • Desperate: Re-roll one success (die ≥ 6). 10s are never re-rolled.

Effect (Reward)

  • Limited: Minimal outcome.
  • Standard: Expected outcome.
  • Great: Strong outcome with additional benefit.

Modifiers:

  • Player may spend 1 Boon before rolling to improve Position by one step.
  • GM may spend SB to worsen Position or reduce Effect.
  • The ladder ends. Position never rises above Dominant or falls below Desperate. At either end further modifiers do nothing; they do not convert into dice.
  • This matters because your pool is only ever what you paid for. A character with Attribute 1 and Skill 0 rolls one die at their worst, never none — so the question is always how badly can this go, and never may I attempt it at all. It is also what lets heritage, cultural affinity, assists and gear all push on Position without any of them touching the dice.
  • A fourth source of advantage on an already-Dominant roll is not wasted. It is the GM's cue to lower the DV, widen the Effect, or just say yes.

Effect is spoken, not tracked.

Position has three named steps because you need to know which one you are on before you roll. Effect has three named steps for the opposite reason: so the table has words for a swing after it lands. There is no Effect track, no Effect rating, and nothing to write down. The GM says it — “that's Great Effect, they're outnumbered three to one” — the table hears it, and the fiction carries it from there.

This is why Scale mismatch moves Effect and never the DV or the dice. A giant swinging at a man and a man swinging at a giant are the same roll against the same difficulty; what differs is how much a hit accomplishes. Putting that on the dice would make the giant hard to hit, which is not what being large means.

Say it in the fiction and give the reason. Digital tools may announce an Effect call to the table, but none of them store it, on purpose: the moment Effect becomes a number somebody owns, the table argues about the number instead of describing the swing.

NPCs and Resources

Adversaries track listed Fatigue and Resilience, but no Boons or player Harm-level track. Apply Fatigue costs to their track. A Boon-loss effect becomes a fitting hesitation or loss of advantage, not an invented NPC Boon balance. See the adversary resolution procedure for Armor and overflow.

NPC Special Abilities as SB Spends

Instead of rolling for NPC abilities, the GM may spend Story Beats to trigger narrative events.

Example SB Spends:

  • 1 SB “The cultist’s eyes burn – mark 1 Fatigue (near miss).”
  • 2 SB “The spider drops on you – you are Grappled (escape DV 3).”
  • 3 SB “The drake breathes frost – everyone in Near, test Body+Athletics (DV 3) or take Harm 2.”
  • 4+ SB “The lich raises its hand – test Spirit+Endurance (DV 5) or be Paralyzed for 1 exchange.”

The GM narrates the effect; players roll to resist.

Wards and Traps

Wards are magical barriers that block passage, teleportation, and shadow-walking. Traps are mundane or magical hazards that trigger on intrusion. Both use the same base resolution structure.

Wards (Magical Barriers)

A ward is defined by its Base DV (2–5), chosen by the GM when the ward is placed. The ward’s other properties derive from this DV:

  • Harm: A creature that triggers the ward suffers Harm equal to max(0, Base DV – 2).
  • Spirit Leash: A spirit (including summoned creatures) attempting to cross the ward suffers Leash ticks equal to the ward’s Base DV.

Crossing a Ward. A creature cannot teleport, shadow-walk, or use any effect that bypasses intervening space to cross a ward without first overcoming it. To cross, the creature must succeed on a test of the appropriate attribute (usually Wits or Presence) + Spirit against the ward’s Base DV. On a failure, the crossing fails and the creature suffers the ward’s Harm (if any). On a success, the creature crosses but may still suffer other consequences (e.g., alerting the ward’s creator).

Disabling a Ward. A character may attempt to disable a ward with a Wits + Arcana test against the ward’s Base DV. Success renders the ward inert for one scene (or permanently, at the GM’s discretion for non-recurring wards). Alternatively, a spell with the [DISPEL] or [FATE] tags may suppress or destroy the ward as described in the spell’s effect.

Traps (Mundane or Magical)

Traps function similarly to wards but do not impede teleportation or shadow-walking, nor do they inflict Leash ticks on spirits. A trap has a Base DV (2–5) and deals Harm equal to max(0, Base DV – 2) when triggered.

Disarming a Trap. A trap can be disarmed with a Wits + Deception test against its Base DV. On a success, the trap is neutralised. On a failure, the trap may trigger (GM’s discretion).

Example. A Lesser Wardstone (DV 3) bars a doorway. A character attempting to shadow-walk past must roll Wits + Spirit (DV 3). Failure inflicts Harm 1 (since 3–2 = 1). A spirit crossing the same ward gains 3 Leash ticks. A Wits + Arcana (DV 3) success disables the ward.

Maximum Dice Pool

Maximum die pool = 10d10. Any additional dice beyond 10 become automatic successes (1 auto-success per extra die).

Character Creation

Starting Build Points

Characters begin with 32 XP to allocate during initial creation. This represents a balanced baseline for competent starting characters.

Enhanced Starting Builds

Players may exceed the standard 32 XP build through narrative engagement:

  • Bonds: Up to two player-defined mutual bonds may be taken for +2 XP total. (See the coordination rules for bond-driven Boon rules.)
  • Complications: Up to two initial complications may be accepted for +4 XP total. Scenes start with +1 banked SB per character with a complication until their complications have been resolved in play.

This allows a maximum starting build of 36 XP. Players are encouraged to aim for 32 XP and use bonds and complications to enhance characterization rather than pure mechanical optimization.

The starting caps — attribute 3, skill 2 — are not raised by any of this. The one exception is the Prodigy talent, which buys a single attribute at 4 and charges for it in breadth, permanence and attention.

Attributes and Skills

Attributes

Every character has four attributes rated from 1 (weak) to 5 (exceptional). Attribute ratings determine the base number of dice for any action roll.

Skills

There are twelve skills. Each is rated 0 (untrained) to 5 (master), and the rating is added to an attribute to form the dice pool. Twelve is deliberate: a shorter list means fewer arguments about which skill applies, and every one of these is broad enough that the interesting question at the table is which attribute you are bringing to it, not which skill you are allowed to use.

CraftLay knowledge. Making, mending, and tending: smithing, carpentry, masonry, cooking, brewing, locks and mechanisms — and medicine, which is the same skill pointed at a body. Craft is what a person knows by having done it.

Craft is slow magic. A rite disturbs the Weave; a craft does not. That is the only real difference, and it is a difference of speed rather than of kind. The smith who has kept one fire for forty years and the witch who calls something up in an evening are reaching for the same thing by different roads — and the smith's road, being slower, leaves no wake for anything to notice.

This is why dedication and mastery in a craft can produce results the world will call miraculous without a single tag being spent: a blade that will not dull, a hearth that will not go out, a splint that sets a bone straighter than it began. Mechanically these are Wonders, and they cost XP and Obligation like any other. Narratively, nobody involved would agree they were doing magic. The line between Craft and Witchcraft is genuinely blurred, and it is meant to stay that way — a Game Master should never have to rule on which side of it a village blacksmith's best work falls.

Skills an expansion may add

Twelve is the core list, not a closed one. An expansion may introduce a thirteenth skill, and there is standing precedent for it — but the bar is deliberately high, because every added skill is a new argument at every table about which one applies.

A new skill has to clear one test: it must not be expressible as an existing skill under a different attribute. "Resolve" failed that test — it was Spirit + Endurance wearing a hat. "Command" failed it — Presence + Sway. If your idea reduces to one of the twelve with the right attribute in front of it, you have found a good use of a skill, not a new skill.

Rush Rule

When you buy or upgrade an ability during downtime, you may skip the normal training time by accepting a risk.

Rush Rule Procedure

  1. Declare: The player states they are rushing their training.
  2. Set Timer: The GM creates a Haste Timer [4].
  3. Push Forward: Each downtime action during the rush, the player rolls Spirit + Endurance (DV 3) to represent pushing through fatigue and distraction.
    • Clean Success: No tick. Progress continues smoothly.
    • Partial: The timer ticks +1. The training is harder than expected.
    • Miss: The timer ticks +2. Something goes wrong—a pulled muscle, a broken tool, a moment of doubt.
  4. Complete: If the timer reaches 4 before training completes, the ability or asset is flawed.
    • Attribute/Skill: The new rating is gained, but the character suffers a temporary -1 die penalty until they spend a full downtime session retraining (removing the flaw).
    • Asset: The asset is acquired but has a hidden complication (GM chooses: a rival knows about it, it requires unusual upkeep, or it's in a bad location).
    • Talent: The talent works, but the GM gains 2 SB to spend on a complication tied to its rushed nature (e.g., the technique is flashy but draws attention, or it leaves an opening).
  5. Clean Success: If the timer never ticks (0 ticks after all required downtime actions), the ability is learned without flaw.

High-Tier Play and Boons

As characters advance, the role of Boons shifts.

  • At early tiers, Boons primarily buy reliability — negating Fatigue, stabilizing risky actions, or nudging Position.
  • At higher tiers, Boons increasingly buy authority. A single Boon may force a truth, seal a pact, redirect consequences, or resolve a phase of a conflict outright.

High-tier characters typically have fewer Boons to rely on, but each spend carries greater narrative weight. This is intentional. Power at high tier comes from preparation, Bonds, oaths, and hard choices — not from inflating Difficulty Values. The world does not become harder to roll against simply because characters are stronger; instead, success and failure both reshape the story in larger ways.

High-Tier Example: The Archmage's Choice

A Tier IV archmage attempts to counterspell a world-ending ritual. She has 0 Boons left. She rolls 8 dice (Wits 5 + Arcana 3) vs DV 4. Result: 3 successes — a Partial.

Without Boons: She cannot re-roll. The GM rules: "The ritual slows, but a backlash rips through the ley lines. A nearby village is consumed by wild magic. You save the world, but at a cost."

With a Boon: She could re-roll a failure and possibly achieve a Clean Success, avoiding the backlash.

The scarcity of Boons made the Partial outcome feel meaningful, not like a failure. At high tiers, every Boon is precious—spend them wisely.

Advancement Notes

  • Attribute cost scaling encourages diversification over single-stat spikes.
  • Skill mastery yields tangible, fiction-first benefits.
  • Prestige abilities (6+ XP) should be gated by narrative milestones or patron bargains.

Weapons by Weight Class

A weapon's weight determines its Harm, its dice modifier at different ranges, and its tempo. Harm is applied directly when an attack hits; the dice modifier affects the attack roll, not damage.

Weight is not distance. Light, Medium and Heavy describe the weapon in your hands. Close, Near, Reach and Far describe the gap between you and the person you are trying to hit. The two sets of words never share a member, so "a Medium weapon at Near range" is unambiguous and always will be.

WeightHarmCloseNearReachTempo
Light1+2d+1d—Fast
Medium2+1d+2d—Standard
Heavy3–1d+3d+0dSlow

Notes

  • Fast tempo: You may move and attack in the same turn (Move + Action).
  • Standard tempo: You may move or attack in a turn, not both.
  • Slow tempo: Attacking requires a Set action before the attack. Set on one turn and swing on the next, ordered Move then Set, then Swing then Move — the two Actions land back to back (see 7.1) and you keep both Moves. You cannot Set and swing in the same turn unless a talent says otherwise.
  • Heavy weapons at Close: Suffer –1d due to limited maneuvering room.
  • Reach is where a hafted weapon can still touch you. A Heavy melee weapon threatens the Reach band natively — halberd, greatsword, war-scythe. It does so at no bonus: Reach is where such a weapon can reach, not where it does its best work. That is still Near, where its momentum arrives.
  • A Light or Medium weapon threatens Reach only if it carries the Hafted tag (spear, quarterstaff, light polearm), and then also at +0d.
  • A weapon cannot attack beyond its maximum listed range unless a talent or special ammunition extends it. A dash (—) means the weapon does not threaten that band at all.

Equipment Condition

All gear can become Neglected or Compromised.

  • Neglected: Weapons suffer –1 die on attack rolls; armor conversion worsens by one step (e.g., Light behaves as Unarmored). Neglected gear is usable but unreliable.
  • Compromised: Weapons suffer –1 die on the first attack each round; armor provides no conversion (all Harm applies directly). Compromised gear is barely functional.

Repair

  • Neglected → Functional: Requires a short rest (1 hour) and access to basic tools.
  • Compromised → Neglected: Requires a scene of dedicated work (Craft test, DV 3) or a visit to a smith.
  • Compromised → Functional: Requires significant downtime (a full day) and a successful Craft test (DV 4) or a professional smith.
  • Enchanted items require the same repairs; enchantments are inactive while the item is Compromised.

Action Economy

Turn Structure

Each character takes 1 Action and 1 Move on their turn. Actions and Moves may be taken in any order. A character may use a Boon to re-roll their action at the expense of their Move if they still have it available.

Ordering is the whole answer to two-part actions. Because the order is free, a character who takes Move then Action on one turn and Action then Move on the next has taken two Actions back to back, with nothing of their own in between, and still moved twice.

That is how anything needing two Actions resolves — setting a Heavy weapon and swinging it, loading a Slow weapon and firing it, any talent that says "then, on your next turn". None of them need a rule of their own. They need the ordering freedom this section already grants, and a player who knows to use it.

What it costs is not time but commitment. Everyone else in the round still acts between your two Actions. You have announced where you will be and what you are about to do, and the table gets a turn to make you regret it — which is the tension a slow weapon exists to create, and a good thing for the GM to spend a Story Beat on.

Move

  • Shift one range band: Close ↔ Near ↔ Reach ↔ Far.
  • Reposition within the same range band (e.g., from behind one tree to another). This does not change your range band but may affect cover or line of sight.
  • Stand: Use your Move to stand from prone.

Actions

Attack

Make a melee or ranged attack versus DV set by the GM and fiction. Ranged attacks made while within Close range of an enemy are Desperate (re-roll one success).

Disengage

Action (not a Move). You prepare to retreat safely. After taking Disengage, your Move this turn does not provoke opportunity attacks. You cannot also Attack this turn.

Dash

Action. Move two range bands (e.g., Close → Reach). Your next defense roll is Desperate.

Charge (Full Turn Action)

Move up to two range bands toward an enemy and make one melee attack. Costs 1 Fatigue. Your next defense is Desperate. Consumes both your Action and Move. Requires open ground (no difficult terrain). If your target is behind full cover, you cannot complete the Charge — you may move up to the cover and end your turn there; the attack is lost.

Two bands is the limit, so a Charge from Far arrives at Near, not in the target's face. Crossing the last band to Close is another turn. This is correct: sprinting the length of a field into knife range, under whatever is being aimed at you, is not one beat of a fight.

Skirmish (Full Turn Action)

Move up to two range bands away from an enemy and make one ranged attack. Costs 1 Fatigue. Your next defense is Desperate. Consumes both Action and Move. Requires open ground. If you start your turn hidden, you may Skirmish: leave cover, move, shoot, and may end your turn in new cover if available. You are exposed during movement and the shot; opportunity attacks apply normally.

Observe / Change Position

Take a beat to read the field or set angles. Gain +1 Position for one action this turn (e.g., Controlled → Dominant). Limit once per turn; cannot exceed Dominant.

Setup (Teamwork)

Create advantage for an ally. On success, grant their next action +1 Position or step up Effect (GM's call).

Assist (Teamwork)

Spend 1 Boon to give an ally +1 die on their current roll. You share appropriate risk or consequence.

Protect

Adopt a guarding stance or body-block. Choose a nearby ally. Until your next turn, you may intercept one hit targeting them and roll to resist it. On success, reduce or negate Harm; you take any fallout the GM assigns.

Interact

Lift, pull, flip a lever, shove a foe, break an object, apply a poultice, reload, draw or stow a weapon, hide, or search. GM sets DV and Effect.

Defend

Spend your Action or Move to enter a defensive stance. Until your next turn, you count as Defending. When resisting any attack or effect, improve your Position by one step (or gain +1 die if already Dominant).

Free Actions

Short shouts, dropping an item, a quick glance. Longer or tactical assessments require Observe / Change Position or Interact.

Reactions (Out of Turn)

  • Opportunity Attack: When an enemy leaves your Close range without using Disengage, you may make one free melee attack (Controlled Position, no action cost). This does not consume your action for the round.
  • Protection may trigger when an ally is hit and you are in position.
  • Class and asset reactions fire as written (e.g., counter-runes, ripostes).

Actions that provoke opportunity attacks when performed while engaged in Close with an enemy:

  • Making a ranged attack (bow, crossbow, thrown weapon).
  • Casting a spell that does not have the [TOUCH] or [WARD] tag.

Sidestep Strike

As part of a melee attack action, you may move one range band (Close ↔ Near, Near ↔ Reach, or Reach ↔ Far) without using your Move action for the turn. If you do so, your Position for that attack worsens by one step (e.g., Controlled becomes Desperate, Dominant becomes Controlled). You cannot also use your regular Move action in the same turn.

Shove

Action. Opposed Body + Athletics vs. target's Body + Athletics. On success, push the target back one range band (Close → Near). The target suffers –1 Position on their next action.

Grapple

Action. Opposed Body + Melee vs. target's Body + Athletics. On success, both characters are engaged (cannot move apart). The grappled target suffers –1 die on all actions. Breaking free requires a new opposed roll as an action.

Hiding and Cover

  • To hide, a character must be behind full cover (e.g., a large tree, a wall) or in heavy concealment (dense fog, darkness).
  • Take the Interact action to hide, rolling Stealth (or appropriate skill) vs. the Perception of any observers.
  • Once hidden, you are not a valid target for attacks unless an attacker first uses an action to Search (Perception vs. your Stealth) or you reveal yourself by attacking or moving into the open.
  • If you move out of cover to attack, you are no longer hidden for the duration of the attack and may be subject to opportunity attacks if you leave an enemy's Close range.

Ready and Overwatch

Players may hold an action with a clear trigger (e.g., "I shoot the first enemy who comes through that door"). If the trigger occurs, resolve the held action before the next exchange starts.

Refresh Windows

  • Once per scene effects reset at scene end.
  • Once per exchange effects reset at the next beat.

Combat

Combat uses the same core resolution loop as any other encounter: declare intent, set Position and DV, roll, apply outcome. This section provides specific rules for violence, movement, and tactical positioning.

Rhythm of Battle

Fate's Edge does not use fixed initiative. The GM spotlights characters based on the fiction, circumstances, and tension.

  • Ambushers act first. Targets respond after the opening exchange.
  • Spotlight shifts mid-scene if fictionally appropriate (e.g., reacting to a falling ceiling, seizing a moment).
  • Players may suggest acting when it makes sense in the fiction.

Turn Structure

Each character's turn consists of 1 Action and 1 Move. Actions and Moves may be taken in any order. Repeating the same Action is not allowed unless noted.

Move

Move up to one range band (Close → Near → Reach → Far, or vice versa). You may also use your Move to reposition within the same range band (e.g., from behind one tree to another at the same distance). This does not change your range band but may affect cover or line of sight.

  • Stand: Use your Move to stand from prone.
  • Dash (Action): Gain an extra Move. You may move two bands total in your turn. Your next defense roll is Desperate.

Disengage (Action)

Prepare to retreat safely. After taking Disengage, your Move this turn does not provoke opportunity attacks. You cannot also Attack this turn.

Range Bands

BandDefaultNotes
CloseTouchingGrapples, knives, short weapons. Inside the arc of anything longer.
NearA step and a swingOne-handed weapons at their best. Thrown weapons, short bows, most magic.
ReachA haft awayWhere a spear, polearm or greatsword still touches you and a sword does not.

Four bands, and each is named for what crosses it. Close is decided by hands, Near by a sword, Reach by a haft, Far by a bow. Absent is not a fifth distance — it is the scene declining to contain someone.

Reach exists because a polearm is not a long sword. A spear held at the haft governs a stretch of ground a blade cannot answer, and the person standing in it is in a fight the swordsman has not joined yet. A band is the honest way to say that: it is a place on the table that both players can point at, rather than an exception carried on one weapon's card.

On names. Three vocabularies describe a weapon in play and they share no word between them. Distance is Close, Near, Reach, Far. Weight is Light, Medium, Heavy. Tags are Hafted, Infighting, Accurate, Brutal, and the rest. Any sentence combining them reads exactly one way, which is the whole reason the lists are kept apart.

Melee Flag

Two parties are "in Melee" when they are directly engaged and within a band each can threaten — Near for most weapons, Reach for a Heavy or Hafted one. A spearman at Reach against a swordsman is in Melee; the swordsman is not, until one of them closes.

  • Enter Melee: 1 Move from Near (Controlled if under fire).
  • Leave Melee: 1 Move to break off. If threatened, you must take the Disengage action. Without Disengage, leaving Close range provokes a free opportunity attack.

Opportunity Attacks

Moving out of an enemy's Close range without using the Disengage action provokes one free melee attack from that enemy. The attack is resolved normally (Position usually Controlled) and does not consume the enemy's action for the round.

Additionally, the following actions provoke an opportunity attack if performed while engaged in Close with an enemy:

  • Making a ranged attack (bow, crossbow, thrown weapon).
  • Casting a spell that does not have the [TOUCH] or [WARD] tag.

Full Turn Actions

Charge

Move up to two range bands toward an enemy (e.g., Far → Reach → Near) and make one melee attack. The track is four bands long, so a charge from Far arrives at Near — sword distance — and not in the target's face. Closing the last band takes another turn, or a talent.

  • Costs 1 Fatigue.
  • Your next defense is Desperate.
  • Consumes both your Action and Move.
  • Requires open ground (no difficult terrain) unless you have a talent that overrides this.
  • If your target is hidden behind full cover, you cannot complete the Charge. You may move up to the cover and end your turn there; the attack is lost.

Skirmish

Move up to two range bands away from an enemy and make one ranged attack.

  • Costs 1 Fatigue.
  • Your next defense is Desperate.
  • Consumes both your Action and Move.
  • Requires open ground.
  • If you start your turn hidden, you may Skirmish: leave cover, move, shoot, and may end your turn in new cover if available. You are exposed during movement and the shot; opportunity attacks apply normally.

Cover and Hiding

Cover

  • Partial cover: +1 DV to target you.
  • Full cover: You cannot be targeted directly.

Hiding

To hide, a character must be behind full cover or in heavy concealment (dense fog, darkness). Take the Interact action to hide, rolling Stealth (or appropriate skill) against the Perception of any observers.

  • Once hidden, you are not a valid target unless an attacker first uses an action to Search (Perception vs. your Stealth) or you reveal yourself.
  • Revealing yourself by attacking or moving into the open ends the hidden state. You are exposed for the duration of the attack and may provoke opportunity attacks if you leave an enemy's Close range.

Height Advantage

Attacking from higher ground (e.g., a ledge, rooftop, or horse) grants +1 die or +1 Effect (GM's choice).

Difficult Terrain

  • Costs 2 Move actions to traverse one band (or a full action to Dash).
  • Charge and Skirmish require open ground unless the character has the Relentless talent.

Mounted Combat (Simplified)

If you have a War Mount and the Cavalier talent (or equivalent):

  • Charging into melee from Far → Near: +2 dice.
  • Firing while disengaging Near → Far: +2 dice.

The bonus applies only when movement is part of the action. Misses and Partials often cost mount Fatigue, equipment damage, or narrative complications.

Combat Action Options

In addition to standard actions (Attack, Disengage, Dash), characters may take:

  • Shove (Action): Opposed Body + Athletics vs. target's Body + Athletics. On success, push the target back one range band (Close → Near). The target suffers –1 Position on their next action.
  • Grapple (Action): Opposed Body + Melee vs. target's Body + Athletics. On success, both characters are engaged (cannot move apart). The grappled target suffers –1 die on all actions. Breaking free requires a new opposed roll as an action.
  • Protect (Action): Adopt a guarding stance. Choose a nearby ally; until your next turn you may intercept one hit on them and roll to resist it. On success, reduce or negate Harm; you take any fallout the GM assigns.
  • Setup (Action): Create advantage for an ally. On a success (appropriate roll, DV set by GM), grant their next action +1 Position or step up Effect.
  • Defend (Action or Move): Enter a defensive stance. Until your next turn, improve your Position by one step when resisting any attack or effect (or gain +1 die if already Dominant).

Default Combat Assumptions

To keep play moving, the GM may assume:

  • Default range = Near.
  • Default Position = Controlled.
  • Default Effect = Standard.
  • Move one band per Move action unless terrain or fiction says otherwise.

Example: Melee Exchange

Thane charges a goblin archer.

  • Thane declares Charge (Far → Near → Close). Costs 1 Fatigue.
  • GM: "Controlled, DV 3. On a Miss, the goblin gets a parting shot."
  • Thane rolls Body 3 + Melee 3 = 6 dice: 9, 7, 6, 4, 2, 1 → 3 successes vs DV 3. Clean Success.
  • Thane deals his weapon's Harm (Medium weapon = Harm 2). The goblin is injured.
  • Thane's next defense is Desperate due to Charge.

Integration with Fatigue and Harm

Combat frequently marks Fatigue (from Charge, Skirmish, and enemy abilities) and Harm (from successful attacks). See §9, Health, Fatigue, and Harm.

Health, Fatigue, and Harm

Fatigue Track

Each character has a Fatigue track equal to their Body attribute (minimum 1). Fatigue represents exertion, strain, and the accumulation of minor physical toll.

Marking Fatigue: Mark Fatigue for the following:

  • Performing a Charge or Skirmish action (1 Fatigue).
  • Forced marches, extreme effort, or environmental strain (GM discretion).
  • Magical costs (e.g., Pushing a Rite, Backlash, Obligation overflow).
  • Converting a Boon effect to Fatigue (substitution rule).

Effect of Fatigue

Each point of Fatigue worsens your Position by one step:

Dominant → Controlled → Desperate

If you are already at Desperate Position, each additional point of Fatigue instead imposes a –1 die penalty on all your rolls (cumulative).

Harm Levels

Harm represents serious injury. There are three levels:

Harm LevelEffect
Harm 1–1 die on actions directly related to the injury (GM discretion).
Harm 2–1 die on most actions until treated.
Harm 3Incapacitated or dying (cannot act; requires stabilization).

Taking Harm and Fatigue — The Procedure

When you take Harm from an attack, hazard, or magical backlash, resolve in the following order:

  1. Convert. Use the Armor Conversion Table to turn incoming Harm into Fatigue. Minimum 1 Fatigue per hit, even if conversion would give 0.
  2. Apply remaining Harm. If any Harm remains after conversion:
    • Clear all existing Fatigue (the shock of a wound resets exhaustion).
    • Increase your Harm level by the number of Harm points remaining.
  3. Add Fatigue. Add the Fatigue from step 1 to your Fatigue track.
  4. Check for Overflow. If your Fatigue total equals or exceeds your Body (track size):
    • Increase Harm by one level.
    • Clear all Fatigue.
    • Any excess Fatigue beyond the track size is lost.

Check overflow once per resolved hit or separate Fatigue event; discard excess Fatigue.

Armor does not reduce incoming Harm directly. Instead, it converts Harm to Fatigue according to the table below.

Recovery

Fatigue Recovery

  • Short rest (1 hour): Remove 2 Fatigue.
  • Full night's rest (8 hours): Remove all Fatigue.

Harm Recovery

  • Medical care: A successful Craft roll (DV 3–5, depending on severity) can reduce Harm by one level after a scene of treatment. A critical success may remove two levels.
  • Magical healing: A [HEAL] spell or Rite can reduce Harm by one level (see Magic system).
  • Natural recovery: Without medical or magical aid, one level of Harm heals after one week of rest. Harm 3 requires intervention.

Substituting Fatigue for Boons

If you have no Boons remaining (or wish to conserve them), you may pay 1 Fatigue in place of 1 Boon to activate an active, in-the-moment effect that normally costs a Boon:

  • Re-rolling a die.
  • Activating an Asset (GM discretion; delicate assets may not be usable while exhausted).
  • Improving Position by one step before a roll.
  • Powering a Rite or ability that explicitly requires a Boon.

This substitution cannot be used for:

  • Boon-to-XP conversion (downtime only).
  • Effects that require a Boon as a narrative resource (e.g., certain Talents or Patron bargains).

The GM may veto this substitution if the fiction makes it implausible (e.g., activating a delicate spy network while exhausted). This does not change the effect's outcome; it merely changes the resource spent.

Example: Armor and Overflow

Heavy armor, Body 2, currently Fatigue 1, Harm 0. Hit with Harm 3.

  1. Convert: Harm 3 → Fatigue 2 (from table).
  2. Apply remaining Harm: 0 remaining (all converted). No Harm applied, no Fatigue cleared.
  3. Add Fatigue: 1 (current) + 2 = 3. Track size is 2 → overflow.
  4. Overflow: Harm 0 → 1. Clear all Fatigue. Excess lost.
  5. Result: Harm 1, Fatigue 0.

Death and Dying

At Harm 3, a character is incapacitated. They cannot take actions. They may still speak weakly or crawl at the GM's discretion.

Stabilization

An ally may use an action to stabilize the dying character with a successful Craft roll (DV 3).

  • On success: The character remains at Harm 3 but is stable (no further deterioration). They can be moved carefully but cannot act.
  • On failure: The dying character suffers 1 Fatigue (the effort of trying to save them takes a toll). The healer may attempt again, but each subsequent attempt increases the DV by 1 (DV 4, DV 5, etc.).
  • On a second consecutive failure: The character's condition worsens—they are now Dying and will die at the end of the current round unless stabilized by a Critical Success (three successes above DV) or magical intervention.

Timing: Without stabilization, a character at Harm 3 will die within approximately 10 minutes of in-game time (or before the scene's tension resolves, whichever is shorter). The GM may extend this window if the fiction supports it (e.g., a skilled healer is nearby, a Patron intervenes).

Raising Harm Above 3

If a character at Harm 3 takes additional Harm, they die immediately. Resurrection or miraculous recovery is possible only through major rituals, Patron intervention, or narrative circumstances (GM discretion).

Encounters

All encounters — combat, social, environmental, magical — use the same core engine: Position, Effect, DV, Boons, and Story Beats (SB). Timers track progress toward resolution or the escalation of threats.

Common Framework

  1. State Intent & Approach. What do you want to achieve, and how?
  2. Set Position & DV. GM determines risk (Dominant/Controlled/Desperate) and difficulty (2–7+).
  3. Roll Attribute + Skill. Each 6+ = success; each 1 = SB for the GM.
  4. Apply the Outcome Matrix. Clean Success, Success with SB, Partial, Miss.
  5. Spend SB. GM introduces complications, shifts Position, or advances timers.

Timers in Encounters

Timers track cumulative progress or increasing pressure.

  • Progress Timer (4–8 segments): Fill to succeed (e.g., persuasion, escaping a maze).
  • Threat Timer (4–8 segments): Fill to trigger a disaster (e.g., reinforcements, structural collapse).
  • Contest Timers: Two timers — one for each side; first to fill wins.

Ticking Timers:

  • Partial: 1 tick (GM discretion).
  • Clean Success: 2 ticks.
  • Miss: may tick opposition timers.
  • GM may spend SB to tick a timer.

Social Encounters

Use Persuasion Timers for extended negotiations, debates, or intimidation.

Setting Up

  • Target's Resolve: Easy (4-segment), Moderate (6-segment), Hard (8-segment).
  • Initial Position: Based on relationship, leverage, and setting (Dominant = mutual trust, Desperate = hostile audience).
  • DV: Standard ladder (3–7+) with modifiers for cultural gaps, time pressure, etc.

Non-Face Characters Can Help

  • Setup: Create an advantage — produce evidence, cause a distraction, or perform a display of force. Roll an appropriate skill. On success, grant the primary speaker +1 Position or step up Effect, as for any Setup.
  • Assist: Spend 1 Boon to give +1 die (narrated as a timely interjection or meaningful glance).
  • Interrupt: On a Miss, a non-face character can take the fallout (e.g., absorb a social rebuke) by marking 1 Fatigue.

Persuasion Rolls

Each distinct argument or lever advances the timer:

  • Clean Success: +2 segments.
  • Partial: +1 segment; GM may spend SB for a complication (e.g., "They agree, but you will owe a favor").
  • Miss: No progress; GM may tick an Opposition Timer (4–6 segments).

When the Opposition Timer fills, the target hardens — Position worsens or the ask becomes impossible. When the Persuasion Timer fills, the target agrees as framed.

Environmental Encounters

Terrain and Positioning

  • Height Advantage: +1 die or +1 Effect for attacks from above.
  • Cover: Partial cover (+1 DV to target you); Full cover (cannot be targeted directly). You may Hide behind full cover (Stealth vs Perception).
  • Difficult Terrain: Costs 2 Move actions to traverse one band (or a full action to Dash). Charge/Skirmish require open ground unless the Relentless talent is used.

Hazards and Timers

Active hazards (fire, flooding, toxic gas) are represented by Threat Timers:

  • Tick the timer on missed rolls, GM SB spends, or at scene intervals.
  • When the timer fills, the hazard triggers (e.g., collapse, wave of fire).
  • Characters may attempt to slow or disable the hazard (e.g., Craft to seal a gas leak, Magic to freeze spreading flames). Treat as a Progress Timer.

Example: Collapsing Bridge [6] — ticks on each Partial or Miss. At 3 segments, footing becomes Desperate; at 6, the bridge falls.

Magical Encounters

Adjudicating Spells in Combat

  • Free Caster (Spellcraft): Weave then Cast (2 actions). Push (mark Fatigue) to compress to 1 action. Casting while Engaged worsens Position by one step unless the spell has [TOUCH] or [WARD].
  • Runekeeper (Rites): Invoke a Rite as 1 action; mark Obligation. Push It (+1 Obligation) for increased effect.
  • Invoker (Symbols): Ritual takes DV+1 rounds. Crack the Seal (instant) marks +2 Obligation (+3 for High Rites); Symbol becomes Compromised.
  • Cantor (Songs): Sing a Song (1 action, resolves at start of next turn). Push to resolve immediately (mark Fatigue + advance Corruption).

Traps and Wards

  • Discover: Wits + Insight vs DV 4–5.
  • Disable: Contested roll vs trap's tick. Clean Success = neutralized; Partial = disable but generate SB; Miss = trap activates.
  • Activation: GM spends SB to trigger trap (damage, condition, timer advance).
  • Wards: Magical barriers with a DV (3–6). Crossing without dispelling triggers the effect and ticks any relevant Leash by the ward's DV.

Summoning and Spirits

  • Leash Capacity: Cap + Spirit segments.
  • Ticks: Spirit takes Harm (1 per Harm level); acts against its nature (1); crosses a ward without dispelling (ticks = ward's DV); split focus (1).
  • Leash fills: Spirit acts once to its nature (potentially hostile) then departs.
  • Boon Finesse: Summoner may spend 1 Boon to clear 1 tick (once per round).

Extended Rituals and Downtime Encounters

Use a Progress Timer (4–12 segments). Each segment represents a scene or a block of time. Players describe their approach and roll an appropriate skill; success advances the timer. Complications (GM SB) may add segments, require rare components, or draw unwanted attention.

GM Reminders

  • Let non-specialists contribute — use Setup and Assist actions, and allow creative skill use.
  • Spend SB to escalate, not punish. A well-spent SB changes the situation, creating new choices.
  • Timers make progress visible. When a timer ticks, describe what changes in the fiction.
  • Follow the procedure for Armor & Fatigue: convert, apply remaining Harm (clears existing Fatigue), then add Fatigue. Minimum 1 Fatigue per hit.

Complications & Timers

Complications are the narrative, mechanical, and environmental consequences that arise from failure, pressure, or the GM spending Story Beats. Timers are the primary tool for tracking accumulating threats or progress.

Story Beats (SB)

Story Beats are the GM's currency for introducing complications. Each die result of 1 generates 1 SB. Re-rolling a 1 does not erase its SB; if the re-rolled die also shows 1, it generates additional SB.

SB Spend Menu

SB CostEffect
1Minor pressure: noise, trace, tick a 4–6 segment timer +1, worsen Position by one step.
2Moderate setback: alarm raised, lose cover/advantage, a lesser foe appears, split the party briefly.
3Serious trouble: reinforcements arrive, key gear breaks (temporarily), a major twist shifts the scene.
4+Scene-shaping turn: a trap springs, authority arrives, the environment changes dramatically, a Patron acts.

Limits on SB per Scene

  • Base SB budget: 4 + highest character Tier (e.g., Tier I = 5, Tier III = 7).
  • Maximum 12 SB for standard scenes, 16 SB for climactic scenes.
  • Unspent SB may carry over between scenes (GM discretion, max 4).

Boons (Player Resource)

Boons are the players' resource for mitigating complications or turning the tide. See §3, the Core Mechanic, for how they are earned and spent.

Timers

Timers track accumulating threats or progress. They are a visible way to manage pressure.

When to Use a Timer

  • Threat Timer (4–8 segments): Fills with enemy action, environmental decay, or social collapse. When full, a defined disaster occurs.
  • Progress Timer (4–8 segments): Fills with player successes to achieve a goal (e.g., escape, research, negotiation).
  • Contest Timers: Two timers race – first to fill wins.

Ticking Timers

Timers advance when:

  • The GM spends SB (e.g., 1 SB = tick a relevant timer +1).
  • A failed roll directly relates to the threat (GM discretion).
  • A scene transition or scripted event occurs.

Hazards

Hazards are ongoing complications that require action to avoid or mitigate.

Environmental Hazards

  • Active: Fire spreading, flooding, toxic gas – represented by a Threat Timer that ticks each round or on SB spends.
  • Passive: Difficult terrain, darkness, extreme heat/cold – impose Position penalties or DV modifiers.

Social Hazards

Magical Hazards

The Deck of Consequences is a narrative tool that turns Story Beats into thematic, unpredictable complications. It replaces or supplements GM fiat with randomized twists.

Deck Structure

Use a standard 52-card deck (jokers optional). Suits map to complication themes:

  2. Draw a number of cards equal to the SB spent (minimum 1, maximum 3).

NPC Templates

This section provides ready-to-use templates for non-player characters, from common guards to supernatural spirits. Each template includes a Threat Level (capability tier), Harm output, typical dice pools, and special abilities. GMs may adjust values up or down by one step to suit the fiction.

Rating an Adversary: Threat Level

Every adversary in Fate's Edge is rated on one axis: Threat Level (TL), an integer from 1 to 10. TL is the whole rating. It sets how hard the thing hits, roughly how big its pools are, and how much punishment it absorbs before the scene turns.

Three words that look like ratings are not ratings, and never appear on an adversary:

  • Tier means a player character's progression band by XP (Tier I Novice through Tier V Mythic). A creature does not have a Tier.
  • Cap means a Follower's Capability, 1–5, bought at Cap² XP. A creature does not have a Cap. A Follower who fights is rated by Cap because you paid for them; an adversary is rated by TL because you did not.
  • Class, where it appears in older material, is a taxonomy — Class I spirits, Class IV spirits — not a difficulty. It is a name for a kind of thing, not a number.

Resilience, not Hit Points

An adversary has no Hit Points. It has Resilience: the number of times it can be made to bleed, break, or bend before the fight is over. There are exactly four legal values, and every adversary has one of them:

  • 3 (standard) — TL 1–4. Three points of Resilience loss defeat it.
  • 8 (advanced) — TL 5–6. Named things, meant to last.
  • 8 per phase — TL 7–9. When a phase's Resilience is exhausted the phase ends and the next begins, with its own pool and its own moves.
  • None (puzzle) — the one override, and it is available at any TL. Use it when the fiction says the thing cannot be killed by hitting it: an Avatar, a demon that must be exorcised, an Echo that must be witnessed, a door that was never opened. It is resolved by a bargain, a true name, a witness, a debt paid — something other than attrition. A TL 2 spirit can be a puzzle; a TL 9 dragon can be a fight. If your adversary has no Resilience, it must have a stated Resolution.

Apart from that one override, Resilience follows from TL. If you find yourself wanting a TL 3 creature with eight Resilience, you do not want a tougher TL 3 — you want a TL 5, and its attributes and Harm should come up to meet it.

Note the two senses of the word and keep them apart on the page: Harm on a stat line is what the creature deals. Resilience is what it absorbs.

SB Moves

An adversary has no separate initiative turn. Threats frame player responses; new independent attacks and special moves cost SB as described in the resolution procedure. It costs Story Beats. Every stat block lists a short menu of things the GM can buy with the SB the players hand over by rolling 1s, and that menu is the creature's entire personality in mechanical form — write the moves that only this thing would make.

  • Two to five moves. A TL 7+ creature may run to seven, because a boss needs options; below that, five is already generous and three is usually better.
  • Cost 1 to 3. One is a jab, two turns the scene, three changes the situation.
  • One Signature move may cost 4 or 5, and only on a creature of TL 5 or higher. This is the thing it is remembered for. A creature gets one; there is no second Signature.

Move Tags

A move may be prefixed with a bracketed tag. Tags are a filing convenience, not a rule — nothing keys off them mechanically — but keeping to one vocabulary means a GM scanning a page finds the lever they want. There are two kinds, and they do not mix.

Function tags say what the move does, and are the ones worth being consistent about:

[TIMER] advances or starts a clock • [ENVIRONMENT] changes the ground everyone is standing on • [REINFORCEMENT] brings more • [AMBUSH] arrives from somewhere unaccounted for • [SOCIAL] works on people rather than bodies • [BARGAIN] offers something, which is always worse than it sounds • [HAUNT] presses on the mind • [POSSESSION] takes a body • [ILLUSION] makes them wrong about what is there • [MEMORY] takes or rewrites what happened • [CURSE] leaves something behind • [TRAP] punishes a choice already made • [TACTICS] repositions • [OMEN] tells the truth about what is coming.

Flavour tags name the element or medium and are free-form — [FIRE], [ICE], [STONE], [WIND], [TIDE], [BLOOD], [VENOM], [SHADOW], [DREAM], [SONG], [FEY], [PRESSURE]. Invent what you need.

Not to be confused with the block icons some supplements print beside an entry — [W] weakness, [C] timer, [L] lair, [P] phase, [D] drop. Those annotate the entry; these annotate a move.

Where a published creature gives no attributes, Harm or Fatigue, read them off its TL band and get on with the scene. Threat Level rates a creature. It does not rate an army, and trying to make it do both is how the war supplements ended up with three incompatible ladders. Scale is only size, and therefore only reach: how much ground a unit covers and how much of the field one order moves. It is never quality — a Scale IV levy is a large bad army, and the two facts are recorded separately because they matter separately.

ISquad or teamA skirmish. Individual characters still matter most.
IIPlatoon or companyA raid. The party commands and also fights.
IIIWing or battalionA battle. The party commands; fighting personally is a choice with a cost.
IVHost or armyA campaign. The party decides; someone else swings the swords.

Scale mismatch. When formations of different Scale meet, the difference changes Effect, never the DV and never the dice:

  • One step larger: +1 Effect on anything that covers ground — volleys, charges, encirclement.
  • One step smaller: −1 Effect, unless the action is precise rather than broad. A squad cannot rout a host, but it can absolutely kill the host's commander.
  • Two or more steps apart: the smaller formation cannot engage the larger head-on at all. It can raid, scout, assassinate, sabotage or delay — and those are the interesting options anyway.

Units still use timers, not hit points. A formation tracks Supply and Morale as ordinary named clocks of 4, 6, 8 or 10 segments — Supply starts full and empties through use, Morale starts empty and fills through loss, and a unit whose Morale fills routs. Objectives are clocks too. Nothing about warfare needs a second resolution engine; it needs bigger clocks and someone willing to spend them.

Where this is used. The Art of War and Black Banners are the two supplements that field armies, and both now read Cap and Scale from here rather than defining their own. The Forge of Souls runs its whole campaign layer on the same two axes plus GM Guide Appendix B. If you are rating a single creature, however large, you want Threat Level above, not Scale — a dragon is TL 9, not an army of one.

Building Custom NPCs

Use these guidelines to create your own NPCs quickly.

Step 1: Choose Threat Level

Creation-Only Talents

A few talents represent something a character was rather than something they learned. They can only be taken at creation, and they cannot be taken later at any price.

Prodigy 8 XP Creation only

“Some are born with a gift others spend years cultivating. You are one of them — and you have already made a choice you cannot unmake.”
  • Effect: One attribute begins at 4, above the normal starting maximum of 3.
  • The gift does not grow. That attribute cannot be raised again until you reach Tier II. Everyone else catches up before you improve.
  • It cannot be given back. Prodigy is the one talent that can never be retrained. Every other talent may be replaced once per downtime; this one is who you are.
  • The world notices. Until you reach Tier II, scenes start with +1 banked SB for the GM, exactly as an unresolved Complication does. Being conspicuously good, conspicuously early, attracts attention you did not ask for.

Why it costs what it costs. Raising an attribute from 3 to 4 is 12 XP by the build table, and is not permitted at creation at any price. Prodigy sells that for 8 — a quarter of a 32 XP build spent on a single number — and takes back roughly a Complication's worth of trouble for as long as the gift lasts. The result is a character who is genuinely better at one thing than anyone should be that early, and correspondingly thinner everywhere else. That is the whole idea; a Prodigy who is also well-rounded has not bought this talent, they have been given a bigger budget.

GM guidance. Offer this to a player who wants to play a specialist and understands they are trading breadth for it, not to a player looking for a larger character. The +1 SB per scene is not a punishment — it is the story the talent is for. Spend it on people noticing: a recruiter, a rival, a master who wants to see for themselves, an enemy who decides you are the dangerous one.

Magic System

Magic in Fate's Edge is expressed through six interconnected paths. Each uses the same core resolution engine (Position, DV, Boons, SB) but manages risk differently.

  • Caster (Freeform): Flexible, improvisational magic. Risk of Backlash.
  • Runekeeper (Rites): Structured magic through a Patron's Codex. Risk of Obligation.
  • Invoker (Symbols): Ritual magic through consecrated Symbols. Slow but safe; can be forced.
  • Cantor (Songs): Perform Low Rites as songs. Risk of Corruption.
  • Witchcraft (Hedge Magic): Subtle, low-cost magic through thresholds and knots. No resource tracking.
  • Summoning: Bind and command spirits. Risk of the Leash.

Caster (Freeform / Weave & Cast)

Free casters shape raw magic by combining elements and effects. They are flexible but unpredictable.

Requirements

  • Spellcraft (6 XP): Required for Weave & Cast freeform magic.

Procedure

  1. Weave (Action): Describe the spell and choose its tags.
  2. Cast (Action): Roll Attribute (usually Wits or Spirit) + Arcana. The GM sets DV based on the tags.

Cantrip: A spell with a single tag can be cast as a Cantrip: one roll, standard action, no Weave phase. Cannot bypass wards or create large-scale effects.

Spell DV

DV = 1 + number of tags

Dangerous tags ([FOLD], [GATE], [LEAP], [CREATE], [SUMMON], [TRANSMUTE], [ANIMATE], [TRANSFORM], [DOMINATE], [REWIND]) add +2 DV each instead of +1.

Backlash

On a Partial or Miss, or on a Hit that shows two or more 1s, the GM applies backlash colored by the dominant element(s). See Backlash (Free Casting) for details.

Runekeeper (Rites)

Runekeepers bind structured magic through a Codex and a Thiasos (familiar spirit). They specialize in reliable, thematic magic.

Requirements

  • Bond to a single Patron.
  • Thiasos (Familiar talent, 2 XP): Enables Patron's Gift.
  • Codex (4 XP): Grants access to the Patron's Rites and starts the Obligation ledger.

Invoking a Rite

  1. Declare the Rite (1 action).
  2. Mark the Rite's Obligation cost (listed in the rite description, e.g., Obligation: 6 segments).
  3. If the Rite calls for a roll, roll Attribute + Ritual. The GM sets DV using the formula:

    DV = max(Obligation Cost − Spirit, Rite Tier)

    where Rite Tier is 1 for Low, 2 for Standard, 3 for High/Epic.

Push It: Mark +1 additional Obligation to increase duration, range, or potency.

Patron's Gift (Imbuement)

Requires Thiasos. Once per scene as 1 action, imbue a held item with +1 Melee and +1 Thematic Skill (determined by your Patron) for the scene. Push It to extend one additional scene (+1 Obligation).

Invoker (Symbols)

Invokers channel power through consecrated Symbols. Rituals are slow but safe; Crack the Seal offers desperate speed.

Requirements

  • One or more Patron's Symbols (4 XP each). Each Symbol is a Minor Asset.

Ritual Invocation

  1. Display the Symbol (must be visible).
  2. Perform the Rite. Takes DV + 1 rounds (Significant Time), where DV is calculated as above.
  3. Mark the Rite's Obligation cost (listed).
  4. If the Symbol is displayed openly during the ritual, reduce the Obligation cost by 1 (minimum 0).

Crack the Seal (Instant Cast)

  1. Invoke the Rite as 1 action (instead of ritual time).
  2. Mark double the Rite's Obligation cost (or +2 minimum, +3 for High Rites if the base cost is unclear).
  3. The Symbol becomes Compromised (inert). Restore it during downtime (Arcana or Lore test, DV 3) or pay 1 XP.

Borrowed Grace (Invoker Talent)

Once per scene, spend 1 Boon or 1 Fatigue (1 action) to gain +1 die on a combat action, or +1 Effect on a social or stealth action. Requires visibly displaying your Patron's Symbol. If the Symbol is Compromised, Borrowed Grace works at –1 die.

Cantor (Songs)

Cantors perform Low Rites as Songs, channeling power through voice and performance. They track Corruption instead of Obligation.

Requirements

  • Cantor's Path (8 XP).
  • Performance 2+, Lore 1+, Presence 2+.

Singing a Song

  1. Begin the Song (1 action). It resolves at the start of your next turn.
  2. Roll Lore + Performance vs DV (typically 2–3).
  3. On success, the Low Rite effect occurs. No Obligation cost.
  4. Mark Corruption accumulation (see below).

Push It

Resolve the Song immediately (instead of next turn). Mark 1 Fatigue and advance your Corruption Timer by 1 segment.

Corruption Timer

Your Corruption Timer has segments equal to your Spirit attribute. Mark a segment when you:

  • Push a Song.
  • Perform a Resonant Rite (a powerful thematic working designated by your Patron).
  • The GM spends a Story Beat on your occult activities.

When the timer fills, you immediately gain a thematic boon and burden from the last Patron whose Rite you performed. Then reset the timer to zero (cannot go below your current Tier − 1).

Witchcraft (Hedge Magic)

Witchcraft is the magic of thresholds, knots, and small favors. It is subtle, low-cost, and accessible to any character who pays attention to the world's edges. Unlike other magical paths, Witchcraft requires no Patron, no Codex, and no resource track—just a talent and a willingness to work at the boundaries.

The Two Speeds of Witchcraft

Witchcraft operates at two speeds:

  • Hedge Gifts: No-roll, no-cost magical effects that work within a limited scope. These are the "short road" of Witchcraft.
  • Quick Workings: Single-action rolls that produce subtle, scene-limited effects. These are the "short road" with a bit more power.
  • Full Rituals: Extended workings that require Significant Time, a threshold, and often a witness. These are the "long road" of Witchcraft.

Requirements

  • Craft of the Hedge (4 XP): Required for Hedge Gifts and Quick Workings.
  • Optional: A witch mark (tattoo, scar, or birthmark) for flavor.

Hedge Gifts

Hedge Gifts are simple, reliable effects that require no roll. A character with Craft of the Hedge learns two Hedge Gifts of their choice. Additional Hedge Gifts cost 2 XP each.

Quick Workings

A Quick Working is a single-action roll that relies on the witch's knowledge of thresholds, elements, and will. Its effects are limited, subtle, and always carry the risk of consequence.

Procedure:

  1. Name the Threshold: Door, tide line, wound, vow, breath.
  2. Choose a Layer: Echo (what has been), Veil (what is), or Flow (what wishes to be).
  3. Choose a Single Tag: From the magic tags list.
  4. Set Position: Controlled (if you have time) or Desperate (if threatened).
  5. Roll: Wits+Lore, Spirit+Insight, or Presence+Sway (GM's choice).
  6. Apply Outcome: Use the standard Outcome Matrix.

Example Effects:

  • A door sticks shut or opens.
  • A candle gutters (brief darkness).
  • A rope knot tightens or loosens.
  • A pursuer's footing slips (1 Fatigue).
  • A whispered name is heard or forgotten.

Full Rituals

A Full Ritual requires Significant Time, a proper threshold, and often a witness. Its effects are lasting or permanent: blessings, curses, bindings, or the hallowing of ground.

General Procedure:

  1. Identify the Threshold: Name the edge, learn its disposition.
  2. Choose Your Witness: A person, spirit, or Hollowed who watches.
  3. Name Your Will: What change you intend.
  4. Set Your Price: Memory, name, lock of hair, promise, blood.
  5. Make the Exchange: Roll (Spirit+Lore, Spirit+Insight, or appropriate), resolve, pay the price on success or partial.

Example Rituals:

Unravel the Wound (Heal Harm)

Requires: Significant Time, a threshold (doorway or basin of water), a witness.

Effect: Restores 1 level of Harm.

Price: A lock of hair, a memory of a childhood injury, a tear in a vial.

Roll: Spirit + Lore vs DV (3 for Harm 1, 4 for Harm 2, 5+ for Harm 3 requires a coven).

Success: Harm reduced; witch pays the price. Partial: Harm reduced, but witch gains a minor Condition or a Promise Timer. Miss: Wound does not heal; witch gains the Condition she tried to remove.

Hallow Threshold (Permanent Ward)

Requires: Significant Time, a permanent threshold (door, well, bridge, hearthstone), a witness who will maintain the consecration, an offering (bread, salt, coin, spoken promise).

Effect: The blessing lasts until the threshold is physically broken or the offering removed.

Roll: Spirit + Lore vs DV 4 (home), 5 (village well), or 6 (city gate).

Success: Threshold hallowed. Spirits, undead, and cursed creatures crossing must test Spirit+Endurance (DV 5) or be turned back. Partial: Blessing holds but has a weakness (the witch names one thing that can bypass it). Miss: Blessing fails; threshold becomes cursed for one season (all who cross feel unease, –1 die to first action).

Promise Timers

Many workings create obligations. Represent these with a Promise Timer (typically 4 segments):

  • Mark the timer when the promise is ignored, deferred, or disrespected.
  • When filled, the price comes due—socially, spiritually, or narratively.
  • Examples: sharing grief, tending a grave, protecting a hearth, reconciling rivals.

Talents of the Hedge

  • Craft of the Hedge (4 XP): Requires Lore 1+ or Insight 1+. You learn two Hedge Gifts; you may perform Quick Workings; once per session, when you fail a Quick Working roll, you may convert the Miss into a Partial Success by marking 1 Fatigue.
  • Hedge Gift of the Patron (6 XP): Requires Craft of the Hedge and a Bond to a Patron. Once per session, when you would use a Hedge Gift, you may instead invoke your Patron's Imbuement. Gain +1 die to all actions tied to your Patron's domain for the scene, but mark +1 Obligation immediately. No action required.

Obligation (Runekeepers and Invokers)

Obligation measures a Patron's claim on a character. Track it per Patron. The default Obligation capacity is:

Capacity = Spirit + Presence

Marking Obligation

Obligation is marked according to the specific Rite or action:

  • Invoking a Rite (Runekeeper or Invoker): mark the Rite's listed Obligation cost (e.g., Obligation: 6).
  • Pushing a Rite: mark +1 additional Obligation (added to the Rite's base cost).
  • Crack the Seal: mark double the Rite's base cost (or +2 minimum, +3 for High Rites).
  • Using Rites from two different Patrons in the same scene: mark +1 Obligation to the second Patron.

Overflow

If your total Obligation (across all Patrons) exceeds your Capacity:

  • Mark 1 Fatigue per segment over Capacity.
  • You cannot invoke new Rites or perform Patron rituals until Obligation is reduced below Capacity.

Clearing Obligation

  • Off-screen: Perform an Act of Service aligned with the Patron (provide an Intricate Description). Clear 1 segment of that Patron's Obligation.
  • On-screen (Focused Devotion talent, 3–5 XP): Once per scene, spend 1 Boon to clear 1 segment (cannot clear segments accrued in the same scene).

Backlash (Free Casting)

Backlash is the unpredictable fallout from shaping raw magic. It occurs on a Partial or Miss, or on a Hit that shows two or more 1s.

Backlash Menu (choose one on Partial, two on Miss)

  • Position Shift: Your Position worsens by one step for your current or next action.
  • Fleeting Harm/Condition: Minor injury (1 Fatigue or Harm 1) or a temporary Condition (Dazed, Singed, Disoriented).
  • Exposure/Noise: Draws unwanted attention — guards alerted, a door slams, a rival notices.
  • Resource Drain: Lose a component, a tool wears out, or mark 1 Fatigue.
  • Collateral Spark: A nearby ally, object, or environmental feature is threatened (GM describes).

All backlash is colored by the dominant element(s) used in the spell. See the Elemental Backlash table referenced elsewhere in the tags & effects rules.

Obligation Capacity and Overflow Summary

Capacity = Spirit + Presence

  • Exceeding Capacity: Mark 1 Fatigue per segment over. Cannot invoke new Rites or perform Patron rituals until reduced.
  • Clearing: Act of Service (off-screen, 1 segment) or Focused Devotion talent (on-screen, 1 Boon per segment).

Corruption Timer Summary (Cantor)

Timer Size = Spirit

  • Mark a segment when you Push a Song, perform a Resonant Rite, or when the GM spends SB on your occult activities.
  • When full: gain a thematic boon and burden from your last Patron; reset timer to zero.

Tags & Effects Library

Tags are keywords that define the mechanical and narrative behavior of spells, rites, talents, and assets. They do nothing on their own; they only function when granted by a rule (Spell, Rite, Talent, Ability). Unless noted otherwise, the Duration of a tag effect is Scene, and the DV for contests or resistances is set by the fiction (typical range 2–5).

The Eight Elements

Every spell or magical effect is built from one or more of the eight fundamental elements. The first four are physical; the second four are metaphysical.

The Four and the Four

The two halves rhyme. Fire and Life both consume a fuel to make something; Air and Fortune both refuse to be aimed; Water and Obishaal both close over a thing and give nothing back on demand; Earth and Fate are both weight, and what weight does to whatever is near it.

That is a way of remembering the list and a source of imagery for backlash. It is not a mechanic, and nothing in the game asks you to work out which half a given effect came from.

The Elemental Planes

Every element has a plane. Two of the eight have been described:

PlaneKnown asWritten up in
Obishaal (Death/Dreams)The Ways Between — dream-roads and spirit-paths, where travel is measured in choices rather than miles: a wrong turn leads to yesterday, a right one to the version of you who decided differently.Player's Guide ch. 14, GM Guide ch. 8, and the Psionics chapter.
FireThe Realm of Fire, holding the City of Cinder — an infernal bazaar where the currency is concepts rather than coin.Ashes of the Infernal Accord, which is set there.

The other six exist and are unwritten. Build them when a story needs them.

The metaphysics are deliberately forgiving. This game is for sharing stories, not for adjudicating whether the Realm of Fire is exothermic. There is no cosmological chart to be consistent with, no canonical count of layers, no correct answer about how the planes touch. If your table wants the plane of Fortune to be a card game that has been going on for nine thousand years, it is, and nothing in this book will contradict you.

What the two described planes have in common is worth borrowing, not because it is a law but because it plays well: neither is reached by distance. The Ways Between charge you choices; the Realm of Fire charges you concessions. Arriving somewhere by paying for it is a scene. Arriving by walking a long way is a travel roll.

One thing to get right. Unwritten is not the same as nonexistent. There are eight planes; six are simply waiting for somebody's story. A supplement should leave room for them rather than write past them.

Effect Tags

Effect tags describe what the magic does. They are combined with element tags to form spells.

Common Effect Tags:

[AREA] [MARK] [BANISH] [MEND] [BARRIER] [OATH] [BIND] [PASSAGE] [CLEANSE] [REPAIR] [COMMAND] [REVEAL] [CONJURE] [SANCTIFY] [COUNTER] [SEAL] / [UNSEAL] [CURSE] [DEFEND] [STRIKE] [DISPEL] [TRANSPORT] [FORTIFY] [VEIL] [HEAL] [WARD]

Building a Spell

A spell is a combination of one or more element tags and one or more effect tags. The base Difficulty Value is:

DV = 1 + total number of tags (element + effect)

Dangerous Tags

Some effects are so powerful that each dangerous tag adds +2 DV instead of +1. Dangerous tags include: [GATE], [FOLD], [LEAP], [CREATE], [SUMMON], [TRANSMUTE], [ANIMATE], [TRANSFORM], [DOMINATE], [REWIND].

Stacking Identical Effect Tags

You may include the same effect tag multiple times to increase its potency. Each additional copy adds its normal DV cost. Example:

  • [AIR][DEFEND] – 2 tags → DV 3. Grants +1 die to one defense roll.
  • [AIR][DEFEND][DEFEND] – 3 tags → DV 4. Grants +2 dice to one defense roll.

Stacking is limited by the caster's Tier (max 3 copies per effect at Tier III, 4 at Tier V).

Repair & Restoration

Two tags handle mending objects: [MEND] for quick, temporary fixes in the field, and [REPAIR] for thorough, lasting restoration.

[MEND] (Quick Fix)

Quick Fix

Restore a damaged object to functioning order for the duration of the scene.

  • DV: by fiction (severity of damage, size, material). Typical DV 2–4.
  • Hit: Remove one level of Neglected or Compromised from a single item for the rest of the scene. The item works normally.
  • Partial: The item functions for the scene but becomes Neglected (or worsens one step) when the scene ends.
  • Miss: No repair; the item may become Compromised or break (GM discretion).
  • Duration: Scene. After the scene, the damage returns (or worsens) unless followed by a proper [REPAIR] during downtime.

Example: A snapped bowstring is magically re-knotted for the remainder of the fight; the next day it frays again.

[REPAIR] (Permanent Fix)

Permanent Fix

Fully restore a damaged object, tool, or piece of equipment.

  • DV: by fiction (severity, material, magical nature). Typical DV 3–5. Magical items add +1 DV.
  • Time: One downtime action (a few hours of focused work). Can be used during a scene only if the caster has Significant Time and appropriate tools.
  • Hit: Remove all levels of Neglected and Compromised from a single item. The item is restored to full function.
  • Partial: Remove one level of damage (e.g., Compromised → Neglected), or the item becomes Neglected but usable.
  • Miss: No repair; the item may become Compromised or gain a new flaw (GM discretion).

Example: A shattered shield is reforged overnight; a rusted lock is cleaned and oiled to working order.

Influence Over Mortals

These tags affect the minds and loyalties of living beings. Using them to alter free will carries narrative and ethical consequences.

[BOND]

Beneficial

Strengthen or create a positive emotional tie between a target and a person, place, or ideal.

  • DV: by fiction (target's current loyalty, resistance to magic). Typical DV 3–5.
  • Hit: Increase a follower's Loyalty state by one step (e.g., Strained → Faithful). The target genuinely feels the bond.
  • Partial: The bond forms but is fragile; any subsequent external pressure may break it (GM may downgrade loyalty on a future setback).
  • Miss: No effect; the target may become suspicious or resistant to further magical influence.
  • Ethical note: Forcing loyalty removes agency. The GM may rule that such magic is taboo, attracts Patron attention, or creates hidden complications (e.g., the follower secretly resents the caster).

Example: A Runekeeper of Inaea weaves a [BOND] rite to mend a hireling's shaken trust after a failed mission.

[SUBVERT]

Dangerous (+2 DV)

Coerce obedience or twist existing loyalties.

  • DV: by fiction (target's will, opposing magic). Typical DV 5–7.
  • Hit: The target follows one simple command or shifts loyalty one step in the caster's favor (e.g., Faithful → Strained toward their original master). The effect lasts one scene or until the command is fulfilled.
  • Partial: The target hesitates, reveals information, or acts with clear reluctance; the compulsion is visible to observers.
  • Miss: The target resists; the caster marks 1 Fatigue and the target's original loyalty increases by one step (they become more devoted to their original cause).
  • Backlash: On any roll showing two or more 1s, the GM may impose a Patron's Disfavor or a Scandal timer (the act becomes known).

Example: A desperate Invoker of Maelstraeus uses [SUBVERT] to force a gate guard to look away. The guard later testifies under oath, and a bounty appears.

Control & Countermagic

[DISPEL]

Countermagic

End an ongoing magical effect or construct.

  • DV: by fiction (usually equal to the effect's Tier or casting DV).
  • Hit: Effect ends or is suppressed per source text.
  • Partial: Effect suppressed briefly (one exchange) or reduced in scope.
  • Miss: No effect; GM may gain SB.

[COUNTER]

Countermagic

Interrupt a spell, rite, or ritual in progress.

  • Timing: During the casting window (declared action or ritual phase).
  • DV: by fiction (complexity of the interrupted effect).
  • Hit: Cancel the action; resources used are lost.
  • Partial: Worsen the caster's Position or impose Fatigue.
  • Miss: No interruption; GM may spend SB to escalate.

[BARRIER]

Control

Create cover, obstruction, or a physical wall.

  • DV: by fiction (size, material, duration).
  • Hit: Place a barrier with Integrity Timer [2/4/6]; blocks movement/attacks until cracked.
  • Partial: Barrier is fragile or has a gap.
  • Countered by: [DISPEL], brute force (Athletics), or clever route.

[SEAL] / [UNSEAL]

Control

Magically lock or unlock a container, door, or portal (not spirits).

  • DV: by fiction (quality of lock, magical binding).
  • Hit: Locked/unlocked as intended; specify any bypass conditions.
  • Partial: Seal is temporary or leaks (can be forced open with +1 DV).

Concealment & Revelation

[VEIL]

Concealment

Obscure a person, object, or zone.

  • DV: by fiction (scale, conditions).
  • Hit: Impose –1 die to Notice or perception rolls against the veiled subject; specify limits (angle, distance, scent).
  • Partial: Works only at a limited range or in dim light.
  • Countered by: [REVEAL], strong light, physical proof.

[REVEAL]

Revelation

Expose illusions, disguises, or hidden clauses.

  • DV: by fiction (complexity of the concealment).
  • Hit: Surface the truth; describe how the revelation manifests (brief glow, visible flaw, etc.).
  • Partial: A tell or clue, not the full picture.

[MARK]

Tracking

Tag a target for tracking or leverage.

  • DV: by fiction (resistance, visibility).
  • Hit: Place a visible or invisible mark; once per scene, you or an ally gain +1 die when acting directly against the Marked target.
  • Partial: Mark is noisy or short-lived.
  • Countered by: [CLEANSE], [DISPEL], or listed materials (salt, iron).

Boons & Burdens

[CURSE]

Hindrance

Inflict a sticky hindrance with a clear release condition.

  • DV: by fiction (severity, target's resilience).
  • Hit: Apply a named Condition (e.g., Shaken, Weakened) that persists until the release condition is met.
  • Partial: Condition is milder or intermittent.
  • Countered by: [CLEANSE] or the listed key (apology, theft return, pilgrimage).

[CLEANSE]

Beneficial

Remove or suppress a Condition (poison, disease, curse, fear).

  • DV: by fiction (potency of the affliction).
  • Hit: Remove one named Condition.
  • Partial: Suppress or reduce severity for the scene.

[FORTIFY]

Beneficial

Harden a person, place, or object against a specific vector (fire, blades, fear, persuasion).

  • DV: by fiction (scale, strength).
  • Hit: Raise Position by one step or reduce consequence severity for attacks of that vector this scene.
  • Partial: Limited scope (one ally, one doorway).

Influence & Oaths

[COMMAND]

Influence

Issue a clear order to a sapient target.

  • DV: by fiction (authority, leverage, fear, ritual standing).
  • Hit: Target complies now or suffers an immediate cost stated by the GM.
  • Partial: Target hesitates, bargains, or partially complies.
  • Miss: Refusal or blowback (GM may spend SB).

[OATH]

Binding

Bind parties to terms; breaking has teeth.

  • DV: by fiction (witnesses, sanctity, stakes).
  • Hit: Enforceable pact with stated boon for keeping and consequence for breaking.
  • Partial: A loophole exists in the oath.
  • Countered by: Release clause, [CLEANSE] if text allows.

[SANCTIFY]

Consecration

Consecrate a zone to a code or Patron.

  • DV: by fiction (size, opposition).
  • Hit: Specify allowed/prohibited acts; violations start at Controlled Position or impose a soft consequence.
  • Partial: Patchy coverage (e.g., only the altar, not the whole room).
  • Countered by: Opposing rite, profanation (bloodshed, lies), time.

Movement & Making

[PASSAGE]

Movement

Declare a route as permitted or easy.

  • DV: by fiction (length, danger).
  • Hit: Allies on that path gain improved flow (+1 Position or ignore one level of difficult terrain).
  • Partial: Only one ally or one segment benefits.

[TRANSPORT]

Movement

Move a target across an obstacle (blink, lift, pull).

  • DV: by fiction (mass, range, warding).
  • Hit: Relocate within listed range; state any tells or costs (SB, Fatigue).
  • Partial: Arrive off-balance, drop gear, or suffer worse Position.

[CONJURE]

Making

Create a useful object, cover, or hazard.

  • DV: by fiction (size, complexity).
  • Hit: Conjure an item or zone with Integrity Timer [2/4/6] (if destructible) or a ticking hazard (burn, freeze, haze).
  • Partial: Fragile or short-lived (lasts one exchange).
  • Countered by: [DISPEL], force, time.

Knowledge, Movement & Transformation

[TRUTH]

Knowledge

Ask the GM one yes/no or short-answer question about the present situation. The GM answers truthfully.

  • Hit: Full, clear answer.
  • Partial: Cryptic or incomplete answer (still true).
  • Miss: No answer; GM gains 1 SB.

[SECRET]

Knowledge

Reveal a deeply hidden fact about a person, place, or object – something the target actively conceals.

  • Contest: Spirit+Lore vs. target's Spirit+Endurance (DV 4 for passive secrets).
  • Hit: GM reveals the secret.
  • Partial: A clue or hint toward the secret.
  • Miss: No information; the secret becomes harder to find (+1 DV next time).

[READ]

Knowledge

Sense the target's current dominant emotion or a single surface thought. No roll required; narrative permission only. Cannot read deep secrets or memories.

[OMEN]

Knowledge

Glimpse a likely future tied to a specific action or decision.

  • Hit: GM gives a cryptic warning (one sentence). You may reroll one die later this session if it relates to the omen.
  • Partial: Omen is vague; no reroll.
  • Miss: False omen; GM may spend 1 SB to make you act on misinformation.

[LIBERATE]

Beneficial

Automatically succeed on one attempt to escape mundane physical restraints (ropes, manacles, grapple). Magical restraints require a Spirit+Endurance test (DV 4) instead.

[BOOST]

Beneficial

Gain +1 die on a single related roll, or clear 1 Fatigue. This tag does nothing on its own; the spell description must state which benefit applies.

[TRANSFORM]

Dangerous

Take on a new form for the scene. Choose one benefit:

  • +1 Armor
  • +1 die to a specific skill (e.g., Stealth, Athletics)
  • A minor supernatural ability (e.g., flight for one exchange, water breathing, claws that deal +1 Harm)

Partial: You gain the benefit but mark 1 Fatigue when the scene ends. Miss: No transformation; you mark 1 Fatigue.

[ERASURE] (Multi-Mode)

Dangerous

Choose one of the following when you cast:

  • Annihilate: Destroy a single mundane object (size chest). Magical objects resist with a [DISPEL] test (DV 4).
  • Oblivion: Erase a specific memory from a living target. Contest Spirit+Endurance (DV 4). On a Miss, you forget instead.
  • Nullify: Void a formal debt, oath, or contract. Requires the original document or a witnessed recitation.

[PRESSURE]

Battlefield Control

All enemies within the affected zone suffer –1 die to physical actions (attacks, Athletics, etc.) for the duration.

[DREAM]

Knowledge

Enter a trance or dream state. Ask the GM one question about a past event or a possible future; the answer is always cryptic (one sentence). On a Hit, you may also ignore the first Fatigue from sleep deprivation this scene.

[TIME]

Situational Danger

Alter the flow of time in a small zone (Near range). Choose one:

  • Slow: Enemies act at Desperate Position for one exchange.
  • Haste: Allies gain +1 band of movement this turn.

[THRESHOLD]

Boundary

Interact with a boundary – door, gate, portal, or conceptual threshold (e.g., between life and death).

[BURDEN]

Self-Sacrifice

Stacking & Duration Rules (Global)

  • No Same-Source Stacking: Multiple scene-long effects from the same source do not stack; take the best instance.
  • Identical Tag Collision: If two abilities would grant the same Tag in the same window, use the stronger; the weaker is suppressed while it lasts.
  • Default Duration: Scene, unless a source states otherwise (e.g., "until the end of your next turn").
  • DV by Fiction: Potency, preparation, place, materials, and opposition set DV (typical 2–4; 5+ for set-pieces).

Rites and Invokers

This chapter covers universal rituals (available to any character with time and components) and the Invoker path (characters who channel Patrons through consecrated Symbols).

Universal Rituals

Any character, regardless of magical talent, may attempt a universal ritual. Requirements:

  • A clear intent (what you want to achieve).
  • A method (e.g., summoning circle, prayer, sympathetic object).
  • Components (consumed or used up).
  • Time (at least one scene of uninterrupted work).

Ritual Resolution

The GM sets a DV (typically 4–6) based on scope. The player rolls Spirit + Lore (or another appropriate skill).

  • Clean Success: The ritual works as intended.
  • Success with SB: Works, but with a cost (backlash, extra time, compromised component). GM spends SB.
  • Partial: Partial effect; player gains 1 Boon.
  • Miss: Fails; GM may impose a complication (wrong spirit answers, ward backfires). Player gains 2 Boons.

Invokers (Symbol Path)

Invokers channel power through consecrated Symbols — physical tokens tied to a specific Patron.

Requirements

  • Patron’s Symbol (4 XP each): Minor Asset. Grants ritual access to that Patron’s Rites.
  • One Symbol per Patron; Invokers may carry Symbols of multiple Patrons.

Ritual Invocation

  • Takes DV + 1 rounds to complete.
  • Display the Symbol openly (visible).
  • Always marks +1 Obligation to that Patron.
  • If the Symbol is displayed, DV –1 and Obligation –1 (minimum 0 Obligation).

Crack the Seal (Instant Cast)

Resolve the Rite immediately (1 action) by:

  • Marking +2 Obligation (+3 for High Rites).
  • Setting the Symbol to Compromised (inert until restored).
  • The GM may immediately spend 1 SB on a thematic complication.

Restoring a Symbol

  • Requires a Downtime action.
  • Roll Wits + Arcana or Lore (DV 3).
  • Clean Success: Symbol returns to Maintained.
  • Partial: Symbol becomes Neglected (future rituals +1 Obligation).
  • Miss: Symbol remains Compromised; requires a quest or Patron service to restore.

Multiple Symbols

  • Carrying 4 or more different Symbols causes the first Invoker ritual each scene to mark +1 additional Obligation.

Summoning

Summoning is the art of calling beings from beyond the mortal veil — spirits, elementals, the dead, fey, demons, or stranger entities. It is always dangerous, often costly, and never without consequence.

Who Can Summon?

Any character may attempt a summoning ritual, but only those with the Pact-Whisperer talent can reliably bind and command spirits in combat time. NPCs and plot events use the same principles: a summoner prepares a circle, pays a cost, and risks backlash.

Summoning for Narrative (Greater Powers)

Not all summonings are tactical. Sometimes the story demands calling a greater power — a patron-level entity, a forgotten god, or a primordial force. These rituals are not resolved with a single roll; they are extended challenges that drive the plot.

Ritual Structure for Greater Summonings

  • Preparation Timer [6–12]: The summoner gathers rare components, secures a threshold, and learns the entity’s true name (or equivalent).
  • The Calling: A dramatic scene where the summoner rolls Spirit + Lore vs. DV (set by the entity’s power). Failure may summon something else.
  • The Bargain: Greater powers do not serve for free. They demand a price — a memory, a firstborn, a deed, or an ongoing Obligation (tracked as a campaign front).
  • Consequences: On success, the entity manifests (briefly) and may grant a boon, answer a question, or perform a single impossible task. On failure, the entity corrupts, attacks, or curses the summoner.

Example: Summoning the Pale Shepherd’s Herald. A village elder attempts to summon a death-herald to save a dying child. Preparation timer [8]: gather grave-ash, a silver bell, and the child’s true name. Calling roll: Spirit 2 + Lore 3 = 5 dice vs DV 5 → Partial. The herald appears but demands a year of the elder’s remaining life. The elder accepts; the child lives.

PC Summoner (Pact-Whisperer)

Player characters who specialize in summoning take the Pact-Whisperer talent (2 XP). This allows them to call and bind spirits for temporary aid in scenes.

Summoning Talents (Summary)

The essential summoning talents are:

  • Pact-Whisperer (2 XP): Base access to summoning.
  • Lesser Pactwright (2 XP): Call spirits of TL 1.
  • Greater Pactwright (4 XP): Call spirits of TL 3.
  • Spirit Link (10 XP): Spirits act on your turn; all commands are free actions; reduce Leash ticking for natural behaviors by 1.

Core Mechanics – Call, Bind, and the Leash

Summoning a spirit follows three steps: Call, Bind, and Leash management.

Call (1 Action)

The spirit manifests at Near range. Choose a spirit template (see sample templates below).

Bind (1 Boon or 1 Fatigue)

Spend 1 Boon or mark 1 Fatigue to establish control. The spirit is now bound and will follow simple commands.

Leash Capacity

The spirit has a Leash — a track of segments equal to your Spirit + the spirit's TL. TL is 1 for a Lesser spirit, 3 for a Greater. The Leash represents the strain of keeping the spirit in the mortal world.

Ticking the Leash

The Leash ticks forward (gains segments) under the following conditions:

  • The spirit takes Harm (1 segment per level of Harm).
  • You command the spirit to act against its nature (1 segment).
  • You split focus — take another significant action while the spirit is acting (1 segment).
  • The spirit crosses a [WARD] without dispelling it (DV = its TL; tick segments equal to the ward's DV).
  • (Optional narrative trigger) A rival contests control, or the spirit moves too far from you.

When the Leash Fills

When the Leash reaches its capacity, the spirit acts once according to its nature (the GM decides the action, which is often destructive or self-serving), then departs immediately (or turns hostile at the GM’s discretion). The bond is broken.

Boon Finesse

Once per round, you may spend 1 Boon to clear 1 tick from the current spirit’s Leash (cannot be used after the Leash has already filled). This represents appeasing the spirit, reaffirming control, or offering a small token.

Commanding Spirits

  • Quick Command: Simple orders (“attack that enemy”, “move there”, “defend me”, “retrieve that object”) do not consume your Action. You may issue one Quick Command per round as a free action.
  • Complex Command: If the order requires interpretation, multiple steps, or finesse (“guard this door and alert me if anyone passes”, “search the room for a hidden key”), it uses your Action for the turn.
  • Spirit Link (Major Talent): Spirits act on your turn; all commands are free actions; reduce Leash ticking for natural behaviors by 1.

Spirit Bond Progression

For spirits you summon regularly (e.g., a favoured elemental or a recurring shade), track a Spirit Bond Timer [4].

  • Mark segments for successful commands, shared victories, or mutual aid (once per type per session).
  • At 2 segments: Gain +1 die to communicate with this spirit type.
  • At 4 segments: The spirit becomes Favored. Choose one: Leash Capacity +1, or –1 tick per command (minimum 0). Additionally, the spirit grants you +1 Boon when it departs naturally (not destroyed or forced out).

GM Guidelines for Summoning

NPC Summoners

Treat enemy summoners as having the same limitations: they must Call (action) and Bind (Boon or Fatigue). Their spirits have a Leash timer. The GM may choose not to track Leash for minor NPCs if the fiction does not demand it, but for major antagonists, use the same mechanics — this creates tactical choices (e.g., attack the summoner to break control, or force the spirit to cross a ward).

Plot-Level Summonings

  • Create a Ritual Timer [6–12] that the party can disrupt.
  • Each segment represents a component (blood sacrifice, alignment of stars, chanting cultists).
  • The party can sabotage components, kill cultists, or break the circle to halt the summoning.
  • If the timer fills, the entity arrives — now the party must bargain, fight, or flee.

Hazards of Greater Summoning

  • Uncontrolled Manifestation: The entity may be hostile to everyone, including the summoner.
  • Collateral Damage: Reality warps, wards fail, the dead rise, or the environment becomes a hazard.
  • Debt: Even if bound, the entity extracts a price — an Obligation timer that ticks per scene, demanding sacrifice or service.

Example: The Cult of the Ninth Bell. Cultists attempt to summon a fragment of the Ninth (void entity). A 6-segment timer: collect nine souls (segment 1), ring the forbidden bell (segment 2), etc. The party can interrupt at any segment. If the timer fills, the Ninth Rim appears — treat as a Tier IV hazard with a Bargain (offer a memory) or a Catastrophe (reality starts erasing the town).

Crafting: The Bench and the Codex

Crafting is not shopping. It is a project — something you start, that takes time you could have spent elsewhere, that the world gets a vote on, and that ends up carrying the marks of how it was made. A thing you bought is a thing. A thing you made is a record.

What You Can Make

WonderAn enchanted thing. Priced as a Talent: Minor 2 XP, Major 4 XP, Prestige 6 XP, Epic 8 XP (§6.8).XP and Obligation — see 6.9.5.[6], [8] or [10]

The Roll

Attribute + Craft for a Provision or a Work; Attribute + Arcana for a Wonder. Which Attribute depends on how you are working: Wits to plan and measure, Body to force and forge, Spirit to coax, Presence to command a workshop of other hands.

DV is set by ambition:

DVWhat you are attempting

5+Something nobody has made. The GM should ask you who told you it was possible.

Position is set by the bench, not by you. This is the whole materials rule — no inventory arithmetic, no counting reagents against a recipe:

PositionWhat that means at the bench
DominantThe right materials, the right tools, the right place, and time you are not being chased through.
ControlledAdequate. It will do. Most work happens here.
DesperateImprovised materials, borrowed tools, the wrong roof, or someone counting down outside.

So the answer to “do I have enough silver?” is never a subtraction. It is a Position, and it is the GM's to set from the fiction you have established. Gathering materials, buying them, stealing them, or being given them is how a player argues for Dominant.

Outcomes

One roll per downtime action, or per scene you spend at the bench while the world waits. When the Timer fills, the thing exists.

Flaws

A Flaw is not a penalty. It is a property the object now has, and it is usually the most interesting thing about it. Name it when you take it:

  • Thirsty — it wants something first. Blood, oil, a spoken name, a coin left overnight. Give it what it wants or roll at one Position worse.
  • Loud — it announces you. Using it hands the GM 1 SB.
  • Remembering — it does what its materials' previous owner wanted, occasionally, and the GM decides when.
  • Brittle — on a Miss while using it, it breaks. Not damaged: broken.
  • Marked — anyone who knows the craft can recognise the hand that made it, which is your hand.

You may buy a Flaw off later: a second project at DV 4, Timer [4]. Or keep it. A GM should be generous with Boons to a player who leans into a Flaw at the worst possible moment, because that is the entire point of the mechanic.

Wonders, and What They Cost

You cannot make magic. You can only borrow it and put it somewhere it will stay. So a Wonder costs three things:

  • XP, at its Talent-equivalent tier: Minor 2, Major 4, Prestige 6, Epic 8 (§6.8).
  • Obligation, marked at the moment of making, equal to the tier's step: 1 for Minor, 2 for Major, 3 for Prestige, 4 for Epic. You did not buy this. Someone lent it.
  • Attunement, thereafter — see below.

A caster with no Patron marks the Obligation anyway. It goes to whatever answered.

Attunement and Upkeep

  • You may be attuned to three Wonders at once. Attuning or releasing one takes a scene of quiet.
  • Each attuned Wonder takes upkeep each downtime, on the standard curve: max(1, its XP cost ÷ 3, rounded up) in XP, or 1 XP and a downtime scene spent with it.
  • Skip the upkeep and it decays one step: Maintained → Neglected → Compromised, per §6.7. A Compromised Wonder's enchantment is inert until repaired.

Artifacts — things you found rather than made, and things older than the people who named them — take no XP and no upkeep and never decay. They take Obligation instead, and they choose when.

Gathering

Once per downtime, a character may gather: Wits + Lore in a settlement or an archive, Wits + Endurance in the wild, DV set by how strange the thing is (2 for common, 3 for uncommon, 4 for rare, 5 for the sort of ingredient that has a name of its own). A success establishes in the fiction that you have the right stuff — which is to say it buys you Dominant at the bench for one project.

There is no reagent count. If the table starts doing arithmetic about how many petals are left, something has gone wrong upstream of the rules.

Followers and Assets

Followers and Assets represent the lasting impact of your character's actions on the world. They are not abstractions or passive bonuses — they are people, resources, and structures that exist in the fiction and can act independently of your character.

Together, Followers and Assets allow characters to project influence beyond a single roll or scene, shaping the world through relationships, preparation, and legacy.

Followers

Followers are named NPCs who have a personal relationship with your character. They may be students, allies, retainers, agents, family members, or dependents. Followers act in the fiction and can grow, change, or be lost as the story progresses.

What Followers Do

  • Assist you during scenes through Bonds and Assists.
  • Act offscreen to advance goals, gather information, or manage responsibilities.
  • Accumulate narrative weight and may eventually become player characters.

Followers are not disposable tools. They have motives, limitations, and vulnerabilities, and the GM may place them at risk when the fiction demands it.

Using Followers

Followers typically influence play in three ways:

  • Assists: Granting +1 die or narrative positioning when they are present and able to help.
  • Bond Activation: Spending a Boon to draw on trust, loyalty, or shared history for insight or support.
  • Offscreen Action: Handling tasks that would otherwise demand the PC's time or attention.

Follower States

Instead of tracking numeric timers, note a follower's Loyalty and Fitness as one of three states each.

Limits

  • Followers do not replace player actions.
  • Followers do not make rolls unless the GM explicitly calls for it.
  • Followers cannot solve major conflicts without consequences.

When Followers succeed or fail, those outcomes become part of the story.

Assets

Assets are durable resources, positions, or structures under your character's control. They represent leverage — things that allow you to influence the world at scale.

Examples include:

  • A safehouse, laboratory, or fortress.
  • A mercantile network or smuggling route.
  • A political office or religious authority.
  • A powerful relic, symbol, or institutional role.

What Assets Do

Assets:

  • Create advantages that persist beyond a single scene.
  • Resolve problems that cannot be solved with a single roll.
  • Shape the direction and scope of the campaign.

Assets are not passive bonuses. They matter only when invoked in the fiction.

Asset Status

Assets have a single Status that describes their overall health and usability.

StatusMeaningEffect
FlourishingWell-maintained, respected+1 die on rolls involving the asset, +1 Yield.

The Status worsens when the asset is neglected, attacked, or suffers a public failure. It improves through dedicated downtime actions or successful missions that benefit the asset.

Activating Assets

Most Assets require the expenditure of Boons to activate.

When you spend a Boon on an Asset, you may:

  • Advance or halt a Timer.
  • Introduce or remove a complication.
  • Secure resources, protection, or access.
  • Influence events happening offscreen.

The GM determines the exact impact based on the scale of the Asset and the current situation.

Activation Examples:

  • "My spy network delays the patrol — tick the Alarm timer backward by 1."
  • "The safehouse has a hidden exit — we escape at Controlled Position."
  • "My trading post provides fresh horses — remove one Supply segment."
  • "My guild contact suppresses the rumor — cancel an incoming complication."

The GM may veto an activation that exceeds the Asset's scope.

Upkeep

During downtime, each Asset and Follower you maintain requires one of the following:

  • Attention (Intensive): Spend a scene interacting with them (repairing, training, socializing).
  • Resources (Efficient): Spend 1 XP (or a relevant String) to cover costs offscreen.

If you provide neither, the Asset or Follower degrades one step:

  • Assets: Flourishing → Stable → Strained → Collapsed.
  • Followers: Faithful → Strained → Broken.

Two consecutive downtimes without upkeep collapses the Asset or loses the Follower entirely.

Terrestrial Patrons

Not all power comes from cosmic entities. Terrestrial Patrons are mortal factions or individuals — nobles, guildmasters, crime lords, generals, merchant princes — who offer tangible aid in exchange for obligation.

How Terrestrial Patrons Work

When you accept a favor from a Terrestrial Patron (access, resources, protection, an Asset), you mark +1 Obligation to that patron. Your Obligation Capacity (Spirit + Presence) applies normally, and exceeding it causes Fatigue exactly as with cosmic Patrons (see the Magic System rules).

Favors are typically Diamonds — leverage, rewards, or Assets that would otherwise cost XP.

Examples:

  • A noble grants you a safehouse (Minor Asset) in exchange for a future service.
  • A guildmaster provides legal immunity (String) for one heist.
  • A crime lord lends you enforcers (Cap 2 followers) for a single mission.

Clearing Obligation

Unlike supernatural Patrons, terrestrial obligations are cleared by performing tasks for the patron. The GM sets the scope:

  • Minor task (deliver a message, pay a bribe): clears 1 Obligation.
  • Significant task (escort a caravan, break a siege): clears 2 Obligation.
  • Major task (assassinate a rival, broker a treaty): clears 3+ Obligation.

Each task becomes a story hook. Refusing a task when Obligation is full may turn the patron into a rival or enemy.

Benefits of Terrestrial Patrons

  • No XP cost for favors — only Obligation.
  • Access to Assets, Strings, and Followers you could not otherwise afford.
  • Patrons escalate with your tier: a Tier I patron offers local favors; a Tier III patron offers regional power.

Player Trusts

A Trust is a shared pool of XP that players contribute to, used to acquire and maintain party-owned Assets and Followers. Trusts represent a collective enterprise — a mercenary company, a trading guild, a spy network, a religious order.

Forming a Trust

Any player may contribute XP to the Trust pool. Contributions are voluntary and cannot be withdrawn. The Trust has no individual owner; spending from the pool requires majority agreement.

High-Tier Play

As characters advance, Followers, Assets, Patrons, and Trusts become increasingly important. Higher-tier Talents and Assets consume Boons, forcing players to choose between immediate success and long-term influence.

At Tier III and above, your personal dice pool may be maxed, but a Cap 3 Follower adds +3 to assist rolls, and a Major Asset can shift the outcome of a scene without a roll.

Victory is no longer just surviving the roll. It is choosing which parts of the world move when you do.

Travel Framework

Journeys are more than distance; they are sequences of pressure, discovery, and consequence. The travel framework turns long roads into a series of dramatic legs without micromanaging miles.

Legs and Travel Timers

  • Break a long journey into legs, each representing a significant change in terrain, danger, or political region.
  • Each leg uses a Travel Timer (default size 4 segments).
  • The timer advances through progress actions, encounters, or scene resolution.
  • When the timer fills, the party arrives at the destination (or the next region). Unresolved complications carry forward.

  • Resupply at settlements, through successful foraging, or by spending a Cache token (see Asset rules).

  • Let players showcase talents in their chosen roles.
  • Mix discovery and hardship to keep legs tense but rewarding.
  • A failed travel roll should never result in "nothing happens." Use SB to introduce an encounter or tick a hazard timer.

Deck of Consequences (Optional)

The Deck of Consequences is a narrative tool for injecting drama, setbacks, and twists. It replaces or supplements GM fiat with randomized complications that remain thematically consistent. Its use is entirely optional.

Deck Structure

Use a standard 52-card deck (jokers optional). Suits map to complication themes:

SuitThemeExamples
HeartsSocial / EmotionalBetrayal, scandal, broken trust, awkward moment, ally under strain.
SpadesPhysical / HarmBruise, injury, collapse, gear damage, ambush.
ClubsMaterial / CostResource loss, gear failure, debt, ruined supplies, theft.
DiamondsMagical / SpiritualOmen, curse, Patron attention, reality bend, haunting.

Card Ranks & Severity

  • Ace Scene-altering twist; compels immediate response.
  • King, Queen, Jack Major complication with lasting effects.
  • 10–8 Moderate complication that reshapes the current scene.
  • 7–5 Minor complication; a nuisance that creates tension.
  • 4–2 Subtle complication or foreshadowing omen.

Jokers (Optional)

  • Red Joker: Catastrophic event (environmental collapse, Patron intervention).
  • Black Joker: Dark boon (immediate help, but with lasting cost or debt).

When to Draw

The GM may draw from the deck when:

  • A roll shows multiple 1s (e.g., three or more).
  • The GM spends 2+ Story Beats at once.
  • A major timer fills (scene or campaign timer).
  • During travel to generate an unexpected encounter.

Aim for 1–2 draws per session; more if the tone skews chaotic.

Procedure

  1. Note the number of SB spent (or generated).
  2. Draw a number of cards equal to the SB spent (minimum 1, maximum 3).
  3. Synthesize a single narrative complication from the combination of suits and the highest rank.
  4. Do not reshuffle until the deck runs out or the session ends — this creates a natural "fate budget."

Synthesizing Complications

Combine the suits and the highest rank into one fiction-first twist. Examples:

  • Hearts + Spades: Emotional betrayal that leads to physical harm ("Your ally flees, and the portcullis drops between you.").
  • Clubs + Diamonds: Resource drain with magical backlash ("The ritual consumes your healing herbs and leaves a scar on the threshold.").
  • All four suits: A crowd riots, loots your supplies, and a spirit feeds on the chaos.

Integration with Fiction

  • Translate the raw card result into a fiction-first complication. Do not apply mechanical penalties alone.
  • The complication should build on what is already happening in the scene, not restart the story.
  • Respect player agency — allow clever mitigation, but ensure the consequence lands.

GM Quick Cues

  • Use the deck to surprise yourself as well as the players.
  • If a card does not fit the current fiction, draw again or adjust the rank up or down by one.
  • For campaign-scale foreshadowing, draw a card at the end of a session and note its theme; introduce the complication in a later session.

Crown Spread (Optional)

The Crown Spread is a campaign-framing tool that uses a spread of cards to establish the long arc of a story. It provides seeds for GMs and players to weave motifs, omens, and foreshadowed events. Its use is entirely optional.

Setup

  • In Session Zero (or before the campaign begins), lay out 5–7 cards in a semicircle (the "Crown"). Use either the Deck of Consequences or a standard card deck.
  • Each card anchors a motif, omen, or looming event.
  • Record the spread openly on a Campaign Sheet or digital log.

Interpreting the Spread

PositionMeaning
Position 1 (Root)The underlying tension or theme of the campaign.
Position 2 (Crest)A key faction or patron influence that will rise.
Position 3 (Crown)The climax image or major confrontation the arc builds toward.
Position 4 (Left Hand)A bond, ally, or relationship that anchors play.
Position 5 (Right Hand)A rival, betrayer, or challenger who pressures the party.
Positions 6 & 7 (Optional)Expansions for setting-wide twists (environmental, mystical, or political).

Using the Spread in Play

  • Each drawn card becomes a Foreshadow Timer [4] attached to its motif. Advance the timer when events lean toward that omen (e.g., when the GM spends SB on related complications, when a major scene involves that faction, or when a travel leg passes through an area tied to the motif).
  • When a Foreshadow Timer fills, the motif manifests concretely in play — a faction rises, a betrayer reveals themselves, a curse activates.
  • Tie Spread cards to Campaign Timers for pacing.

Campaign Timer

The Campaign Timer tracks rising stakes across the entire arc.

  • Default size: 8 segments.
  • Advance the Campaign Timer when: multiple SB overflows in a session (e.g., three or more 1s on a single roll), when travel legs resolve with major cost (e.g., a character reaches Harm 2), or when Spread omens manifest concretely.
  • When the Campaign Timer fills, the Crown confrontation arrives. Play through its fallout as the campaign climax.

Ending & Legacy

  • After the Crown confrontation resolves, hold an epilogue session.
  • Resolve any remaining Foreshadow Timers as epilogue vignettes.
  • Players may mark Legacy Bonds — new anchors for future campaigns or descendants.

GM Quick Cues

  • The Spread is not a railroad — it foreshadows, not dictates.
  • Reinterpret cards liberally as play evolves; symbols matter more than literal events.
  • Remind players of their omens between arcs to build tension and payoff.

Player Archetypes at the Table

Fate's Edge gameplay flexes to different player archetypes. These roles are not strict classes but rather approaches to play that help a group balance spotlight and tone.

The Solo

Focus: Mastery of self, independence, one-on-one drama.

Strengths: Quick spotlight scenes, duelists, scouts, specialists.

Risks: Can drift into isolation or hog solo arcs. Needs explicit ties to group goals.

GM Tools: Use Bonds, rival duels, and one-on-one omens to keep engagement tethered to the group.

The Mixed

Focus: Hybrid adaptability — both support and lead.

Strengths: Bridges gaps in group dynamics; excels in team tactics.

Risks: May feel overshadowed by extreme specialists.

GM Tools: Spotlight them when versatility matters: flexible magic, leadership, negotiation, or sudden pivots.

The Mastermind

Focus: Schemes, plans, and command over the long arc.

Strengths: Drives strategic play, coordinates others, excels in intrigue.

Risks: May over-plan or dominate spotlight with metagame thinking.

GM Tools: Challenge them with shifting information, Patron demands, and rivals who anticipate their moves.

Balancing Archetypes

  • A healthy table mixes all three archetypes, ensuring solo tension, group cohesion, and strategic play coexist.
  • Encourage players to flex between archetypes scene by scene; they are fluid, not fixed.
  • Spotlight balance: in a 3-hour session, each archetype should anchor at least one major scene.

GM Quick Cues

  • Use archetypes as a lens for framing scenes: duels for Solos, shifting tactics for Mixed, grand reveals for Masterminds.
  • When spotlight imbalances arise, rotate complications or bonds to another archetype's strength.
  • Archetypes inform how Patrons and factions court PCs: Solos as champions, Mixed as envoys, Masterminds as plotters.

Scene Example: The Bridge at Dusk

This example shows the core resolution loop in practice across multiple player actions.

Setup

The party—Lyra (rogue), Thane (fighter), and Sera (caster)—is on a narrow stone bridge overlooking a ravine. Cultists are chanting on the far side, completing a ritual that will summon a demon. The GM has already established a Ritual Completion [6] timer with 3 segments filled.

GM: "You're on the bridge. The cultists are 40 feet away, their chant rising. You have 3 segments left on the Ritual Completion timer. What do you do?"

Player Action 1: Lyra Scouts Ahead

Lyra: "I creep forward, keeping to the shadow of the bridge's railing, trying to see how many cultists there are and what they're doing."
GM: "Controlled, DV 3. On a Miss, you dislodge a stone and they notice you."

Lyra rolls (Wits 4 + Stealth 2 = 6 dice): 9, 7, 4, 3, 2, 1.

  • Successes: 2 (9, 7) vs DV 3 → Partial
  • SB: The 1 gives the GM 1 Story Beat.
  • Boons: Lyra gains 1 Boon.
GM: "You get a good look. There are six cultists—four chanting around a stone altar, two watching the bridge. You count the segments: the ritual is at 4 of 6. They're close. But your foot scrapes a loose stone. The watch cultists are looking your way."
Lyra: "I freeze."
GM: "They don't see you. But they're alert now. What does everyone else do?"

Player Action 2: Thane Charges

Thane: "I charge the watch cultists while they're distracted. I want to take them out before they raise the alarm."
GM: "Charge is a Full Turn Action. Move two bands and attack. Costs 1 Fatigue. Your next defense is Desperate. The bridge is narrow—open ground, so no penalty."
GM: "Controlled, DV 3. On a Miss, you stumble and the cultists get a free shot."

Thane rolls (Body 3 + Melee 3 = 6 dice): 10, 8, 6, 5, 4, 2.

  • Successes: 4 (10 counts as 2, 8, 6) vs DV 3 → Clean Success
  • SB: None.
  • Boons: None.
GM: "You barrel into the first cultist—he goes down hard (Harm 2 from your medium weapon). The second cultist stumbles back. The bridge shakes. I'll tick Collapse [4] +1—you're at 1 of 4. The ritual's chant falters, but doesn't stop."
Thane: "I'm in the middle of them now. Desperate defense?"
GM: "Yes. Your next defense is Desperate."

Player Action 3: Sera Casts a Spell

Sera: "I cast a [FIRE][AREA] burst to scatter the remaining cultists and disrupt the ritual. I want to hit the altar."
GM: "That's two tags—DV 4 (1 + 2 tags). It's a two-action spell (Weave then Cast). You're not in melee, so Controlled Position. On a Miss, backlash."
Sera: "I weave." (Action 1)
GM: "The fire gathers in your palm. The cultists see you. The chant quickens. Roll to cast."

Sera rolls (Wits 4 + Arcana 2 = 6 dice): 1, 1, 8, 7, 6, 3.

  • Successes: 3 (8, 7, 6) vs DV 4 → Partial
  • SB: Two 1s → GM gains 2 Story Beats.
  • Boons: Sera gains 1 Boon.
GM: "The fire bursts outward. Two cultists are thrown back (Harm 1 each). The altar cracks—the ritual chant stutters. But the backlash: fire licks back at you. Mark 1 Fatigue, and the bridge timbers catch fire. I'll tick Collapse [4] +1—you're at 2 of 4. Also, I'll spend 1 SB: the lead cultist survives and screams, 'The Ninth will rise!'"

Spending Story Beats

The GM now has 3 SB total (1 from Lyra, 2 from Sera). The GM spends 2 SB:

GM: "I spend 2 SB. The lead cultist's scream echoes—and the bridge groans. You feel the stone shift. Tick Collapse [4] +1 (now 3 of 4). And a new timer: Reinforcements [6] starts ticking—something is coming from the far side of the ravine."

Resolution

The party has 1 segment left on the Ritual Completion timer, 1 segment before the bridge collapses, and a new threat incoming. The scene escalates.

GM: "The bridge groans. The fire is spreading. The lead cultist is reaching for a bell. What do you do?"

What This Example Teaches

  • Position and DV are set based on fiction—not arbitrary numbers.
  • Partials give Boons and progress, but with costs.
  • 1s give SB even on successful rolls.
  • SB are spent to escalate tension, not punish.
  • Timers are visible and ticked openly.
  • Fail forward: Even Partials and Misses change the situation—the story never stalls.

Summoning Leash

  • Capacity: Cap + Spirit segments.

Action Economy (Quick)

  • Each turn: 1 Action + 1 Move.
  • Move: shift one range band (Close ↔ Near ↔ Reach ↔ Far).
  • Dash (Action): move two bands; next defense Desperate.

  • Charge/Skirmish (Full Turn): move two bands and attack; costs 1 Fatigue; next defense Desperate; requires open ground.

Opportunity Attacks

Moving out of an enemy's Close range without Disengage provokes one free melee attack (Controlled Position). The following actions also provoke if performed while engaged in Close with an enemy:

  • Making a ranged attack.
  • Casting a spell without [TOUCH] or [WARD].

Resolving an adversary

Adversary damage and Fatigue

Adversaries track their listed Fatigue and Resilience, but never player Boons or a player Harm-level track. Harm printed on an adversary is damage it deals. Its Fatigue track uses the listed capacity (or the listed governing Attribute).

Resolve a damaging hit

Before rolling, state the attack’s Harm, Position, DV, Effect, and consequences. On a successful damaging hit, start with weapon or ability Harm and apply explicit Harm bonuses. Effect describes what the action accomplishes; it does not secretly multiply Harm. Any change to damage from Effect must be stated before the roll. A Partial grants the stated lesser progress, not automatically full weapon damage.

Armor, then Resilience

If Armor applies, use the Armor Conversion Table. None or ignored Armor leaves all incoming Harm unconverted and creates no armor Fatigue. Ignoring 1 point of Armor means one category lower for that hit: Heavy becomes Medium, Medium becomes Light, and Light becomes None. An ability that ignores Armor entirely uses None. For incoming Harm above 3, convert the first 3 using that column and leave the excess as Harm. Subtract remaining Harm from Resilience, clearing existing Fatigue if any Harm remains. Then add the converted Fatigue. Do not also subtract one Resilience merely because the attack hit.

NPC Fatigue overflow

When Fatigue reaches or exceeds its capacity, subtract 1 Resilience, clear Fatigue, and discard excess Fatigue. Check once per resolved hit or separate Fatigue event; do not loop over discarded excess. Fatigue never increases the adversary’s outgoing Harm. In a player-facing test, each marked NPC Fatigue improves the opposing player’s Position by one step, capped at Dominant; excess improvement adds no dice and does not lower DV. Apply the player’s own penalties as usual.

Ending the opposition

At zero Resilience the adversary is defeated in the manner established by the fiction. For a phased adversary, end that phase and start the next with its listed Resilience and cleared Fatigue; excess damage does not cross phases unless its rules say so. A puzzle adversary with no Resilience still requires its stated Resolution. Bargains, escape, surrender, and decisive fictional solutions can end opposition without attrition.

Worked hits

The Silken Blade has Resilience 8, Fatigue capacity 3, and Light Armor. A successful Harm 2 hit becomes 1 Harm and 1 Fatigue: Resilience 7, Fatigue 1. A later unconverted wound clears that old Fatigue before adding its new armor Fatigue. Three separate Harm 1 hits against her Light Armor each add 1 Fatigue; the third fills the track, clears it, and removes 1 Resilience. Against her Light Armor, a Harm 3 Backstab ignores its one category and removes 3 Resilience and clears existing Fatigue.

Player-facing opposition

Adversaries have no separate initiative turn. Describe their intent and ask how the player responds. Before the player rolls, set DV by the actual opposition (normally 3, or 4 for hard resistance), set Position, and name the consequence. A printed NPC pool is a competence reference, not a second roll or a formula converting dice into DV. For NPC opposition, references to an opposed test mean this player-facing test.

Resolve the response

Use the usual player outcome matrix: meeting DV achieves the declared intent; a Partial makes the declared lesser progress; a Miss makes none. Apply any stated consequence through that outcome and the relevant SB procedure. A successful defense avoids the threatened hit; it does not deal retaliatory weapon Harm unless that was the declared action. A Partial defense uses the lesser protection agreed before the roll. Never charge damage twice for one consequence.

When the GM spends SB

A threat, warning, or demand is free to describe. Harm from a previously announced failed response is the consequence of that response, not a free extra NPC attack. To introduce a new attack or special complication independently, spend SB: use the creature’s listed move, or 1 SB for an ordinary attack that prompts a player-facing response. Pay a named move’s printed cost and respect its trigger; Poisoned Cut’s 1 SB buys the poison after a hit, not a second attack. An expressly free opportunity attack remains free and prompts the same response procedure. A paid move does not bypass a defense or automatically inflict Harm unless it expressly says so.

Lock stakes before rolling

Position, DV, Effect, and the announced consequence are fixed when the roll is framed. Resolve that roll before spending its newly generated SB. Those SB can change the next action or subsequent fallout, not retroactively change the roll’s Position, DV, success, or damage. Earlier SB may shape a new test only before its stakes are locked.
