The Invoker's Guide

Reference 51 Patrons Generated Author: Nicholas A. Gasper

“I have carried a dozen Symbols and broken half of them. The weight does not diminish; it only redistributes. This is the Invoker's art: to know which Patron to call, when to call them, and what they will demand in return. The Triune is not a religion. It is a map.”

For the Invoker, the Patrons are not gods to be worshipped but forces to be understood — tides, storms, laws, hungers and silences, each with its own nature, its own demands and its own price. The Invoker does not kneel. They study. They learn the shape of a Patron's attention, the weight of its Obligation, and the exact moment a Symbol must be shown, a Rite invoked, or a Seal cracked.

This document is built, not written. Every Patron below is generated from the same files the toolkit reads, so the catalogue cannot drift out of step with the Patron browser, and adding a Patron adds it here for free. What is authored here is the frame: the Triune, the archetypes, and the Cross-Resonance table.

The Triune

The Triune divides the Patrons into three Orders, each ruled by one of the three sisters who are not sisters, the three angels who are not angels. It is a framework of comprehension rather than worship — a way of predicting what a Patron will ask before you are standing in front of it.

Beyond the three lie Patrons the architecture cannot hold. The wise Invoker knows when to reach for the known and when to reach for the strange.

The Seraphic Order

Ruled by Inaea, Angel of the Spider
“In the web, all vows are caught.”

Law, flame, and connection — the principles that bind and structure.

Seraphic Patrons offer reliable, structured power. Their Rites are predictable, their Obligation costs moderate, their Acts of Service plain. The price is accountability: these Patrons remember what you swore, and they will hold you to it.

Also known as: The Sacred Geometry, Kon'reh

The Sacred Geometry is the Ykrul name for Kon'reh. To a Ykrul this is not a comparison and not a metaphor: the board is the shape of the world, so the Patron of perfect forms and the eight figures on the board are the same subject, approached with a stone in your hand instead of a prayer. They will tell you a well-played game is an act of devotion and mean it literally, and they are not troubled that this makes every teahouse a shrine.

The Aelinnel use the same term and are not clear about what it means. Ask three of them and you will get an order of architects, a discipline of measure, and a game — with no sense from any of them that these are supposed to be one answer. Whether the meaning was lost on the way west or was never as fixed as the Ykrul believe is exactly the sort of question the Sacred Geometry is unhelpful about.

This is also why the Ninth Rim opposes it. The board has eight figures. The Ninth is the rim past the edge of a thing that was supposed to contain everything, and there is no polite way for the architecture to hold that.

Guises

  • The Bloody-Handed Matron — The Northern Earls. Her oldest continuous worship, in the hills and highlands between Ubral and Viterra. The Earls are hill-king stock and keep her the way they keep everything — quietly, at home, and not in front of the Crown. Among the Renards she is a grandmother rather than a goddess: consulted before a feud, blamed after it, and never mentioned to a bishop.
  • The Debt — The Mound (her cult). Her organised worship, and barely a name at all — which is the point. Faith kept in secret becomes bookkeeping: the Mound speaks of what she is owed and when she will collect, and has largely stopped speaking of her. This is the guise a devotee slides into when the Cold Hand is the only one they are still willing to hold.
  • The Bloodied Mother — Vhasia. Answers prayers in kind, not in mercy. Vhasian worship of her is disproportionately Renard and disproportionately deniable.
  • Myrmis Aecuta, the Reckoner — Thepyrgos. Her imperial name, and effectively an academic one now. In the city she is a subject rather than a patron: cited in old proofs about supply and attrition, taught in a footnote, prayed to by nobody. Thepyrgos is not hostile to her. It has simply moved on, which she would recognise as a kind of ending and might even approve of.

Provenance

Thepyric, of the Astroegro imperial period.

She is a southern goddess. Astroegro was a war-empire before it was a cautionary tale, and Myrmis is what its war looked like when it was still working — the drill and the ledger and the burning village, held together in one pair of hands and called a virtue. When the last emperor broke his covenant and the empire went into the water, most of its gods went with it. Hers did not. Her cult walked.

The Earls say she crawled north, which is their phrase and is not meant literally — it is how highlanders describe something that arrives slowly, from below, and is in the house before anyone agrees to have let it in. What actually travelled was worship: veterans, camp-followers, a handful of masks, and a practice of putting a stone down afterward. It took root in the Viterran Highlands because the Earls had a use for it and no bishop close enough to object.

A goddess who counts the dead is portable in a way a goddess of imperial victory is not. Astroegro's Myrmis was the patron of an army that won; what survived the empire was the half of her that knew what winning cost, which is exactly the half a hill country full of feuding earls could use. She lost an empire and kept the arithmetic.

Thepyrgos remembers her the way a university remembers a discipline it no longer teaches: there are proofs with her name on them and nobody left who prays.

The Two Hands

Myrmis holds war with both hands and refuses to put either down. The Red Hand is war as it is suffered: wrath, the thing that happens to people, the scream and the burning thatch. The Cold Hand is war as it is made: a craft with tolerances, supply lines, siege geometry, a mind that can look at a valley and see how many days it feeds an army.

Every atrocity in the world's history was authored by someone holding one hand and disclaiming the other. The planner says he only drew the lines. The soldier says she only did as she was told. Both are lying, and both believe themselves. Myrmis will not let either of them put the other hand down: to take her Symbol is to accept that the plan and the scream are one act, and that you did it.

Then she counts the dead. The Cairn-Mother's grief is not softness bolted onto a war-goddess — it is what makes the arithmetic honest. A calculus that never tallies the cost is not a calculus, it is an excuse, and her devotees are required to walk the field afterward and put a stone on every one of them. Her cruelty and her mourning are the same discipline observed at different hours.

She is the patron for a character who intends to do something terrible on purpose and for a reason they can state out loud. She is wrong for a character who wants the reason without the doing, or the doing without the reckoning. When a player asks whether Myrmis approves, the question to hand back is always: and afterwards, who carries the stones?

Also known as: The Ninth, The Ninth Rim, Ninth Rim

Two names, two registers. The Ninth is the general name — the thing behind the Taboo, spoken of by people who have no board in front of them and would rather not be specific. The Ninth Rim is the Kon'reh term: the rim beyond the eighth figure, off the edge of a board that only has eight — that board being the Sacred Geometry, which is the Ykrul name for Kon'reh. The Ykrul hold that Kon'reh is not a game about the world but a model of it, so a word for something off the edge of the board is a word for something off the edge of everything. To a Ykrul the two names are the same statement made at different volumes; to anyone else, the Kon'reh term is the one that sounds like it means less, which is why it survives in places the other cannot be said aloud.

Beyond the Triune

Older than the taxonomy, or too singular to sit inside it.

These Patrons offer specialised, unorthodox power. Their Rites are often unique, their costs vary wildly, and their Acts of Service are cut to their own peculiar shape. The price is singularity: they ask you to accept their particular vision of the world. They are not lesser. They are unclassifiable, and an Invoker who understands them holds what the orthodox do not.

Archetypes: The Carried Trade

Most Invokers in play are not scholars of the Triune. They are tradespeople who carry two or three Symbols because two or three Symbols is what the work takes — the ward-setter with a shingle, the exorcist with a bag, the conjuror with a licence and a season. What follows are 3 such combinations, each built on a rivalry from the table below, because the friction between a practitioner's own Symbols is the most reliable trouble in the game.

An archetype is not a class. It is a purchase order: which Symbols to buy, in what order, and what the friction between them will cost you. Everything here resolves with the rules already in this guide — Symbols are 4 XP Minor Assets, invocation marks Obligation, displaying the Symbol buys DV −1 and Obligation −1, Crack the Seal buys instant resolution for +2 Obligation and a Compromised Symbol, and Obligation Capacity is Spirit + Presence. Nothing below adds a subsystem.

Reading an Archetype.

  • Three, not four. Each archetype names three Symbols: 12 XP total, and one short of the four that make the first rite of every scene cost an extra Obligation. The fourth Symbol is available and is always a decision, never a bargain.
  • The spine is a rivalry. Two of the three Symbols are listed rivals in the Cross-Resonance table. Displaying one while invoking the other is Position one step worse and +1 Obligation. That is not a flaw in the build; it is the build.
  • Buy in order. The order given is the order the character learned the trade in, and a character partway through it is a perfectly good starting character. One Symbol is a practitioner. Two is a professional. Three is a reputation.
  • The trade is a trade. These are people with premises, fees, and a waiting list. Play the invoice as seriously as the invocation — half the drama of the profession is being paid in something other than money.

The Doorwright — Ward-setter, seal-breaker, and unlicensed consultant to the watch

“I put the lock on. I know where the lock is weak. Those are the same job, and I charge for both.”

A tradesman of thresholds. Doorwrights hang wards for merchant houses, seal the cellars of the recently and badly dead, and are called by watch sergeants who would rather not put a name in a report. The work is honest, dull, and steady — until the same skill that closes a door is the only skill on hand that can open one. Doorwrights are notorious for the second half of the job, and are hired anyway, and know it. They tend toward coat, cigarette, and a tone of voice their clients dislike and their clients' enemies dislike more.

The whole character is here. To ward a house the Doorwright must believe a boundary is sacred; to get into one they must believe no boundary is. Both Symbols are on the same belt. Every scene where both are in play is Position one step worse and an extra Obligation, and the Doorwright pays it without comment because the alternative is turning down work.

Attributes. Wits and Spirit lead; Body 2 for the parts of the job that involve a crowbar.

Skills. Arcana and Lore for the wards themselves; Insight for reading a room and a client; Craft for the physical half of a seal — chalk, salt, iron, mortar; Deception for the invoices.

Obligation. Capacity is Spirit + Presence, and the Doorwright's Presence is usually the weaker half. The classic failure is a practitioner with excellent judgement and no capacity to carry what that judgement costs.

Signature: Crack Your Own Seal. The move the trade is named for. A Doorwright who hung the ward knows where it is thin, and may Crack the Seal on their own Sealed Gate Symbol to open a boundary they themselves set — instant, +2 Obligation, Symbol Compromised, and the GM gets an SB on the way through. The house they are burgling is usually a house they were paid to protect last season. The client is usually still alive.

When it goes wrong. A Compromised Sealed Gate Symbol is an inert ward on the Doorwright's own door. Enemies learn this. The classic disaster is not the job going badly; it is the job going fine and the practitioner coming home to a threshold that no longer objects to visitors.

The job.

  • A Silkstrand counting-house wants the ward it bought last year tested. The test will show the ward is sound. It will also show the second ward, hung inside the first, by someone else, facing inward.
  • A watch sergeant in Ecktoria needs a cellar opened without a Censorate writ, and is offering the one payment a Doorwright cannot refuse: a name struck from a parish register, put back.
  • A rival Doorwright is undercutting the fee across three wards and hanging seals that fail in eighteen months. Proving it means breaking one, and breaking one means the family inside is unwarded for a night.

Played straight, this is: The coat, the debt and the bad attitude; the practitioner listed in a directory who does jobs for the city watch; the abjurer who is best known for the doors he has opened.

The Quiet Coroner — Exorcist, deposition-taker, and last physician to the already dead

“I am not here to release you. I am here to write down what you say, and then release you.”

Someone has to sit with the ones who did not finish. Quiet Coroners are engaged by families, magistrates, harbour boards and occasionally by the dead themselves; they arrive with a bag, a chalk line, a cheap chair and no hurry. The work is half exorcism and half deposition, and the order matters: a soul that has been laid to rest cannot be questioned, and a soul that is still answering questions has not been laid to rest. The good ones are patient. The great ones are the sort of patient that frightens people.

The Coroner is paid twice for opposite things, usually by the same person. The widow wants her husband at peace. The magistrate wants to know who did it. Karn will hold him at the table as long as there are terms to argue; the Shepherd will open the road the moment it is asked. Attributes. Spirit and Wits; Presence matters more than practitioners expect, because Capacity is Spirit + Presence and this trade fills it fast.

Obligation. Two death Patrons on one belt is the heaviest routine Obligation load in the guide. Coroners are the reason downtime exists. A Coroner who has not cleared Obligation in three sessions is a horror story about to happen to their own party.

Signature: The Deposition. Ring of salt, one chair, one lamp, and a question list. Invoke Varnek Karn's Rite openly (Symbol displayed: DV −1, Obligation −1) to hold the departed to terms, then the Witness to fix what is said. On a Clean Success the testimony is admissible and true; on a Partial it is true and incomplete in the way that gets someone hanged; on a Miss the thing at the table answers a question that was not asked. Close by invoking the Shepherd. Coroners who skip the closing are called something else within a year.

When it goes wrong. A Coroner who takes the testimony and does not open the road has, in the plain language of the trade, kept it. The dead do not object at first. The Shepherd does — a Compromised Shepherd Symbol means the next three funerals in that parish go badly, and everyone will know which practitioner attended the first one.

The job.

  • A Zakov Namer wants a name added to the Roll of the Drowned, but the drowned man says he was not drowned, and will not say what he was.
  • A Covenant court will accept a deposition from the departed as evidence. The opposing advocate is a Recusant, and intends to argue that the witness cannot be sworn because the witness has no standing to lose.
  • Nine houses in one street have gone quiet in the polite way. The families are calm, well-fed, and have all engaged the same Coroner, who is very good, and who has not opened a road in a year.

Played straight, this is: The professional who arrives with a bag and a method; the ghost-finder with his chalk and his lamp and his notebook; the physician of souls who takes a case history first.

The Encore — Licensed stage conjuror, court entertainer, and occasional truth-teller

“Everything you have seen tonight was a trick. Watch closely: this next part is not.”

A working magician in the vulgar sense — playbills, a hall, a season, a manager taking a cut. The Encore performs impossibilities for money in front of people who have paid to be fooled, which is the single best cover in the Amaranthine for performing an impossibility in front of people who have not. Ecktoria licenses them, Viterra distrusts them, Mab's court adores them, and the cleverest of them use the stage exactly as it was always used: to say the one true thing in a room where nobody is permitted to say true things, and to be applauded for it.

The mask and the encore want opposite things. Mab's favour is granted quietly and must be repaid in kind; Palinode's is granted to a room and must be repaid in front of it. An Encore who works a masque carries both, and the moment the crowd demands the trick again is the moment the bargain becomes public. Attributes. Presence first and by a distance; Wits second. Presence also doubles the Obligation Capacity this build needs.

Obligation. The most survivable of the three builds — Presence-heavy, so Capacity is generous — and the most likely to spend it all in one night, because Palinode's Obligation is incurred in front of witnesses who will remember the promise.

Signature: The Unglamoured Turn. Once in a performance, drop the act. Display Solara's Symbol — no glamour, no patter, no misdirection — and address one person in the room directly. Treat as a Presence + Insight action at DV set by how badly that person does not want to be seen. A Clean Success means they are seen, by themselves as much as by you, and whatever they were about to do they do not do. It works exactly once per audience, and it works because the hall has spent two hours being lied to beautifully.

When it goes wrong. Mab does not mind being used as a stage effect. Palinode does not mind being used as a stage effect. Both mind being used as an alibi. When the act is exposed as cover for real work, the Encore does not lose their power — they lose the hall, the licence and the manager, and a practitioner without a room is a practitioner carrying two Symbols that only function in front of a crowd.

The job.

  • The company has a three-night licence in a city where the Censorate reads every playbill. Someone has bought the box directly above the stage for all three nights and has not attended.
  • A Chain-Lantern in the fourth row is not watching the trick. They are watching the wings, where a nine-year-old with a real talent is holding the props.
  • Mab's court has requested the act. Command performance, no fee, no refusal, and the favour will be granted before the encore rather than after — which every Encore knows is how the Queen makes a debt land in front of witnesses.

Played straight, this is: The evening-dress conjuror whose hypnosis is not a trick; the sorcerer who breaks his staff at the end of the show; the professional illusionist who is a magician twice, and only one of them is billed.

Cross-Resonance

Carrying Symbols from rival Patrons generates friction. That friction is the point: it is what an Invoker trades for reach. Edge Loci are places where one side starts a step better in Position; the Friction column is a set of prompts for spending Story Beats, not a list of penalties to apply.

Quick rulings.

  • Position Nudge. In a home locus, start one step better; in a rival's locus, one step worse.
  • Effect Shade. Where a Patron dominates, consider an Effect bump; where opposed, consider Limited Effect unless it is paid for.
  • Symbol Interference. Carrying both sides' Symbols raises the noise: the first rite each scene may mark +1 Obligation, at the GM's call.
  • SB Colour. When spending SB in these matchups, prefer the suit that fits — Hearts for social, Spades for harm and escalation, Clubs for material cost, Diamonds for numinous disturbance.
