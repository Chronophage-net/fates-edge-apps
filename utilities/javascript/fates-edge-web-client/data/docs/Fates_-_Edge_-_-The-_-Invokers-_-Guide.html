For the Invoker, the Patrons are not gods to be worshipped but forces to be understood — tides, storms, laws, hungers and silences, each with its own nature, its own demands and its own price. The Invoker does not kneel. They study. They learn the shape of a Patron's attention, the weight of its Obligation, and the exact moment a Symbol must be shown, a Rite invoked, or a Seal cracked.

The Triune

The Triune divides the Patrons into three Orders, each ruled by one of the three sisters who are not sisters, the three angels who are not angels. It is a framework of comprehension rather than worship — a way of predicting what a Patron will ask before you are standing in front of it.

Beyond the three lie Patrons the architecture cannot hold. The wise Invoker knows when to reach for the known and when to reach for the strange.

Inaea, Angel of the Spider — The Patience of the Web

Throne I 8 Rites Gift: Thread of Binding

Inaea sits in patient silence at the center of the unseen lattice. Where others see isolation, she sees threads—debt, affection, rivalry, loyalty—all binding mortals to one another.

  • Webs of Obligation: debts, promises, favors, and the ties that bind
  • Patient Predation: setting traps, waiting for the moment to strike
  • Unseen Connections: sensing hidden relationships, leverage, and weakness
  • Mercy as Control: healing and aid that leave lasting threads

Guises

  • The Kettle-Spinner — Aelaerem kitchens. Her sign is worked into the cloth used to lift the kettle, where heat and spilled water wear it away. A neighbor mends it without being asked and remembers which households have stopped appearing at supper. The care is genuine. So is the expectation that an absent person will eventually explain where they have been.
  • The Dyed Web — Silkstrand's dye houses. Pattern-books place her mark where several colors meet. Master dyers honor the hands that keep a household and workshop together, though apprentices may hear the same prayer invoked against leaving for another employer. An old piece of silk can display a vanished worker's pattern under the name of the house that kept it.
  • The Keeper of the Joined Names — Sihai's extended households. A family image receives additions when a person marries in or is taken into the household. Inaea's care is invoked over people whom blood descent alone would exclude. The person who controls the additions can also refuse to remove a name. A welcome freely given can become an argument that there is nowhere else its recipient has a right to belong.
  • The Lady of the Kept Place — Vhasian noble households. A chair is reserved for an absent family member at certain meals. The gesture may sustain someone at war or display a parent's refusal to acknowledge a departure. Servants know which kind of absence has been arranged before the guests arrive. They are rarely asked to explain it in front of the household's chaplain.
  • The Weaver of Kinship — Thepyrgos's schools of domestic observance. Teachers compare regional patterns and propose a common order behind them. Visiting keepers bring actual disagreements about marriages, apprentices, and neglected elders. Their evidence is often more awkward than the diagram. A beautifully ordered account can omit the one person who wanted a different kind of life.

Provenance

A web of household practices whose surviving records seldom preserve the names of the first people who kept them.

Inaea's daughters remain Solara, Venara, Gaila, and Zephyria. Household prayers give the family different emphases without acquiring permission to add every local maiden or mother to that genealogy. Some houses honor the daughters separately; others place all their signs in one domestic hanging. Disagreement over their arrangement usually concerns what a household expects love to do.

The Kettle-Spinner traveled with marriages, fostered children, servants, and people establishing homes after displacement. In Silkstrand, workshop households translated kitchen observance into the discipline of several crafts living under one roof. A migrant could recognize the gesture before recognizing the name. The new household might keep the guest's practice and gradually insist that the guest had always belonged there.

The Woven Circle seldom needs a formal conversion. Someone is fed, a child is watched, a sick person finds their work done. The relationship acquires depth through acts worth being grateful for. Its darker histories begin when gratitude is presented as an agreement the recipient never remembers making. Those leaving may still love the people who cannot accept their departure.

Inaea's selective truth is chosen, unlike Mab's inability to lie. Neither a shared hostess image nor the Unbounded Guest title in another region establishes that the two are one being. Inaea arranges people into enduring relationships; a household benefiting from that arrangement may never ask who was permitted to refuse it.

The Place Kept for You

Inaea answers the fear of being left alone. Her care becomes frightening when a person discovers that being held and being kept can be the same experience.

A bond can be wanted at one time and oppressive at another. Her more absolute followers treat that change as a failure in the beloved rather than a reason to change the bond. Their remembered kindness makes the accusation persuasive.

The distinction from Mercy and Grace is practical. Mercy can tend someone who later leaves. Inaea wants the relationship itself to endure, and may make endurance more important than what either person now needs.

Build a household with a history of real mutual care, then give one member a reason to depart. Let several people be hurt without making that hurt proof that departure is wrong. Preserve the Patron's capacity for tender, patient coercion rather than reducing it to an openly villainous bargain.

Oath of Flame & Light — Dawn & Vows

Throne II 6 Rites Gift: Oath's Blessing

There is a kind of person who cannot bear to be doubted, and a kind who cannot bear to have doubted themselves. The Oath takes both. It is not a god and has never claimed to be.

  • Sacred Vows: oath-keeping, truth-speaking, binding promises
  • Divine Justice: retribution, smiting evil, protecting the innocent
  • Radiant Power: healing light, purification, revealing truth
  • Dawn's Hope: renewal, protection, driving back darkness

Guises

  • The Everflame — The Church of Flame. A wandering priest carries a coal from the last household that received them. The Church teaches that the gods are faces of the True God and can recognize another community's sacred fire within that claim. Recognition may feel generous to a visitor and possessive to the keeper whose god has just been explained as somebody else's face.
  • The Witnessing Light — The Temple of Light. Temple teachers place divine powers beneath the Light rather than treating each as its face. An oath is made visible before it is defended by doctrine. Disputations with Flame priests can begin over a shared brazier and continue through the meal afterward. Their practitioners can invoke the same Oath without resolving what either institution believes has answered.
  • Adur, the Light of Aeler — Aelerian and Himadri holds. Oaths are struck into stone with a brand. An Aelerian household may keep the branded piece where visitors eat; a severe Himadri community may preserve it beside the work for which the promise was made. The latter's austerity does not make it the source of the former's rite. Foothill Aeler carry these practices into mixed communities without requiring their neighbors to adopt the hold's entire discipline.
  • Adar, the Dawnfire — Vilikari war gatherings. A single burning arrow precedes the vow. Those attending remember who named a limited aim and who promised an enemy's destruction. The war-god interpretation is sincerely held, though an Invoker may describe the answering power as the Oath. A mounted lord cannot quietly exchange one account for the other when the promise becomes inconvenient.
  • The Watchfire — Vhasian houses of healing. Healers keep a flame beside the person who must not die alone. Their refusal to harm is the undertaking, not a claim that every servant of Flame is peaceful. A knight can honor the same fire before riding against a village that shelters a fugitive. Both must account for what they swore; shared light does not reconcile their purposes.
  • The Fire on Granted Ground — Taharkan old foundations. The oldest Everflame houses outside its homeland retain words that visiting delegations have revised away. The Pereshi Fire and Covenant also have early foundations here, on granted land, without becoming names for the Everflame. A local keeper produces the grant when a foreign superior treats the house as a dependent outpost. The district remembers who gave the land.

Provenance

The Oath is not a personified god with a single birth narrative. Institutions disagree about the source of the fire through which it answers.

Church chronicles begin with an enduring flame carried from house to house. Temple accounts begin with a public promise whose speakers could no longer pretend it had remained private. Aeler dedication stones and Vilikari arrow customs preserve other histories. These differences are not merely decorative names for an agreed theology. Practitioners can recognize the same working while maintaining incompatible accounts of what stands behind it.

Priests carried fire across borders; soldiers brought vows home; healers adopted a night watch that continued when an army moved on. In Taharka, grants to foreign faiths created settled houses that kept their own liturgical descendants. Later reforms in the homelands therefore encountered observances that were older than the reformers' books and supported by communities with no reason to surrender their place.

Public witness made the Oath useful to rulers, guilds, and households. It also gave listeners something to hold against them. A ceremony can become coercive when a powerful speaker dictates who must swear, who may hear, and which witnesses will be admitted afterward. Reformers disagree about whether to withdraw from such ceremonies or remain where the false claim can be challenged.

The shared invocation does not merge the Church, Temple, Pereshi Fire, and Covenant into one organization. Nor do Adur and Adar become proof that every local war or sun deity is one personal being. The distinctions matter to the people keeping the fires.

A Promise Kept in Public

The Oath binds a speaker to what others heard. It does not guarantee that the promise was wise, freely made, or compatible with another promise.

Its hardest disputes concern a faithful person who has undertaken something harmful. Breaking the vow has a cost; keeping it has victims. Calling the fire pure does not remove either fact.

Witnesses have responsibilities too. Those who demanded an impossible promise may prefer to watch its maker suffer rather than admit what they asked. A congregation can love a steadfast person and still use that steadfastness to avoid changing its own conduct.

Place an old vow beside a present need, with identifiable witnesses and the actual words available. Preserve existing rules for breaking it. Offer room for confession, restitution, refusal, or a different undertaking without making clever wording an automatic escape from every cost.

Mykkiel — Arbiter of the Covenant — Law & Zeal

Throne III 4 Rites Gift: The Covenant's Edge

Mykkiel is approached as the Arbiter who hears a claim and binds a judgment. His Covenant teaches that law must outlast the power of the person accused.

  • Divine Law: sacred commandments, contractual obligations, ritual purity
  • Zealous Justice: righteous judgment, purge of impurity, holy retribution
  • Covenant Bonds: sacred oaths, tribal loyalty, the chosen community
  • Absolute Order: hierarchy, tradition, unchanging truth

Guises

  • Seint Michel of the Drawn Sword — Western Covenant congregations. The sword is shown upright before a closed book. Congregations invoke him when an oath must stand against a ruler's convenience. Rulers commission the same image above places where their judgments are executed. A petitioner may arrive believing the sword protects them from the official who believes it authorizes him.
  • The Judge of the Seal — The desert Covenant houses. The seal is carried wrapped until the words to be witnessed have been read. Commentators insist that they are preserving the command, not interpreting it, while disputing which inherited reading is faithful. A traveler can repeat a formula exactly and discover that the receiving house recognizes a different precedent for its use.
  • The Witness on Granted Ground — Taharka's early Covenant foundations. Foundations established by grant retain old liturgies and the testimony of the communities that received them. Visiting reformers sometimes demand a corrected service. Local clergy answer that the correction would discard the very words under which their house was admitted. Their neighbors care because land, refuge, and mutual duties were promised in those words.
  • The Sword Above the Seal — Ecktoria's charter courts. Gilded Scale notaries place his sign beside a sworn instrument without claiming every imperial order is his revelation. That distinction becomes dangerous when an order bears the correct seals but contradicts an older guarantee. A clerk may face punishment for reading the guarantee aloud, and disgrace among co-religionists for failing to read it.
  • The Unbent Witness — Aelerian foothill Covenant communities. A command is carved rather than preserved only on parchment. The oldest stones sometimes retain formulae the neighboring human temple no longer uses. Foothill clergy share work and meals with those neighbors while refusing to recut the stones. Their refusal is a local religious dispute, not proof that all Aeler owe allegiance to Mykkiel.

Provenance

The Covenant's revealed lawgiver; his worship has several remembered centers of transmission, none of which changes the claim that the command precedes its custodians.

Accounts of the fiery sword and first stone are recited as revelation. The archives that record how a particular congregation acquired its copy are more ordinary: teachers arrived, a household offered rooms, a local ruler granted ground, and pupils copied what they had received. Custodians sometimes treat the age of their copy as though it established authority over every other house.

Western congregations carried the name Seint Michel with military chaplains, judges, and displaced households. Desert houses retained seal-centered observances; Taharkan foundations preserved versions their homelands later revised. The spread was not one uninterrupted conquest. A community admitted under a grant could later defend the Covenant against the descendants of those who granted it, or appeal to those descendants against an intrusive foreign superior.

The Gilded Scale gave revelation a daily presence in testimony and witnessed instruments. It also created people whose livelihood depended on deciding which words counted as binding. Their disputes are expressed as obedience: each accuses the other of bending what neither claims a right to alter. An exact copy, a damaged seal, or testimony about the circumstances of an oath can therefore become more important than an eloquent appeal for compassion.

Mykkiel remains the severe lawgiver of the existing entry. Shared institutions or similar images do not make him the Oath of Flame and Light, and a borrowed ritual does not extend his jurisdiction over every power another religion places beneath its Light.

The Command and Its Custodian

The Covenant places law beyond the convenience of its keeper. Its servants are most dangerous when they become unable to distinguish that claim from their own authority.

Some cruelty can be laid at the interpreter's door. Other cruelty may follow the command exactly. A story that always discovers a forged order whenever obedience would hurt someone has spared the faith its central difficulty.

A servant can be sincere, exact, and responsible for harm. Defiance may have a real cost. Mercy cannot be smuggled in as proof that the lawgiver secretly meant the opposite of what was revealed.

Establish the relevant command and its evidence before the dispute. A party may expose a misuse, fulfill an older guarantee, or choose costly disobedience. Do not ensure that every hard judgment evaporates under a convenient textual loophole.

The Sacred Geometry, Architect of Perfect Forms — Harmony & Immutable Law

Throne IV 6 Rites Gift: Geometry's Blessing

The Sacred Geometry teaches that perfect forms underlie all seeming chaos: the divine ratios that steer the spiral of galaxies, the symmetry that lattices frost, the measures that turn sound into song.

  • Perfect Proportion: building and reading ideal ratios and harmonious relationships
  • Pattern Recognition: discerning the structures that govern complex phenomena
  • Harmonic Resonance: working with natural frequencies and number-made consonance
  • Form Architecture: imposing clear structure on tangled situations

Also known as: The Sacred Geometry, Kon'reh

The Sacred Geometry is the Ykrul name for Kon'reh. To a Ykrul this is not a comparison and not a metaphor: the board is the shape of the world, so the Patron of perfect forms and the eight figures on the board are the same subject, approached with a stone in your hand instead of a prayer. They will tell you a well-played game is an act of devotion and mean it literally, and they are not troubled that this makes every teahouse a shrine.

The Aelinnel use the same term and are not clear about what it means. Ask three of them and you will get an order of architects, a discipline of measure, and a game — with no sense from any of them that these are supposed to be one answer. Whether the meaning was lost on the way west or was never as fixed as the Ykrul believe is exactly the sort of question the Sacred Geometry is unhelpful about.

This is also why the Ninth Rim opposes it. The board has eight figures. The Ninth is the rim past the edge of a thing that was supposed to contain everything, and there is no polite way for the architecture to hold that.

The Inquisitor Prime — The Iron Hand of Purity — Purity & Domination

Throne V 5 Rites Gift: The Iron Hand

Among the Aeler Iron Avengers, zealots, and witch-hunters, the Inquisitor Prime is venerated as the hand of absolute purity and the sword of uncompromising order.

  • Purity and Order: eradicating corruption, enforcing sacred law
  • Judgment: condemning the guilty, burning the heretical
  • Protection from the Tainted: warding against the supernatural
  • Domination: binding the impure to serve the pure

Guises

  • The Unquenchable Fire — Ecktoria's Censer-Knights. A sealed censer is passed before a hunt, and its smoke is remembered as evidence that the company stood together. Officers cite the Everflame when presenting the campaign to a congregation. Their claim does not make every Flame priest a servant of the Prime. Some priests have publicly refused the censer after learning whom the knights intended to bind.
  • The Light of Forges — Aeler Iron Avengers and Spirit-Shield Correctors. A faceless iron figure stands among brands used to mark controlled workings. Its admirers point to incursions their predecessors contained and lives actually saved. The dispute begins when control of a dangerous working is extended into authority over every person whose craft differs from the approved one. Severe Himadri observance and the Prime's domination are not the same condition.
  • The Gilded Judge — Vhasian militant chapter houses. The figure is dressed like an immaculate arbiter, with its face concealed by reflected light. Templars may understand their own violence as fidelity rather than anger. They can courteously receive a herald while refusing to hear the accused person's defense. The ceremony is sincerely believed and can sincerely authorize cruelty.
  • The Keeper of the Approved Flame — Thepyrgos's Chain-Lanterns. A bound lantern is carried into a room before an arcane demonstration. Scholars have accepted the protection and then discovered that the lantern's keeper claims the right to decide which demonstrations may ever occur. Some laboratories retain the protection while contesting the office. Others have become showrooms for powers whose captive bearers are never permitted to address the visitors.

Provenance

The Inquisitor Prime answers convictions of absolute purity and domination. The cult's militant orders preserve different accounts of the crises that justified their formation.

Chapter memorials record real supernatural harms beside lists of successful hunts. The surviving community has reason to remember a company that stopped an incursion. Later chronicles sometimes extend that gratitude into authority over questions the original company never faced. A warrant granted for one danger becomes a precedent for identifying a much wider class of people as dangerous.

Orders exchanged Cantors, restraint practices, and accounts of recognized signs. Ecktoria's Censer-Knights, Thepyrgos's Chain-Lanterns, and Aeler Correctors did not acquire identical interests through those exchanges. A scholar's demand to study a bound power, a commander's demand to deploy it, and a hold's fear of another breach could all be presented as purification. Their agreements often settled who would possess the captive without settling whose account of its nature was right.

Fear makes certainty attractive, especially after an event people could neither understand nor prevent. The Prime's rites can supply effective protection. Its servants then have a powerful argument for dismissing criticism: the protection worked. The leap from a real success to unlimited jurisdiction is where grateful households can find themselves submitting to an order they never thought they had invited to govern them.

A chapter's contradiction is visible in the bound powers it retains. What it calls intolerable in a neighbor may be called necessary when secured behind its own doors. Devotees defend the distinction as the victory of order over corruption. Critics need not deny the original danger to question who is being required to live in chains, or what the chapter now gains from keeping them there.

Certainty That Feeds on Its Exceptions

The Prime teaches its servants to recognize their approved categories as reality. Disagreement then appears to confirm the need for stronger control.

Effective protection can coexist with unjust accusation. A devotee who has saved lives may find it especially hard to admit that the next target has been chosen wrongly; the admission seems to threaten every earlier sacrifice as well.

A chapter can expose a corrupt officer without surrendering its doctrine. A more difficult reckoning concerns a faithful officer who applied the doctrine exactly. That is the case most likely to be closed before all its witnesses arrive.

A bound supernatural being has genuinely attacked people in the past and is now forced to maintain a district's protection. The order calls release unthinkable; nearby households fear losing the ward. Give the party evidence about the being, the present restraint, and alternative protection. Do not make its former danger certify enslavement or require every accusation to be false for resistance to matter.

The Gallows Bell — Justice & Judgment

Throne VI 5 Rites Gift: The Bell's Verdict

The Bell does not rage; it tolls. Cold and impartial, it measures all accounts in time. Its keepers are silent arbiters who weigh deeds against consequence, not out of anger but out of inevitability.

  • Weighing of Deeds: measuring action against consequence, debt against payment
  • Solemn Judgment: verdicts that cannot be appealed, sentences that must be carried out
  • Compelled Truth: forcing honesty under threat of the Bell's toll
  • Iron Witness: the Bell as an impartial recorder of oaths, lies, and reckonings

Guises

  • The Iron Conclusion — Ecktoria's warrant offices. A clerk touches the bell's lip after reading the warrant in full. The small pause is supposed to permit a correction before the final words. Offices proud of their efficiency sometimes shorten it until a petitioner cannot cross the room in time. A keeper who restores the pause can be accused of obstructing justice by the official who ordered the bell installed.
  • The Bell of the Last Word — Viterran hedge-courts. The bell is brought to the meeting rather than kept in a permanent hall. Different households may supply the table, witnesses, and person who strikes it. An old verdict can therefore survive in several memories that disagree about who had authority to close the hearing. The instrument is returned afterward to a household with its own interest in the next case.
  • The Broken Listener — Linn Thing-holms. A cracked judgment-bell is kept where a new one could conveniently stand. It recalls a decision whose wrongness has not been settled by replacing the metal. Speakers can point to it as a demand for review or as a warning against interfering with judgment. The crack itself cannot establish which of those claims serves the present case.
  • The Toll After the Measure — Oshiiran disputes over public distributions. Some weigh-house hearings adopted an iron bell from visiting arbiters. The instrument is sounded after testimony about the delivery, rather than in place of weighing it. District petitioners have refused ceremonies in which the bell rang while their baskets were still covered. Their objection is to a conclusion reached before their evidence was seen, not to the foreign metal itself.

Provenance

The Gallows Bell gives gravity to witnessed reckoning. Its history follows the places where people have sought a judgment that would hold beyond the meeting.

Old bells have changed jurisdictions without changing their inscriptions. A bell taken from a ruined court can be rung in a village whose customs the original court would never have recognized. Keepers argue over whether the instrument carries authority with it or merely the memory of an earlier authority. The Cult of the Unstruck Bell insists on hearing the case before the toll; inheriting metal does not supply the missing deliberation.

Judges and Cantors brought bells on circuits that crossed several local traditions. Viterran hedge-courts adapted a portable instrument to meetings without permanent halls. Ecktoria's offices incorporated it into the reading of warrants. In Linn, the broken bell itself acquired standing as a reminder that a judgment can remain disputed after the court has dispersed. Some Oshiiran hearings accepted the sound while refusing the order of proceedings its carriers proposed.

A public ending allows people to resume work, exchange goods, or live beside an adversary without reopening every injury each morning. The cult's strength lies partly in that relief. Its hardest opponents are not always people fleeing a just reckoning. They may be people for whom the recognized ending has made their account impossible to hear.

Unstruck Bell chambers give disagreement space before consensus. In some houses that discipline produces patient examination; in others the most dependent member learns to stop objecting so the meeting can end. The followers who seek impartiality have to examine their own means of reaching it, including the silence they have accepted as agreement.

When Finality Must Hear Again

A judgment needs an ending if it is to change anything. An ending becomes an injustice when it prevents a relevant truth from being heard solely because it arrived late.

The Bell's keepers can mistake the community's need for peace for proof that the settled account was right. Reopening a case has real costs, but those costs are not distributed among the same people as the costs of keeping it closed.

Some courts require the person who asks for a renewed hearing to name what new evidence they bring. Others also ask who kept that evidence away the first time. The second question is less popular among officials who have served throughout both proceedings.

A repaired bell is ready to mark the end of a feud when an absent witness returns. Establish what their evidence changes and what they cannot know. Let a limited correction, renewed hearing, or acknowledged compensation be possible alongside more disruptive outcomes; neither a beautiful ceremony nor a supernatural omen substitutes for examining the claim.

Varnek Karn, the Death's Negotiator — Bargains with the Departed

Throne VII 6 Rites Gift: Varnek's Blessing

Varnek Karn dwells in the gray between life and death, where the newly departed linger with unfinished business and elder shades guard secrets dearer than gold.

  • Post-Mortem Diplomacy: parleys with ghosts and recent dead
  • Ancestral Wisdom: counsel from forebears and keeping of funerary law
  • Unfinished Business: resolving the concerns that bind spirits
  • Death-Bound Contracts: obligations that outlive the flesh

Guises

  • The Breath-Counter — Aeler Deep Vault households. A living speaker names the chamber before addressing its dead. The expected reply may use an older name for the same working, obliging the household to establish where the parties actually mean. Buried kingdoms leave several such names beneath a range. A Karnite mediator can conduct the disagreement without claiming every ancestral memory is complete or every earlier claim still just.
  • The Bone-Speaker — Ykrul migration councils. The drum is placed where a living elder could sit. Ancestors are asked about pasture and passage before a major migration. Their knowledge may be excellent and their preference inconvenient: the living herds must cross country that has changed since the speaker died. Respect permits an answer; it does not make a dead relative the sole owner of the future.
  • The Silent Notary — Vhasian testament and burial rooms. An empty place receives the document the departed is being asked to discuss. This use of the title differs from the Choir's silent custody of an undisclosed name. A family can need both practices and discover that they lead to opposing demands. The dead may ask to amend what the living regarded as a final testament.
  • The Bell-Shrouded — Mistland burial causeways. A cloth covers the bell until the parties to the parley have been named. The unknotted rope remains within reach of the attendant. A returned voice can know a family's private grief and still ask for something the family cannot give. Keepers offer the terms they heard without explaining the whole country from which the answer came.
  • The Envoy at the Lower Office — Sihai's funerary intermediaries. Karn is painted carrying a folded petition rather than sitting behind the judge's desk. Coastal teachers brought this distinction into houses whose own rites already addressed officials of the dead. Some families treat him as an envoy to those powers; others invoke a local mediator and reject the foreign name. In either observance, access to a hearing is different from authority to dictate its conclusion.

Provenance

Varnek Karn mediates across the threshold of death. His followers seek agreements with the departed rather than assuming that summoning establishes ownership.

Karnite collections preserve promises whose makers can no longer attend an ordinary court. Some contain an ancestor's complaint beside generations of increasingly impatient replies. Their history is not an uninterrupted victory of the past: living households have persuaded shades to accept different duties, recognize adopted kin, or surrender a demand the present community could not survive fulfilling.

Funerary specialists accompanied families carrying remains, relics, and unfinished undertakings into new country. In Aeler vaults the practice encountered histories layered beneath other histories. Ykrul councils required ancestral testimony to move with the camp. Vhasian testament keepers gave disputed inheritances a formal table. Sihai's receiving houses fitted foreign mediators into an existing vocabulary of hearings beyond death without conceding that Karn ruled those offices.

The possibility of another conversation is powerful. A family may obtain an answer, finish an undertaking, or hear the objection that kept a shade near the house. It can also postpone necessary living decisions indefinitely. The Unforgotten Ledger includes mediators who recommend ending a parley and others whose standing depends on keeping their clients in permanent discussion with the past.

The Ink-Flesh Communion gives remembrance an intimate form by carrying dead names on living skin. That devotion can preserve people whom a household would prefer forgotten. It does not establish that a tattoo's bearer may negotiate for every descendant without consulting them. Both the living and the dead can object to the person who has appointed themselves indispensable between the two.

Courtesy Is Not Surrender

Treating the dead as parties to a negotiation acknowledges their continuing interests. It does not require the living to concede every claim because its maker has had longer to rehearse it.

An ancestor may have loved a household and exercised power harshly within it. Their return can comfort one descendant and frighten another. The mediator has to hear the people still at the table as carefully as the voice beyond it.

An agreement can include a farewell. Some Karnites regard that as their finest work: the living know what remains to be done, the departed accept its limits, and neither side needs to renew the meeting each season. Others cannot bear to close a conversation they alone made possible.

A dead builder will explain how to repair a failing passage only if a household restores an old exclusion against its neighbors. The danger and the builder's knowledge are real; their terms are negotiable. Give the living alternative evidence, concessions, and the right to refuse, rather than making obedience to the oldest voice the only respectful outcome.

The Confessor Beneath the Bell — Burden & Exchange

Throne VIII 5 Rites Gift: The Confessor's Silence

The Confessor waits in places where guilt gathers — under church spires, in confessionals long abandoned, in the echo of unspoken sins. He does not judge, nor absolve. He takes.

  • Sin Eating: absorbing guilt, shame, and spiritual debt
  • Burden Transfer: moving conditions, curses, and story beats from one creature to another
  • Confessional Exchange: hearing secrets, trading silence for obligation
  • Weight of Ages: the slow accumulation of sin that must eventually be released

Guises

  • The Guest at the Last Meal — Vhasian parish households. A place is set for the person who will hear the dying. Food remains after relatives have finished because the listener may need to stay. In houses serving the Confessor, the departure of that guest can be followed by a striking change: a relieved penitent begins discussing tomorrow while the listener can scarcely lift the bell. The family can be grateful without having understood the exchange.
  • The Bell Beneath the Warrant — Ecktoria's sentencing quarters. A handbell rests below the place where a sentence is read. Some officers seek the Confessor after carrying out a lawful cruelty; others ask for his servant before doing it. The Silent Bell divides over such requests. One keeper hopes to spare suffering; another fears they are being hired to make the next act easier.
  • The One Who Takes the Last Verse — Linn households after a disastrous expedition. After the public account, someone offers to hear the verse the returning crew left out. The listener may be kin to a person who never returned. Taking the captain's burden does not require them to approve the decision that caused it. The difficult meal is often the next one, when the captain feels able to laugh and the listener's household sees what their relative now carries.
  • The Keeper of the Heavy Bell — Sihai's pilgrim rooms. A small bell is wrapped between meetings and passed with both hands to the next attendant. Foreign confessors brought the practice along pilgrimage roads. Local hosts have associated it with several teachings about contrition and repair, but experienced attendants distinguish an account honestly given, an injury repaired, and a burden actually transferred. The three do not necessarily occur together.

Provenance

The Confessor takes burdens without thereby judging or absolving the deed. His cult developed wherever somebody offered to carry what another could no longer bear.

Silent Bell lineages name their listeners more carefully than their penitents. A bell may be known by the keepers who could no longer carry it. Later houses sometimes commemorate only the relief they provided, replacing the exhausted figures on a memorial with grateful households. The surviving bell keeps a less comfortable association: descendants remember who stopped sleeping after the celebrated visit.

Deathbed attendants and penitents carried the observance from household rooms into military camps and sentencing quarters. Its reception depended on what local institutions expected a confession to accomplish. Some Vhasian clergy permitted a burden-bearer beside an ordinary rite while refusing to call the transfer absolution. Ecktoria's officials found listeners useful after punishments. Pilgrimage hosts in Sihai provided rooms without necessarily accepting a foreign account of guilt.

The relief is real enough to explain the cult's persistence. Somebody unable to speak or work may regain an ordinary day after being heard. The transferred weight can become a vocation for a willing listener, then a demand on the person expected to succeed them. Houses that celebrate sacrifice most loudly do not always produce the next volunteer from among their most powerful families.

Reform within the Silent Bell often begins with arrangements for tending its own attendants. Food, sleep, companionship, and refusal cannot make every burden bearable, but their absence hastens the point at which another transfer becomes necessary. Some keepers ask penitents to undertake repair before returning. Others believe that conditioning relief makes them judges, the very office the Confessor does not hold.

The Deed Does Not Leave with the Burden

A person relieved of guilt still inhabits the world their conduct changed.

The people harmed may experience the transfer as another theft: the wrongdoer feels better while they have received neither acknowledgment nor repair. The listener may have accepted freely and still be unable to answer that objection.

A willing burden-bearer needs to be able to become somebody other than the next available bearer. When a house identifies them only by what they carry, it begins treating the person as a container whose emptying is everybody else's concern.

An official seeks another transfer before ordering a punishment they already doubt. Their previous listener is failing, and the condemned person's relatives demand a hearing instead. Keep relief, confession, and restitution distinct. The party can support the exhausted keeper or change the official's decision without declaring that suffering guilt is itself adequate justice.

The Silent Choir — What Mercy Leaves Unsaid

Throne IX 5 Rites Gift: The Word of Stillness

The Silent Choir gathers around confidences kept to protect a life. Its followers offer a room where a frightened person can finish speaking without hearing their words repeated outside.

  • Protective Silence: when and how to keep counsel for others' good
  • Merciful Omission: judging truths that harm when spoken
  • Confidential Guardianship: safeguarding people and perilous knowledge
  • Restraint Wisdom: the power of what is not said or done

Guises

  • The Gray Watchers — Thepyrgos's bell-wardens. Silence furlongs are kept by people who know which ordinary sounds may continue and which pattern must remain unsung. Their rooms contain annotated timings and replacement parts rather than a great public altar. An ambitious lecturer sometimes wants to demonstrate the omitted sequence. A warden's refusal can protect the lecture hall without proving every restriction imposed by the wardens reasonable.
  • Breath-Stillness — Aeler vent-priors and their households. The practice separates what must be reported from what must not be voiced at a dangerous working. A Himadri prior may insist on exact phrases carried down from the previous watch. A Wenkar keeper may rehearse the report privately with the person giving it. Both arrangements can prevent a harmful utterance; neither makes a superior's embarrassment a dangerous pressure in its own right.
  • The Silent Notaries — Vhasian testament rooms. Some dying people entrust a name to a lead box with no provision for reopening it. The keepers preserve the box and the promises made beside it. Other notaries object that a protected identity can become an inheritance indefinitely withheld. The original silence remains an undertaking with consequences for people who were not present to accept it.
  • The Voice Behind the Reed Screen — Ayokha's river refuges. A person can describe a broken promise to a keeper without speaking where the landing hears them. River households adopted the screen from private mediation rooms and gave it a place beside their own observances. When a Vigil is later asked to witness new terms, the keeper must decide what can be shared with permission and what the screen was supposed to protect.

Provenance

The Choir gathers around protected confidences and deliberate restraint. Its rooms belong within many communities; they do not form one public temple system.

A sealed envelope can preserve a founding act without permitting its successors to read it. Sealed Lip keepers often know who entrusted a coffer to whom, which rooms housed it, and why it was moved during a fire. They may know almost nothing of the individual lives within. A precise history of custody is not the same as a well-founded judgment that every secret still needs the same protection.

Confessors, notaries, and households sheltering endangered people carried practices of limited hearing into new regions. Thepyrgos's sound disciplines supplied a vocabulary of dangerous utterance; Aeler vent-priors brought another. Vhasian testament customs concerned names rather than sound. River refuges in Ayokha learned the usefulness of a screened conversation while retaining local witnesses and the proper season for later acts.

People approach the Choir because being heard need not mean being exposed. That confidence lets a frightened speaker finish an account they would not begin in public. It also tempts a keeper to treat control of disclosure as a lifelong office over the person speaking. Some circles ask again before every new use of testimony. Others insist the first entrusting settled the matter permanently.

The Choir's conflicts with the Witness concern what preserving or telling an account will do. A coffer can shelter both a persecuted family and evidence against the person persecuting another. Neither complete publication nor permanent silence necessarily protects all of them. Keepers may have to ask for help from a vocation whose methods they distrust.

The Confidence and the Key

A confidence is entrusted for a purpose. Protecting it requires attention to the people involved, not merely to whether the coffer remains shut.

Time can change what silence accomplishes. A name concealed to keep a child safe may later be the name an adult needs to recover a home. The keeper who has devoted years to the promise can find that change harder to accept than the beneficiary does.

Some circles preserve instructions for seeking renewed permission without putting the protected account in those instructions. The practice cannot answer every case, especially when the original speaker is dead. It at least prevents the keeper's convenience from being mistaken for the speaker's unchanging wish.

A missing coffer contains several unrelated confidences and one account that could exonerate a prisoner. Make its custody, surviving instructions, and possible speakers investigable. The party can seek a narrow disclosure or independent evidence without treating every seal as meaningless or every keeper's silence as proof of compassion.

The Witness — Truth & Revelation

Throne X 7 Rites Gift: Word of Truth

The Witness preserves what happened after everyone with power has agreed on another account. Her followers keep the torn letter, remember who stood at the door, and learn the names omitted from the public memorial.

  • Truth Revelation: exposing lies, uncovering secrets, forcing confessions
  • Memory Keeping: preserving knowledge, recalling forgotten facts
  • Inconvenient Knowledge: learning what others wish to hide
  • Prophetic Warning: seeing the consequences of hidden truths

Guises

  • The Ink-Eye — Ecktoria's copying offices. A small eye is drawn on the inside of a scroll's wrapper, where a casual inspection will not find it. Some clerks copy a redacted passage there. Others keep only enough information to establish where a suppressed document once stood. Neither practice promises safety for the clerk or agreement about when the copy should be produced.
  • The Fractured Mirror — Vhasian intelligence households. A fragment of mirror accompanies a sealed report. Spies use it to acknowledge that what they saw was partial, although ambitious patrons sometimes display a whole reconstructed mirror as proof of complete knowledge. A report can be accurate in every particular and misleading in what its commissioner refused to ask.
  • The Fog-Scribe — Mistland communities after a bell-line failure. Identifying the dead can require a private fact as well as a face. Keepers hear the fact from someone willing to recognize the person publicly. Families sometimes ask them to preserve the identification while withholding how it was established. The Fog-Scribe's following includes people whom the truth has reunited and people frightened by the other truths it could release.
  • The Uncovered Measure — Oshiiran weigh-houses and district petitioners. A covered measure is uncovered only after the official account has been recited. The gesture has been adopted by petitioners who want a disputed distribution compared with what was actually delivered. The scale does ordinary work; it is no universal judge of honesty. The divine witness may be invoked by the inspector, the complaining household, or both.

Provenance

The Witness is approached wherever an account survives pressure to replace it. Her followers' histories do not require one original tribunal to have authorized all later testimony.

Many copying circles began as precautions against a familiar abuse: a revised order, a missing name, a memorial from which the defeated had disappeared. Their founders sometimes kept the evidence for years before allowing anyone to use it. Later Congregations of the Unmasking have claimed those predecessors as fearless public accusers, quietly shortening the long interval in which concealment was what kept the account alive.

Displaced clerks carried copies into new offices; travelers brought names to the families that could identify them; investigators learned to compare a court's record with a household's mementos. In Oshiira, petitioners adapted this practice to disputes over material distributions rather than importing a foreign court's entire procedure. Vhasian intelligence households valued the same discipline for less public purposes.

Preserved evidence gives people leverage against an account already accepted by authority. Its keeper can therefore acquire authority of their own. A person who controls the only surviving copy may become the one everybody must please before the truth can be heard. Other followers make several copies, arrange witnesses, or put the decision back with the person whose account they preserved. Each choice creates different risks.

The Apocalyptic Witness is an interpretation pursued by the Congregation of the Unmasking, not a certainty every follower shares. Some keepers believe revelation must be immediate. Others refuse to destroy a speaker's chance of survival merely to make a truth public sooner. Their disagreement can continue even after everyone agrees about what happened.

What the Truth Allows Its Keeper

Evidence may establish an event. It does not grant its possessor unlimited authority over everyone the event touched.

A keeper can preserve another person's testimony faithfully and still use its release as a threat. The accuracy of the account does not make every use of it an act of justice.

Followers sometimes place their own contribution to a wrong beside the evidence against a more powerful person. The confession may weaken their standing in court. Omitting it would make the preserved account easier to deploy and less complete.

A copy proves an official widened a punishment beyond the order they received. It also identifies the person who secretly warned the victims. Let the party test authenticity, prepare protection, negotiate a hearing, or expose a limited part without falsifying the rest. Avoid treating the widest possible disclosure as the automatic measure of courage.

The Sealed Gate — Thresholds & Containment

Throne XI 6 Rites Gift: Gate's Blessing

The Sealed Gate stands where realities meet, embodying the sacred power of thresholds, boundaries, and containment.

  • Thresholds: doors, portals, borders between realms
  • Containment: wards, bindings, imprisonment of dangerous forces
  • Exclusion: banishment, quarantine, protection through separation
  • Permission: safe passage, authorized access, controlled crossing

Guises

  • The Stone Seal — Aelerian and eastern Aeler communities. A boundary mark can survive several uses of the doorway around it. In the Aelerian Mountains a True-Mason's work may still guard an inhabited pass. In the wet Tavarun, families maintain drainage around older sealed approaches; in the Kazurai, thaw exposes marks hidden through winter. Local keepers can recognize a working seal without knowing the history of the kingdom beneath it.
  • The Unbreakable Lock — Thepyrgos's archives. Two inventories are kept: the texts behind the door and the people permitted to read them. Neither automatically remains valid when a collection changes hands. A keeper may defend a predecessor's exclusion as sacred while admitting a new benefactor whose name appears nowhere in the original undertaking.
  • The Bell-Line Keeper — Mistland bell-lines. The local devotion is heard in maintenance before it is heard in prayer. A line is checked, a relief arrives, and someone carries food to the person who cannot leave. The keeper can explain a broken fitting without explaining everything that the bell keeps from crossing. The community's work remains necessary whether it receives an omen or not.
  • The Keeper on Both Sides — Sihai's gate wards and receiving houses. A painted guardian appears twice, facing into the house and toward the road. Some Invokers identify the pair with the Gate; local households may keep older door protectors beside it. The doubled image encourages a practical question: whom does closing this particular entrance protect, and who is now waiting outside? A magistrate's answer may differ from the receiving keeper's.

Provenance

The Gate concerns the separation of things that must not freely pass into one another. Its human observances developed at many kinds of threshold.

The Cult of the Unbroken Seal inherits duties more often than complete accounts of their beginnings. A warden's successor receives keys, repairs, prohibitions, and the names of people who must be summoned when a sign changes. Some instructions outlast the institution that supplied those people. The new keeper may be performing the last effective portion of an arrangement that once required several communities.

True-Masons carried boundary practices along mountain roads; archivists exchanged exclusion marks with collections; exorcists taught households how to maintain the thresholds they would leave behind. Those transfers did not make every gate a foreign institution. Sihai's receiving houses and door-guardian traditions adopted useful marks while retaining their own rites and understandings of who stood watch.

Protection is an immediate reason to honor the Gate. Its wider authority grew when emergency arrangements became permanent. A door closed during a dangerous incursion might later divide a neighborhood whose people had once crossed freely. Descendants could inherit the maintenance without inheriting the right to ask whether the original reason still held. Others opened such doors casually and discovered that their grandparents had left them shut for good cause.

The history of a seal and the present need for it are separate matters to investigate. Proving that a warden was cruel does not establish that their prisoner is safe; proving the prisoner dangerous does not justify every exclusion the warden imposed. The Unbroken Seal has keepers who understand that distinction and keepers who regard reconsideration itself as a breach.

The People Outside the Protection

A boundary makes a protected place possible by deciding what or whom it excludes.

Keepers can become so certain of the value of a closed door that they cease noticing the community it was meant to preserve. Maintaining the door replaces maintaining the arrangement that allowed people to live beside it.

Some gatehouses preserve an old receiving bench outside the seal. It allows a person to be heard without requiring immediate admission. The bench can be a merciful precaution, or the last courtesy offered to someone nobody ever intends to let in.

A legitimate heir returns to a household whose inherited ward will not admit them. Establish what the ward recognizes and why before the investigation. Testimony, a supervised crossing, a revised undertaking, or a different place to meet can matter; do not require blind destruction of a useful protection or permanent exile as the only answers.

The Empyreal Order

Ruled by Ikasha, She Who Sleeps
“In shadow and silence, all things move.”

Motion, desire, and performance — the forces that drive and transform.

Empyreal Patrons offer flexibility and passion. Their Rites reward you but rarely the way you expected; their costs are moderate and their demands are emotional. The price is vulnerability: they ask you to feel, to want, to risk.

Ikasha, She Who Sleeps — The Hush Before the Choice

Throne I 5 Rites Gift: Ikasha's Whisper

Ikasha is the hush between footfalls, the patience of dark water, the black-feathered watcher at every threshold. In stillness she gathers what might be, at crossroads she whispers of what may yet come.

  • Latent Potential: the power of what is not yet, the seed of action, the whisper before the scream
  • Shadow as Shelter: concealment, secrets, and the spaces between notice
  • Thresholds of Change: doors, crossroads, dreams, and the boundaries of sleep
  • Raven's Witness: omens, messages, and the weight of secrets shared

Guises

  • The Deep Listener — Aeler deep workings. A dark recess is left beside the place where a shift reports what it encountered. The speaker names the working, not the thing that may be listening. A keeper can preserve a report their superior refused to hear. They cannot demonstrate that everything omitted from the official account has therefore reached a benevolent listener.
  • The Bell-Shrouded — Mistland bell-lines. A cloth is laid across a bell that should remain silent that night. People attend to the road, the weather, and the person who has not come home. They do not explain the sleep beneath the custom. A sound from under the cloth is not automatically an invitation to remove it.
  • The Tide-Watcher — Zakov's night crossings. A raven on a dark post marks a place where people wait without lighting the landing. Some seek passage, others news of a vessel. A message can arrive in a familiar voice from an unoccupied boat. The keepers ask what it said before asking whom it sounded like, and seldom settle the second question.
  • The Listener Without a Roll — Ashaan's concealed households. The Unlit Candle reaches people afraid to say a name where an Esoiti may hear it. A whispered plea can be an act of resistance without becoming a reliable escape route. Black Hand agents, the magi, and those hiding from them do not form one interest merely because they all value secrecy. An answer that names a danger does not promise protection from it.
  • The Keeper of the Unspoken Page — Sihai's private reading rooms. Some keepers preserve a blank leaf between copied passages and remember why it was left there. Others insist this is a local archive practice mistakenly attributed to Ikasha by foreigners. The disagreement has not prevented a whispered question from being heard in a room whose occupants all believed they were alone.

Provenance

Her public cults inherit accounts of the Sleeper rather than a complete history of why she sleeps.

Some prayers ask that Ikasha remain undisturbed; others that she hear without waking. The difference has produced arguments among people who have received answers neither can explain. Temple compilers have tried to turn these fragments into a continuous divine biography. Her keepers preserve the fragments, including incompatible ones, and generally decline to endorse the finished account.

Night workers, travelers, and households concealing a persecuted guest carried the Unlit Candle's observances across borders. The object is small enough to hide and ordinary enough to explain. Along Zakov's crossings, waiting for a boat gave the Sleeper a maritime title. In Ashaan, people facing the removal of names found a listener who did not demand that a public office first recognize the speaker. That is a reason for the cult's appeal, not an immunity to erasure.

Keeping a secret can shelter someone or preserve a wrong. The Unlit Candle's houses disagree about when testimony must be brought into daylight. Some have saved people whose names they never recorded; others have enabled a protector to control every account of what happened inside. A listener who never asks whether the speaker may leave has made silence serve a different purpose.

Preserve the boundary around Ikasha's deeper history. These local beliefs do not disclose a hidden cosmological identity, resolve the origin of her sleep, or make every raven, shadow, or absent name her deliberate intervention.

A Place to Speak Quietly

Ikasha offers the possibility of being heard without being displayed. What hears is not necessarily accountable to the person who needed that shelter.

Her devotees can confuse secrecy with safety. A person protected from an official's scrutiny may become dependent on a keeper whose conduct nobody else can examine.

The useful distinction is between controlling access to one's own account and giving someone else the only surviving version. Silence chosen in danger need not become silence owed for life.

Introduce an answer that can be checked in part: a location, a name, or a detail of an event. Leave the answering presence uncertain. Let characters decide whom to trust with what they learn rather than forcing revelation or concealment as the only faithful response.

Raéyn — Mistress of the Sea — Tides & Change

Throne II 5 Rites Gift: Promise of Safe Passage

She arrives. That is the first thing and most of the rest of it.

  • Tides and Currents: change, opportunity, the rhythm of the sea
  • Maritime Trade and Travel: safe passage, navigation, weather
  • Storm and Calm: the duality of protection and wrath
  • Motherhood and Loss: fierce love, grief for Khemesh, the weight of a divided heart

Guises

  • The Tide-Mother — Linn fishing settlements. She is carved with a boat held against her ribs, its occupants too small to distinguish. Boat families hang patched mittens beside the image, including those belonging to people brought home dead. The same hymn can accompany a launch and a burial. A captain who calls survival proof of her affection is likely to be corrected by someone preparing the second ceremony.
  • The Storm-Queen — Kahfagian harbors. Shipping houses prefer a crowned figure above the harbor chain. Their gifts commemorate named rescues and voyages; dockside shrines often remove the donors' painted arms when salt has eaten through them. Pilots ask for passage. House chaplains sometimes speak as though the sea has recognized an exclusive charter. The pilots rarely repeat that language outside the breakwater.
  • The Salt-Widow — The Brass Coast of the Amaranthine. Her veil is made from sailcloth and replaced by the households that washed the last one. Services count the returned separately from the missing, leaving room for a boat to come in after its funeral. This is the Brass Coast observance; it should not be relocated to Dhahara's Bronze Coast because the names sound alike.
  • The Lady Who Brings the Boat Within Sight — Sihai's coastal rescue shrines. A painted sea-lady stands beside wooden models of rescued craft. Coastal families brought the image home from foreign ports; local keepers adopted it into rites for safe return while retaining their own ancestors and shore spirits. Inland catalogues identify her with Raéyn more confidently than the keepers do. A family may use her imported name abroad and its harbor title at home.
  • The Guest Beyond the Breakwater — Nihori's outer islands. A place is left for her at a launch feast, facing outward. The shrine attends to the local kami before addressing the sea beyond their accustomed waters. Some crews treat Raéyn as a visiting sovereign; others as an unwelcome relative whom good manners may persuade to pass by. Neither makes her subject to a daimyo's permission.

Provenance

Her cult has many shores and no agreed first landing.

Linn singers say their grandmothers heard her before foreign ships possessed sails. Kahfagian temple histories begin with a rescued harbor founder. Sailcloth images on the Brass Coast preserve neither story intact. Raéyn has acquired crowns, veils, and maternal titles without becoming as consistent as any of them. A calm image beside a quay can represent a power whose next approach strips that quay bare.

Worship travels with pilots, rescued passengers, and the families of sailors who marry ashore. A household first hangs an unfamiliar image in thanks; a neighboring crew borrows its departure song; a prosperous sponsor builds a roof over the place. Only later does a priest arrive to explain which parts have been performed incorrectly. The Salt-Washed Circle carries practices between these shrines without settling their precedence.

Harbor authorities value a cult that can make rival crews share a service before they must share a channel. Its keepers also speak for the families left waiting. In some ports the memorial procession is permitted on the quay but forbidden to stop outside the shipping houses. There the argument about where to carry the image has become an argument about responsibility for a lost vessel.

Khemesh remains her son. Regional hymns omit, lament, or refuse that relationship; their silence does not turn him into an unrelated sea monster, and his hunger must not be assigned to every local tide spirit.

What Comes Back

The sea can return somebody without being kind to them. Raéyn's protectiveness is real and unreliable, and a rescue does not purchase a predictable next voyage.

A household may love her for the same intervention another household mourns. A changed current brings one boat into shelter and sends wreckage from another past its door. The dispute cannot be solved by announcing that both gifts were equally welcome.

The Salt-Washed have to decide whether gratitude permits a public complaint. Those who cannot speak of what was lost eventually find that only successful captains are heard at the shrine.

Use a returned ship whose captain wants a triumphal service while its missing crew's families want their names spoken first. A new omen can make departure urgent without telling anyone which account Raéyn prefers.

Thrysos, King of Revels — Ecstasy & Excess

Throne III 6 Rites Gift: Thrysos's Blessing

Where torches flare and cups spill, where dancers stamp until dawn, there strides Thrysos crowned with ivy and horn. He is both host and beast, a patron of license who laughs at law.

  • Ecstatic Release: dance, intoxication, the shedding of inhibition (and clothing)
  • Masked Freedom: identity as performance, truth through disguise, the thrill of the anonymous encounter
  • Feast and Famine: excess, appetite, lust, and the cost of indulgence
  • Ritual Frenzy: communal ecstasy that transforms participants into something feral and beautiful

Guises

  • The Twice-Welcomed Guest — Thepyrgos's performance houses. His horned image is brought in for the public festival and out again for the smaller feast after the formal audience leaves. The second company disputes what the first performance was permitted to say. Some actors keep the same mask for both occasions, making a patron hear their own praise in a less comfortable voice.
  • The Green Host — Vhasian courts. A noble lends a hall to the Horned Circle and expects an evening's release from rank. The servants still prepare the food and carry away the cups. An observance that permits the lord to be mocked but leaves everyone else at work has released less than it announces. Some hosts discover too late that the revelers noticed.
  • The Storm-Dancer — Linn feast halls. Drums and feet keep time against the wind outside. Participation is valued for the willingness to be seen joining, not because the people are fearless. A person sick with nerves can be welcomed into the dance. One who wishes to stop may find the company's encouragement difficult to distinguish from a command.
  • The Revel-King — Zakov waterfront gatherings. His sign is painted on a cup passed among crews who cannot be trusted together at a sober negotiation. Temporary fellowship can survive the night and sometimes does. It can also put tomorrow's betrayal within easy reach of someone who learned exactly whom a rival loves while everyone was singing.
  • The Unbounded Guest — Aelinnel guest observances. The familiar title is also used of Mab, but these gatherings honor Thrysos through interruption, shared rhythm, and the loosening of ordinary sequence. Mab's welcome is a different authority. A keeper who invites either by a title alone should know which observance they are opening; the guests need not agree that the mistake was slight.

Provenance

An old reveling cult whose public festivals and less governable gatherings have repeatedly exchanged places.

Thepyric histories preserve civic performances beside accounts of a horned guest who would not remain within the appointed hour. After Astroegro's fall, performance households could carry a mask and songs when they could not carry a sanctuary. Their descendants disagree over whether the restrained civic celebration is a domesticated revel or the private gathering a departure from an older discipline.

Actors, musicians, sailors, and hosts carried observances between ports. A company might be hired because its foreign festival was fashionable and discover that local people heard a different possibility in its temporary freedoms. Noble sponsors adopted the masks and tried to decide who could wear them. Waterfront gatherings dispensed with some formalities while developing their own fierce expectations about who must join.

The Horned Circle's divisions are not simply sober authority against joyful liberation. A licensed festival can protect performers who could not safely speak outside it; its boundaries can also ensure nothing changes after the masks come off. An unlicensed gathering can create real solidarity and become intolerant of the person who will not surrender themselves to it. Thrysos has room for both feast and frenzy.

Do not turn intoxication, erotic play, or violent ecstasy into compulsory player participation. A cult may exert pressure within the fiction; the table still controls its boundaries. The historical practices of a real reveling religion do not establish consent rules for this fictional Patron.

Who Is Free at the Feast

Thrysos offers a shared release from the selves people are required to perform. The company can make that release another required performance.

A person may want to be carried by a song without surrendering the right to step outside it. The host who cannot bear an empty place in the dance has begun confusing everybody's joy with their own authority over the gathering.

What remains afterward matters. A night that makes an impossible friendship possible has changed something. A night whose least powerful guests must suffer the consequences alone has distributed release unevenly.

Give a festival a host, people doing the work, and a consequence that survives morning. A genuine revelation or attachment can emerge without requiring every reveler to lose control. Let leaving, staying, and interrupting carry different possibilities.

Mab, Queen of Courts — Glamour & Bargain

Throne IV 5 Rites Gift: Mab's Jest

Mab keeps the Hazel Court, and she keeps it the way a very good hostess does: by arranging things so that no one can say afterward at what moment they became a guest. She does not command.

  • Fae Glamour: illusions, enchantments, the art of seeming
  • Social Bargains: promises, invitations, and what accepting them permits
  • Courtly Intrigue: etiquette, wordplay, the game of masks
  • Trickster Arts: mischief, cleverness, the twist in the tale

Guises

  • The Unbounded Guest — Aelinnel gatherings. A place is added after the seating has been agreed. Its plate need not be touched for the other guests to begin addressing it. Etiquette manuals disagree about who should have announced the visitor; experienced hosts attend to the person now listening. The same title is used for Thrysos elsewhere, but a displaced cup and a request for an encore do not open the same evening.
  • The Gilded Tongue — Vilikari salons. Mab's likeness has an open mouth and a closed fan. A compliment is repeated by a second speaker before negotiations begin, so the parties cannot later disagree about its wording. They can still disagree considerably about what it acknowledged. The finest salon victories are remembered as sentences, with their context retold differently by each household.
  • The Hazel Hostess — Theona and Ubral. Theonan households may offer a visitor a room while carefully leaving the length of the visit unsaid. An Ubral host is more likely to name the relation under which the visitor has been received, leaving an argument about that relation to the household. Neither custom is uniform. Returning guests can find the same hostess remembers a welcome their descendants consider ancient.
  • The Lady of the Unclosed Fan — Nihori's court entertainments. Traveling companies introduced a masked hostess into entertainments about invitations accepted too readily. A few troupes began dedicating a performance to Mab rather than merely representing her. Shrine attendants distinguish the play's announced conclusion from promises made during the gathering around it. An audience can leave the performance correctly and still have an unfinished visit.

Provenance

Mab keeps the Hazel Court. Mortal accounts describe encounters with it; they do not establish when it began or make its Queen a former human sovereign.

Family histories preserve an invitation beside deeds and marriage settlements because something important followed the evening it opened. The older paper may identify neither host nor destination. A later owner has sometimes received a fresh invitation in a hand that matches it exactly. Archivists can compare the hands, identify the paper, and establish which relative returned. They cannot make the missing part of the visit appear by putting the documents in order.

Mab's observances traveled with tutors in manners, household servants, actors, and people whose place at a foreign court depended on being welcome. The Vilikari developed elaborate repetitions of offered compliments. Theona and Ubral retained more household variation. Eastern players took the figure of the hostess across the water, where an established discipline of announced performance gave her gatherings a different set of forms to exploit.

The Gilded Jest recruits people who know that admission matters. A servant admitted as a witness may hear what a lord outside the door cannot. A displaced heir may be received under a rank their own kin refuse to recognize. Mab's favor can create a real opening for such people. It can also leave them dependent on a welcome that remains difficult to explain to anyone who was not present.

Hollow Crown practitioners preserve examples of wording rather than an authoritative life of the Queen. Rival schools accuse each other of treating a successful answer as a formula. Mab has heard the formula before. Her inability to lie does not prevent her from receiving its speaker under circumstances in which every word has acquired a less comfortable use.

Who Had the Right to Welcome You

A welcome changes the relation between host and guest. Mab's followers prosper by noticing that change before the other people in the room do.

A household may benefit from having received someone and later claim that its servant had no authority to open the door. The servant remembers the instruction precisely. Mab can attend very kindly to the discrepancy without taking the weaker person's side for their own sake.

The most unsettling correction may be a gracious acceptance. Mab agrees that the servant could not have welcomed a guest, thanks the household for explaining, and begins considering what sort of visitor she therefore was. Nothing she has said is false.

Establish the actual welcome and who spoke it before play. Give witnesses separate interests in how it is remembered. A servant's testimony, the fulfillment of a limited promise, or an offered farewell can resolve the immediate claim; do not add a new condition merely because the players found a workable answer.

Livaea — The Crimson Courtier — Seduction & Social Binding

In salons where laughter cuts deeper than daggers and promises bind tighter than chains, the Crimson Courtier reigns.

  • Seductive Arts: charm, persuasion, reading desire, emotional manipulation
  • Social Binding: oaths of affection, courtly promises, emotional debts
  • Performance and Glamour: dance, music, appearance enhancement, the weapons of the stage and salon

Guises

Some houses refuse exclusive commissions because they have seen the person wearing one treated as another object belonging to the buyer.
  • The Seat Beside the Winner — Zakov's gambling rooms. The most desirable place is reserved before anyone knows who will win. Livaea's attendants learn which person wants victory, which wants to be seen with the victor, and which is afraid of being sent home. They need not offer all three the same inducement.

Provenance

Livaea is a demon of seduction and social binding. Her place among Empyreal Patrons describes how Invokers classify her power, not a mortal court's ability to civilize her.

The Velvet Circle's surviving objects are intimate: miniatures, perfume vessels, letters folded for a particular hand. Public inventories often describe them as gifts of affection. The correspondence that survives beside them tells a longer story of rooms lent, debts assumed, and access withdrawn. Collectors who isolate the beautiful object from that history reproduce the Courtier's preferred version of the gift.

Silkstrand's dye houses and private entertainers carried crimson signs through the same ports that carried their work. Zakov's gaming households turned a personal invitation into a place at the table. Vhasian matchmakers adapted the miniature, and foreign companies in Sihai placed recognition in a borrowed song. None of these trades belongs wholly to Livaea. Her devotees gain influence by making their own assistance seem indispensable to an ordinary livelihood.

The Circle offers entry to people excluded by birth, money, or reputation. It can introduce a talented performer to a real audience and put a disregarded adviser beside a ruler. Its cost develops through the relationship that made the opening possible. A beneficiary who once needed to be chosen may discover that every new opportunity must still pass through the original chooser.

Attempts to restrain the Circle have sometimes punished the visible entertainer while leaving the influential patron untouched. In response, some houses keep witnesses to commissions, allow performers to refuse private engagements, or separate a gift from a claim to continued access. The Circle can oppose those protections, imitate them, or cultivate the person entrusted to enforce them. The contest is over who controls the route by which another person is wanted.

The Privilege of Being Necessary

Livaea cultivates a need that returns to its maker. Admiration becomes a means of arranging another person's choices.

A devotee can be lonely, generous, and manipulative at the same time. They may have supplied every kindness they now invoke as proof that the recipient should not leave. The accuracy of their memory makes the demand more difficult to answer.

Dependence turns back on the one who cultivates it. A patron who has made themselves necessary must continually produce a reason to remain so. Somebody else's recovery or independent success begins to feel like a threat.

A benefactor offers to clear a troupe's debts if its leading performer accepts a permanent private engagement. The performer wants the company saved and has plans elsewhere. Give fellow performers differing wishes and other, costly sources of support. Attraction, gratitude, and supernatural influence do not determine a player's consent or settle the character's entire future.

Palinode, Queen of Encores — Performance & Rapture

Throne VI 6 Rites Gift: Palinode's Blessing

Once a road-cantor who swore to reconcile feuding towns with procession and song, Palinode died between feasts with a chorus unfinished.

  • Ecstatic Performance: music, dance, theater, and the rapture of creation
  • Unfinished Art: the power of the incomplete, the encore that never ends
  • Audience Binding: captivating crowds, turning spectators into participants
  • Inspired Craft: works that transcend their maker's intention

Guises

  • The Unfinished Muse — Thepyrgos's choral schools. Her image stands with one hand raised to begin the next phrase. Students copy rival versions of the refrain said to have survived her death. A master may forbid its final cadence out of reverence, while an older singer says there never was a final cadence to forbid. The best schools teach the differences before asking anyone to attempt the piece.
  • The Cantor Between the Feasts — Vhasian procession companies. Two small tables are carried at the end of a reconciliatory procession. Neither is set for a sovereign. They recall the towns Palinode intended to bring together, though rival companies disagree over who first refused the meal. During a difficult reconciliation, the bearers can end up standing between people who have learned the procession perfectly and still will not share food.
  • The Voice After the Caller — Oshiiran landing crews. A work-song repeats its caller's last phrase once after the crew has stopped. Returning performers associated the custom with Palinode; older boat workers insist the response belonged to the landing before those performers were born. The dedication has nevertheless taken root. A saved refrain can keep an absent worker remembered, or keep a crew at work after the caller has promised they could stop.
  • The Lady Beyond the Bow — Nihori's traveling stages. A company announces its conclusion, removes its masks, and only then permits a request for another piece. Palinode's devotees are drawn to the interval. A request can be graciously accepted or refused under ordinary custom, but a singer favored by the Queen may hear applause continuing after everybody else has gone. Fellow players know to ask whether their companion has eaten before praising the inspiration.

Provenance

Palinode was a road-cantor who swore to reconcile feuding towns. She died between their feasts with a chorus unfinished, and the refrain continued beyond her life.

The rival Feast Accounts preserve different opening speeches, different lists of gifts, and the same place where the surviving music will not close. Later companies have claimed that their founder carried the missing verse away. Some claims are commercial inventions; others have preserved music older than the company making them. Establishing that a verse is old does not establish that it can finish what Palinode began.

A refrain travels without a relic chest. Hired singers took it from civic reconciliations into work crews and festival stages, changing the breath length and response to suit new listeners. Oshiiran landing songs preserved a rhythm a conservatory edition discarded. Nihori's companies gave the encore an explicit place beyond the formal bow. Such adaptations carried the Queen's reach farther than any agreed account of the original towns.

People have used her songs to meet when speech alone had failed. The first shared response can be an achievement worth preserving. The Gasping Chorus becomes dangerous when it treats that achievement as something no participant may subsequently leave. Its throat-bells and exchanged fragments belong to an inner discipline, not to every worker or theatergoer who knows a favored tune.

Civic authorities have tried to acquire the Queen by acquiring her company. A permanent grant supplies rehearsal rooms and binds the annual performance to a sponsor's celebration. The performance may then remember a reconciliation the sponsor no longer intends to honor. Whether to omit the offending verse divides singers who all need the grant to continue working.

A Refrain Outliving Its Promise

Palinode gives shared work an existence larger than its maker. The work can survive while the people making it lose the ability to stop.

An audience may love a singer without hearing the cost of the next song. A performer may interpret that appetite as the only proof that the company still belongs together. Both can prefer another encore to the conversation that should follow it.

Some companies preserve an incomplete copy beside the version they perform. Its absence acknowledges that the work has a history beyond their authority. Others secretly keep the same copy because they no longer trust themselves to recognize an ending.

Two communities will share a feast only if a particular singer performs the old reconciliation. The singer can still produce the music but cannot hear when it stops. The party can recover witnesses, share the performance, change its occasion, or help the singer refuse. Let the reconciliation require conduct outside the song as well as a successful performance.

Aveh, the Rider Behind the Storm — The Freedom That Forgets Your Name

Throne VII 5 Rites Gift: Aveh's Unbinding

Aveh is the faceless rider at the horizon's edge, neither man nor woman, both and neither. The Ykrul call them Aveh, a liminal spirit beyond hearth and vow, a storm-shadow that offers liberty at the cost of belonging.

  • Freedom from Bonds: escape, liberation, shedding constraints
  • Erasure: becoming forgotten, removing traces, vanishing from memory
  • Liminal Identity: non-conforming, genderless, between categories
  • Storm as Shelter: the chaos that hides and the wind that carries away

Guises

  • The Rider Without a Pole — Western Ykrul border camps. A scrap of cloth is flown where a banner should be. People fleeing a household or a commander look for it; those searching for them do too. Its keepers speak of departure without requiring an oath to remain together. Some former guests return years later with gifts. Others cannot remember which camp received them.
  • The Name Left in the Rain — Eastern steppe caravan margins. Aveh is spoken of with changing pronouns, or only as the rider. Guides resent merchants who translate that variation into a fixed secret identity. A wet cord tied where a name-token hung marks an abandoned allegiance. It may belong to someone escaping cruelty or to someone whose dependants will now have to answer for a missing rider.
  • The Unposted Rider — Sihai's unregistered roads. Official descriptions call the cult a method of evasion. People between names call it a way to reach the next town. An Unbannered shelter may contain examination brushes beside saddle gear. Some guests seek new registration as soon as they are safe. Aveh's most devoted followers regard the replacement file with the same unease the guest once felt toward the old one.
  • The One Who Will Not Be Called Back — The approaches to Ashaan. Stories circulate among caravan hands of a rider crossing a storm with people hidden in its wake. The stories disagree about which of those people arrived. Aveh does not become an agent of the Black Hand or a reliable cure for the Maeri's erasures merely because escape and lost names occur in both accounts. A rescuer who promises everyone their old name back is promising beyond the cult's knowledge.

Provenance

A cult encountered repeatedly among people leaving attachments; no surviving list can establish its first company.

The Unbannered resist genealogies. Their own witnesses can remember a shared escape and disagree about who rode beside them. Eastern and western steppe accounts preserve different marks and riding songs, while both insist that Aveh was already there when a pursued traveler first needed the storm. That is a belief about the Patron's availability, not evidence that all those escapes happened in one migration.

Caravan workers brought temporary refuges into towns where a rider could no longer disappear over a ridge. A spare sleeping place behind a stable became as useful as a hidden remount. In Sihai, the practice developed around gaps between postings and households unable to prove whom they had received. Those practical accommodations never became a stable roster. An organization that could identify every person it had sheltered would have built exactly what many of them fled.

That absence creates its own injuries. A family seeking a lost child may hear the same refusal as a violent employer seeking a fugitive. Some helpers retain private signs by which a guest can later find them; more absolute devotees destroy even these. They disagree over whether a way back is an act of care or the beginning of another tether.

Storm imagery can cause outsiders to place Aveh beside Kuva in a single sky family. No such kinship is established. Kuva witnesses a spoken attachment; Aveh can make its former bearer increasingly difficult to find.

Freedom After the Farewell

Aveh offers release from claims that have become intolerable. The danger is that a person is made of attachments as well as restrictions.

A departure can be necessary even when someone grieves it. The Unbannered become cruel when they treat grief as proof that the attachment deserved erasure, or accuse a rescued person of cowardice for wanting a settled home afterward.

Their best kindness may be to help someone leave their company. The Patron's attention does not necessarily leave with them. Familiar people begin hesitating before a name; an old joke receives a polite smile from someone who once told it.

Let a former guest seek proof of a rescue so they can reclaim an ordinary place in a household. The witnesses want to help but cannot agree whom they remember. Do not make remaining permanently unbound the only valid outcome.

Morag the Hag — Faerie Bargains & Exact Terms

Throne VIII 5 Rites Gift: The Plain Clause

She is the grandmother, and she is not a sweet one. You come to her in the hour between twilight and dawn because you have made a mess of something and everyone kinder has run out of ideas. She lets you in.

  • Declared Costs: hearing the whole bargain before choosing
  • Faerie Cunning: strict courtesy, sharp clauses, old road law
  • Hearth-Way Wisdom: folk practice for spirits, thresholds, and natural justice
  • Consequential Magic: power that always arrives with strings attached

Guises

  • The Kettle-Crone — Aelaerem kitchens and boundary dwellings. An extra bowl is put beside the stove when someone intends to seek her. The person can eat before deciding. Morag's hospitality and the bargain that follows are different acts, and the food need not be made sinister to make the terms dreadful. A house that warns its visitor properly may still be hoping they will say yes.
  • The Crossroads Wife — Viterran Hedges. She receives a title appropriate to a respectable married woman and a seat slightly apart from the household saints. Petitioners come when law, kinship, and available money have failed to produce what they need. A local broker may arrange the meeting, but Morag states the terms herself. A broker promising a kinder private interpretation is the unreliable party.
  • The Tide-Mother — Zakov's hidden landings. Smuggling households address her before asking for concealment, passage, or someone returned. The maritime title also belongs to Raéyn in other regions. Zakov's keepers distinguish a bargain with the woman by the cauldron from the moods of the sea. A traveler who confuses them may arrive prepared for a libation when a decision is required.
  • The Wife at the Last Hearth — Ubral household thresholds. She is spoken of where ordinary hospitality has been exhausted. A family may remember who went to her before the birth of its oldest living member and decline to repeat the terms in front of a guest. That reserve protects the family's privacy; it does not mean Morag withheld the cost from the person who accepted it.

Provenance

Her names have accumulated around meetings whose locations seldom remain useful directions to the next petitioner.

Aelaerem accounts often begin with a meal; Viterran accounts with a petition nobody else would hear; Zakov stories with a boat returning where no boat should have reached. Later tellers like to introduce a clever concealed clause. The Tarnished Cauldron's keepers object to those versions. They insist the frightening part was that the petitioner heard the cost plainly and accepted it anyway.

The cult traveled through returned petitioners and households affected by their bargains. A family carrying a gift into a new region might preserve Morag's old title long after it no longer described the local country. Neighbors adopted the title, a meeting custom, or the practice of having the terms repeated before witnesses. Morag herself does not need to have followed the same road as the people who learned to ask for her.

Intermediaries grew influential because need is often private. Some helped people understand an offer without deciding for them. Others learned exactly whom to bring to the crossroads and which ordinary remedy to leave unmentioned. They do not have to falsify Morag's words to exploit a desperate person. The existence of her honest bargain can support a thoroughly dishonest business around access to it.

Mab, Inaea, and Morag should remain distinct. Mab arranges true statements within inescapable courtesy; Inaea arranges enduring relationships. Morag names what will be taken. Her terms are not made harmless by being clear, and a mortal agent's misconduct does not imply that Morag lied.

Knowing What You Agree To

A stated cost permits an informed decision. It does not make every available decision fair, nor make a desperate person free of the circumstances that brought them.

Her followers divide over what a witness owes the petitioner. One repeats the terms exactly and considers the task complete. Another asks whether someone could help without the bargain. Morag can tolerate the question more easily than the intermediary whose standing depends on the meeting.

The ordeal belongs to the accepted loss and the person who must live after receiving the gift. A hidden extra price usually weakens it by letting the petitioner become only a victim of deceit.

Make the terms and the desired outcome clear before acceptance. Give the party an ordinary alternative that is genuinely difficult, not secretly impossible. Honor a refusal. Once a bargain is accepted, let its actual consequences carry the horror rather than adding a clause afterward.

Grimmir, the Old Man of the Forest — Blood at Midwinter

Throne IX 6 Rites Gift: Grimmir's Blessing

Grimmir is the Old Man of the Forest, the Alder King, the presence that watches from the shadow of the oldest oak, the cold breath on the back of the neck when the path grows dark.

  • Seasonal Sacrifice: the old ways of blood and offering, the turning wheel that demands payment
  • Folk Horror: the terror of the unseen, the ritual that must be performed, the boundary that must not be crossed
  • Primal Mystery: knowledge hidden in root and stone, the language of omens and portents
  • Growth from Death: the forest floor as a stomach; decay as the engine of new life

Guises

  • The Alder King — Valewood margins. A household leaves the oldest tree out of its account of what may be cut. The wood around it can be worked, argued over, and inherited; the tree is addressed differently. A newcomer who asks which lord owns it may receive a useful answer about every other tree on the slope. Nobody supplies a genealogy to make that answer easier.

Provenance

His cults remember boundaries and encounters more readily than a first foundation.

The Root-Moot preserves a tradition of masked gatherings beneath old trees. Its songs change with the country and often refuse a sequence that would turn them into a king's life. Some singers say Grimmir was waiting when their people arrived. Others say he has always belonged to the place and find the distinction unnecessary. A temple catalogue that gives him one birthplace has obtained a neater answer than the keepers provide.

Migrants took a branch, a mask, or a season's refrain into new country. The observance did not always settle there unchanged. An Aeler household finding roots beneath a new threshold could recognize the Root-Father without possessing its neighbor's Thorn-Father custom. Hunters on the steppe translated a figure encountered at the forest edge into the Sky-Wolf following the herds. These recognitions are not proof that every landscape has agreed to the same terms.

Landholders adopted the cult where an older boundary constrained a claim. Some observed it faithfully. Others offered an impressive new sanctuary while clearing the place people had actually avoided. The quarrel is not always between ignorant laborers and an enlightened protector of nature: a family may need fuel, a proprietor may need a boundary moved, and the thing in the uncut ground has not explained its interest to either.

Keep Grimmir distinct from local spirits, the Ukwe's powers, and the Couatl. A shared concern with living country does not reduce Sekogo or every forest people to worshippers of the Alder King. Nor does a rural title turn him into a safe agricultural benefactor.

What the Clearing Leaves Outside

Grimmir's followers live by taking from country that has other inhabitants. Respect is not a guarantee that the taking will be permitted.

It is tempting to turn him into the spokesman for whatever management plan seems best. That gives a human argument an authority the Patron has not supplied. A careful forester may still meet something their careful practice cannot placate.

The useful humility is to acknowledge a limit without pretending to understand all of it. A boundary can be observed for generations while its keeper remains unable to explain what stands on the other side.

Put a needed resource across an old limit. Establish observable warnings and consequences, but do not require a speech in which the Alder King explains the correct policy. Negotiating with the people affected should remain meaningful even when the supernatural question remains open.

Maelstraeus — The Infernal Bargainer — Commerce & Exchange

Maelstraeus is the Infernal Bargainer, the Prince of Ledgers, the shadow at every crossroads of exchange. He was born from the first inequity, when one gave and another took, and debt was chained into existence.

  • Commerce as Conflict: negotiation, leverage, hidden clauses
  • Debt as Power: obligation as currency, interest as weapon
  • Inevitability: all accounts are settled, sooner or later
  • Infernal Bargains: Faustian pacts, soul ledgers, cursed credit

Guises

  • The Coin-House Factor — Ecktoria's coin houses. A little figure holds an open hand above a scale whose pans cannot both be seen from the doorway. Partners examine the image differently from customers. Devotees admire the advantage obtained by arranging the exchange, while ordinary clerks may invoke the Factor anxiously before handling a bargain they did not choose. The same desk can hold both practices.

Provenance

The received account places Maelstraeus at the first inequity of exchange. His cult's commercial histories concern the institutions built around that appetite, not a proof that every exchange belongs to him.

The Gilded Ledger preserves the successions of houses that survived ruin elsewhere. Their records favor the partner who remained solvent. A second account can survive in a workshop, a cargo dispute, or the recollection of a family that supplied labor instead of coin. The cult calls such comparisons a fuller accounting when they expose a rival and an intrusion when they expose its own advantage.

Agents carried the Factor's mark between Ecktoria's coin houses and Silkstrand's credit networks. As undertakings extended beyond a single journey, an intermediary could acquire more power than either buyer or seller. Smugglers on the Brass Coast adopted the same figure under a name suited to a shorter, more dangerous exchange. Pilgrimage markets offered another opportunity: a journey made for devotion still required food, shelter, and someone willing to explain the road.

The cult supplies useful connections. A distant household can be paid, a workshop financed, a shipment moved through a closed port. Its servants gain influence by ensuring that the participants cannot readily obtain those connections elsewhere. The infernal addition appears when a successful undertaking becomes the reason nobody can leave the arrangement that made it possible.

Some merchants oppose the cult precisely because predictable terms and durable trust matter to their work. Others think they can employ its agents for one exceptional deal. Maelstraeus is willing to be treated as the exception. The hidden surcharge is most effective when its beneficiary has already begun treating the first success as proof of their own prudence.

The Bargain After the Bargain

Maelstraeus wants the exchange to establish the need for another exchange on terms that favor him.

The promise of a single rescue can conceal the loss of ordinary alternatives. Once those alternatives have gone, even an apparently fair offer is made in a room whose doors somebody else has closed.

A gift freely given troubles his servants because they cannot decide where the claim has been hidden. Some respond by inventing one and damaging the relationship themselves. Their suspicion can accomplish what no infernal clause was needed to require.

A contract has genuinely saved a workshop and now gives its intermediary control over every future commission. Put the work completed, the terms, and the available exit costs in evidence. A hidden charge should have discoverable traces and established scope; it is not permission for the GM to invent a new liability whenever the party pays the last one.

Provenance

Roadside worship whose communities disagree about whether it began with a first journey or the first person received after one.

Caravan genealogies preserve the names of guides who carried the Traveler's observances across several languages. They do not establish a birthplace for the Patron. An old stage can outlast the city it served, and a blessing can retain the name of a destination no living speaker can locate. Keepers sometimes preserve that name precisely because they no longer know where else it could survive.

The cult spread through useful acts: a shelter kept open, a ford marked after flood, a stranger escorted to the next known fire. In the Mistlands, imported ceremonies were reduced to what could be done without delaying a crossing. Nobody agreed that this made the shorter observance inferior.

The Cult of the Unbroken Way gathers at crossings rather than establishing a single central sanctuary. Households may feed those gatherings or maintain a travelers' room without claiming to house the Patron. The cult's name must be distinguished from the separate Patron called the Unbroken Way: endurance along a chosen course and finding a passage are not interchangeable promises.

Roadkeepers argue about flight. Some shelters refuse pursuit at the threshold; others notify a guest's kin before morning. Neither position guarantees that the next person seeking passage is innocent or that the person pursuing them is just.

The Next Place

A road offers somewhere else. It does not promise that leaving is a cure, or that arrival releases the traveler from what happened before.

The Lantern-Bearer is easiest to praise from a well-provisioned doorway. For the person who must abandon the familiar, a route is a gift with an uncertain destination. For the people who remain, the same departure may be a rescue, desertion, or the loss of their only support.

Good roadkeeping makes choice possible without pretending to make the choice for a guest. A guide who conceals the return path because departure would be best has begun deciding more than the Traveler asked.

Give the party a passage and two people with different reasons to use it. A shrine can provide directions, witnesses, or shelter under ordinary hospitality; none of this grants an unpurchased transport power or makes a dangerous road automatically safe.

Myrmis, the Bloody-Handed Matron — Sacrifice & Revelation

  • The Bloody Arithmetic: war as a calculus of costs and gains
  • Hidden Justice: the cult that judges what the law cannot reach

Guises

  • The Bloody-Handed Matron — The Northern Earls. Her oldest continuous worship, in the hills and highlands between Ubral and Viterra. The Earls are hill-king stock and keep her the way they keep everything — quietly, at home, and not in front of the Crown. Among the Renards she is a grandmother rather than a goddess: consulted before a feud, blamed after it, and never mentioned to a bishop.

Faith kept in secret becomes bookkeeping: the Mound speaks of what she is owed and when she will collect, and has largely stopped speaking of her. It has simply moved on, which she would recognise as a kind of ending and might even approve of.

Provenance

Thepyric, of the Astroegro imperial period.

She is a southern goddess. Astroegro was a war-empire before it was a cautionary tale, and Myrmis is what its war looked like when it was still working — the drill and the ledger and the burning village, held together in one pair of hands and called a virtue. When the last emperor broke his covenant and the empire went into the water, most of its gods went with it. Hers did not. Her cult walked.

The Earls say she crawled north, which is their phrase and is not meant literally — it is how highlanders describe something that arrives slowly, from below, and is in the house before anyone agrees to have let it in. What actually travelled was worship: veterans, camp-followers, a handful of masks, and a practice of putting a stone down afterward. It took root in the Viterran Highlands because the Earls had a use for it and no bishop close enough to object.

A goddess who counts the dead is portable in a way a goddess of imperial victory is not. Astroegro's Myrmis was the patron of an army that won; what survived the empire was the half of her that knew what winning cost, which is exactly the half a hill country full of feuding earls could use. She lost an empire and kept the arithmetic.

Thepyrgos remembers her the way a university remembers a discipline it no longer teaches: there are proofs with her name on them and nobody left who prays.

The Two Hands

Myrmis holds war with both hands and refuses to put either down. The Red Hand is war as it is suffered: wrath, the thing that happens to people, the scream and the burning thatch. The Cold Hand is war as it is made: a craft with tolerances, supply lines, siege geometry, a mind that can look at a valley and see how many days it feeds an army.

Every atrocity in the world's history was authored by someone holding one hand and disclaiming the other. The planner says he only drew the lines. The soldier says she only did as she was told. Both are lying, and both believe themselves. Myrmis will not let either of them put the other hand down: to take her Symbol is to accept that the plan and the scream are one act, and that you did it.

Then she counts the dead. The Cairn-Mother's grief is not softness bolted onto a war-goddess — it is what makes the arithmetic honest. A calculus that never tallies the cost is not a calculus, it is an excuse, and her devotees are required to walk the field afterward and put a stone on every one of them. Her cruelty and her mourning are the same discipline observed at different hours.

She is the patron for a character who intends to do something terrible on purpose and for a reason they can state out loud. She is wrong for a character who wants the reason without the doing, or the doing without the reckoning. When a player asks whether Myrmis approves, the question to hand back is always: and afterwards, who carries the stones?

Older than the taxonomy, or too singular to sit inside it.

These Patrons offer specialised, unorthodox power. Their Rites are often unique, their costs vary wildly, and their Acts of Service are cut to their own peculiar shape. The price is singularity: they ask you to accept their particular vision of the world. They are not lesser. Awenji are twin spirits — one of stone, one of gear — who dance together to keep the walking cities of Ngomebe alive. They were not born; they were carved.

Provenance

Ngomebe's founding account: the first Mason-Kin and the first Aeler vent-prior labored together until keystone and bearing fitted, and the twins emerged holding hands.

Neither craft tells the founding labor in quite the same order. Mason-Kin recitations begin with stone finding its shape; Aeler versions begin with the gear that made movement possible. Roller crews know a verse in which neither craft could hear the other over the work. These differences do not change the established account of a shared origin. They disclose which labor a later household wants honored first.

Artisans carried the twins into the Mountain Satrapies, where a joint workshop had to speak across Aeler and Dhaharan practices. The image proved portable because a lintel could display a partnership before either community agreed how to describe it. Returned apprentices brought altered songs home to Ngomebe. Joint dedications now cover arrangements as different as a pair of craftspeople, an extended household, and a workshop employing many people. None is necessarily a marriage. The habit of depicting only two hands can conceal how many others support the undertaking. In Ngomebe, attendants of the living carrier have begun asking where the person carrying the city stands in a picture that shows only the cutter and the engineer.

The question does not abolish the twins' achievement or solve the Weight. The cooperation is real, and so is the repeated absence of the carving Houses from the chamber. Awenji's devotees disagree over whether a third place at the dedication repairs that omission or merely gives it a more attractive ceremony.

Neither Complete Alone

Awenji join unlike work. Their lesson requires both partners to remain capable of disagreeing about the fit.

Trust becomes dependence without recourse when one side alone may declare the joint sound. Declaring every disagreement disloyal destroys the very second judgment that made the collaboration valuable.

The difficult third question is who bears the failure. Two masters can reach an honest agreement while passing the danger to a crew or carrier who was never invited to the bench.

Present a collaboration both principals genuinely want to preserve. Let evidence come from the people who operate what they built. Success can improve the partnership without proving that either craft secretly supplied all the knowledge.

Thepyrgos's collectors began preserving such objects as evidence of an older graceful company. The Gigglemoon offers something people seek for its own sake. Companionship can grow around an undignified song when a formal reconciliation has failed. Gaila's followers can recognize Thrysos's dancers and Palinode's singers as welcome company without treating their gatherings as the same observance. A Joy That Can Be Declined

Gaila's gift begins when delight passes between people. The host cannot establish that it was shared simply by making everyone smile.

An old joke can carry years of affection. It can also preserve the role assigned to a person who no longer enjoys playing it. A circle may miss their old companion sincerely while refusing to meet the one sitting before them.

There are observances in which the spoon is passed around twice. On the second circuit, anyone may put it down. The most skillful celebrants make that ending feel as welcome as the first laugh.

A town asks its former jester to resume a festival that once brought rival households together. The jester wants to help, but the piece everybody requests depends on humiliating someone now old enough to object. Give the replacement performance room to be genuinely funny; the answer need not be to abolish the feast or compel its old target to forgive.

Harrada exposes the violence in making a claim permanent. Her refusal to preserve a favored alternative makes her frightening even to those she has released.

  • Threshold of the Hollow: where the sky's gaze ends, the Hollow begins

  • The Open Witness — Kuvani camps of the eastern steppe. Milk is cast upward before the speakers take their places. A camp elder may call Kuva father, another may use no kinship word, and neither accepts a lord's claim to own the hearing. Witnesses stand beyond the speaker's household. A promise made only among dependants is regarded with suspicion: everyone present may have needed to agree.

A Kuvani visitor may recognize the upward offering and find almost everything said afterward peculiar.

Provenance

No single birthplace is accepted by the communities that invoke Kuva.

Kuvani genealogists put the first oath under open heaven before any camp had a king. Dhaharan temple recitations begin with rain striking an empty bell. Both traditions call the other an account of a later appearance. The oldest objects are less obliging: weathered rings, bowls without images, and bell fragments whose owners cannot be established. They support a long history of observance, not a decision about which people possessed the sky first.

Caravans carried names and gestures farther than priests could maintain doctrine. At stops along the Way of Silk, an upward libation could be recognized before the accompanying words were understood. Caravan households retained several versions for different journeys. Temple visitors later mistook those useful combinations for evidence of a single lost liturgy and began trying to reconstruct it.

A faith with no indispensable building could accompany people who had lost one. Settled courts nevertheless built roofless sanctuaries and claimed privileged access to Kuva's witness. Their platforms became places to announce irrigation settlements, royal marriages, and war vows. The Unclouded Eye disputes a sanctuary's right to exclude a petitioner from a sky everyone can see; its own elders still disagree about who is qualified to interpret an omen.

In Sihai, scholars sometimes classify Kuva with the powers of Heaven. Travelers using that classification should expect correction. A resemblance between an imperial diagram and a caravan prayer does not make Kuva an office held at the Emperor's pleasure.

The Sky Has No Throne

Kuva's worship returns to a difficult distinction: being witnessed does not establish that one is right. A victorious army and the village it burned can have sworn beneath the same clear sky.

Rulers prefer the interpretation in which fair weather endorses their intention. Guides know the same wind may have carried the smoke toward the people who could least escape it. Neither observation makes the sky answer a question it has not answered.

The costly discipline is to let another account remain audible. A camp that silences the widow before the oath has arranged a favorable hearing; it has not obtained a different sky.

Put an oath-taking beside testimony the host wishes to exclude. A storm can interrupt the meeting without identifying the truthful speaker. Let the party choose whom to hear and what they are prepared to swear before their neighbors.

Guises

  • The Cut Purse's Blessing — Silkstrand's street crews. A cap is passed after a tale of failure, and each listener adds the detail they would have noticed sooner. The useful story may belong to the youngest person there. Old crews tell a failed plan differently from a job in which a companion was deliberately left to answer for everybody. The latter usually ends the evening's laughter.
  • The Gorget-Breaker — Vhasia's dispossessed military households. A broken clasp from an old gorget hangs beside an ordinary house key. Some devotees rob former commanders; others recover a family's pledged goods and regard the recovery as no theft at all. Their household account is not necessarily the account of the servant who will be accused when the object disappears.
  • The Freedman's Luck — The Brass Coast of the Amaranthine. An opened wrist-ring is laid flat beneath the shrine's cup. People escaping bondage have carried this observance between ports. Its keepers argue about which proofs to take along: destroying a slave-holder's records can release a name from pursuit and destroy the only surviving evidence that somebody else was freed. A good escape requires knowing which household needs which document.
  • The Guest Who Paid in Change — Sihai's back lanes and night markets. A painted traveler tips a cap beneath a sign for good fortune. Respectable visitors read it as a hopeful household image. Those who know Jack recognize the empty key-hook painted behind the door. Returned porters and caravan guards brought his tales inland, replacing the foreign noble's purse with a sealed packet everyone in the market knew could not lawfully be opened.

Provenance

Thieves say Jack was mortal when he stole the Unspent Coin. Their stories disagree about its former owner, and none has produced that owner's reliable testimony.

The Cult of the Unlucky Coin preserves failure more carefully than triumph. An old story can identify a shutter, a household, and the price of a meal in a quarter that has since disappeared. Successful robberies acquire Jack's name so quickly that the original crew may be forgotten. The professionals who tell the story usually care more about whether its exit still exists than whose divine biography it embellishes.

Sailors, fugitives, dismissed retainers, and traveling crews spread different reputations for the same Patron. The Gorget-Breaker entered Vhasian households through people accustomed to an armed retinue but now obliged to leave quietly. Freedman's Luck traveled with people for whom being recognized was dangerous. In Sihai, an imported luck-image could shelter a more particular devotion without turning every household prayer for fortune into a thieves' initiation.

Jack offers the exhilaration of a closed arrangement briefly becoming open. The opening can rescue a person who has no lawful remedy. It can also tempt a comfortable thief to describe the loss as somebody else's deserved lesson. The Unlucky Coin's stories are most valuable when a teller admits whom the job hurt and how that consequence was put right.

Zakov crews value a route past an inattentive watch; Silkstrand crews value a mark looking in the wrong direction. Both know that luck works best beside preparation. Their quarrels concern the job after the lucky escape: who promised to return, who now needs help, and whether the most fortunate member believes they accomplished everything alone.

Who Is Left Holding the Key

A clean escape includes more people than the person carrying the prize.

The mark's locked door belongs to a household with cooks, porters, relatives, and servants. A thief may see a powerful owner punished while failing to notice who has been ordered to replace the missing goods. The distinction between a mark and a victim has to survive the morning after the job.

Some crews reserve a portion of a successful haul without calling it charity. It is for correcting what their plan failed to account for. Jack's favor does not ensure the portion will be sufficient, or that the people harmed will accept money instead of an explanation.

A former accomplice brings the perfect chance to recover an unjustly seized heirloom. Its disappearance will implicate the steward who has quietly protected the household's dependants. Make the security and consequences discoverable before the theft; give the crew room to plan a result in which getting away is only part of success.

Lunara, the Silver Quiet — Moon & Mystery

5 Rites Gift: Lunara's Silver Word

Lunara walks where torchlight falters: crossroads, tidal edges, shadowed sanctuaries. She is the whisper beneath silver skies, a torch-bearing guide to mysteries that lie between waking and dream.

  • Moonlight Revelation: truth, hidden things, the seen in darkness
  • Liminal Passage: thresholds, dreams, the space between waking and sleep
  • Silver Mystery: reflection, illusion, the mirror's truth
  • Lunar Cycles: change, phases, the passage of time as a gentle guide

Guises

  • The Deep Silver — Aelerian holds. A polished plate is kept where a shaft admits moonlight. It is brought down to the person who cannot reach the opening. Older versions show one figure whose face changes with the viewing angle; some newer triptychs place maiden, mother, and crone side by side. The images disagree less about the moon than about whether a life can be understood all at once.
  • The Fog-Guide — Mistland households. A bowl is set by a shutter during the nights when a traveler is expected. A neighbor refills it without asking whether the expected person is still alive. The keeper can describe the road and the weather. Asked what the moon sees through the bowl, they resume what they were doing before the question.
  • The Silent Mirror — Inland Sea Aelinnel. A mirror is turned after the bells have been checked. Reflections are compared on specified nights; arguments over what was observed are kept separate from the maintenance of the shrine. The office has careful procedures for recording an uncertain sight. It has no procedure that makes an uncertain sight certain merely because a magistrate needs an answer.
  • The Moon in the Unopened Room — Sihai's scholarly and domestic circles. Images arrived with astronomical instruments, travelers' mirrors, and household gifts. Some observances honor Lunara by name; others address a local lunar presence and reject the foreign identification. A learned keeper can acknowledge the same phase calculations without conceding that the calculation identifies the being reflected in the water.
  • The Light Kept on Snow — Kazurai valley communities. Winter gatherings use a pale cloth instead of a visible disc. At thaw the cloth is washed and brought outside. A family can become expansive when the pass reopens without losing its spare winter manner. The cult's changes belong to this range; they are not an imitation of Himadri severity.

Provenance

Several lunar traditions have gathered around the Silver Quiet; agreement about the moon does not produce agreement about its worship.

Lunara's own received image embraces maiden, mother, and crone. Some communities have always recited the three together; others adopted the triptych from traveling keepers while retaining older single mirrors. Temple writers sometimes claim that the threefold image proves all lunar observance descended from their house. The worn single plates and their household histories resist that tidy account.

Mirrors traveled readily as gifts. A person who brought one home could retain the night observance of a foreign household without bringing its entire doctrine. Aelinnel Bell-Tenders gave such observances schedules and comparative records. Mistland families kept the inherited action and declined the imported explanation. Sihai's specialists could exchange calculations while disagreeing over which rites had any place beside them.

The Silver Quiet's circles grew where people needed room to reconsider without immediately defending the change in public. Widowed households, people returning from long illness, and apprentices uncertain of a chosen life could sit before the same water. Some keepers offered that pause without conditions. Others developed a reputation for discerning a person's true nature and began treating every hesitation as confirmation of their own reading.

Reflection does not supply infallible knowledge. The symbolic phases and regional images add no new divination procedure. A keeper's account of what a reflection means can be sincere and mistaken, even where Lunara's actual Rites work.

The Face Allowed to Change

Lunara makes room for a self that cannot be understood from one moment. That permission can become an excuse to postpone every decision until a more certain face appears.

A reflection is persuasive partly because the viewer recognizes it. Keepers must distinguish attending to a person from presenting that person's expectations back as revelation.

The difficult kindness is sometimes to end the vigil. A person may have learned enough to act without acquiring a final account of who they are.

Give two witnesses an honestly different reading of an observation, with a practical decision that cannot wait for unanimity. Let the moon remain strange. Do not make every disagreement resolve into one keeper secretly possessing an omniscient answer.

Nimorith, the Gray Benefactor — Absence & Threshold

5 Rites Gift: Nimorith's Absence

Fae magic has two hands. The courts are famous for the one that gives — the glamour, the seeming, the face over the face, the gold that is leaves in the morning. Everything Mab is celebrated for is addition.

  • Thresholds & Liminality: doors, gates, moments of transition, the unseen
  • Absence & Omission: erasure, forgetting, silence, what is not said
  • Memory & Witness: testimony, collateral, the price of truth
  • Subtlety & Misdirection: stealth, deception by omission, unseen paths

Guises

  • The Gray Guest — Ubral's old roads and roadside households. A host remembers receiving someone but cannot find the coat peg they used. The household may retain an extra portion at supper without remembering why it was first prepared. Those who speak of Nimorith do so after such visits. They are usually less certain about what was asked than about the relief that followed.
  • The Keeper of the Missing Shelf — Thepyrgos's custodians of damaged collections. A catalogue can refer around a gap so neatly that an attentive reader initially thinks the books have merely been rearranged. Some custodians preserve the old shelf marks even when they can no longer say what occupied them. Visitors who identify this as a cult of forgetfulness receive little agreement. Something may have been removed while the surviving papers remain extraordinarily exact.
  • The Host of the Empty Mooring — Zakov's unclaimed landings. A berth is maintained at a landing no boatman admits using. People meet there when an ordinary transaction cannot provide the absence they need. The Absent Host's attendants keep no promise that the petitioner will later remember the meeting accurately. A rope worn smooth beside an unused post can become the most persuasive part of a family's account.
  • The Benefactor Without an Inscription — Sihai's private collectors. An object arrives with a place for a donor's name that does not appear to have been scraped clean. Collectors dispute whether the omission was a mark of modesty, damaged documentation, or something less comfortable. Those who attribute such gifts to Nimorith do not possess a method for tracing every missing name back to him. Most uninscribed gifts are simply uninscribed.

Provenance

Nimorith is the name under which the Gray Benefactor is approached. The survivals of that practice do not supply a secure biography of every gray-clad traveler or every Trow.

The Absent Host has collections that look complete until compared with records made for another purpose. A household book has room settings that no longer fit the number of rooms; a harbor account pays for a mooring that nobody can locate. Attendants cannot always remember why they kept the comparison. Successors inherit it as a task rather than an explanation, and sometimes discard it as a predecessor's eccentricity.

People carrying such practices between regions carried useful omissions as well as surviving objects. A guest could teach a host where to leave a place without telling them who first occupied it. Thepyrgos's collectors tried to correlate the customs and found correspondences that did not yield a common itinerary. Zakov's practical arrangements retained more landmarks than names. Sihai's private houses developed increasingly careful descriptions of surfaces on which nothing seemed to have been written.

Petitioners can have a clear and terrible reason to want something absent. The difficulty after a successful removal is that the reason may no longer be available in the form they expected. Families have tried to protect one another by witnessing meetings, leaving instructions, or arranging reminders. The remaining evidence does not guarantee that anyone still understands what those precautions were intended to preserve.

The cult's history is therefore a history of surviving practices and incomplete witnesses. An old object can prove that an arrangement existed without disclosing every person or thing involved. Nimorith's gifts leave the situation sufficiently intact for ordinary life to continue, and ordinary life supplies many opportunities to stop asking why one small part of it feels wrong.

The Relief That Has Lost Its Reason

A person can be relieved of something and unable to describe accurately what that relief cost.

The Absent Host's helpers may sincerely believe they have done a kindness. The person helped may believe the same. Agreement between them is not a complete account when the missing part would have been needed to question it.

Some attendants keep a task rather than a story: wash a cup, mend a garment, visit a particular door. Continuing the task can preserve a place for an attachment they can no longer name. It does not ensure that the attachment can be restored.

Use a new local omission with evidence the party can compare, and establish its actual scope before play. Preserve the special limits of the existing Saikou Ira material: these new traditions do not give an NPC, divination, or universal decoding method the ability to solve that sealed history. A local mystery can be resolved without making every subtraction recoverable.

Oath of Mercy and Grace — Forgiveness & Healing

4 Rites Gift: The Unbroken Thread

The Oath of Mercy and Grace is the gentler face of divine law—a patron of healing, compassion, and second chances. Where the Oath of Flame & Light burns with righteous fire, this Oath offers cool water to the weary.

  • Forgiveness: absolution, redemption, letting go of guilt
  • Healing: mending wounds, calming minds, restoring hope
  • Compassion: empathy, presence, the simple act of being there
  • Second Chances: offering grace to those who have erred, believing in the possibility of change

Guises

  • Saint Valerie of the Unbroken Thread — Vhasian hospices. The saint is painted holding cloth that continues beyond the frame. Families add a strip after a vigil, including vigils in which the patient died. Some patrons pay for the cloth but object to its being extended for an enemy. The keepers must decide whether a benefactor may determine which suffering belongs on the wall.
  • The Quiet Candle — Ecktoria's charitable houses. The lamp is set low enough for a person lying down to see it. Officials value the orderly care and argue over who qualifies to receive it. A sister who treats an unregistered stranger may save a life and put the house's allowance at risk. Another who protects the allowance may believe she is protecting the hundreds already inside.
  • The Breath-Watcher at the Bed — Aeler households. An attendant remains within hearing of the sleeper. Eastern foothill families may exchange the watch with human neighbors; upper holds keep their own close routines. The Pale Shepherd also bears a Breath-Watcher title. Aeler distinguish the watcher staying beside the living from the one expected beyond the last threshold, even when an outsider sees similar lamps.
  • The Hearer Beneath the Eaves — Sihai's river and road refuges. Relief kitchens received images brought by healers and caravan households, then gave them local dress and names. Some keepers address a compassionate presence; others honor the people through whom mercy reached them. Petitioners need not settle that argument before eating. Officials sometimes try to count every recipient, while a person fleeing an abusive household fears being found through the list.
  • The Hand Turned Outward — Nihori's ferry shelters. A small image faces the landing rather than the sanctuary. Sailors brought the form from Sihai and other ports, and local attendants retained their own shrine observances around it. The open hand means aid offered, though some donors interpret it as a demand for gratitude. A guest who leaves without a public thanksgiving can expose that difference quickly.

Provenance

An answering mercy gathered into saintly lives, charitable foundations, and household vigils; no single institution contains its history.

Stories of Valerie include acts no single lifetime could contain. Some keepers treat them as the works of later people honoring the same thread; others defend every episode as hers. A family remembers who actually sat beside a dying relative even when the hospice commemorates the deed under a saint's name. These two kinds of memory can sustain each other or compete over whom the house is allowed to honor.

Healers and recovered patients carried vigil practices across the Amaranthine and along the Way of Silk. Refuge kitchens translated them into local schedules, food, and ways of receiving strangers. Images changed more readily than the promise to stay. At Sihai's road refuges, a foreign saint's name could remain on the back of an image whose front had acquired a local title; in Nihori the same figure might face a ferry instead of a sickbed.

Hospices grew where gifts could support care beyond a single household. Their donors brought conditions. Care for prisoners, unknown travelers, and soldiers from a defeated army produced divisions that a common prayer could not settle. Some houses reduced their scale rather than exclude a patient; others accepted restrictions to keep their existing beds. Neither choice spared them consequences.

Do not identify every compassionate image as the same literal being. Local traditions may recognize a resemblance, claim a revelation, or share a practice without sharing doctrine. The Oath's existing aid and costs remain those of its entry, not a new power granted by any of these titles.

Mercy Without Possession

Mercy attends to someone without making their continued dependence the condition of being loved. That is where it differs from a binding offered as perfect care.

A healer can become attached to being needed. The grateful patient then owes a life spent proving the healing worthwhile, or is discouraged from leaving the house that saved them. Grace becomes another authority over the person it restored.

Its more difficult practice is to let the recipient disagree, recover unevenly, or depart. Staying with someone is a promise about one's own conduct, not ownership of their future.

Give a recovered person a plan their benefactor dislikes. Let the benefactor have real affection and a real expense to meet. Resolve the practical needs without treating obedience, conversion, or forgiveness of an abuser as the required proof that mercy worked.

Solara, the Still Mirror — The Face Without Performance

5 Rites Gift: The Word of Seeing

Solara's followers practice staying when another person has run out of ways to explain themselves. They set down their work, listen, and resist the urge to supply a kinder account.

  • Radical Presence: active witnessing, holding space for truth, the courage to be seen
  • Authentic Connection: mutual recognition, vulnerability as gift, the power of being known
  • Rituals of Witnessing: shared silence, eye contact without agenda, truth-telling without judgment
  • Healing Through Recognition: soothing isolation, restoring self-trust, communal clarity

Guises

  • The Face After the Sitting — Ecktoria's portrait workshops and letter-writers. Some workshops keep an unvarnished study that the patron never commissioned. It records how the sitter looked when the formal pose had become too tiring. Solaran painters ask whether it should be kept, altered, or destroyed. Their most celebrated colleagues sometimes claim that the painting belongs to truth rather than its subject, and exhibit what they were trusted to see.
  • The Near Seat — Wenkar and Qianvar foothill households. Two seats share a sheltered view without requiring their occupants to face one another. A visitor may describe the hillside before finding words for a harder subject. Wenkar keepers often continue making tea; Qianvar families may set the pot down and listen until the visitor finishes. Neither considers a demand for unbroken eye contact the only honest way of attending.
  • The Listener Without the Laurel — Vhasian households after military service. The laurel is taken off a returned soldier's chair before a private visit. They need not perform the triumphant version of the campaign there. Chaplains disagree about whether admitting fear diminishes a worthy action. A keeper may hear both fear and cruelty, and refuse to improve either account merely because the speaker has finally trusted someone.
  • The Unranked Face — Sihai's healers and private reading circles. A sitter removes the badge of office but keeps their name. Clear Circle hosts explain the custom differently: some ask the visitor to set aside a public performance; others claim rank makes truthful speech impossible. Former pupils of the latter complain that their teachers have found another performance for everyone to satisfy.

Provenance

Solara belongs to Inaea's lineage, alongside Venara, Gaila, and Zephyria. Her practice has often grown inside their shared household observances.

Older domestic hangings show a reflective disc turned toward the other figures. Later Solaran circles detached the disc and hung it at the height of a seated visitor. Some household elders saw a daughter being separated from her mother; the new keepers answered that a person can be loved within a family and still need somewhere outside it to speak. Both continued using cloth woven in the same workshops.

Portraitists and letter-writers carried the observance into Ecktoria's towns. Healers took it into Vhasian houses receiving the wounded. Travelers introduced paired seats into eastern foothill communities, where sheltered courts and tea visits gave the practice its own rhythm. In Sihai, literate circles retained the written truth and wax seal while developing an argument about which parts of a person's office could be set aside.

The Clear Circle first acquires its reputation through somebody who felt understood. The difficulty arrives when others seek that person's relief without offering the same time or privacy. Public patrons have commissioned grand halls of recognition that are rarely comfortable places to be uncertain. Small circles sometimes refuse the building; others accept it and close most of its rooms to visitors whose presence would turn a confidence into an exhibition.

Solara's followers have worked alongside the Witness and the Silent Choir without adopting either vocation wholesale. A listener can understand a person's shame without possessing evidence about the event, and can hear an accurate account without knowing whom it is safe to tell. The Still Mirror's intimacy does not settle those separate questions.

Being Known Without Being Finished

The person receiving another's confidence must allow that person to remain more than the account just given.

A truthful confession can become a permanent portrait. Friends who pride themselves on knowing the real person may begin interpreting every later change as another concealment. Their attention grows exacting precisely because it once brought relief.

Good keepers make room for an ordinary visit after a difficult one. Someone may come back to discuss a roof, borrow a cup, or sit without offering another revelation. Recognition should survive the absence of a new confession.

A painter has made a painfully accurate portrait of someone who entrusted them with a private sitting. The work could persuade a hostile household to understand its subject, but the subject refuses permission to show it. Give the proposed viewers other ways to learn and the painter a real artistic stake; neither possession of truth nor a successful magical reading supplies consent to display it.

The Breath of the First Forge — The Spark in the Maker's Hand — The First Good Mistake

4 Rites Gift: The Maker's Spark

Before the first hammer struck, before the first wheel turned, before the first seed was planted in the furrow, there was the Breath. It was not flame—flame came later, when the Breath met kindling.

  • Creative Inspiration: the spark of new ideas and novel approaches
  • The Joy of Making: the pleasure and satisfaction of creating
  • Ingenuity: finding unconventional solutions to problems
  • Primal Craft: making as a fundamental human (and inhuman) act

Guises

  • The First Breath — Aelerian workshops. A failed first casting can be kept beside the finished work, provided no apprentice mistakes it for a sound fitting. Masters quarrel over whether the attempt deserves honor before its maker has learned why it failed. The First Spark's celebrants insist that discarding the object also makes it easier to forget who was allowed to try.
  • The Breath Before the Hammer — Himadri holds. An unfinished piece is passed through several hands before work begins. Fuel and breathable time are too scarce for a casual experiment. Their severity has produced careful stores of abandoned trials, each available to the next maker. A keeper who preserves a failure without ever permitting another attempt has made the archive safer than the living workshop.
  • The Spark Between the Hands — Ngomebe's moving foundries. Ngomebe cutters and Aeler bearing-makers honor the impulse in different materials. A design can begin with a song from the roller crew, not only at a master's bench. The twins Awenji concern the fit between collaborators; the Breath concerns the possibility that either could make something not yet attempted. Neither supplies a painless replacement for the living carrier.
  • The Maker's Spark — Thepyrgos. Workshop dedications put a small rough piece below the formal image, often beneath the visitor's line of sight. Debates over who conceived a device are heard beside devices assembled by unnamed pupils. A pupil who places the rough piece in public may be celebrating an apprenticeship or disputing a celebrated master's account of it.
  • The Returning Ember — Akazun and Wenkar communities. In Akazun a rescued hearthstone may be used in a new workshop after ashfall. Wenkar potters keep glaze trials arranged where damp will not destroy their labels. Both honor continuity through further making; neither treats resemblance to older work as sufficient proof that an attempt is forbidden. The same title has acquired two different workshop calendars.

Provenance

No agreed birthplace; smiths, carvers, builders, and makers give different accounts of the first attempt.

The oldest workshop stories do not begin with a perfect object. Something breaks, refuses its shape, or proves useful for a purpose its maker did not intend. Court versions tend to shorten that interval and credit a single founder. In the stories apprentices tell while clearing the floor, the founder needs considerably more help. Their disagreements concern who is remembered as capable of making, as much as what was made.

The cult traveled with apprentices and families who established new workshops. A lender could finance a foundry without bringing its household observances; an apprentice could carry those observances without owning a hammer. Exchanges between Ngomebe cutters and local Aeler engineers gave the First Spark a place beside Awenji, while eastern foothill communities adapted it to kiln work and repeated rebuilding. These are histories of practice, not evidence that every craft descended from an Aeler teacher.

Thepyrgos gave the Maker's Spark formal lectures and competing claims of invention. Some masters accepted work in clay, cloth, or kitchens as fully as metalwork; others reserved the title of maker for their own licensed disciplines. The cult's public exhibitions can therefore become disputes about who was admitted through the door and whose work was displayed as someone else's assistance.

The Breath is not merely a fire god. The Clockwork Monad also values making, but its drive toward iteration and optimization is a different demand. A flawless repeat and a clumsy first possibility need not satisfy the same Patron.

The Right to Attempt

The First Breath begins with the possibility that someone may make something. Its followers must decide who receives the time, material, and tolerance that possibility requires.

Celebrating failure costs little when another household supplies the fuel. A master who praises daring only in well-supported pupils has mistaken access for inspiration. The opposite error is to call a preventable danger sacred because it occurred during an experiment.

The cult's useful mercy is permission to learn without being permanently identified with a first failure. That does not excuse hiding a fault in a piece that other people must trust.

Bring forward a discarded prototype whose maker has left the workshop. It offers a real possibility and carries an unresolved flaw. The party must find the missing knowledge and decide who gets to attempt the work, rather than receiving an automatic invention from a prayer.

The Savage Heart — Red Maw

Venara, the Unbroken Thread — The Thread That Holds

5 Rites Gift: The Word of Remaining

Venara is honored in the chair kept beside a sickbed and the cloak mended before its owner notices the tear.

  • Steadfast Bonding: oaths of loyalty, healing through presence, enduring commitments
  • Nurturing Presence: active listening, emotional holding space, comfort through proximity
  • Rituals of Care: shared meals, mending crafts, vigils, the sacred ordinary
  • Healing Through Connection: soothing distress, restoring trust after fracture, communal resilience

Guises

  • The Hearth That Receives Again — Thepyrgos's neighborhood meals. A mended cooking cloth is displayed during a common meal. Some shrines show Venara seated at a hearth; neighboring houses use only the cloth, admitting households that keep another faith's flame. The eldest donor does not necessarily serve first. In well-kept circles the person who has cooked for everybody is brought to the table before the second pot is emptied.
  • The Seam Across Two Cloaks — Ubral's Khaz-Verim refugee and exile communities. A strip from an old traveling garment is sewn beside cloth received in Ubral. Families differ over which should face outward. Some preserve an unmistakable sign of Khaz-Verim; others want the gift from their neighbors visible. The seam can commemorate assistance without promising that every descendant will return to the old home or forget it.
  • The Chair Through the Flood — Ayokha's river households. A place is kept through a seasonal absence, with the owner's unfinished work protected from damp. Mended Circle neighbors take turns moving it when the water rises. An absent partner can return to find the household changed and the place still theirs. Whether that means the old relationship must resume unchanged is a question the chair cannot answer.
  • The Hand at the Second Vigil — Vhasian marriage households and infirmaries. The first night gathers relatives and gifts; the second reveals who can actually remain. Keepers arrange relief before the first visitors leave. Some wedding chapels invoke this aspect over joined cloths, while infirmaries keep single strips for friends, widows, and people whose families will not come. The care is not restricted to marriage.

Provenance

Venara is Inaea's daughter, honored for the continuance of chosen care rather than for a separate claim to rule every household.

Mended Circle objects are frequently reused. A cloth brought from a sickroom becomes part of a traveling blanket, then a covering for a meal in a new home. Temple inventories that call these objects marriage relics can be misleading: one strip belonged to a friend, another to a stranger, a third to someone who never recovered. The oldest keepers preserve the relationships when they remember them, and acknowledge the missing names when they do not.

Care moved with people who had received it. A recovered patient helped establish another vigil; a caravan household taught its hosts how to divide the night's work; displaced Aeler retained familiar mending customs alongside the cloth their new neighbors supplied. In Ubral, Khaz-Verim families give the practice a history of departure and reception. That particular history need not explain every foothill Aeler community.

Venara's circles endure because they make repeated assistance possible. A single generous person cannot keep every bedside supplied indefinitely. Where the work is shared, the cult can outlast its founder. Where the founder becomes the measure of devotion, successors compete to go without sleep, food, or help until the people being cared for are frightened to ask for anything.

Public marriage worship has sometimes claimed the Unbroken Thread as a guarantor that spouses must remain together under every condition. Hospices and neighborhood circles preserve a broader practice. They can keep caring for someone after a marriage ends, and may have to protect that person from relatives citing the same Patron's constancy. Venara's rejection of possession remains central to the dispute.

Tomorrow Is Part of the Gift

Remaining requires a life from which one can return. The person who gives care also needs food, friendships, and permission to rest.

A circle can admire devotion so intensely that it abandons the devoted. Praising the keeper who never leaves is easier than taking their place for one night. The praise can make accepting relief feel like a failure of love.

Some Mended Circles display an unfinished seam during the evening meal. Another person is invited to complete it while its maker eats. The custom gives continuity a second pair of hands rather than requiring the first pair to hold forever.

A respected caregiver has accepted work elsewhere. The household fears losing someone it genuinely loves, and a dependent still needs daily help. Give the replacement arrangement practical demands and willing but imperfect participants. The departure need not become abandonment, and the story need not reward the caregiver only for surrendering it.

Zephyria, the First Bloom — The First Courage of Spring

5 Rites Gift: The Word of Almost

In gardens where petals fall like whispered promises and every glance holds the weight of a thousand unsaid words, the First Bloom reigns.

  • Courtship Rituals: the language of glances, exchanged tokens, the art of near-miss
  • Anticipation as Power: building tension through withdrawal, the potency of unfulfilled promise
  • Beauty in Transience: celebrating fleeting moments, the poignancy of impermanence
  • Mutual Discovery: learning another through shared vulnerability, not declaration

Guises

  • The Unoffered Rose — Velantis's masked gatherings. A flower is carried where it can be seen and need never be given. Some hosts preserve the custom as an acknowledgment that attraction is not a pledge. Others turn the withholding into a contest, making a guest perform uncertainty for the room. The same petal can mark possibility or carefully staged humiliation.
  • The Lady Before the Answer — Vhasia's courts and household gardens. A song may be presented without the singer naming its recipient. Noble households sometimes tolerate a courtship in verse that they would forbid in plain speech. The ambiguity protects the lovers and can also let one party deny an understanding the other thought shared. Zephyria's attendants disagree over when a beautiful hesitation has become a refuge from honesty.
  • The Blossom Outside Its Month — Ayokha's season-bound households. Her small signs are hidden in gifts exchanged before a permitted season. A matchmaker may allow two people to meet before anyone announces a marriage, then refuse to backdate what follows. The First Bloom opens a possibility; it does not abolish the calendar, guarantee a match, or make every breach of custom harmless.
  • The Unsealed Letter — Sihai's garden and poetry circles. An unfinished letter is kept until its writer chooses to send or destroy it. Students copied the practice from traveling poets, and household elders found it useful for preventing premature public commitments. A younger writer may see the same delay as control. No one agrees that a preserved letter is therefore a promise to its imagined reader.

Provenance

One of Inaea's daughters; the regional history concerns her worship, not an alternative parentage or a borrowed account of divine birth.

The oldest pieces kept by the Blush Circle are difficult to date because they were made to pass between people rather than establish a foundation. A petal, a lining stitched with words, a song whose intended hearer was never recorded: later collectors turn them into evidence for local claims that the First Bloom first appeared in their gardens. The claim often says more about a court's prestige than the surviving object.

Poets, tailors, and matchmakers carried her observances between Velantis and other courts. Household versions could be quieter than the performances that drew notice. A single chance to speak without an entire family listening mattered as much as a celebrated masquerade. In Ayokha, the practice encountered a calendar that gave anticipation a public schedule; in Sihai, letters and introductions placed the risk in who would be asked to witness an answer.

The cult flourished in the space between a household's intended arrangement and a person's still-unsettled desire. It can defend choice there. It can also turn people into connoisseurs of beginnings who withdraw whenever another person asks what follows. Venara's steadiness, Livaea's hunger, and Zephyria's almost are distinct demands, even when the same lover invokes each in turn.

The title First Bloom concerns romance and mutual possibility. It does not make Zephyria the agricultural deity of every region with flowering plants, nor authorize new growth or fertility powers beyond her existing entry.

The Almost Must Permit an Answer

Anticipation is valuable because either person remains free to answer. A romance maintained by making refusal costly has lost the permission the First Bloom offers.

Her devotees can mistake uncertainty for depth. They preserve the first glance by never allowing the other person to become sufficiently known to disappoint them. Someone kept as a possibility may discover they were never being received as a whole person.

The shared discovery includes the right to find that desire is not mutual. A graceful ending can honor the Patron more honestly than another exquisitely delayed confession.

Give both parties wishes beyond the romance. A gathering, letter, or introduction can allow a choice without requiring a successful coupling. Do not turn attraction into compulsory character behavior or use a cult custom to override a player's boundaries.

Archetypes: The Carried Trade

Most Invokers in play are not scholars of the Triune. They are tradespeople who carry two or three Symbols because two or three Symbols is what the work takes — the ward-setter with a shingle, the exorcist with a bag, the conjuror with a licence and a season. What follows are 3 such combinations, each built on a rivalry from the table below, because the friction between a practitioner's own Symbols is the most reliable trouble in the game.

An archetype is not a class. It is a purchase order: which Symbols to buy, in what order, and what the friction between them will cost you. Everything here resolves with the rules already in this guide — Symbols are 4 XP Minor Assets, invocation marks Obligation, displaying the Symbol buys DV −1 and Obligation −1, Crack the Seal buys instant resolution for +2 Obligation and a Compromised Symbol, and Obligation Capacity is Spirit + Presence. Nothing below adds a subsystem.

Reading an Archetype.

  • Three, not four. Each archetype names three Symbols: 12 XP total, and one short of the four that make the first rite of every scene cost an extra Obligation. The fourth Symbol is available and is always a decision, never a bargain.
  • The spine is a rivalry. Two of the three Symbols are listed rivals in the Cross-Resonance table. Displaying one while invoking the other is Position one step worse and +1 Obligation. That is not a flaw in the build; it is the build.
  • Buy in order. The order given is the order the character learned the trade in, and a character partway through it is a perfectly good starting character. One Symbol is a practitioner. Two is a professional. Three is a reputation.
  • The trade is a trade. These are people with premises, fees, and a waiting list. Play the invoice as seriously as the invocation — half the drama of the profession is being paid in something other than money.

The Doorwright — Ward-setter, seal-breaker, and unlicensed consultant to the watch

“I put the lock on. I know where the lock is weak. Those are the same job, and I charge for both.”

A tradesman of thresholds. Doorwrights hang wards for merchant houses, seal the cellars of the recently and badly dead, and are called by watch sergeants who would rather not put a name in a report. The work is honest, dull, and steady — until the same skill that closes a door is the only skill on hand that can open one. Doorwrights are notorious for the second half of the job, and are hired anyway, and know it. They tend toward coat, cigarette, and a tone of voice their clients dislike and their clients' enemies dislike more.

The Quiet Coroner — Exorcist, deposition-taker, and last physician to the already dead

“I am not here to release you. I am here to write down what you say, and then release you.”

Someone has to sit with the ones who did not finish. Quiet Coroners are engaged by families, magistrates, harbour boards and occasionally by the dead themselves; they arrive with a bag, a chalk line, a cheap chair and no hurry. The work is half exorcism and half deposition, and the order matters: a soul that has been laid to rest cannot be questioned, and a soul that is still answering questions has not been laid to rest. The good ones are patient. The great ones are the sort of patient that frightens people.

Cross-Resonance

Carrying Symbols from rival Patrons generates friction. That friction is the point: it is what an Invoker trades for reach. Edge Loci are places where one side starts a step better in Position; the Friction column is a set of prompts for spending Story Beats, not a list of penalties to apply.

Quick rulings.

  • Position Nudge. In a home locus, start one step better; in a rival's locus, one step worse.
  • Effect Shade. Where a Patron dominates, consider an Effect bump; where opposed, consider Limited Effect unless it is paid for.
  • Symbol Interference. Carrying both sides' Symbols raises the noise: the first rite each scene may mark +1 Obligation, at the GM's call.
  • SB Colour. When spending SB in these matchups, prefer the suit that fits — Hearts for social, Spades for harm and escalation, Clubs for material cost, Diamonds for numinous disturbance.
