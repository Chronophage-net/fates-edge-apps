Commentary on the Books of the Belworth

Not fiction. This is the designer's apparatus for The Widow's Hand, A Roll of the Fallen and Count Your Exits — what each book is doing, what it withholds, and why.

It is a spoiler for all three and should be read after them, or not at all. The novellas are built so that the reader arrives at these readings themselves; arriving at them here first converts a discovery into a confirmation, which is the one thing the trilogy cannot afford.

A Game Master preparing the succession crisis may find it useful. A reader should be warned off it twice.

I. On The Widow's Hand

This is a long tale and it was written for the page. It is not meant to be read aloud and it will not survive the attempt; there is no scene in it that a table would let stand, because almost everything in it is a thing somebody does with a face, and faces do not carry across a hall.

It is meant to be read alone, in one or two sittings, by somebody who will notice the second wedding.

The cadence is borrowed openly. The Belworth country is a country of advocates on both banks, and an advocate's prose does two things at once and lets you find the second one yourself: it states, flatly and at length, what is on the record, and it arranges the flatness so that the omission has a shape. That is the war-song voice turned indoors — the same stately declarative line the old chansons use for a charge, pointed instead at a woman being buttoned into a dress. If you want the real-world ear for it, it is in the lais and the pleadings of the twelfth-century French: the marriage contract and the love song written in the same grammar by the same hand on the same afternoon, and only the reader to tell them apart.

Underneath that, in Adria and Ethor and Istovar, there is something older and rougher, which surfaces four times in this book and is never explained: the Ember Truce, the tally-sticks in the third chest, the parley of the third day, and a song about a gateway that every serious historian has dismissed and every single one of them has quoted. Those cantons were kingdoms before either crown existed, and their customs are not law and never were, which is exactly why breaking one is free and why the bill always arrives anyway, four hundred dead or one king in a gate.

The book withholds three facts. Two of them are in the past and are withheld deliberately, in narration, in dialogue and in interiority, from the first page to the last. The third is in the future and is withheld because nobody in the setting knows it either.

The first is the paternity of Valdais. It is not coy about withholding it: it gives you the dates on the fourth membrane, in a clean hand, counted out — and then makes you watch a clever, decent young king do exactly what you have just done, with exactly the same materials, and very nearly get there.

The second is what happened in the gateway at Brigh, and — which is the harder and better question — what was in Elianor's voice in a cloister at Elden on the seventh day, when she gave a proud exhausted man perfectly sound advice in the one tone guaranteed to make him refuse it.

The third is what the war between Valdais and Veralyn is actually for, and the book gives you four answers and refuses to rank them, because a reader who has watched Elianor Longblood work for five-and-fifty years should not be permitted to believe that motives are the sort of thing that gets established.

She did not know. That is not an evasion; it is the point of the whole book. A woman who has spent fifty years making certain that no one will ever have to ask her anything eventually arrives at a place where she cannot ask herself either, and the last honest thing left to her is the sentence she gave to one person, once: I have never been able to find out, and I have looked.

The love story is told in refusals. Elianor and Baldvyn speak plainly to one another four times in eleven years — the empty room, the stable in the rain, the orchard at Corbenoit, and a memorandum — and on the fifth occasion neither of them says anything at all; there is only a button, and a man laughing badly in a chapel yard, and two kingdoms getting it wrong in writing.

And nobody in it is a villain. Her father is doing arithmetic. Wymund is tired. Harald is right, about everything, for the best reasons anybody in the book has, and it kills him. The Bishop of Verrain is at market and says so. Oswyn takes the deal, and keeps it to the letter for three-and-forty years, and grows heavy and exact and formidable under it without ever once becoming a worse man, which this book declines to present as a tragedy. Every harm here is done by somebody acting correctly within their role, and the largest of them is done by a woman writing a clause to save her son's life by taking his crown away.

The soup is always free. The price never is.

II. On A Roll of the Fallen

This is a companion and not a sequel, and it is built to be read either way round.

The Widow's Hand is written in the register of the Vhasian advocate: long stately declaratives that state what is on the record at length and arrange the flatness so that the omission has a shape. That is a courtroom voice, and Elianor Longblood lived and died inside a courtroom, and the prose is the instrument she used.

This one is written in the other voice entirely, because Alaysha Renard was never allowed into that room. Short sentences. Weather. Iron. Animals. Things counted out loud. The cadence of a folk tale told by somebody who already knows how it ends and is not going to hurry for you — and then the third winter came — with the flat unbothered brutality the sagas use to report a killing. If you want the real-world ear for it, it is in the Carpathian and Polish wonder-tale, which is a form that has never once in its history pretended that the price was not going to be collected.

The magic never performs. Not once, from the first page to the last. Every supernatural event in this book survives a skeptical reading, the skeptical reading is always available, and it is always very slightly worse than the other one. An old woman on an empty road may have taken a sheep path. A word too fine to read may be a smudge. A dog that will not go into a cellar is a dog. A blade nobody remembers carrying in was carried in by somebody who has since died, which is what happens at a sack.

Shadowsteel is the model for the whole book and it is the model on purpose: the finest blade anybody ever handled, carried for years by a woman who does not know it is anything, in a court of four hundred people of whom not one has ever suspected. That is the register. If the reader ever gets a moment where the goddess does something, the book has failed.

And nobody in it is a villain except Valeran Renard, who barely qualifies — because he wants nothing. He is not ambitious; ambition has an object and can be satisfied or thwarted. He is a mechanism with no destination, running correctly, for forty years, and the only pleasure he ever takes is in the running. Everything else in this book is done by people acting reasonably inside their roles: a king who is right about a tenure, a queen who built the one instrument available to her and could not have known it would sit in the doorway, a devout dull cousin who heard eleven words in a passage and kept them for sixteen years, and a girl of fifteen who was told what she was getting and thought it was a fair price.

The book withholds one thing from its protagonist and it does not withhold it from the reader, which is the inversion that makes this the middle volume rather than a repeat of the first.

Alaysha Renard spends her life on which hand — the clerk, the creditor, the one who settles, the one who is not in the book — and every one of those four is an office her family invented out of scribal variation in a ledger, in a locked room, over four generations, without once walking up that road to ask.

The actual doctrine is two hands. The Red is the war as it is suffered and the Cold is the war as it is made, and the entire discipline is that you may not hold one and disclaim the other: the plan and the scream are one act, and you did it. And then you walk the field and put a stone on every one of them, because the grief is not decoration bolted on to a war-goddess, it is the thing that keeps the arithmetic from being an excuse.

So the four readings are not an enquiry. They are a screen, and the reader should be able to see it from about the third chapter: in three of them the thing that happened in that stable court happened to her, and in the fourth it did not happen at all, and there is no reading anywhere in her column in which she is the one who did it.

That is the disclaiming. It is the only sin that goddess recognises. And a woman who has been committing it in a fair upright hand for seven-and-twenty years is not being punished for it by anybody, which is worse, because it means the account is simply open and nobody is coming and the stone is still not on the grave.

And afterwards, who carries the stones?

Nobody in this book. That is what it is about.

The soup is always free. The price never is.

In Vhasia that is a proverb.

In the Aelerian foothills it is a description of an office, and the office has more than one hand, and nobody has ever established that they speak to one another.

III. On Count Your Exits

This is the third of three and the last, and it is the one with friends in it.

That is the whole design. The Widow's Hand is a woman alone in a courtroom for fifty-five years. A Roll of the Fallen is a woman alone in a cold house with a book. Both of them are told in the voice of the instrument the woman used — the advocate's long declarative, the folk tale's flat brutality — and both of them are lonely on every page.

This one is told in the coldest voice of the three and contains the warmest scenes, and the reason is arithmetic: if the reader does not love four children on a dyke in the Belworth, the last movement is a campaign map. The narration is a reconstruction — forensic, counting, faintly bloodless, the prose of somebody working a position up from two folders of notation and a tourney roll — and inside it are the only happy years anybody gets in eight hundred pages. The gap between those two registers is the book.

Kon'reh is not decoration and is not a metaphor. Every tenet quoted in these pages is in Rekedim's Field Manual: Canray by Convoy, which is in the Concordance, and can be looked up, and is a real doctrine that real Ykrul children have before they can hunt. The reader is never taught the game and should be able to see what Veralyn is doing about forty pages before she can, which is the correct relationship between a reader and a player.

Three recorders, and what each of them cannot write. The trilogy has one obsession and it is records. Alaysha's codex prices things. Rekedim's folders keep plays. Tylan Vayle's sheets keep positions — where a person was, and for how long — and nothing else, ever, in nine years. That third instrument is the cruellest of them, because it never accuses and never omits: it puts down that the steward's boy saw the queen in the muniment room, and then puts down that the boy is nine years old, and stops. The reader does the rest and the reader is responsible for it. Valdais is the fourth case and the inverse: a man with four-and-twenty years of immaculate rolls and no interior at all, which is not concealment but a price accepted so young that it never had to be written down. The single unpriced line in those rolls is the only thing he ever said about it, and it is four words long.

Elyas. He is set up in the first movement as a boy who decided at nine to be impossible to dislike, and it works from childhood into his twenties, and the book promises twice that it fails. If the second failure is not on the page the promise is a cheat. It is on the page, in a yard, in front of eleven witnesses who all noticed, and the folded leaf in the back matter is the thing behind it — kept for the rest of his life, never sent, never redrafted by a man who drafted everything four times. He is Veralyn's mirror and the mirror gets worse with time: she was spent and chose it; he was spent and was never asked.

The five names are not a flourish. Kon'reh, K'thra, Canray, Kanry and Kanrāy are all in the Concordance, several of them only in passing, and one of them only in a copyright notice. Collecting them was the point: a discipline that five peoples each believe they invented is a better object than a game, and the man who holds all five names and belongs to none of them is the reason book three has a teacher in it at all. The one word Rekedim will not translate is not withheld from the reader as a trick. It is withheld because he does not translate it, and a narrator who knows more than the evidence is a narrator this book has spent three volumes refusing to be.

The sword never performs. Not once, in three books. In four years it has not glowed, moved, warmed, sung, resisted, or turned in her hand; four hundred people have seen it and two armourers have handled it and a Thepyrgan sceptic pronounced it the finest ordinary object he had ever held. It is implied to be a player. It is never, at any point, permitted to take one decision away from her. What it may have done — deniably, unprovably, visible only in the margin of a logician's sheets — is remove four seconds. Every move in this book is hers, reasoned, and defensible. She has simply stopped counting, and the only man who ever measured her counting has two folders and no column for it.

And the three books withhold three things, and they are the same shape and they get worse.

Whose son is Valdais — which is a fact about the past, and is knowable in principle, and is simply lost.

Which hand answered a girl of fifteen with a hound — which is not knowable even in principle, because there is no office that takes the question.

And whether the Queen of Viterra is the player or the piece — which is not settled, is not settleable from the page, and is going to be decided south of the Belworth in the spring by people who are not reading.

Her great-grandmother could not produce a figure. Her grandmother counted to six four hundred times and never got further. Veralyn Everblood has never asked the question at all, and that is either the healthiest thing about her or the thing that kills her.

The trilogy's proverb, in its three dialects:

The soup is always free. The price never is.

A roll of the fallen; each name marked with the price paid.

Every ounce of gold is an ounce of grain. Opportunity is always cost.

Three countries, three instruments, one law, and nobody anywhere in eight hundred pages who gets out of it without paying.

IV. The three books together

Three women, three instruments, one law.

Elianor Longblood owned the record — the rolls, the chancery, the recitals — because the law was the only lever on the table for a woman who had been Item the Fourth of somebody else's peace.

Alaysha Renard was shut out of that record, because an instrument that cannot be struck also cannot admit a second party, and so she signed the ledger instead: a book with the one column the chancery has never kept, which is what a thing cost the person who paid for it.

Veralyn Everblood inherited both and was handed a third thing at eleven years old by a Ykrul sergeant with no lands and a board: the proposition that you may price anything if you are willing to count exits, and the discovery, at three-and-twenty, that the only move worth making is the one the manual forbids.

And the proverb, in its three dialects:

The soup is always free. The price never is.

A roll of the fallen; each name marked with the price paid.

Every ounce of gold is an ounce of grain. Opportunity is always cost.

Three countries, three instruments, one law, and nobody in eight hundred pages who gets out of it without paying.

The ear for each of the three. The Widow's Hand takes its cadence from the Vhasian advocates, who are the only people in either kingdom who write as though a sentence might be produced in evidence later; the real-world ear is the lais and the pleadings of the twelfth-century French, where the marriage contract and the love song are written in the same grammar by the same hand on the same afternoon. A Roll of the Fallen is deliberately not that: short sentences, weather, iron, animals, things counted out loud — the wonder-tale told by somebody who already knows how it ends and will not hurry for you, with the flat unbothered brutality the sagas use to report a killing; the Carpathian and Polish tellings are the ear. Count Your Exits is reconstruction from incomplete records, and says so. These notes used to sit at the back of the novellas themselves, which meant telling a reader what he had just heard.

The names repeat on purpose and the confusion is the point. Four Wymunds, three Haralds, an A— for nearly every daughter in two generations. A house that puts a name back in the box when a man dies and takes it out again in eleven years is a house that treats a person as an item in a series, which is the trilogy's whole subject, and it would be a mistake to spread the names out for the reader's comfort. Two characters notice: Elianor at sixteen, in a letter her father never answered, who solves it by making a list and writing a use beside each name — and Rekedim, who assigns numbers and declines to discuss it. Nobody at that court thinks it worth remarking, because to them it is not a fact about the court; it is what a name is.

One knot, not nineteen. The Dornhall wardship is the whole method and the other eighteen are the same act with other names on it. Veralyn does not put the knot in. She finds a real defect in a quarter of an hour that four offices missed, explains it twice, makes the man say it back, writes the correct officer's name on his satchel in her own hand — and sends the matter into the one procedural lane in Viterran law from which nothing emerges without the Parlement. Ruthold Carne goes away the happiest man in Valora and writes his earl a very warm letter, every word of which is true, and dies in 839 with a docket in a clerk's hand reading await the wardship. His earl's seal is on the document with eleven seals.

Valdais is read, not explained. The four observations are now in Veralyn's own hand on the back of a fodder return, with a fifth line under them that is not a conclusion: Do not finish this. Finishing it is what he is selling. An earlier draft had the narrator enumerate what the four were consistent with, which is the author hedging aloud at the end of a book that has spent three volumes refusing to.

On The Diwān of Roads

A fourth book, thirty years earlier, and not of the Belworth. Everything above concerns the trilogy; this concerns the other one, and is the same kind of spoiler.

It is written against the trilogy on purpose. The Belworth books are chancery: clause, testimony, exact verb, the thing proved set beside the thing true. The Diwān is image first and water throughout, and it counts in threes and sevens where those books count in nines and elevens. If a paragraph of it could be dropped into Count Your Exits without anybody noticing, it has failed.

There is no reveal and there was never going to be one. She suspects both lies inside a tenday and hunts them for two years. What the ending supplies is the answer to four words she wrote in a margin at seventeen and that he pointedly declined to answer — and that the reader has been carrying since the Roof of the Way. A table can know he is lying from the second session and lose nothing.

The three coins carry the book and will carry a campaign. The one that does not clink; the one cold in a vault; the one warm and alive. The third is the one he shouts, and it is the one he has taken, and he knows it, and the two instruments in the back are what he did about it.

Morag is never named by the narrator more than three times and is never confirmed. The matron feeds her first, states the terms once and entire and truly, and forbids her the literacy to read the rest — which is cut into a board in the hall where she may look at it whenever she likes. That is the whole design and it does not require a goddess to be standing in the room.

The well is the same restraint. Two columns and no conclusion: eleven languages that arrive like faces, and a name she can see through the back of the page and cannot assemble. She says twice that she does not believe it. Never confirm it, and never let a GM confirm it either.

The father is deliberately unclosed. He is vivid for one stage and then gone, and she goes back at forty-four with money and a coat and cannot say the word that would make the clerk turn the page. That is the price of the well arriving twenty-seven years late and it is the only place the book lets itself be cruel to her. Do not solve it. There is no story at the end of that office.

The Five Daughters appear once, as a Ykrul marriage-song in five parts, and are never named or systematised. She is fluent in three of the five. The book does not explain which two she lacks, because the preceding forty pages have.
