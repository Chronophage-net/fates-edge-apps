The Last Blade

A working treatment of the Belworth campaign novel

What this is. The working plan for the novel. The six sequences below now sit within three acts: sequences One and Two in Act I, Three and Four in Act II, Five and Six in Act III. The current manuscript is `the_last_blade.md`.

Draft position. A complete first draft now runs from the prologue through Chapter 29, with three acts. Chapters 22–24 cover the revised ambush dispositions, Riarhin's witnessed encounter with Kargath at the fuller's yard, the capture of the train, casualties, and the use of the pay rolls. Chapters 25–26 resolve Orvain's pursuit into a witnessed account of his death and Pell's custody for the homeward journey, then follow the disintegration of Kargath's support through disputed payments and negotiated withdrawals. Chapter 27 retains the six-step confrontation, the burial of the brothers' blades, and Riarhin's keeping of the Last Blade. Chapters 28–29 separate the surviving party: Elyas carries Pell and the evidence east, back towards Tarlington, with Alric and Lyra; Rekedim stays with the Ykrul by choice; Riarhin escorts the homeward party to the crossing and turns south to pursue information about Kalrantha. Chapter 22 now seeds that lead: eavesdropping at the fuller's yard, Riarhin hears Kargath interrogate his officer about an Oshiiran correspondent and name the amulet, complaining that four men have sold him the same paragraph and no one has held the thing. Rekedim, given her verbatim report, sets the detail aside as not being on the road or in the wagons. Chapters 26 and 28 pay it off. The former Tarlington return and board coda are superseded, not events that remain to be written.

The working names are Edras Venn (registry contact), Lord Merrow (fund signatory), Veyra Keld (Edgewalker), Teren Vaul (True Mason), Serekh (captive scout), Dareth, Oskel and Sava (independent Ykrul commanders), and Pell (the captured clerk). Kargath is the quiet man at the ferry in Chapter 8, recognised by Riarhin in Chapter 22. Mara remains in Tarlington. Braedyn's condition and the eventual adjudication at Tarlington are not resolved through an off-viewpoint epilogue.

Viewpoint. Close third-person limited throughout. The prologue belongs to Riarhin; Act I belongs to Elyas; Acts II and III belong to Riarhin. The prologue retains the caravan and Whiterun encounter. During Act I, Riarhin's pursuit and discoveries reach the reader only when Elyas witnesses them, hears her report, or encounters their consequences. No cutaways into Orvain, Kargath, or any other character's private thoughts.

The attack and the first discovery. Orvain wounds Braedyn in the corridor outside his chambers and flees into Tarlington. Riarhin wounds Orvain with a thrown knife, helps keep Braedyn alive until assistance arrives, and hunts him through the city. Elyas accompanies her; his knowledge of Orvain gives him something useful to contribute. A prepared room, a waiting horse, and a warrant connect the flight to a Vigil-supported institution. Gyllan supplies context, including the Duke's former involvement. The evidence establishes influence and assistance, not that every Vigil faction ordered the attack. Orvain remains alive and at large at this stage.

The corrections it is built on. Rekedim's people fled south and east off the Violet Steppe. His father was displaced by Tulgoth, not Kargath; the Last Blade is a line's name and Kargath is Tulgoth's hidden heir, alive now. Riarhin's mother was a Rocke and her father a Lethai-al styled a prince, so she is Lethai-al on her father's side. Braedyn does not go north. He is struck, and the wound is called a sickness, and Seniro and Braedyn the Younger go in his stead while Elyas is sent away with Rekedim. At the end Elyas takes the road east, home to Tarlington; Rekedim stays with the Ykrul and Riarhin turns south after a Kalrantha lead. The earlier plan to bring all three back to Tarlington is superseded. Kalrantha is an object, never a person — an amulet of green glass in a brass frame, known to everyone concerned only through one repeated northern paragraph. Kargath wanted it and never got it, and his frustration at that is the whole of the connection; he never possessed it, never used it, and it did not cause his campaign.

PART ZEROWhat Was Wrong With It

Six faults, named plainly, because the rest of this document is the repair.

One: the first two parts are the same part. Both are meet a Vigil faction, be double-crossed by the informant, discover the Aeler behind it. So are the third and the fourth — both are track the Edgewalker through Acasia in company with a True Mason. Twelve chapters of outline contain six chapters of story. The fix is not to trim; it is to make the second half of each pair do a different thing to the same material, which is what the gambit names were already promising and not delivering.

Two: the game is narrated instead of played. In the outline, characters say it is like a game of Canray eleven times. Every one of those is the book explaining its own metaphor, and every one of them is a place where the reader is told what to notice instead of being allowed to notice it.

The rule for the whole novel, and it is absolute: no character ever compares anything to the game. Not once. What they do instead is use it — with the vocabulary the corpus already has, which is a working vocabulary and not a poetic one. Count your exits. Fork the far mouth. Two exits, both priced. Seed is a depot, not a daring. Lane welding. She never checks behind. When a character says the far mouth is forked, they are not being literary. They are giving an order.

The reader who has read Count Your Exits will be able to notate the plot. The reader who has not will follow a competent thriller and never feel lectured. That is the whole design, and it only works if nobody in the book points at it.

Three: the board remains unfinished. The author has replaced the planned return to Tarlington with separate departures. Preserve the board as unresolved personal history. It does not supply a late solution to the investigation, and no other character decodes it in Riarhin's absence.

Four: Gyllan dies once, at the Act II–III boundary. The archive betrayal costs trust and causes injuries but leaves Gyllan alive. He has laid down the sword and uses his staff throughout the journey. His last defence of Lyra is the first time he takes up a sword in a live battle since laying it down. Treat this as an immediate practical choice, not an oath-breaking ceremony.

Five: the Vigil's three factions are a diagram. Populist, Aristocratic, Clandestine is a taxonomy, not a conflict. Given below is what they actually disagree about, which is procedural and therefore arguable, and which makes all three of them correct.

Six: the cultural reveal has already happened. The outline's late twist is that Kon'reh is Ykrul and the world forgot. It has not forgotten. Count Your Exits opens by saying so, on the page, in the second paragraph: the steppe's game, the steppe set the first board, and the Aelers and the Vhasians and the Ecktorians all know perfectly well where it came from and play it anyway under their own names.

Which is better. Kargath's grievance is not that the world forgot. It is that the world remembers, credits them, borrowed the only thing his people ever made that everyone wanted, and it bought them nothing — not a border, not a treaty, not one year of not being ridden down. Acknowledgement without consequence is a worse injury than theft and it is a far better motive, and it is the motive of a great many real people.

THE SPINE

Three Names for One Thing

The novel has one idea and it is stated three times in three registers, and it is never explained.

The game has three names. Kon'reh on the grass, Canray at court, K'thra in the under-vaults. One board. Three peoples who each believe their version is the serious one.

The woman carries two hands. Tulgoth had a dagger for each of his sons and one for himself, and his was Fate's Edge, and Riarhin took it off him and has called it Toothpick ever since. The last dagger he had made he called the Last Blade, and it went to the son nobody was told about, and that son answers to the knife's name and not his own.

And across her back is Remorse, which is a sword — narrow, straight, double-edged, no belly and no working edge. It is wrong for a Lethai-al blade in the one way that matters: a Lethai-al blade is also a tool, and this one will not cut a rope or quarter a deer or shave a stake. It is for killing people and for nothing else. She knows its name. She has no idea what the name means and has never asked anybody, which for a woman of sixty-six is not an oversight.

And she draws them for different work. Remorse for the clean kind — a rescue, a duel taken up for somebody else, a thing done in daylight where it can be seen. Toothpick for the other kind. She sorted her entire life into two hands without ever deciding to.

This is the novel's quietest structure and it must never be remarked on. The reader should be able to track it from about the third killing. Lyra works it out somewhere in Part Four and says nothing, and the fact that she says nothing is the last kindness she does her. Riarhin does not notice in this book. She will not notice in any book. If a character ever says you use the sword when you think you are right, the scene is wrong and the line comes out.

The man has three names. Tulgoth was the Last Blade. Kargath is called it now and is entitled to nothing. And Rekedim Many-Scars is what the other answer to the same injury looks like: a Ykrul born in the Fens with the steppe face and the steppe hands, whose companions assume he cannot speak the steppe language, who wrote the manual, and put Canray on the cover, because it was for Vhasian soldiers and he was an honest man about audiences.

Kargath and Rekedim are the same man given the same wound and a different quarter-hour. That is the book. Everything else is roads.

THE CAST

Who Is Load-Bearing

Riarhin. Prologue viewpoint and lead viewpoint of Acts II and III. Sixty-six and looks six-and-twenty; Lethai-al on her father's side out of Silkstrand, a Rocke yarl's grandchild on the other. Her father's mother was Lethai-thora, his father Lethai-al; from that admixture she carries the Lethai-thora gift of the mind — perfect recall — and the Lethai-al peak physicality at every stage of life, and she effectively stopped ageing at twenty-six. None of this is secret and none of it is a revelation. She knows it, states it flatly when asked, and otherwise does not think about it. Seed it at moderate density: recall used as a working tool rather than a trick, others doing the arithmetic, a day's riding that does not tell on her. That her conception was arranged — her Lethai-al father “encouraged” to produce a half-elven child — is a later reveal and is not in this book at any strength. She is never described or implied to have been bred. She never knew her father. Familial memories of practical instruction belong to her brother. She obtained her gear from a corpse in the Valewood; it must not be described as a gift or inheritance from her father. Scout in the Fenwood army at Lence; reported to the marshal and to nobody else; found Coldmorrow doing her own work and reported it correctly, and then said the name aloud at a fire where a fifteen-year-old girl could hear it, and has never offered that in her own defence.

She is not hot-headed. The outline makes her the impatient one against Gyllan's caution, and that is a stock pairing and it is wrong for her. She is fast, and she is right, and she is used to being the only one in the room who is going to live long enough to regret it. Her impatience is not temper; it is arithmetic — she has watched slow careful men be slow and careful and then be dead, for forty years, and she is tired of the pattern and cannot say why she knows it.

Her flaw is not recklessness. It is that she does not look at things she has decided not to look at, and she can hold that refusal for sixteen years, and it is the same refusal that keeps her from the board and the same one that keeps her from Coldmorrow.

Rekedim Many-Scars. Forty. Supply sergeant, logistician, author of Canray by Convoy, teacher of four children in a long room off a counting-house. Not a general and never will be; the book must resist making him one. His power is that he converts a country into a corridor and a culture into a lane. His experience makes him attentive to what arrangements omit, but he can be surprised and does not possess other people's local knowledge. At the end he chooses to stay with the Ykrul, not merely because work remains but because he wants a part in what they make next.

Elyas Everblood. Act I viewpoint. The Summer Knight; Oswyn's youngest; Braedyn's ward at Tarlington. Sent away with Rekedim, ostensibly for safety. He is the mirror of Veralyn and the book should let that be visible without saying it. He reads the world through story and myth and he is right about the pattern and wrong about what to do with it, every time, which makes him useful and dangerous in the same scene.

Gyllan. Old knight, teacher, and former servant of both the Vigil and the duke. He has laid down his sword and uses a staff. This does not erase his experience or prevent ordinary practice; the later distinction is his first live sword fight since laying it down. He survives the archive and the Acasian journey, then dies defending Lyra at the end of Act II. Give him disagreements, humour, habits, and relationships throughout that time. No bound blade, ritual cord, or speech about breaking an oath.

Lyra. Gyllan's student, capable of healing magic with real limits. The novel's moral engine and its best character, and the outline already knows it. She wants the count to come out even. It is not going to. Her arc is not corruption; it is the discovery that a ledger is not justice, and her refusal to accept that, and the fact that her refusal is honourable and gets people killed.

Alric. Junior scholar; Lyra's shadow; the reader's proxy and the one who asks plain questions. He is injured and healed at the archive, survives the later camp attack, and is not substituted for Gyllan as a second sacrificial victim.

Serekh, the Scout-Commander. The captive guide to the opposition camp. His information is used without pardoning him; he remains under guard for a witnessed hearing when the party leaves. Cooperation does not settle responsibility for his apprentices. The camp's independent fighters have their own commanders, obligations and grievances.

Kargath. Tulgoth's hidden heir. Appears in Part One, in person, unrecognised, in a scene the reader will only understand on a second reading. Do not hold him off the page until Part Five; a villain first seen in the fifth part of six is a rumour with a sword.

Braedyn Fenwood. Dying through the whole book, off the page after Part One, and the gravity of every part.

PART ONEThe Duke's Discretion

Canray. A piece is given up, and the giving is not announced.

The gambit. At court one does not concede a piece; one discovers that it was never defended. A Canray player of the Vhasian school gives ground in a way that can afterward be described as an accident, so that the concession costs him no standing while it costs his opponent the tempo of having to decide whether it was one. The whole part is Braedyn Fenwood doing this with his own life.

The shape. After the Harwick opening, Orvain wounds the duke outside his chambers. He has access because he is trusted. Riarhin wounds him, then must help Braedyn before she can pursue him. Elyas witnesses the attack and accompanies the hunt into Tarlington.

The wound is mortal and everybody in the room knows it inside the hour. What Braedyn does with the hour is the part the book is about. He gives out that he is sick. He signs nothing that would date the truth. He sends Seniro and Braedyn the Younger north in his stead, the marshal and the talker, because that is the pairing the house reaches for when the head of it must not be seen to be absent — and Seniro goes without being told why and does not ask, and Braedyn the Younger, who has no name of his own on any instrument, is for once extremely glad of it.

And he sends Elyas away with Rekedim, which reads to the household as getting the ward out of danger, and which is also getting the two people most likely to work out who ordered it four hundred miles from anyone who could stop them.

He gives up his own death as a piece, quietly, and does not announce it. That is the Duke's Discretion, and the book should never say the phrase.

Beats.

1. Harwick and the return. Elyas settles the road dispute but cannot control the meaning the household gives his duel. Orvain is a familiar companion with an errand of his own. Riarhin returns because Braedyn asked. 2. The corridor. Orvain addresses the duke, strikes, and cries out in Vhasian. Riarhin's thrown knife wounds his shoulder. He escapes while she tends Braedyn and Elyas summons help. The attack is brief, physical, and comprehensible from Elyas's position. 3. The hunt. Blood and witnesses carry them through the merchant quarter. Elyas recognises a house Orvain has visited. Clothes and a room were prepared before the attack; a horse was paid for in advance. Riarhin hunts while Elyas notices how much of his friend's life he has failed to question. 4. The river. Orvain is lost to sight. Do not certify his death or invent access to his thoughts. The pursuit produces evidence even though it does not yet produce a prisoner. 5. The Vigil influence. Gyllan identifies the institution and the warrant's political purpose. Its practical work and its factional ambitions overlap. The Duke once supported it. Elyas discovers a compromised part of his own household's world, rather than an isolated enemy outside it. 6. The injured duke. Braedyn's wound is publicly called an illness. Decisions about Seniro, Braedyn the Younger, Elyas and Rekedim follow the attack; they must not precede it in a way that puts Orvain in two places. 7. The board and the departure. Elyas sees Riarhin pass the board without looking. He cannot narrate what she is refusing to remember. The investigation broadens beyond Tarlington, under the Duke's need for discretion.

The sequence includes the pursuit but ends with its consequences: the connections exposed, the Duke's decisions, and the next journey. The pursuit is not a substitute for the investigation.

PART TWOThe Unfinished Game

Canray. The investigation leaves the house.

The current shape. Gyllan's contacts, the Vigil's disputes, the archive and the betrayal broaden Elyas's account of Orvain's attack into the supply investigation. The shipping evidence has to be followed, interpreted and tested. The journey is not rendered unnecessary by an answer hidden in Braedyn's study.

The board is reserved. The former proposal that Braedyn encoded the intelligence into a changed position is superseded. Riarhin does not return to read it in this book. Its personal meaning remains open; do not replace the removed coda with an explanation elsewhere.

The Vigil's three factions, rewritten so that they are arguing about a procedure instead of being labelled:

The Vigil exists to restore the Utaran order. It has existed for three hundred years and has never restored anything, and the three factions disagree about what an empire actually is, which means they disagree about what would have to be rebuilt first.

  • The Instrument. An empire is its law. Restore the Censor's Hall, the writs, the indiction, the standard weights, and the thing is back whether or not anyone is crowned. These are archivists and lawyers and they are the faction Braedyn was nearest to, and they are the reason he was a member, and they have never hurt anybody and they are not harmless.
  • The Road. An empire is its logistics. Restore the roads, the posts, the bridges, the grain, and the law will follow the carts. These are engineers and merchants, and they are the faction that has been quietly doing business with the Aeler for ninety years for entirely honest reasons, and they are how the Aeler got in.
  • The Crown. An empire is a person. Find the blood, put it on a chair, and everything else can be argued afterward. These are the ones with money and no patience, and they are the smallest faction and the loudest, and they are the ones who paid for the knife.

All three are correct about what an empire is. That is why the Vigil has never restored anything and never will.

Beats.

1. Gyllan's contact. A former knight, now an informant of the Instrument faction. The scene is not noir-atmospheric; it is administrative, which is far worse. The man has a desk. 2. The archive. Not a heist. The Vigil's archive is not guarded; it is catalogued, and the catalogue is the defence. What they need is not stealable because it is not filed under anything a person would look for. Rekedim gets them in by asking a question about where the building's coal is delivered, and this is the first time in the book he does the thing he does. 3. The document, and what it actually is. Not a conspiracy. A shipping schedule — Aeler-crafted goods moving through Vhas on the Road faction's own perfectly legal account, in quantities that make sense only if somebody is arming somebody. Fragmentary. True. Useless without the other half. 4. The double-cross. The informant was honest and was also selling to the Crown faction, and did not consider these to be in conflict, and says so, and is not lying. 5. Lyra and the ledger. She works out, on her own, four pages before anyone else, that the quantities do not reconcile — and she is right, and nobody acts on it fast enough, and this is the first crack. 6. The ambush and the loss of trust. Gyllan uses his staff and survives. Lyra heals Alric's injured arm, with effort and limits; Gyllan continues to teach her afterwards. His mistake in trusting Venn must cost him authority without turning him into a fool or initiating Lyra's bereavement early. 7. The Aeler name. They come out of it with one: an Edgewalker, working the Acasian marches.

The sequence ends with them turned west with an Aeler name, evidence of a prepared escape, and Gyllan alive to answer for his misplaced trust.

PART THREEThe Merchant's Step

K'thra. Invest, and make the other side show a hand it has not been asked for.

The gambit. In the under-vaults they do not play K'thra for pleasure; they arbitrate with it. Two factors with a disputed toll sit down over a board and are finished by evening and the settlement binds, and each walks out having agreed to something he would not have signed at a table. The Merchant's Step is the opening move of an arbitration: you put something on the board you are willing to lose in order to find out what your opponent is not willing to lose.

The Edgewalker. Keep her — elitist, cold, contemptuous of humans, arming warlords and seeding rumours through a loyal retinue so that her hands stay clean. But give her the one thing the outline does not: she is right about something. The Acasian marches are going to burn with or without her, and she says so, and the reader should be unable to prove her wrong.

And make her a deliberate counter-image of the Edgewalker in the Diwān of Roads — Rurik Dornvael, rich past use, who bought a girl and freed her the same hour and never told her. Two Aelers who go out of the mountain and become enormous, and one of them is lonely and one of them is not, and the book never mentions the other one.

The True Mason. Keep entirely. He is the best invention in the outline and he is canon-native: a True Mason is an Aeler who does not lie, not as a virtue — as a method, and this one is an old retired mason walking the human roads repairing bridges his ancestors built, as a religious duty, and he is pleasant, and helpful, and not altruistic, and he will say so if asked.

Give him one hard exchange with Elyas, who wants him to be a saint, in which he explains without heat that the roads are maintained because a road that fails takes the toll with it, and that he does not require Elyas to find this beautiful.

Beats.

1. The Step. Rekedim's plan: they cannot find the Edgewalker, so they put something on the board. They let it be known, through three channels, that a buyer is in the marches with Fenwood coin looking for exactly what she sells. Two of the channels are hers. They are not trying to trap her. They are trying to find out which warlord she moves to protect, because the piece she defends is the piece that tells you what she is actually building. 2. The road. The journey with the True Mason. Slow, good, funny in places. Alric asks the plain question and gets a plain answer and is more disturbed by it than by anything so far. 3. Lyra learning. Her competence increases while Gyllan is alive: practical healing, the limits of repair, the difference between a balanced account and a just outcome. They can argue and then work together. Save the hardening born of grief for the end of Act II. 4. The hand shows. The Edgewalker moves to protect a warlord she has no commercial reason to protect. Rekedim sees it. The warlord is a Ykrul. Not a chieftain — an exile, a man with no tribe, sitting on the Acasian side of the grass with money from somewhere. 5. Riarhin's refusal. Her first real chance at the Edgewalker, and she does not take it, and the book must be honest that this is not wisdom: she does not take it because she has been sixty-six years old for forty years and knows what happens to people who take the first chance.

PART FOURThe Bridge's Collapse

K'thra. Do not take the piece. Take the thing it stands on.

The gambit. The under-vaults' cruellest move and the one they are proudest of. You do not capture. You remove the support — the toll, the lane, the credit, the bridge — and the piece is still on the board and is worth nothing, and no one can point to the move that killed it.

What makes this part different from Part Three, and this is the fix for the doubled Aeler act: Part Three is about finding the Edgewalker. Part Four is about discovering she is not the top of it, and the discovery is made by doing to her exactly what she does to others.

They do not fight her. They take her credit. Rekedim and the True Mason between them work out which four bridges and one salt-lane her whole operation stands on, and the Mason — who has spent fifty years maintaining those bridges — declines to maintain one, for one season, on a technicality, in writing, with perfect courtesy. It is the only violent act of his life and it is a filing.

And when her support goes, the money does not stop.

Somebody else is paying. Somebody who has been paying all along, and who is not Aeler, and who does not need her.

Beats.

1. The confrontation. It happens early in the part, not late, and it is a conversation. She is not afraid of them. She explains her business model. She is contemptuous and she is precise and the precision is what frightens them. 2. The unmaintained bridge. The Mason's single act. Make the reader understand exactly what it costs him and let him do it anyway. Then let him say, at the end of it, that he will be repairing it himself in the spring and that this does not undo anything and that he is aware. 3. The collapse. Her warlords go quiet. Her retinue thins. And the Ykrul exile is still buying. 4. The reversal. She comes to them, once, at the end, not for mercy — for information, because she has worked out at the same time they have that she was never the top of it, and she is more insulted by that than by being ruined. She gives them the name and the shape of the money and she does not ask for anything and she leaves.

She is not punished, and she is not redeemed, and she does not appear again. Resist giving her a death. The corpus does not kill people to tidy them. 5. The Mason's parting. Keep his warning from the outline, but cut the foreshadowing-flavoured phrasing. He says something small and practical and it lands harder. 6. The name. Kargath. Tulgoth's son. Alive. The discovery gives context to the scouts who have followed them since mid-Acasia. Rekedim's recognition is not a reason to make him withhold urgent tactical information.

PART FIVEThe Stalking Wolf

Kon'reh. Pursue while closing the exits, and never chase.

Here the novel changes hands. Everything in Parts One to Four was played on boards belonging to other people — a court, an archive, a market. Part Five goes onto the grass, where the game is played in dirt with pebbles and sheep-bones and is given to children before they are permitted to hunt, and where none of the party's advantages are advantages.

This part absorbs the Road of Ash & Grass material, which is the strongest writing in the whole outline and should be kept nearly as drafted.

The Act II–III hinge: the camp. Move this event to the end of Act II, after sustained time in Acasia. Kargath's scouts have been following them since mid-Acasia. The senior scout is highly experienced; his two apprentices are trained and capable, not careless boys. Riarhin has failed to detect them. Earlier details should allow recognition in retrospect without making her ignore obvious evidence. She blames herself even when the others point out the quality of the men following them.

Riarhin, Rekedim, and Elyas are away confronting the Edgewalker and her cadre. Gyllan, Lyra, and Alric are at camp. The senior scout has doubts about whether Kargath's plan can succeed and wants to parlay. His apprentices, chafing under his stern restraint and eager for a fight, disobey him. From Riarhin's limited viewpoint, these causes can only be established later through testimony and observable evidence; do not cut away to narrate the attack's beginning.

The three return just in time to see Gyllan fall while defending Lyra. He has taken up a sword: his first live sword fight since laying it down. No declaration or ceremonial emphasis. Riarhin shoots the two apprentices who killed him. Lyra is weeping over Gyllan and trying to heal him. The wounds are too great. Establish enough earlier success for this failure to hurt without suggesting that more effort would necessarily have saved him.

They capture the senior scout. He accepts responsibility for his command without pretending he ordered the killing: "Under my ring. Their zeal is my weight." His wish to parlay is not proof of innocence and does not settle Lyra's grievance. He offers to lead them to a camp where opposition leaders are rumoured to be meeting and weighing their options. The lead is uncertain; it is neither a guaranteed coalition nor an instant pardon. Verify what can be verified, maintain custody, and let the decision to use his help cost the party something.

Act III begins with the living consequences. Lyra's hardening starts here. She is furious at accepting guidance from the commander whose men killed Gyllan. Riarhin tries harder to soften towards her, fails at familiar forms of reassurance, and remains present rather than retreating into a clever refusal. The disagreement must remain real. Alric survives; do not maim him to manufacture a second version of this loss.

Rekedim's Ykrul. He can speak it. The earlier claim that he cannot is a viewpoint error, not a lost language that must be magically recovered. Seed grammar and accurate names of Kon'reh moves without staging a conspicuous secret. In Act III he can conduct substantive exchanges with the captive scout and the leaders; his companions realise they never asked. Language does not grant him automatic cultural belonging or agreement with Kargath.

The three chiefs. Their concerns arise from actual camp work, not a declared sequence of tasks buying an alliance. The strangers' help earns a hearing, not ownership of the leaders' decisions.

  • Lock-Count Velkan investigates discrepancies between water collected and water delivered. Rekedim follows the weights and split-stick entries; Lyra tests a supposedly defective skin. The missing water went to Kargath's riders.
  • Ash-Wind Iserin repairs a shifted crossing after a mare is injured. The party helps recover seasoned timber from beneath a loaded wagon. Cutting live trees would undermine the channel bank. This is shared work with material consequences, not a reward bargain.
  • Sluice-Elder Amru receives the scouts' rings without treating their return as settlement of Gyllan's death. He hears the witnesses and keeps Serekh in custody. The wind-knot crossing remains: three words permitted, and Lyra's “hold” prevents Riarhin from pulling them into a hidden runnel. Her help does not signal forgiveness.

The refusal and the forgotten watch. The gathering formally furnishes neither soldiers nor intelligence against Kargath. Its members have kin riding with him and cannot all accept the costs of open opposition. That night the coughing guard at the records tent goes to the healers, and no replacement arrives until dawn. The lamp remains filled. No one invites the party inside or hands them a solution. Rekedim and Riarhin examine cumulative, contradictory intelligence: false routes, a slipped ford approach, incompatible replacement wheels, hired draught teams, oats, renewed rope, and payment dates. These establish a plausible southern route, which Riarhin must verify on the ground.

Enough people to fight. Sixty-three Ykrul join independently after the refusal. Dareth leads the bow riders, Oskel the spear fighters, and Sava the remaining party. Twenty-seven carry bows; six people are needed for remounts and baggage. Scouts and former wagon workers contribute specific local knowledge. These are sufficient numbers for a planned ambush against the roughly fifty guards reported with the train, not a token escort or certainty of victory. Their commanders negotiate roles, treatment of captives, claims on the money, and care of the wounded. Iserin facilitates her niece's departure without issuing a council levy; supplies bought by Elyas are receipted. Serekh stays for the hearing, with a written undertaking sent through Tarlington. Rekedim proposes using captured rolls and money to settle arrears for riders who leave Kargath, reducing the danger of an unpaid force feeding itself from nearby households.

Kargath, properly, at last. He gets one long scene in this part, witnessed by Riarhin. He addresses a subordinate, not her, and she can observe the subordinate's distraction. The scene must give her a concrete reason to be present or within hearing; no independent Kargath viewpoint. His explanation is what she hears, not privileged access to his thoughts.

His grievance, stated once and never repeated: the world took the board. It took it openly, credited it, plays it in three languages, teaches it to its children, writes manuals about it — and his people got nothing for it. Not a border. Not a treaty. Not one year of not being ridden down. He does not complain of theft. He complains of acknowledgement without consequence, which he regards, correctly, as the more contemptuous of the two.

And then the thing that makes him a person rather than a thesis: he has read Canray by Convoy. He owns a copy. He thinks it is very good. He knows exactly who wrote it and what Rekedim is, and he refers to him — without heat, as a matter of fact — as the one who took the offer.

PART SIXThe Last Blade

Kon'reh. The strike that ends it, which is only available because everything before it was.

Keep the outline's architecture. The war-chest, the allied strike, the forced duel, the six steps, the burial of the brothers' blades, and the keeping of the Last Blade.

Three corrections.

One: the chest is not treasure, it is credit. Silver, salt, coin and portable ledgers. Taking it does not make Kargath poor; it makes him unable to pay on time, which on the grass is worse, because a man who cannot pay his riders this week is a man whose riders are listening to someone else by the new moon. Rekedim says this in one sentence and the sentence is the climax of his entire arc, and it is about bread.

Two: the duel must be a disappointment, on purpose. The outline promises a duel that is the culmination of every strategy in the book. It must not be. Kargath is out of pieces before he draws; the duel is not the last move, it is the receipt for a game that ended two chapters ago, and he knows it, and the reader should feel the deflation and then understand it.

Six steps. She names the number before it starts — what she owes and all he has left. It takes six. It is spare and quick and there is nothing beautiful in it.

And do not give him a speech as he dies. The outline has him reflecting on the irony of Kon'reh with his last breath. He has already said all of that, once, in Part Five, to a man who was not listening, which is the right place for it. Here he should say something small and irrelevant and true. He is allowed one sentence. It should not be about the game.

Three: Lyra, not the duel, is the ending.

Riarhin wins, and buries the brothers' daggers with him, and keeps the Last Blade — which means she now carries both ends of Tulgoth's line, the father's knife and the youngest son's, one in each boot, and the sword she does not understand across her back.

And she draws Remorse for the duel. In daylight, for somebody else, taken up on terms. The book states which blade she draws and does not say one word about why.

And Lyra does not forgive her. That is the ending. Their break at the Act II–III boundary is not repaired in the final sequence, and the last exchange between them is short and civil and final, and the warmth does not come back.

Because Lyra is right: mercy for strangers and a knife for her own. And Riarhin is also right: she kills the one who chose the killing, and the commander tried to prevent the attack but still bears responsibility for his men. His cooperation does not cancel that responsibility. Both are right and the count does not come out even, and a book that lets them reconcile is a book that has decided it does.

THE DEPARTURES

Three Roads

This replaces the earlier Tarlington coda. Riarhin and Rekedim do not return. The book stays in Riarhin's limited viewpoint to its final line; it does not follow Elyas home in a new viewpoint or resolve the board through hearsay.

Rekedim stays with the Ykrul. The captured money does not create peace by itself. Claims, grain exchanges, grazing rights and independent commands remain. He negotiates a bounded role linking grass camps and river factors, not Kargath's leadership or a sudden promotion to general. His choice has desire in it as well as duty. He sends a letter to Braedyn with Elyas.

Elyas goes east, back to Tarlington. Orvain is dead at the mill; it is Pell who is taken alive, heard with witnesses, and placed in Elyas's custody, with the written account of Orvain's death attached to the papers recovered there. Pell's testimony and the copied financial records travel with them. These establish a case against the undertaking without claiming that a distant court has already ruled on Merrow or every Vigil faction. Alric carries the record; Lyra accompanies the homeward party and intends to return for Serekh's further hearing. Riarhin helps them reach the eastern crossing, then leaves them capable of continuing without her.

Riarhin follows Kalrantha. She has heard the name twice before the catalogue: once in the northern books she can recite, and once in Kargath's mouth. That second hearing is the motive. Her reasoning is not that the amulet is powerful but that a careful and successful man spent years and money on it, came away with a rumour, and minded — which is worth an account and a dealer. Beneath it, unstated, is that the southern road is also a way of not going back to Tarlington. A catalogue in Pell's collateral papers lists an undelivered account of the Himadri temple with inserted leaves and a drawing of green glass in brass. A valuation dispute supplies a dealer, a forwarding house on the southern coast, and an Oshiiran correspondent. The book, not the amulet, is the lead. Its existence and contents still require checking. Do not imply Kargath possessed or used the amulet, that it caused the campaign, or that Riarhin has learned all its secrets from a price list. The relevant written sources are the Kalrantha Amulet section of `ttrpg/reference/travel/fates_edge_kingdoms_of_brass.tex` and the regional references; their rumours remain rumours. The new dealer correspondence is a fictional lead built within that established uncertainty.

The personal ending. Riarhin and Lyra part civilly without reconciliation. Rekedim and Riarhin's farewell is practical and affectionate. She gives Elyas an honest, insufficient message for Braedyn, then takes the southern road. The final request for directions is ordinary assistance, not her first-ever admission that someone else knows something.

APPENDIX

Working Rules

On Ykrul thought. The Ykrul are not humans differentiated only by steppe clothing and customs. Their slightly alien, geometric thought should affect what they notice and what constitutes a complete answer: separations, accessible approaches, relations between obligations, and the shape created by several positions at once. It is often shown by where they stand or move an object before words are needed. Dareth separates supporting positions to prevent one charge reaching both; Sava arranges prisoners so each can choose without passing the person who would forbid it; Oskel can retain gratitude to Kargath while opposing him. This is not universal emotionlessness, supernatural calculation, or a single shared opinion. Kinship, affection, fear, rivalry, mistakes and grief remain individual and real. Rekedim's Fenwood upbringing changes his experience without making him human. Use the existing Count material and the Gray Wanderer's Ykrul section as grounding; do not turn every utterance into an arithmetic proverb.

On magic. Consult `ttrpg/reference/expansions/gray_wanderers_grimoire.tex`, especially “Free Casting — The Grammar of the Raw Weave,” the Heal tag, and “Backlash — The Weave's Revenge.” Lyra's current on-page practice uses focused words, gesture and limited repair without introducing an unestablished Patron, Symbol or Thiasos. Healing is not generic luminous energy or an unlimited reserve spent until a body is whole. Repairing some flesh need not repair every injury or undo fatal blood loss. At Gyllan's death she improvises beyond the small repair previously established and suffers a minor physical backlash; the wounds remain too great. In the aftermath she uses ordinary care, keeps to a familiar formulation, and still cannot save every patient. The narrative shows this through Riarhin's observations rather than giving her access to the caster's mind or printing game mechanics. No new combat magic has been added to the confrontation.

On Riarhin's register. Her brevity is economical, not a performance of cleverness. With Rekedim, let the humour come from shared practical understanding, habitual differences, and unspoken assistance. With Elyas, teasing begins after she sees an unclaimed kindness: in the revised archive aftermath he cleans up Alric's broken bowl and pays for it without asking anyone to notice. Do not impose a quota of one-word replies or one emotional crack per chapter.

On her mother and the repertoire. Her mother was a courtesan and taught her social grace and protocol. Riarhin knows the current forms as well as the older ones, and sometimes lets Elyas assume her knowledge is fifty years out of date. He is observant about people but makes assumptions; he is not stupid. She dislikes deliberate inauthenticity and pageantry, not femininity, beauty, or pleasure. The blue silk in Chapter 8 is something she enjoys, not bait or a disguise. Keep the distinction alive without repeatedly explaining it.

On the trade she refused. Riarhin became a thief to avoid becoming a sex worker. This is a private biographical cause, not a declaration she makes or a slogan the narration repeats. Her mother's work and training need not be condemned for her refusal to be real. Show what she accepts for herself, what she will not wait to be paid for, and the attention which became useful in theft. Do not confuse this refusal with disliking sex, beauty, femininity, or her mother.

On the two gifts and the limits of knowledge. Riarhin has perfect memory and the gift of the body: peak physicality at every stage of her life. Her paternal grandmother was Lethai-thora; her conception was an experiment in breeding back both gifts. These are authorial facts, not evidence that Riarhin knows the purpose of her conception. They do not establish a childhood of laboratory tests, memory subtraction, a body that automatically solves its surroundings, an exact mechanism for arrested ageing, or immunity to fatigue, injury and pain. Do not import those extrapolations from the accompanying commentary. She never knew her father.

Her early reading came from the libraries of her mother's wealthy clients: selective collections of Ecktorian and Thepyric works, often assembled for display. Perfect retention preserves the limitations of those sources as faithfully as their useful contents. Remembering is distinct from noticing, understanding, judging and knowing whether a source was reliable. Her substantial northern experience can correct what she read; it does not give her firsthand knowledge everywhere. Preserve the draft's requests for local knowledge and other people's expertise. The GM comparison is an external metaphor, not an in-world role or a claim of omniscience.

On crossing the Amaranthine later. Reserve the sustained confrontation with book-learned assumptions for her later travels into Oshiira and eventually Ashaan. She can speak their languages as she learned them from books; this does not confer every living register, custom or local implication. Her gifts need not fail for her sources to prove partial, dated or misleading. Do not reduce all her languages to poor trade speech or declare every northern account false. The Amaranthine's Mediterranean analogy is a geographical aid for the author, not wording for the fiction. Seed the provenance of her reading lightly here, without turning the present novel into a preview of that journey.

On reading and teaching. Riarhin is well read and can cite chapter and verse accurately. She often misses the point of a quotation or axiom while correcting its wording or original context. Gyllan can misremember the text while using it to teach a moral or practical judgment. Neither is uniformly right: exact scholarship does not settle the lesson, and a useful lesson does not make a misquotation accurate. Let their exchanges do work for Lyra or Elyas rather than making either elder the butt of a repeated joke. The healing discussion in Chapter 11 establishes the difference without naming it in the narration.

On languages. Riarhin can speak any human language. She detests most of them, especially Acasi because it reminds her of Silkstrand, but the preference and its cause are only implied. Let sensory associations, a change of language, and her willingness to leave translating to somebody else carry it. Gyllan thinks he is the translator. He can be competent at it and still be unnecessary; the joke is the assumption, not his stupidity. She can answer the clause he has not translated or make a precise request after avoiding the language, then leave him the job. Do not make her confess the whole repertoire or explain the joke. Rekedim's Ykrul remains his own unadvertised competence.

On the later court book. Reserve the sustained disguise at Veralyn's court for the later Riarhin-viewpoint work described by the author. Veralyn and Jaennis recognise her; she uses that awareness while still facing real danger from others. She remains competent, hates the required performance, and must work hard at it. Seed the repertoire here; do not import the later court's chronology or claim that she is incapable of using the skills she dislikes.

On the game. Nobody compares anything to it. Ever. They use it. The vocabulary is operational: count your exits; fork the far mouth; two exits, both priced; seed is a depot, not a daring; lane welding; the ninth rim; she never checks behind. If a line could be cut and replaced with "it was like a game of Canray," the line is wrong and the scene has not done its work.

On the three names. Never gloss them. A Vhasian says Canray, an Aeler says K'thra, a Ykrul says Kon'reh, and the book never stops to tell you they are the same, and the reader gets it inside forty pages and feels clever, which is the correct transaction.

On the blades. The name Remorse is never printed in the corpus and is not printed here; she has the sword, she says the word perhaps twice, and no one in the book knows what it means, including her. Toothpick is Toothpick and was Fate's Edge and she does not use the old name. The Last Blade is a knife and also a man and also the sixth gambit, and the novel is not going to point that out.

And the two hands are never mentioned by anybody. Every fight in this book should specify which blade she drew. That is the whole of the device. Six words a scene, twenty-odd scenes, and by the end the reader has a ledger she does not have.

On the Hollow and the Ninth. Neither belongs in this book. The Ykrul material will pull toward them — the ninth step, the ninth rim, the counting — and it must be allowed to pull and never arrive. Whatever is not explained here is not being withheld coyly; it is not the subject.

On numbers. The Belworth books count in nines and elevens and this is one of them. Six steps is the exception and is load-bearing precisely because it is the wrong number for this shelf: it is a Ykrul count in a Vhasian book, and it is the moment the novel stops being played on the party's board.

On the next volume. Roughly two years pass and the party is new. Volume two opens in Silkstrand, which she hates, with an ageing Ykrul warrior, Na'ar, and an antagonist out of her childhood there: Lian, granddaughter of someone she knew before she left, now a crime lord and a member of the Velvet Court. Nothing of this is stated here. The permissible seeding in this book is the southern road, the forwarding house on the coast, the thin shutters of a Silkstrand courtyard, and Riarhin declining to think about any of it.

On telling the Ykrul apart. Three commanders arrive inside one paragraph in Chapter 21 and a reader will lose them. Each carries one physical handle, planted at first mention and re-used: Dareth the healed bowstring cut across her right palm, which Iserin turns over and holds too long in Chapter 28; Oskel the short spear carried reversed, used as a pointer on the ground and the drawing and never his hand; Sava the knotted cord round her saddle horn, which becomes the count of the dead in Chapter 24. Iserin has the burn scar and the leather between her teeth, Velkan the ink on the pad of his thumb, Amru the clouded eye. Do not add a second handle to anybody and do not explain any of them.

On Lyra's silence. She works out the two hands somewhere in Act III and says nothing, and the saying nothing is the last kindness she does her. The beat is in Chapter 28, after the stitching: she looks at what Riarhin set down on the end of the bench, arrives somewhere, and declines to come back with it. It is the first thing she has ever refused to tell her, and it is what makes Goodbye, Riarhin a decision rather than an absence. The narration never states what she worked out and Riarhin never learns.

On Elyas and the loss that teaches nothing. His education is otherwise legible to him, which is the danger; every cost gets converted into a lesson he can carry. One must not convert. In Chapter 28 a letter forwarded twice reaches a dead man: one of the bridge men Gyllan found work for is gone, with a wife and a child, through no fault of his own, and his name is nowhere in the papers because Gyllan wrote those letters from the men's own account of themselves and the names went with him. Elyas files the sheet where it does not belong and does not make it into anything. Resist the urge to let him say what it cost or what he would do again.

On Braedyn and Riarhin. They are contemporaries. They served together under the black banners and came out of the same war, he as a man who keeps a ledger and she as the other thing, and neither has ever found a use for saying so aloud. This is never explained and never flashed back to. It is carried in two places only. In the corridor in Chapter 4, Riarhin's hands are steady because she has done that exact work before, on somebody she knew, and the steadiness is the worst part of it: “Keep pressure here. Don't lift it to look” is a field instruction, said in a voice with no teaching in it, and Braedyn supplies the other half of the same drill from the floor. Because Act I is Elyas's limited viewpoint, he sees all of it and understands none of it, which is the point. In Chapter 8 Gyllan names the campaign once, flatly, and stops. Nothing else in the book returns to it; the sequel may.

On the Edgewalker. Canon, and not a leftover. Edgewalker is the Aeler licence for a rogue sent out of the holds to prospect for leads — the structural opposite of a True Mason. Profitable chaos against responsibility; the roads ye shall not enter against the roads ye shall keep. Both are Aeler, and the resemblance ends there. The novel already stages the pair without naming the pairing: Vaul declines to certify the north bridge and loses his contract and his men; Keld keeps her hands clean and is right about the marches anyway. Vaul names her title once, flatly, in Chapter 12, because a True Mason answers what he is asked; the narration never glosses it and nobody compares the two aloud.

On the four knives. Tulgoth had a dagger made for each son and one for himself, and his was Fate's Edge, which Riarhin took off him and calls Toothpick. The three brothers' blades are alike and unalike on purpose: shared turn of guard and seam of grip, but the weight and the grip of each were made to a particular man and his particular way of fighting, and none was given better work than the others — they were equal in Tulgoth's eyes. The Last Blade carries the same family marks and disagrees with the set in every other respect, because Tulgoth never met Kargath. It was made to a description rather than to a man: correct everywhere, shaped to nobody's hand, of a length that does not follow from the other three. It is not a better knife and must never be written as one. It is what Tulgoth hoped the son he was not shown would turn out to be. Riarhin reads this off the object in Chapter 27 before Kargath says “You were kept away,” and the book does not explain it further.

On the axis of travel. The book runs east to west and back again: Tarlington and the Belworth in the east, then Vhas and the Acasian marches, then the Violet Steppe furthest west. Outbound is west — the western road, the western registry, the western receipt, the marches, the grass. The return in Act III is therefore east, through Acasia and Vaul's bridges to the Belworth. Rekedim's people fled south and east off the steppe into the Fens, which is the same geometry seen from the other end. Riarhin's departure is the third direction: south, towards the coast. Check every bearing against this; the earlier draft sent the homeward party west.

On length and shape. A Riarhin prologue and three acts, containing the six planning sequences above. Act I is Elyas's close third-person limited viewpoint; Acts II and III are Riarhin's. Sequence Two is the longest and Sequence Six the shortest. The novel is front-loaded with procedure and back-loaded with grass, and the hinge is Gyllan taking up a sword to defend Lyra at the end of Act II. His death leads directly into the captive scout, the disputed offer of help, and the journey to the opposition camp.
