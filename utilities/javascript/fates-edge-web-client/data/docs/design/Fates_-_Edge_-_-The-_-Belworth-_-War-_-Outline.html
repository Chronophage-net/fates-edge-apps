The Belworth War

Chapter outline — working draft

What this is. A drafting outline built from `the_belworth_war_treatment.md`. The treatment governs whenever the two disagree. This document turns each treatment chapter into scenes, beats, plants and payoffs, and records what each viewpoint knows at each point. Anything marked (prov.) is a proposal made here to let drafting start: a name, a place, a title, a scene mechanism. The author can replace any of them without changing the agreed arcs.

Current structure. Thirty-five chapters and seven viewpoints. The author's return chronology supersedes the earlier interleaving: Elyas left Vhasia as Valdais reached Sennet; Elyas and Lyra reach the Belworth after Fairport is sacked. Chapters 1–13 follow the war, Fairport, and Jaennis's court without either returned traveller. Their return opens Part III in chapter 14. The final fifteen chapters retain their numbering. Treatment and outline use this same order.

Family correction. Braedyn the Younger is the third of five siblings, not the youngest.

Settled, carried over from the treatment. The decisive southern battle and Valdais's forced fallback. Veralyn's bargain with Fayd, priced at a permanent intelligence office. The coordinated attacks on Marcott (from the north, through the middle cantons) and on Fairport (from the sea). Fayd stays a Vigil man, and by the end he means to groom Veralyn as empress of a new Utaran Empire. Jaennis loves Veralyn and means to draw her into the fair company. Riarhin is absent from the main action. Braedyn and Oswyn are both dead in the prologue, and neither death is given a date.

Continuation. Another book follows this war novel, opening with Ouen Longblood. His separate preview is in `the_belworth_war_sequel_preview.md`. Kalrantha’s gift belongs to the later continuation, with its exact placement open; the earlier proposed graveyard prologue must not displace the newly requested Ouen opening. The pear-tree ending remains the end of this manuscript.

Future canon recorded here. Tylan Vayle ends this book as Lord Marshal. In later books he marries Veralyn and gives her an heir. Both of them know what the marriage is and what it isn't. See "Tylan Vayle." Nothing in this book states the marriage; it is seeded only.

Draft decisions from the editorial pass

The current manuscript uses Anneth throughout; Bessa was her earlier draft name, not a second northern lover. Preserve the horse recovery, mutual affection, refusal to go south, and fond farewell together in her arc. Lyra's memory is attentive and fallible: she checks disputed wording against contemporary notes.

The author's latest reading notes retain the ordinary ending at Elyas and Mara's prospective house, with the pear tree and kitchen. Constano's return, the unanswered letter to Riarhin, and the board belong earlier in the closing chapter. The board is no longer the final image.

Strengthen Ysabeau's visible withdrawal at the first bridge, give the released carters physical presence, and connect Jaennis's and Fayd's road negotiations directly to Seniro's military requirements. Valdais remains outside the viewpoint structure; a captured supply instruction near the battle adds his practical voice. Veralyn's commitment of the last mounted reserve towards the Kettling link, on an incompletely checked report, carries the late Myrmis turn without explaining it.

Shape and length

  • Target length: about 115,000–125,000 words. The book is as long as the material needs and no longer. The limit is no filler, not a word count.
  • Structure: a prologue, then thirty-five chapters in five parts. Chapters average about 3,400 words. Battle and sack chapters may run to 5,000; transitions and Tylan's chapters can be shorter.
  • Viewpoints: six principal and one minor, all in close third person. Fayd and Valdais never get one. Both are present as people through scenes and their own words: Fayd in the suppers, the demonstrations and the window; Valdais through two letters (ch. 11 and ch. 31), his terms (ch. 13), and a woman who rides for him (ch. 22).

Prologue — The men who could answer (Constano)

Place: Tarlington, the evening after Braedyn's funeral observances. News of Oswyn's death reached the house some days earlier.

Does not know: when Elyas will arrive, or whether Elyas has heard of either death.

Beats

  1. The household meal nobody wants. A servant sets the duke's cup through habit and takes it away again without being told. Seniro left before the meal to go back to the river. Braedyn the Younger is at table and does most of the talking, because talking is his work. Constano's foot aches from the day's standing.
  2. The letter to Istovar. An answer is owed on a river-toll matter that Braedyn would have written straight to Oswyn, old man to old man. It will now reach Harald, who will read it three times and ask for assurances. Constano drafts it twice. The second draft is shorter. Then a third letter, to Riarhin, telling her of Braedyn's death, addressed care of the last commission she wrote from. He does not know where it will find her. It is the shortest of the three.
  3. The oral promise. A Fairport carrier (prov.: a grain factor named Wat Crane) waits in the hall. Braedyn promised him, face to face, that the house would stand behind his losses if he kept running barges through the war. There is nothing in writing. Constano believes him. He also knows his father's personal credit does not pass to him intact. He gives his own guarantee, with a limit on it, in writing. It is his first decision as head of the house, and it concerns living people.
  4. The study. He goes in for a paper. The board stands where it has always stood, with the dust on its velvet. A servant asks whether it should be put away now. Constano says to leave it. One line; no more.
  5. Close. He commits himself to going to Fairport to run the army's supply. An order leaves the room under his own seal. Someone downstairs, Crane, now expects him to keep a promise.

Plants: Crane's barges (paid off in Ch. 4 and Ch. 10). The limit Constano set on the guarantee. The letter to Riarhin (Ch. 35, and the later continuation). The board (for the later continuation). Tone: plain, domestic. The funeral is already over when the book opens; there is no deathbed.

Part I — The War Without Them

1. A day bought at the crossing (Seniro)

Place: a bridge on the Viterran bank above Kettling. Valdais's advance guard is on the far side.

Does not know: that Corbel has kept his levy at home.

  1. The order behind the stand. Seniro holds the bridge because a convoy of wounded and powder barges is not yet past the bend below. He has worked out the hours and the barges' speed. The bridge matters only for those hours.
  2. The fight. It is Caesarian and exact. Valdais probes, Seniro makes him deploy, and deploying costs Valdais the afternoon. Seniro is loud with his men and precise with his captains. A Vhasian officer takes the loudness for simplicity and nearly loses a company finding out otherwise. Dame Ysabeau de Corvaunt pulls it out before the trap closes. Seniro sees who did it and says so to his captains, with approval.
  3. The discovery. The convoy should have been past by noon and is late. The reason is that Corbel's two hundred, who were meant to hold the ferry landing, never came. Seniro has been buying time for a movement the rebellion had already stopped.
  4. Close. He fires the bridge at dusk, as planned, and sends a rider to Corbel. The letter is courteous. His captains know how he sounds when he is being courteous.

Plants: the Fenwood officers' knowledge of the raised roads, which Seniro uses without comment (paid off in Ch. 30). Braedyn the Younger arrives as liaison to the barons and is the man who reads Corbel's reply.

2. The empty encampment (Veralyn)

Place: the flint hills, at the ground the earls chose for battle.

Does not know: how far the valley barons have gone. She knows only that "some are slow."

  1. The ground. Tylan's scouts bring her onto a field the earls prepared well: stakes set, a ditch dug, a stream dammed. Nobody is on it. Smoke is rising from three valleys, in three directions. Veralyn sees at once what the dam cost the meadows below and whose oxen hauled the stakes, and she says so. Tylan counts positions, not costs, and notices that she is counting something else. (Myrmis stage: she sees the price.) At the edge of the field an old herder will not bring his sheep across the stream. He gives no reason, and Tylan moves the horse-lines to where he does graze.
  2. What Tylan saved. Tylan had sent a half-troop round the back of the field the night before. It has taken the earls' baggage road, forty prisoners, and a granary. Wenna, Tylan's chief scout, found the road. The day is not wasted. It is still a day.
  3. The two letters. A letter from Jaennis says the valley is unquiet and the court is managing. A letter from Seniro, eight days old, says he is buying days. Veralyn does the arithmetic out loud: go south now and leave the north to burn behind her, or stay and let the south fail. Tylan does not tell her which. He tells her how many horses are fit.
  4. The evening. At the steward's house where she is billeted, the steward's widowed daughter, Anneth, tells Veralyn her dancing is better than her taste in wine. Veralyn begins it. It is mutual, easy and adult, and nothing punishes her for it. Anneth will not have Shadowsteel in the bedroom. She lays it outside the door with the courtesy owed to a guest. In the morning a servant has brought it back in, and says so. There is a stone-shelf by the hearth in Anneth's house, and Veralyn doesn't ask about it.
  5. Close. Before dawn she writes to Jaennis. The letter is warm, specific, and mentions Anneth by the song Anneth sang rather than by name.

Plants: Tylan's sheets, where he writes down positions and nothing else (from the older books). The letter to Jaennis, which reaches her in Ch. 8. Shadowsteel on Veralyn's hip, doing nothing, and a household that keeps it outside a door. The stone-shelf.

3. The first invitation (Jaennis)

Place: Valora, the dowager's household. Jaennis holds the seals that Veralyn left her.

Does not know: about Veralyn's letter mentioning Anneth, which is still on the road. Fayd is not yet at court.

  1. The supper, with the petition inside it. One continuous scene.
    • Music, good food, and a room other people were not invited into. Joan Hask is seated at table with an ease that makes a visiting canon's wife go still. Lady Margery answers Jaennis in a tone her husband would know if he were there. Nobody explains any of it, and the reader doesn't need them to.
    • Between courses an assessor brings the matter of Corbel's toll-post to the table itself, because the dowager hears petitions where she sits. It establishes the toll disputes Elyas will encounter on his later return. She settles it with a narrow, enforceable order without letting the music stop. Margery watches her do it and likes her better for it.
  2. The pleasure is written as pleasure. Make it delicious and exact. Late peaches in wine; river eel with verjuice and green sauce; a dish of Fairport oranges that came up on Crane's barges against the odds and are handed round like jewels. A viol, and a Vhasian singer whose words the canon's wife pretends not to understand. Margery in a green almost too bold for Valora. A game of forfeits in which Jaennis loses once, on purpose, and pays the forfeit well. Keep it in Jaennis's point of view: whom she wants beside her, and whose departure she puts off.
  3. A line from Pellam. He notes quietly which parts of the order the delegated seals do not actually reach. She hears him and signs anyway. One line. The test comes in Ch. 8.
  4. Close. Margery stays. The dressing room. Keep it to implication.

Reader: delight. The reader should want to be at that table, and should notice, pleasantly, that the most capable person in Valora is also the one enjoying herself most.

Plants: the limits of the seals (paid off in Ch. 8, Ch. 13 and Ch. 34). Pellam, before Lyra meets him. Margery's husband's office.

4. What Fairport can send (Constano)

Place: Fairport, the Fenwood counting house on the river quays. There is a crossing to Marcott.

Does not know: that the middle cantons have agreed terms with Valdais.

  1. The work. Choices with consequences:
    • which captain is paid first (the one whose men will desert, not the one who shouts);
    • which grain convoy gets the escort;
    • whose guarantee a frightened family will accept because it is Conlin's.

His competence works through people as well as figures.

  1. Marcott. He crosses in Piero Tireno's boat to dine with Lucrezia. They are old friends with a long common history, and they have a joint arrangement: Tireno boats, Fenwood credit, grain for the army moved through Vhasian Marcott, where Valdais's customs cannot reach. It works because both of them are exposed if it fails. Show the people who have agreed to take the risk together.
  2. The hard consignment. A powder and grain convoy for Seniro. Constano gets it moving by spending standing he cannot replace. He calls in an old favour from a Fairport guild-master, who will never do him another.
  3. Warning signs. Lucrezia mentions that cantonal buyers from the middle cantons have been paying in advance for Marcott warehouse space. Constano writes it down. It is a real piece of information, and he reads it wrongly as speculation.
  4. Close. Crane's barges go up-river loaded. Constano's foot is bad on the quay steps. He takes them one at a time, as he always has.

Plants: Lucrezia, Piero, the Tireno warehouses, the counting house records, the harbour captain Dunstan, the seaward works (the mole and the chain tower), and the middle-cantons buyers.

5. The offer from an earl (Veralyn)

Place: the flint hills, at a shepherds' hall under truce.

Does not know: about Jaennis's order on Corbel's post.

  1. Dunmere's terms. He will submit, keep his lands and his court, and hand over the Earl of Hask's hiding place. It would shorten the campaign. It would also teach every other earl what waiting can buy.
  2. Tylan tests it. He does not argue character. He sends two riders to the hiding place Dunmere named, with orders to be seen coming. They find it recently empty. The offer was real, and Dunmere has already warned Hask.
  3. Veralyn decides. She accepts Dunmere's submission, but without the court. His son goes to Valora as a ward. It is a real decision and she owns it. Dunmere dislikes it and signs. Tylan offered a milder form. She declined it without arguing, and she enjoys the son's removal slightly more than the merits call for. (Myrmis stage: she cannot let a wrong go.) Tylan finds that he has written down where the sword lay overnight. He records the positions of people, never of things, and he crosses the entry out.
  4. Anneth. The second night is easier than the first. Veralyn notices she has assumed there will be a third. Anneth notices too, and says so, lightly.
  5. Close. A courier from the south brings a list of baronial levies that have failed. The list is longer than the last.

6. The levy that will not come (Seniro)

Place: the valley, at Corbel's hall.

Does not know: that a Vigil agent has been meeting the barons.

  1. The barons' case. Seniro rides to Corbel with forty men and Braedyn the Younger. Corbel's grievances are real:
    • Fenwood drainage took his wet meadows in his grandfather's time;
    • the toll-rights on the river crossings were Fenwood-granted and never shared;
    • the queen's peace took his men in 841 and sent back two thirds of them.

The Vigil has supplied the words and the confidence, not the wrongs.

  1. The temptation. Seniro could make Corbel afraid enough to obey. He has the men to hang Corbel's toll-sergeant at his own post. His captains want it done. Braedyn the Younger says quietly that three other barons are watching to see whether he will.
  2. The choice. He does neither thing cleanly. He burns the toll-post and takes the sergeant as a prisoner, not a hanged man. He tells Corbel to his face that the old drainage grievance will be heard after the war. That costs him the Corbel family's friendship, which his father's name had kept intact for thirty years.
  3. Close. On the ride back, Braedyn the Younger says phrases Elyas will later hear in Ch. 14 are being used at Maresse and at Lisle.

7. The war that will not stand (Tylan)

Place: the flint hills, about ten days of Reaping, along the relay line and the drove-roads.

Does not know: that Fayd has been received at court, or that Seniro has burned Corbel's post.

  1. The kind of war it is. The earls will not give battle. They take relay posts at night, fire peat-stacks, run off forage, and are gone by morning into country they were born in. Each raid is small. Together they are a campaign against the army's winter. Tylan sees it as exactly that: they are not fighting her soldiers, they are fighting her fodder and her letters.
  2. Reading the hills. Wenna teaches him, and he lets her, because she is better at it than he is.
    • The herders are bringing the flocks down from high pasture. Where flocks move freely, the earls' men are not. Where a herder will not graze, somebody has been.
    • The ridge-cairns are boundary marks and waymarks both, and hill people read who has passed by what has been added or taken.
    • The shepherds' halls are truce-ground with their own law: nobody draws steel under a hall roof, and every hall-keeper knows every rider for a day around.

Tylan puts none of it on a map. He writes down which hall-keepers will talk.

  1. Grain and exits. He stops guarding passes and starts guarding hay. His troopers go onto the halls' hay-barns and peat-stacks and along the drove-roads, so that it is the earls' horses, not the queen's, that go into the cold hungry. He leaves the earls their roads home on purpose. An earl who can go home will.
  2. The fight at Scarth Fold (prov.). Hask's riders catch a forage party at a sheepfold in the rain. Tylan comes with the relief himself. This is the one fight in the book seen from inside his body, and it should frighten the reader a little. He is the largest man on the field and the fastest, faster than anything that size should be, and the hill riders know his name. Keep it short and exact. What he sees is where the raiders' horses are tethered and which way the ground drains, and he goes for the horses, not the men. The men who meet him directly do not get up. The rest break before he reaches them. Afterwards he is irritated, because having to be there means his arrangements failed. He does not enjoy what the men say about him, and he uses it.
  3. The crows. Before the march back, a hill sergeant counts the crows over the pass aloud, gets an odd number, and says the pass is bad. Tylan holds the column a day, because men who believe a pass is bad march it badly, and morale is a supply. Next day the pass is empty. At the top there are fresh horse-droppings, a night old. He writes down the droppings and not the crows.
  4. Veralyn from outside. He has known her since they were children at Tarlington. He sees what the war is doing to her the way you see weight come off a friend: not in any one day. She judges the raider who burned a relay-keeper's house. The judgement is right, it comes too fast, and she doesn't look at the man's hands, which she once would have. Tylan says nothing. That night he moves the picket line so that it no longer passes Anneth's door, without being asked and without telling anyone why. He has known where the queen's heart goes since they were fourteen and has never needed her to say it. The pickets are the whole of his part in it.
  5. Close. His sheet for the ten days: posts held, posts lost, fodder in days, mounts fit. The relay line is cut in three places. He adds a line for the queen: letters from the south will take twelve days now, not eight.

Reader: cold, and the patience of a war that will not give battle. One burst of real fear when Tylan fights. Then, for the first time, Veralyn seen from outside by someone who loves her.

Plants: the cut relay line (the news of Fairport arrives twelve days late in Ch. 12). The halls, the herders and the cairns (paid off in Ch. 24). Tylan's name in the hills. The pickets moved from Anneth's door.

Part II — The Port Burns

8. The limits of the seals (Jaennis)

Place: Valora: the chancery bench, the dowager's dressing room, and her audience chamber.

Does not know: that Elyas knows Fayd, or how close Marcott is to falling.

  1. The seals tested in public. Baron Corbel's steward contests the Ch. 3 order at the chancery bench. The dowager's delegated seals reach tolls on crown roads, he says, not a baron's own ferry-landing. He is right, and Pellam told her so. Jaennis gives up the point in public, gracefully. Then she wins the substance through a household instead: Corbel's wife's sister is in the company, and the landing reopens by private arrangement inside a week. The law lost and the household won. For the first time she sees clearly how much of her government runs outside the seals, and she likes it more than she should.
  2. The company as a network. A morning at the dressing table. News arrives with the laundry, the hairdresser and the sisters' letters, and Joan sorts it. Establish the company's method here, before Fayd's office is a rival: fast, warm, hard to check. Jaennis weighs a rumour by who is repeating it, not by what it says.
  3. Fayd, the second meeting. He reports the release of five ferrymen, an earlier undertaking distinct from the twelve carters and Tomas in Ch. 17. She spars with him in open audience over whose road it was, and it's quick and funny and nobody else in the room keeps up. She sees in the same sentence that he is useful and that he is dangerous, and she thinks each is the price of the other. Afterwards she has Joan see that one of the company is always within hearing of his anteroom. He notices within a day and says nothing, which tells her he has done the same to her.
  4. The report checked. Jaennis asks for the ferrymen’s examinations as well as Fayd’s report. She notices an unnamed carrier and asks who paid for the transport. Lyra and the western evidence have not yet returned.
  5. The letter from the north. Veralyn's letter from Ch. 2 arrives at last. Jaennis reads it twice, the second time for what it doesn't say. Anneth appears only as a song. Jealousy is not quite the word for what she feels. She writes down the name of the song.
  6. Close. A letter from her father at Fairport: Conlin writing to his daughter, short and practical, with a joke about the harbour captain. It is the last letter she will have from him for weeks.

Reader: the pleasure of watching competence at work, and at the close a small chill, because the reader knows what is coming to Fairport and she doesn't.

9. Across the river (Constano)

Place: Fairport and Marcott.

Does not know: that the corsairs have been hired, or that the sea attack is timed to Marcott.

  1. Pressure. Middle-canton companies move south on the Vhasian bank above Marcott. Lucrezia asks Constano, plainly, whether Fairport will receive her people if it comes to that.
  2. He prepares for refuge without calling it that: warehouse space in Fairport for Tireno stock; the refugee quay, an inland quay up the harbour cut, designated; boats assigned. It is sound work. It moves harbour craft and men inland.
  3. Credit. He works out what displacement will do to credit, boats and warehouses, and to the lightermen who work for Piero. The Fenwood money stands behind the Tireno name. It is written, and it is limited.
  4. Harbour captain Dunstan reports sails off the headland: fishing boats, he thinks. Constano asks for a watch on the chain tower. It is kept for two nights and then the men are needed at the refugee quay. The reader should notice this.
  5. Close. A Marcott signal fire at night, from the north side of the town.

10. The wrong horizon (Constano)

Place: Fairport, over a single night and into the morning.

  1. Marcott falls from the north. Middle-canton companies take the upper town. The Tirenos and their dependants cross the river in Piero's boats. Fairport commits boats, crews and attention to the refugee quay.
  2. The corsairs come from the sea. Their sails are the "fishing boats" from Ch. 9. The watch on the chain tower was lifted to work the refugee quay, so the chain is down for the lighters coming through. Fairport is caught doing necessary work, not neglecting its duty.
  3. The breakdown, stage by stage:
    • a landing on the mole cuts the chain tower off from its defenders;
    • fire cuts off the counting house's inner store;
    • Master Odo Fenn cannot reach the refugee quay because the harbour cut is burning;
    • Constano gives an order to hold the quay gates, and ten minutes later has to reverse it when the gates would trap the refugees.
  4. People and records. He can save the ledgers of the Fenwood credit or the Tireno children, and he does not choose the children because it is noble. He can carry one box and the children can walk, so he takes the box. Then he sends the one steady clerk with the children, which leaves him alone with the box and his foot. Aldith, his secretary of eleven years, refuses to leave him. When his foot gives out on the mole steps, she takes the box. (Prov.: what he actually saves is the credit books and seal, Lucrezia and eleven Tireno dependants, Crane, and four clerks.)
  5. The escape. It is physically hard and is not humiliating. He is carried over one wall in a coal basket by two lightermen. He hates it, and he thanks them by name.
  6. Close. From a salt-marsh dyke at dawn, Fairport burning and the corsairs' boats already loading.

Reader: harrowing, and specific. The refugee quay is where the Marcott families were sent for safety, and it sits between the burning warehouses and the water. Constano hears a Tireno child calling for Piero by name, and he cannot go back. The system breaking is the frame. That voice is what the reader keeps.

Knowledge after this chapter: Constano knows it was coordinated. Nobody else will know for days.

11. The supplies that do not arrive (Seniro)

Place: the river line.

Does not know: that Fairport has fallen.

  1. A barge does not arrive. It is one of Crane's, and it is not there.
  2. Contradictory reports follow: Fairport is burning; Fairport is holding; Marcott has fallen; Marcott is resisting. Then a demand from a baron's steward to protect a withdrawal already under way from Fairport's up-river depots.
  3. The position. Seniro holds a road junction that lets the depot convoys escape, at the cost of the Fenwood mills at Ashford (prov.), which Valdais burns. The house will not easily replace them. The mills fed half the valley.
  4. Valdais takes advantage of the gap immediately and well, and presses a crossing Seniro can no longer afford to contest.
  5. Valdais in his own words. Under a flag, a Vhasian herald brings a letter for the Marshal of the Belworth in Valdais's own hand. Quote it at length in the draft, read aloud by Seniro to Braedyn the Younger.
    • It offers to exchange the wounded taken at the burned mills, man for man and rank for rank, at fair ransom. The list of names is attached, every name spelled correctly.
    • It observes, without heat, that the Kettling crossing will be his by the new moon, and suggests Seniro's men be spared the trouble of dying for it.
    • It asks after Constano's health, courteously, as a man asks who knows there can be no answer.
    • The courtesy is complete. It is the most frightening thing in the chapter, because it is the voice of a man who has already done the arithmetic Seniro is only beginning. Seniro accepts the exchange, answers in his own hand, and keeps the letter.
  6. Close. Seniro asks for Constano's report, as he has done for twenty years. The rider has none. That is when he understands.

12. The price of turning south (Veralyn)

Place: the flint hills.

  1. Reliable news of the sack arrives, twelve days late, by the relay line the earls have cut (Ch. 7).
  2. The weight she carries: horses, wounded, three hundred prisoners, and a northern settlement that is only half made.
  3. Tylan has planned for part of this. He has already sent a remount column south and has fodder at two relay stations. He could not plan for the loss of Fairport's credit, which pays for both.
  4. The cost of going is as real as the cost of staying. Hask is still in the hills. If she turns south now, Dunmere's submission will not hold. She states the price of each choice aloud before Tylan can. (Myrmis stage: she speaks in prices.) Northern soldiers have started setting a small stone on Shadowsteel's chape when she hangs it up. She sees it and does not stop them. When Tylan asks, a sergeant reports it as a hill custom, which it is.
  5. Anneth. Veralyn asks her to come south. Anneth says no: her household is here and her father's steward's books are here. Part of her reason, never spoken, is what she has seen happening to Veralyn. She glances at the hearth-shelf once while refusing. It is Veralyn's first refusal in the book. She takes it well, and it surprises her that she has to.
  6. Close. She sends a rider to Seniro saying she will come. The order is dated, and the rider will take eight days.

Reader: weight. Every choice here costs something the reader has seen. The only relief is Anneth's refusal, and it hurts.

13. A court after the news (Jaennis)

Place: Valora.

  1. Hospitality becomes practical. People who need beds, money, introductions and news of missing relatives already know where to go: to the dowager's rooms. The relief arrives and the argument arrives with it, in the same hour: the company disagrees about who comes first. Joan wants the Fairport guild-wives. Margery wants her husband's officers' families. Catrin wants the children.
  2. Constano is missing. He is her father. His last letter, the one with the joke about the harbour captain, is still on her table. The news breaks her composure in private and without an audience, in a way a missing office would not.
  3. Fayd's second result. He clears a real obstruction between relief and the people who need it: a toll-baron's stores are opened to the refugees. He begins receiving requests that would once have gone to separate officers. Jaennis notices how they are accumulating and accepts it.
  4. Room for someone she wants near. Catrin's house burned at Fairport. Jaennis lodges her in the dowager's wing, with a sound public reason and a private one the reader understands.
  5. The first race. Both networks go looking for Constano. Fayd's office asks at the fen ferries and the carriers' posts. The company asks the wives. A lighterman's wife writes to her sister, who dresses Margery's hair, that a lame gentleman and a great many children came through a fen village three days ago. The company has it a day before Fayd's office does. Jaennis sends him the word, unasked, and he thanks her, with a note that he might have found it by Thursday.
    • This is also the first of the standing working suppers, twice a week from now on: petitions and a jug. They are too busy for anything more.
  6. Valdais's terms. A Vhasian herald reaches Valora by way of Marcott, under flag, carrying terms in Valdais's own hand. They are drafted as amendments to the Peace of Belworth, item by item, and Ethor is one of its parties. (Prov.) He asks for three things:
    • the Kettling crossing and the Vhasian-married valley baronies (Maresse, Lisle, Corbel) to pass under Ethor's protection;
    • the Belworth tolls to be levied at Marcott's rates;
    • Fairport's staple right, now ashes, to lapse.

There is no claimant in it, no crown, and not a word about the queen's title. He wants the river, and he wants it in the Peace's own language. Jaennis knows the Peace nearly by heart and admires the drafting. The seals cannot answer terms of peace, and for once their limit is a relief: she sends the terms north to the queen. They are signed in Valdais's name and the cantons', not the Vhasian crown's. Jaennis notes the absence and cannot tell whether it means distance or discretion. Fayd reads the terms over her shoulder. She says it before he does: the lords Valdais offers protection are the lords Fayd has been writing to, so the turn will be a bidding war. He concedes it with pleasure.

  1. Close. Pellam brings Jaennis a list of the offices whose petitioners now go to Fayd first. She reads it and puts it under the seals.

Reader: at first the numbness of a court doing necessary work, and then the one private break, which should land harder for being the only one.

Part III — The Return

14. A road with three masters (Elyas)

Place: a valley road two days north of Tarlington, at Maresse’s landing, where Hew holds the passage after Fairport’s fall.

Does not know: the details of either death, if the author chooses (see the note below). He also does not know that Fayd is in the valley.

  1. The crossing. Elyas rides with Lyra, Alric, the witnesses and Pell under the hired escort. At the post, three claims meet on one passage:
    • Maresse's new charge, backed by his watch;
    • the Fenwood safe-conduct the carriers already paid for;
    • frightened carriers who have now paid twice.
  2. His name opens the gate. The toll-sergeant knows the Summer Knight and lets the party through with bows. Elyas watches the next wagon pulled aside the moment his back is turned. His name settles his own passage and nobody else's.
  3. He goes back. He secures one concrete thing: a carrier's mule held as surety, and the carrier's daughter held with it as a "witness to the debt." He gets both out by putting his own name to the carrier's toll. The carriers are grateful, loudly. He has taken on an obligation to Corbel's post that he cannot enforce once he leaves.
  4. What people repeat. At the evening halt, carriers and a hedge-priest repeat phrases that sound borrowed: the queen's peace ends where the queen's army ends, and a baron who feeds his own is no traitor. Lyra notices that three different people use the same words. Nobody shows him a manifesto.
  5. The deaths. (Drafting choice.) Either a Fenwood rider meets them with the news of both deaths, or Elyas learns of Braedyn's death at the halt and Oswyn's only in Ch. 16. The recommended version is that he learns both here, from a rider who does not know which to say first. The reader already knows.
  6. Close. Tarlington's roofs from the rise, where Riarhin once stopped. He does not stop.

Plants: Corbel's post and Corbel's men. The carrier's daughter, who returns in Ch. 20. The borrowed phrases, which return in Ch. 17 and Ch. 23.

15. North without the office (Constano)

Place: marsh roads and a fenland village north of Fairport.

  1. The load. Constano and his reduced company: Lucrezia and eleven Tireno dependants, Crane, four clerks, the credit books and the seal. Keep faith with what he saved. The box is heavy, and the children are hungry.
  2. Improvised routes. His letters go out by a peat-boat, a priest's boy and a drover. Some reach people who can no longer obey them. One reaches a Fairport depot that was abandoned three days earlier.
  3. A local success that proves he is still himself. He finds shelter and food for the people with him by pledging Fenwood credit to a fen-reeve who has never heard of Constano but has heard of Conlin. His name still works across a counter.
  4. It cannot rebuild Fairport's machinery. That contrast is the injury.
  5. Close. His absence produces a decision elsewhere that he would have disputed if anyone could have asked him: Jaennis confirms a Fairport grain contract through Fayd's intermediary. The reader sees it in a letter Constano will receive two weeks too late.

16. The people who stayed (Elyas)

Place: Tarlington, and then Valora.

Does not know: that Fayd is about to be received at court.

  1. Mara. She lived through Braedyn's decline and death while Elyas was away. She met the physician every morning and sat with the duke on the bad nights. Elyas starts to tell her about the west and stops himself. He asks her instead, and listens. She tells him about the last week. Their private time is private and warm.
  2. The duke's study. Constano is already gone to Fairport. Elyas stands in the doorway and does not go in. (Point of view is Elyas, so the board only registers as the one dusty thing in the room.)
  3. The evidence. The papers against Merrow go into a named custody: the Crown's chancery at Valora, received by Pellam under a docket signed by both of them. Alric goes with the damaged pages. Pell is lodged under guard. The witnesses are given hearings, with dates.
  4. Lyra decides. She had meant to go back to Amru's hearing in the west. She writes to Amru and to Dareth, asking for a delay, not a release. She stays to see the witnesses heard. Pellam asks her back after the first session, because she spotted that two witnesses had been lodged together who were meant to be heard separately.
  5. Marriage, first as a conversation. In Mara's room at Tarlington, not at court. He wants to marry her. She asks him to describe the house they would live in and who would cook. He can't. She laughs, and then doesn't. They agree to talk again, and she makes him promise to tell no one first.
  6. Close. He has returned and found the people he looked for. Returning has not given him back his old place among them.

17. A man with an agreement (Lyra)

Place: Valora, the chancery hearing room, and then Jaennis's audience chamber.

Does not know: Fayd's present intentions. She knows his past.

  1. Her work. She has been with Pellam for about three weeks, reading witness accounts against one another. Small, real corrections. Pellam teaches her to separate what she saw from what she has inferred.
  2. Fayd arrives. He brings a limited result that can be checked. Baron Lisle's men release twelve Fenwood carters held as surety, and a road reopens. He asks for authority to finish that one piece of work, nothing more. His manner is modest and his terms are exact.
  3. Lyra recognises him from the west. She remembers the earlier bargain and says nothing yet; exact wording must be checked against records.
  4. Dame Ysolt shows her something. Within an hour of Fayd entering the room, two petitioners who had been waiting for the ordinary officers leave and go to wait in his anteroom instead. Ysolt does not interpret it. She tells Lyra to count them tomorrow as well. Later in the audience, Jaennis laughs at something Fayd says. Nobody else in the room found it funny, and Lyra can't work out what the joke was. Ysolt has seen it too, and says only: "She doesn't laugh at people she hasn't placed."
  5. Checking. Lyra rides out with Pellam's clerk and meets two of the released carters. The release was real.
  6. Close. She writes her first observation for Pellam. It concerns the change in who approaches which officer. It says nothing about Fayd's design, because she knows nothing about it.

18. A squire and a brother (Elyas)

Place: Harwick, at Aeryl's hall, and then Istovar.

Does not know: that Fayd is in Valora.

  1. Berent's release. A formal transfer from Baron Lorran Aeryl's household to Elyas's, witnessed. Aeryl is grudging and fair. Edran is quietly pleased for the boy. Elyas takes on Berent's keep, training and conduct.
  2. Berent's first task. Carrying the carriers' toll money back to Corbel's post, the debt Elyas took on in Ch. 14. Corbel's post has been burned by Seniro, so Berent comes back with the money and a question he doesn't know how to ask. He admits he spent a copper of it on bread. Elyas makes nothing of it.
  3. Istovar. Harald receives him courteously. There are Oswyn's rooms, and Oswyn's portrait at forty-something, with a face Elyas has been told all his life is his own. One sentence, from Elyas's point of view: he looks at it and finds it looking back.
  4. The decision. The Istovar levy is wanted on the river. Harald wants Elyas's name on the request and will not give Elyas the authority to answer it. He asks for figures, then asks again, and the rider waits in the yard until dark.
  5. Close. Berent asks, on the ride back, why the duke didn't just say yes. Elyas says his brother is careful. Berent writes it down, which he has seen Alric do.

19. The inconvenient acquaintance (Lyra, with Elyas present)

Place: Valora, Jaennis's council chamber.

  1. Elyas recognises Fayd in the anteroom. He asks to speak to Jaennis first, and does, with Lyra there as a witness to the western events. He tells Jaennis exactly what the earlier bargain was, and what it cannot prove.
  2. Fayd does not panic. He acknowledges all of it. He argues that his access to people hostile to the crown is exactly what makes him useful for negotiating with them. He does not claim the Vigil's contacts or ambitions have gone away. He offers to make them serve a particular settlement.
  3. Jaennis authorises a bounded task. Fayd is to secure a meeting between Baron Maresse and a crown officer, with written terms that someone can later enforce. It is a political choice with a known risk.
  4. Lyra afterwards. She compares her account of the meeting with Pellam's. They disagree on one point: whether Fayd said the Vigil's contacts or my contacts. They check it against the secretary's notes, and her version was right. Her record goes into the chancery file. It does not disappear because Jaennis has given the authorisation.
  5. Elyas and Jaennis. A short exchange. She is the widow of the king he served, and a Fenwood by birth. There is family affection, and neither of them is fooled by it.
  6. The first pass. When the others have gone, Fayd stays behind to thank the dowager. Lyra is still at the side table finishing her notes, and both of them treat her as furniture. In passing, he names one member of her company, and a supper that member attended which Jaennis had kept out of the household book. He does not bargain with it. He lays it down and leaves it: "I'd be a poor servant not to know it, and a worse one to use it." Jaennis answers in kind, and better. She tells him that one of his own couriers has been selling copies to a Merrow factor. It's true, and he didn't know. His face shows nothing, except that he is pleased. Each has shown the other what they can see, and each has declined to press. Lyra hears all of it. She understands perhaps half, and records only that the two of them spoke privately for about as long as it takes to drink a cup of wine. The rest she keeps, because she doesn't yet know what it's evidence of.
  7. Close. Fayd has a foothold, Berent is in Elyas's household, and the institutions have made deliberate commitments.

Reader: the first pass between Jaennis and Fayd should be a delight: two sharp people fencing in front of a witness they both forget is there.

20. Whose people (Elyas)

Place: the barrier at Maresse's crossing.

Does not know: whether Constano is alive.

  1. The barrier. Refugees from Fairport and Marcott reach a barrier held by Baron Maresse's men. Maresse had recently agreed, through Fayd's meeting, to submit. The carrier's daughter from Ch. 14 is among the refugees.
  2. The limits of a promise. Elyas cannot settle it by repeating the promise. Maresse's steward has grain for Maresse's people and not for four hundred strangers.
  3. Mara. She is present because she chose to come: she rode out with Tarlington's relief wagons, which she organised herself. She knows how households absorb strangers, a family at a time and a name at a time. She proposes lodging refugees by household at named farms, with Tarlington grain paying for each.
  4. Berent sees both things: the obligation to take people in, and the shortage that makes it hard. He spends the day carrying the list of households from farm to farm.
  5. Close. The barrier opens by households, slowly. Elyas has promised Tarlington grain that he does not control. He will have to answer to Seniro for it.

Part IV — Who Can Give an Answer

21. The promised marriage (Elyas)

Place: Tarlington.

  1. The decision. Elyas and Mara agree what they intend: an announced betrothal, with a wedding when the war allows.
  2. Mara's condition. She keeps the post she has made for herself. She is running Tarlington's relief households since Ch. 20, and she will not give it up to become an ornament. She also refuses to be presented at court before the wedding. Her life is not a prize handed over with a ring.
  3. The objections, each different:
    • Braedyn the Younger wants an alliance marriage for Elyas, to bind Istovar and a valley house.
    • Seniro's captain fears a split over the Istovar succession, because Harald has no sons (prov.; check against the family record).
    • Joan Hask, visiting from Valora, tells Mara what the court will do to her, specifically and kindly. She knows that Mara will pay socially for privileges Elyas treats as easy to give.
  4. Harald's reply comes by letter. He wants his brother happy and cannot support him when support costs something. He asks for time to consider.
  5. Mara's own choice. Gisla, the Fenwood field nurse, writes to Tarlington's relief households asking for women who can dress wounds at the river stations before the fighting comes south. Mara decides to go. It is not about Elyas. She was good in the duke's sickroom, she liked being good at it, and she wants to learn the work from someone who knows it. She leaves the relief households, still under her name, with the widow she trained to run them. She tells Elyas after she has decided, which is the reverse of the promise she asked of him in Ch. 16, and he hears it as she means it. It costs her:
    • Braedyn the Younger thinks it unseemly and says so;
    • Joan Hask's warning now applies to something Mara chose, not something done to her, because a woman who goes to the camps is talked about as a camp woman;
    • she will be near the river when the battle comes, where nobody can keep her safe.
  6. Close. Mara reads Harald's letter aloud in a perfect imitation of Harald. They laugh. Nothing is settled.

Reader: the pleasure of seeing Mara want something of her own, and a small fear for her.

22. The ground Seniro leaves (Seniro)

Place: the river line.

  1. Constano is out of reach. Seniro makes a withdrawal he once would have called dishonourable: he gives up the Hollin Bridge line northward, to the causeways.
  2. Clear military purpose: a shorter line, the causeways as a defensible spine, and time for the queen.
  3. The painful household cost: Fenwood tenants are left on the wrong side of the line, and the house's oldest drainage works are abandoned to Valdais.
  4. The troops do the work: dismantling the Hollin crossing, bringing out the wounded, holding the causeway road while civilians pass. It is Xenophonic and concrete. Seniro gives the causeway rearguard to Hob Carrow and Ralf Pennick together, knowing why they hold best side by side.
  5. The exchange of wounded. The exchange Valdais offered in Ch. 11 happens on the causeway under flag, man for man and rank for rank. The Vhasian side is commanded by Dame Ysabeau de Corvaunt. Seniro recognises her from the bridge in Ch. 1 and says so. They talk for the length of the exchange, soldier to soldier, and she describes her commander without meaning to. He knew her name, her canton and her horse's name before he let her ride with him. He learned the ransom rate of every rank in the Viterran army before he crossed the river. He is taking crossings and barns, not towns. From that Seniro understands what Valdais wants from this campaign: the river, not Valora. She asks after Constano because Valdais told her to, and Seniro can answer now, from Jaennis's letter: alive, somewhere in the fens. She looks relieved, which neither of them expected. She also asks which of the queen's men is Vayle, the way soldiers ask.
  6. His changed understanding shows in an order, not in a speech. He leaves the Hollin Bridge standing but mined, which he would never once have done. "Let him come to a bridge he doesn't trust."
  7. Close. The queen's eight-day rider reaches him.

Reader: the grief of giving ground and pride in the men who do the work of giving it. Hob and Ralf holding the causeway together is the image to keep.

Plants for the battle: the bridge left mined; the Upper Causeway; Corbel's Barns, where Valdais is gathering stores; the Kettling link.

23. The people Fayd can bring (Lyra)

Place: Valora.

  1. Fayd shows the larger change. He names the lords whose men, roads, crossings or grain would alter the campaign: Maresse (the Hollin approaches), Lisle (the lateral roads), and Corbel (the barns). For each one, he explains what the lord must be able to tell his own household.
  2. The limited undertaking. Maresse is to release the Hollin ferry to crown use, as proof. Here the two networks work together because each alone would fail. Fayd's office has the barons. The company has the barons' wives, stewards' wives and daughters in service. The ferry release is checked through both before anyone trusts it. Neither side enjoys needing the other, and both are efficient about it.
    • Lyra learns from both. From Joan Hask she learns how a household letter carries news no dispatch would: the sister, the hairdresser, the laundry. From Fayd's chief clerk (prov.: Master Oswin Tarr) she learns how an office cross-files sources, so that no report stands on one man. She notices that the company's knowledge is warm and fast and hard to verify, and that the office's is slow, checkable and filtered through one man. She keeps both methods. She joins neither.
  3. Lyra checks. The proposed witness for the ferry's release is Maresse's own steward, the man whose performance he would be certifying. She points this out, and an independent witness is arranged: Pellam's clerk. The release proves genuine. She has made the bargain more believable without making Fayd more trustworthy, and her teachers do not pretend those are the same thing.
  4. Her concern, stated to an officer who will listen, in this case the chancellor through Pellam: the court must keep its own access to the people whose reports Fayd brings. She does not ask that he be refused. On the way out she passes Jaennis and Fayd in a gallery, talking over the list of lords. They finish each other's sentences about Baron Lisle's debts, and both stop the moment they see her, at exactly the same instant. Lyra writes down what they said about Lisle, which she can check. She does not write down the rest, because she can't.
  5. The queen's return changes the household. Lyra has been to the Lantern once, as a friend's guest, and enjoyed it, and knows now that the fair company is one such room among several. She sees Jaennis rearranging places and introductions, and understands that a welcome is being prepared, without knowing the private intentions behind it.
  6. Close. The offer goes north to the queen with the evidence and the limits of the evidence both intact. Lyra wrote the limits herself.

24. What holds when she leaves (Tylan)

Place: the flint hills, the passes and the shepherds' halls, in Leafturn, in the days after the queen decides to turn south.

Does not know: Fayd's offer, which is still on the road, or Valdais's terms, which are on their way north.

  1. The problem. The queen goes south with the army, and the north must be held without it. Tylan has to build a containment out of what the hills already have, because nothing else will be left.
  2. The structures, used. Each is something the reader has seen in Ch. 2, 5 and 7.
    • The halls become the posts. Each hall-keeper is named reeve of his hall's ground under the royal captain. The hall keeps its own truce-law and gets a stipend in grain.
    • The herders become the watch. Hask's riders have been killing sheep to feed their men all autumn. The herders will report them to whoever pays for the dead sheep, and Tylan pays.
    • The cairn-line. At a hall council, Dunmere's men and the hall-keepers agree which ridge-cairns mark where the royal captain's writ runs. Nobody writes it down except Tylan. The hills already know how to read a cairn.
    • Dunmere's men under a royal captain pen Hask in his valley, and Dunmere's son is at Valora. Tylan chooses the captain for being dull and stubborn.
  3. Winter as the garrison. The herders bring the flocks down a week early this year. Tylan asks why, and an old man says the snow will be early. Tylan moves his whole timetable on the word of a man who cannot explain himself. The skeptical reading is that herders know weather. Once the high passes close, Hask cannot raid until spring, so the containment only has to hold until the snow takes over.
  4. The last raid. Hask tests it once, at a hall above the pass. The hall-keeper's boy runs the drove-road at night to the royal captain's post, and the captain comes, as arranged. It holds. On the way back the boy is killed by a rider who does not know the raid has already failed. Tylan writes the boy's name on his sheet with the place he fell. It is the one name in the book he writes down that is not a position.
  5. Four hundred horses. At night he walks the horse-lines with a lantern and knows which four hundred will die if the army marches now. He will give her both figures and not tell her which to choose (Ch. 25).
  6. Anneth. She comes to find him, not Veralyn. She asks how many horses the queen will lose. He tells her. "She'll pay it," she says, and it isn't a question. She asks him to see that the queen eats on the road. He says he always has. It is the one exchange between the two people who love Veralyn and can see what is happening to her.
  7. The heir question, once. At the hall council Dunmere, submitting, asks Tylan who the queen's heir is, since he is being asked to hold the north for her line. Tylan says it is not a question for a hall. Dunmere says it will be a question for somebody. Tylan knows the answer and knows that she knows it, and neither of them has ever said it. One line, and leave it.
  8. Close. His handover sheet to the royal captain: pass by pass and hall by hall, who holds, with what, and food in days. The whole containment fits on one page. That night the first snow lies on the high cairns.

Reader: grim satisfaction in an arrangement made out of the hills themselves, the boy's death as its price, and tenderness in the Anneth exchange.

25. Enough of the north (Veralyn)

Place: the flint hills.

  1. The local resolution: containment. Tylan's arrangement from Ch. 24 is in front of her on one page: the halls, the herders, the cairn-line, Hask penned in his valley by Dunmere's men under a royal captain, and Dunmere's son at Valora. For the winter, the earls cannot threaten her rear. She does not get everything she wanted, and she knows the snow is doing half the work.
  2. Named responsibilities, each held by people with men to enforce them: a reeve for each hall, a captain for each pass, and a reeve for the prisoners. She changes one name on Tylan's list, gives her reason, and he accepts it.
  3. Tylan's southern movement is ready without having stripped the north of what it needs to survive. He has counted mounts, fodder and men's condition. He gives her the figures for leaving now and for leaving in a week. Leaving now kills four hundred horses.
  4. She orders the turn before it is comfortable. She accepts the loss of the four hundred horses, and this time she doesn't try to work out whether the price could have been lower. On the morning they march, a cairn at the pass is one stone taller than it was the evening before. The rearguard reports it, and a sergeant adds a stone of his own before he rides on.
  5. Anneth at the gate. Easy, fond, and final.
  6. Close. Fayd's offer reaches her on the road south, two days out.

26. The welcome (Jaennis)

Place: Valora, the dowager's working room and her household, in the days after the queen turns south.

Does not know: the terms Veralyn will make with Fayd (not yet made), or what the northern campaign has made of the queen.

  1. The supper where he refuses. One of the standing working suppers from Ch. 13: the two of them, the day's petitions and a jug. It's the best hour she's had in a week, and it annoys her that it is. He is the only person at court who needs nothing explained. He picks up her half-sentences and reads the petition she hasn't mentioned yet. They argue about what should go north with his offer, and she catches the one point he missed: Corbel's drainage grievance, which will become a term in Ch. 28. She doesn't tell him she caught it until he's already written it wrong. Then she makes a test. She invites him to the company's evenings, the room everyone else is shut out of. He declines, gracefully. He is the only person who ever has. She sees at once that the refusal is a position and not a scruple, because inside the company he would be one of hers. She doesn't press. She has a queen coming home, a father on the fen roads, and a court to run.
    • Each is circling the other's plan for Veralyn. Jaennis suspects him of wanting power over the queen, and he suspects her of wanting the queen. Each is partly right.
    • What passes between them is rivalry in a shared language. Neither is courting the other.
    • Tonight's argument is whether allegiance is owed to a person or to a house. He says a person, because houses fail. She says a house, because persons die. Each is arguing the case that suits their design for the queen, and each knows it.
  2. The song. A private act of care, with no audience and no advantage. Late that night she answers Veralyn's letter with a gift chosen for the woman she loves: the song Anneth sang, written out by Jaennis's own musician, with the second verse Veralyn always forgets.
  3. The planning. The same night, and the same knowledge, she works out the queen's place in the company: who sits beside her, whose music, which door. The planning is a pleasure too, and write it as one: she tastes the Fairport wine Crane saved, chooses the musicians' pieces, and has the queen's old dancing shoes resoled. Both acts are true, and neither cancels the other.
  4. Catrin declines. Jaennis sets Catrin's seat for the welcome supper, beside the queen. Catrin will not sit in it. She is frightened and proud and would rather choose. What Jaennis does next reveals her. She lets Catrin choose her own seat, and doesn't punish her, and means it. Then she moves Margery into the empty place, which uses the refusal. Both are care, and one is also advantage.
  5. Close. A rider: the queen is two days out, and Fayd's offer has reached her. Jaennis has the dressing room aired and the musician rehearse the second verse.

Reader: the supper should be electric, two people speaking fast, each half a step from being caught out. The song should be tender. Catrin's refusal should give the reader a small shock of recognition when the empty seat is filled.

27. A command Harald cannot give (Elyas)

Place: Istovar.

  1. What Elyas needs. He has undertaken, to Seniro and through Mara's households, to hold the Hollin approaches with the Istovar levy. He cannot do it on Harald's behalf.
  2. The confrontation. It starts courteous. Harald re-checks figures. The rider waits. Harald seeks assurances that nobody can give.
  3. The choice. Elyas takes a bounded royal commission to command the Istovar levy on the river, from the queen's seals by way of Jaennis. It is quicker than Harald. Harald experiences it as usurpation, even though it takes no title from him.
  4. Berent watches Elyas choose a duty that makes someone dislike him. Berent's later conduct in Ch. 32 shows what he took from it, imperfectly.
  5. The marriage question and the command question meet. The Istovar household asks whether Elyas has become reliable or impossible. Mara's answer to the future he proposed is not replaced by Harald's permission. Harald finally gives it, and Mara writes back that she didn't ask for it.
  6. Close. Elyas leaves Istovar with the levy and without his brother's goodwill.

28. The queen's bargain (Veralyn)

Place: a manor on the southern road, a day north of the causeways.

  1. Veralyn meets Fayd and makes the deal herself.
  2. The terms (prov.).
    • For the lords: conditional pardon, and confirmation of specified rights (Corbel's drainage grievance goes to a commission of adjudication), in return for immediate military acts. Maresse holds the Hollin approaches. Lisle closes the lateral roads to Valdais. Corbel denies Valdais the barns.
    • For Fayd: the permanent intelligence office. The appointment is written, and it takes effect when the named undertakings are performed.
  3. The ground on the table. In the manor's kitchen, Tylan lays out the Hollin ground for the queen, and the reader should leave this scene able to draw it on a napkin.
    • A cord for the Belworth. A thinner cord for the Ashbrook, joining it. A split twig for the Hollin Bridge. A bread-knife for the Upper Causeway, Valdais's lateral road. A salt cellar for Corbel's Barns, his supply point. A spoon at Kettling ford, his way home.
    • One pebble for each lord, set where he has promised to be: Maresse on the Hollin approaches, Lisle across the causeway, Corbel at the barns.
    • Then Tylan moves Valdais's stones through what happens if each lord fails, one at a time, and the queen watches the table go wrong three different ways.
    • The whole plan in four moves: fix the front, cut the causeway, burn the barns, threaten Kettling.
  4. Tylan asks what makes it executable. Which lords will act? What happens first? How will their captains recognise the queen's orders? (Prov.: by a token in the form of Tylan's own position sheets, one line from each.) What if one lord is late? Fayd answers enough to earn a risk and not enough to remove it.
  5. Elyas's earlier account is in front of her. She does not need to believe Fayd is good. She needs to believe the promised acts can be had at the agreed price. She tells Fayd plainly, to his face, what his office will cost the realm, and that she is choosing to pay it. (Myrmis stage: she names the cost to the one who must pay it.) It is the moment he decides she is the sovereign he wants. Across the table he looks at Shadowsteel a moment too long, the only time his composure shows anything. When Veralyn mentions the dowager, his courtesy toward Jaennis is exact and complete, and it tells her nothing at all. She notices the same careful blankness in the dowager's own letters whenever they mention him, and files it. Two people who never discuss each other freely are, to a queen raised at court, one kind of fact.
  6. The first test, small and observable. The Hollin ferry is already in crown hands (Ch. 23). The next test: Lisle's men must let a royal convoy through the lateral road before the army commits. It passes.
  7. Close. She signs. The appointment is a dated instrument in her own hand.

Reader: tension, then clarity. The table scene should leave the reader holding the whole battle, and feeling the risk in it: every stone on the table is a man who might not come.

Part V — The Lords Who Turn

29. The answer sent too late (Constano)

Place: still in the fens, now moving toward Tarlington.

  1. He sends the best advice his information allows: do not trust Lisle, who owes Tireno money and will want it back. It is the right warning about a lord who has since agreed terms through Fayd.
  2. The reader sees the discrepancy without being asked to laugh.
  3. He succeeds at one immediate responsibility. Lucrezia's youngest has fever, and he gets a physician by pledging the seal.
  4. He cannot get back his old place. His absence has let another man take on work that used to belong to several reliable people. Whether he would have refused the bargain is unknowable. Nobody asked him while an answer still mattered.
  5. Silkstrand money. An Ecktorian factor finds him at a fen inn and offers to buy Fenwood notes at a third of their face. Ecktoria has watched the war from across the water and is buying it cheap. Constano refuses, politely, knowing some of his creditors will not. It is the first sign that the war has buyers outside the two kingdoms.
  6. Close. At the ferry above Tarlington, a boatman tells him there's been a battle. He doesn't yet know who won.

30. The men Seniro must admit (Seniro)

Place: the Hollin causeways, the day before the battle and the morning of it.

  1. The agreement becomes orders on the ground. Lords who held back levies or blocked the defence must now be allowed to move armed men through Fenwood positions. Seniro has reasons to distrust each of them. Some of his soldiers have buried people killed in the disorder.
  2. Safeguards. He obeys the queen and puts in place the precautions that make obeying possible: turned companies are routed along set causeways, with Fenwood pickets at each junction and the passwords from the queen's token.
  3. Fenwood engineering knowledge. He knows where a column can leave the raised road and where it cannot, because Fenwoods drained this valley. It gives him an advantage, not a winning device.
  4. One lord performs. Maresse's men hold the Hollin approaches on time.
  5. One is late and short. Lisle brings a third fewer men and arrives two hours late. Seniro holds the necessary ground through the gap, with the mined bridge as the threat Valdais will not test.
  6. Close. The queen's army reaches the position from which the changed allegiances matter. This is what every day he bought was for.

31. The southern battle (Veralyn)

Place: the Hollin causeways, over a single day.

The plan is visible because it was prepared across earlier chapters. The execution is confused from inside her point of view.

  1. Morning. Veralyn's attack fixes Valdais's main force on the causeway front. The fighting is fierce and personal. She is in it, and it does not decide the day.
  2. The turned lords act, each at a place planted earlier:
    • Lisle's men block the Upper Causeway, Valdais's lateral road.
    • Corbel's steward fires the barns, Valdais's supply point.
    • Maresse admits royal troops over the Hollin approaches, a passage Valdais expected to be held against them.
  3. The reserve. The restored routes let Tylan move a reserve along the Upper Causeway toward the Kettling link. Valdais cannot safely ignore it.
  4. Valdais responds intelligently. He tries to reopen the Upper Causeway and keep his crossing. He loses the fight for the ground that would have let him keep the offensive going.
  5. Her decisive act is an order on bad information, not a duel. She commits the last reserve to the Kettling road on a report that turns out to be half wrong. She gives the order with a certainty the report does not justify. The half that was right is the half that mattered. (Myrmis stage: she declares a target.) At evening, Shadowsteel is clean and she cannot remember wiping it. The skeptical reading: battle fog, and a squire who wipes blades.
  6. Close. Valdais orders the fallback before he can be trapped on the wrong bank. It is a decisive royal victory in this battle, and not the destruction of every army he can raise.
  7. Valdais's second letter. At dusk his herald asks leave to collect his dead under truce and carries a note for the queen in his own hand. It is four lines. It concedes the field and not the war. It thanks her for the courtesy he expects her to show his wounded, and names the day he expects to see her again. She grants the truce and reads the note twice.

Reader: real uncertainty. Lisle is late, the reserve goes on a half-wrong report, and the reader should not know how it will go until Valdais orders the fallback. Then relief, and Valdais's four lines to take the edge off it.

32. What turning costs (Elyas)

Place: the Hollin approaches, the same day seen from a different part of the field.

  1. Elyas's part. With the Istovar levy, he secures the passage for the turned lords and stops an agreed surrender turning into a massacre: Maresse's men come in with raised pikes and one Fenwood company does not believe them. His acquaintance with the lords gets the agreement carried out. It does not make frightened captains obey orders they haven't received.
  2. Valdais's counterstroke hits the Hollin route. It is hard, specific fighting, and it has a cost.
  3. Berent's task under danger. He brings a remount and carries one bounded message along a known causeway to Seniro's picket. No childish flash of insight wins the battle. He gets the message there and falls off at the picket. Gisla, the Fenwood field nurse, catches him, looks him over and sends him back. Mara is at Gisla's station behind the picket with her sleeves pinned, and does not look up. Elyas sees her for the length of a breath and rides on, because she is working and so is he.
  4. The refused pursuit. Elyas turns down a pursuit that would leave the people in his care exposed. It costs him a visible chance at glory, and it keeps the duty he accepted.
  5. Valdais falls back with part of his force still in order. The part still in order is Dame Ysabeau's horse, covering the Kettling crossing. Elyas sees her hold it, and does not pursue. The victors hold the disputed ground. The reader can say why he had to retreat, and which human decisions made it happen.
  6. The queen on the field. At dusk Elyas sees Veralyn walking the causeways among the dead of both sides. She won't leave the stones to others. She kneels by a Vhasian pikeman, puts a hand on the wound, and says flatly to nobody what the blow cost the man who struck it. Elyas has no category for this. He reports it to Mara in ch. 35 as "she was counting," and leaves it there.
  7. Close. A herald is already composing the dispatch. Elyas hears the first line and it has one name in it.

Reader: dread in the counterstroke, then the stillness of the field at dusk: the queen kneeling among the dead, and nobody speaking.

33. The useful office (Lyra)

Place: Valora: the council chamber, and then the chancery, in the days after the queen's return.

  1. The two reports. Each network brings its account of which lords performed and which didn't, and Lyra has them side by side. They agree on Maresse and Corbel and disagree on Lisle. Fayd's office credits Lisle in full. The company, through Lisle's steward's wife, knows the third of his men who never came were kept back on purpose. Lyra can see why the company is right, because she has learned how a steward's wife knows things. Jaennis's version stands. The honours list is corrected before it reaches the queen, and Fayd takes the correction with good grace, noting the source.
  2. The office is confirmed. The lords did enough of what they promised to change the battle, and the queen keeps her side. Fayd's appointment as Master of the Queen's Correspondence (prov.) has a sponsor, duties, access, funding, and a reporting line to the reigning queen. Other instruments pass the chancery the same week. The one that names Tylan Vayle Lord Marshal is the only one with no dissent recorded against it, and Lyra notices that, because it is rare.
  3. Lyra answers exactly. Asked to confirm a point from the old western testimony, she answers exactly, including an awkward limit. The office is confirmed anyway. She is another attentive witness, and she does not stop a bargain the queen has chosen to keep.
  4. Fayd's further request. He asks for authority to open correspondence with houses far beyond the defeated lords, across the old imperial lands, to cultivate their allegiance to Veralyn herself. Lyra sees Jaennis notice how far it reaches: a stillness in her hands. Lyra writes the request down word for word and underlines allegiance without knowing why. The reader, remembering his politics, sees a change of method rather than a break with the Vigil. Nobody says so.
  5. Her place. Pellam offers her a post examining petitions and witness accounts, with direct access to the chancellor. Tarr offers her a place in the Correspondence. She takes Pellam's. Tarr is not offended, and says the office will read her reports anyway. From now on both networks will ask for her notes, and she will give both the same ones.
  6. Close. Supper at Dame Ysolt's, and the companion of thirty years, in one line. Lyra walks home through a city lit for a victory, carrying a notebook with allegiance underlined in it.

Reader: quiet satisfaction for Lyra, who has earned her place, and underneath it the unease of watching a sound bargain grow teeth.

34. The celebration (Jaennis)

Place: Valora, the queen's welcome.

  1. What she helped build. The office has rooms in the old wing now. People bring matters straight to him. Pellam's list from Ch. 13 is now the whole list. Jaennis lives with it: she made this possible, and she would make it possible again.
  2. The celebration. She prepared Veralyn's return as carefully as the audience that got Fayd's offer.
    • Margery, in the seat Catrin refused, catches the queen's interest. Catrin is at supper by her own choice, in a seat she chose.
    • Jaennis eases a conversation along, and on the same evening receives Veralyn's familiar affection. How far the queen joins in stays a matter of behaviour and implication.
    • Beneath the pleasure, the queen talks almost entirely of costs: the horses, the barns, the drowned at the ford. Jaennis reads it as tiredness. The song's second verse is played, and the queen knows it's a gift and from whom.
    • Glitter. The long gallery lit end to end with wax bought on credit Constano would never have allowed. Musicians in two rooms. A masque of the river that Jaennis cut by half because it ran long. The queen still dances, and dances well. Tylan hates dancing and partners her once because it is expected, and she laughs at him.
    • Precedence. An Ecktorian envoy who arrived the day after the news is seated exactly one place lower than he expected, and understands why.
    • The Lord Marshal. The queen names Tylan Vayle Lord Marshal before the whole room. It is the one appointment of the night nobody argues about. Jaennis notices that he is the only man the queen touches without thinking. Fayd notices it too, and Jaennis sees him notice. Neither of them considers what it could mean.
    • The herald's one-name dispatch is read aloud, and Veralyn lets it stand. She is not being vain. In the Matron's sense it is true: she ordered the plan and the dying.
    • A musician moves his stool so he isn't next to where Shadowsteel hangs, then moves back, embarrassed. Jaennis sees Lyra see it, and sees her write it down.
  3. The window. Late in the evening Jaennis and Fayd stand together at a window, watching the queen across the room. She asks what he means to do with the peace. He says, truthfully, "serve her." He asks her the same. She says, truthfully, "keep her." Each answer is honest and tells the other nothing. They raise their cups, each knowing the other has a design on Veralyn and neither knowing what it is. Neither will take it further tonight. Both are looking forward to the next time.
  4. Close. Two separate projects take their first successful steps, Fayd's and Jaennis's. Neither knows the other's intentions. Both depend on a queen who has just shown that she makes formidable choices of her own, and who is becoming something neither of them has allowed for.

Reader: warmth and glitter, the room at its best, and under it the tension of the window. The reader should leave the party knowing more than anyone in it.

35. The household he chooses (Elyas)

Place: Tarlington.

  1. Elyas and Mara both come back to Tarlington from the river. He does not treat surviving the battle as an answer to the question he asked her, and she does not treat her season at the stations as one either.
  2. A publicly stated intention to marry, accepted by Mara on the terms they discussed, including her post. The date of the wedding stays open.
  3. Constano is home, diminished in reach and not in himself. He has known Mara for years as part of his father's household. Now he addresses her, correctly, in the form for a lady of the house. It is the first time anyone at Tarlington has done so. One line.
  4. Berent is visibly part of their household. Harald's inadequate reply remains a fact of their lives. The rebel lords are back in obedience on new terms. Fayd's position can no longer be dismissed as a temporary necessity.
  5. Pleasure is allowed. The scandal carries on outside the room. Inside it they laugh, argue about something ordinary (who is to have the south room), and start making arrangements.
  6. The letter to Riarhin. Constano asks whether there has been an answer from Riarhin. There has not, and nobody knows whether the letter has found her. One line. (Prov. It says nothing about where she is or why.)
  7. Close — author-selected revision. Elyas and Mara visit the prospective house, taste the last pear, discuss repairs, and go to look at the kitchen. Keep the board and unanswered letter earlier in the chapter, with Constano home. The final movement is the ordinary household they intend to make.

Veralyn, Myrmis, and Shadowsteel

The thread. Over the whole book, Veralyn comes more and more into tune with Myrmis. By the end she is closer to being the Matron's instrument than to being a queen who happens to pray to her. Nobody names it, and she does not convert or swear anything. The change shows in what she notices, what she will not waive, and how she talks. The stages below draw loosely on the patron's corruption tiers. They are an inspiration, not a checklist, and each should read as ordinary character development under the pressure of war.

The two readings. Every sign supports two readings. In one it is Myrmis. In the other it is a hard woman getting harder, and a very fine sword being read by the people around it. The reader decides. As A Roll of the Fallen requires, the skeptical reading is always available and always a little worse than the other.

Her lineage. Shadowsteel came to Veralyn from Alaysha Renard's line, and the Renards keep the Matron "as a grandmother rather than a goddess." That lineage is the only link. The book never states it outright.

The two hands. Myrmis will not let the planner disclaim the scream. Ch. 32 ends with the herald's dispatch, which gives the victory one name, hers. That simplification is historically false; the reader has seen the lords, Seniro, Elyas and Tylan make it happen. In ch. 34 Veralyn accepts the one-name version, and not out of vanity. In the Matron's sense it is correct: she ordered the plan and the dying, and she will not put either hand down. The dispatch is wrong about who did the work and right about whose act it was. Nobody explains that. The reader holds both.

What it does to the other threads.

  • Jaennis's design. Jaennis is preparing a welcome for the Veralyn who left: easy, appetitive, fond of songs. The queen who comes back still has her appetites, but she speaks less and less of anything that is not war, sacrifice or justice. The invitation lands on a slightly different woman (Ch. 34). This is irony at Jaennis's expense, not a failure of her plan; she still gets the evening she arranged.
  • Fayd's design. What he takes for imperial material, the ability to name a price and pay it, is partly the Matron. He is building an empire around an instrument he believes he is shaping.
  • Tylan. He sees her change from outside, in Ch. 7 and 24, as someone who has known her since they were children. His is the reading most inclined to see nothing but a hard woman getting harder, and even so he moves the pickets and counts the horses for her.
  • Anneth and the north. In the flint hills the Matron is kept at home, quietly, "not in front of the Crown." Anneth's household keeps a stone-shelf by the hearth. She is the first to see what is happening to Veralyn, and it is part of why she stays behind in Ch. 12. She never says so.

Shadowsteel, more ominous and never cursed. It stays what it has always been: the finest blade anyone has handled, doing nothing anyone can prove. It never thirsts, never whispers and never moves by itself. Myrmis's style is the reckoning, not appetite. Its menace is carried by the people around it:

  • 2. Anneth will not have it in the bedroom. She lays it outside the door with the courtesy owed to a guest. In the morning a servant has brought it back in, and says so.
  • 5. Tylan finds he has written down where the sword lay overnight. He records the positions of people and never of things, and he crosses the entry out.
  • 18, 25. Northern soldiers begin setting a small stone on its chape when she hangs it up, the way the Matron's devotees set stones on the dead. Veralyn sees and does not stop them. When Tylan asks, a sergeant reports it as a hill custom. It is one.
  • 28. Fayd looks at it for a moment too long across the table, the only time his composure shows anything.
  • 31. After the battle it is clean. She cannot remember wiping it. The skeptical reading: battle fog, and a squire who wipes blades.
  • 34. At the celebration, a musician moves his stool so he isn't sitting next to where it hangs, then moves back, embarrassed. Lyra sees both moves and does not know what they mean. She records only what she saw.

Portents in general. If a portent appears at all, a character acts on it or reports it. It is never narrated as omen. The north of Viterra lies nearer the Dolmis and keeps more of the old unexplained things. Its portents may stay subtextual and unexplained, at the level of A Roll of the Fallen, and every one of them should survive a skeptical reading:

  • herders who will not graze a field and give no reason;
  • a cairn one stone taller than yesterday;
  • crows counted aloud at a council, and the count acted on.

As the story moves south toward the Amaranthine delta, causes get given and portents thin out, and that thinning is itself the texture. Fairport, Marcott and the court run on reasons and paperwork. Nothing in the south is left unexplained.

Tylan Vayle

Who he is. From the older books: he was raised at Tarlington from fourteen with Veralyn, Elyas and Jaennis, and he is the most prepared person Veralyn has ever met. He is the second son of a new county, which is a man standing on ice. His sheets record where people stood and nothing else. His humour is dry.

The most feared single fighter in the cycle. He is freakishly fast for a man his size, and strong. Hill riders know his name, and Vhasian officers ask which of the queen's men he is (Ch. 22). He rarely fights, because a commander who has to fight means his arrangements failed. When he does fight, it is short and it ends things. Show it once from inside (Ch. 7). Everywhere else, show it through other people: raiders who break before he reaches them, and a Vhasian dame who asks after him the way soldiers ask. He treats the fear he causes as a tool, not as a fact about himself.

Veralyn. He loves her like a brother, and he is fiercely loyal and protective. He has known where her preferences lie since they were young and has never needed her to say it. His protection is practical: pickets moved, horses counted, food on the road. He doesn't argue with her choices and he doesn't flatter them. He is the one person in the book who sees her change and does not need anything from her.

Where he ends. Lord Marshal: the chancery instrument in Ch. 33, named before the court in Ch. 34.

Future canon (not in this book). Tylan will marry Veralyn and give her an heir. Both will know what the marriage is and what it isn't. In this book it is only seeded:

  • Dunmere asks Tylan about the heir, and Tylan turns the question aside (Ch. 24);
  • the Lord Marshal appointment is the one nobody disputes (Ch. 33, 34);
  • Jaennis and Fayd both see that he is the only man the queen touches without thinking, and neither considers what it could mean (Ch. 34).

Both of their designs on the queen will have to reckon with it later. Never state it here.

Jaennis and Fayd, circling

They are intellectual rivals who speak the same language, and each knows it within an hour of meeting. Each is the only person at court who plays at the other's level. Each builds the government people actually meet: she through the household, he through the office. And each has a private design on the same woman.

  • Jaennis is the sharper of the two. She reads him faster than he reads her. She catches the point he missed, knows his couriers better than he does (ch. 19), and never shows him the whole of what she's seen. What she recognises in him is usefulness and danger, and she thinks each is the price of the other. He is the best instrument available to the crown, and he belongs to the Vigil.
  • She spars with him and keeps tabs on him. That is all she has time for. She is running the court, the relief, the seals and her own household through a war, with her father missing. Fayd gets a corner of her attention. She uses it well: a sharp exchange when their paths cross, and one of her ladies always in earshot of his anteroom. She would like to be closer to him without seducing him, and the company is exactly what she won't use on him. Inside it he would owe her something, know it, and guard himself.
  • Two rival organisations with a similar purpose. Her ladies and his office both exist to know what the court needs to know before the court knows it. The company works through households: wives, sisters, laundries, dressing rooms. It is fast, warm, and hard to check. The office works through correspondents, informants and paid agents. It is slow, cross-checked, and everything passes through one man. They compete when a secret is worth having first, and cooperate when neither can manage alone.

Desire, gender, and the open secret

The court of this book should have more sexual and gender variety than one dowager's household. It should be written as the setting would carry it: through open secrets, and through gatherings the official world does not see. Never salaciously, and never as a token. The rule is that nobody's desire is evidence of their morals. Queer characters are found among the admirable, the dull, the heroic and the compromised in about the same proportions as everyone else. Nobody dies to make a point.

Viterra versus Vhasia.

  • Viterra holds that degree and order are God's arrangement, so disorder is sin rather than politics. Desire outside the approved shapes is therefore private: an open secret that everybody knows and nobody records. A household knows its lord's arrangement and keeps it out of the books. The official silence is sincere, and so is the tolerance underneath it. Write both without winking.
  • Vhasia is more open, above all among its nobility. The code allows more than the Viterran one does, and a Vhasian dame can keep a companion without anybody pretending otherwise. It is still a code, with its own forms and its own cruelties.

Women in arms (Vhasia). Generations of canton warfare left Vhasian women defending their own houses while the men were away at other wars. In time they demanded, and won, a recognised place in combat. Among the nobility, duchesses and dames holding military commands are unremarkable in Vhasia and remarked on in Viterra. Among common folk, women fighters in the line are rarer. They are more often scouts, field medics (still called nurses, from the older word), and secretaries in the classical sense: the officer's confidential writer and keeper of papers.

People to put on the page (prov.):

  • Dame Ysabeau de Corvaunt, a Vhasian dame commanding a canton's heavy horse in Valdais's army.
    • In ch. 1, the officer who mistakes Seniro's loudness for simplicity is not her. She is the one who pulls that company out before it's lost, and Seniro notices.
    • In ch. 31–32, she commands the part of Valdais's force that falls back in good order and covers the crossing. A competent enemy, respected as one, and never a novelty.
  • Wenna, Tylan's chief scout, a Viterran commoner. She is unusual in the north, and she is the reason Tylan's half-troop found the earls' baggage road in ch. 2.
  • Tylan Vayle knows where Veralyn's preferences lie and has known since they were young. He keeps her open secret the way a brother would. In Ch. 7 he moves the pickets away from Anneth's door without being asked. (Their later marriage is future canon; see "Tylan Vayle.")
  • Gisla, a Fenwood field nurse at the causeways. Mara comes to her stations as a volunteer in Ch. 21, and Gisla teaches her. Elyas sees her work in Ch. 32, and she takes charge of Berent when he falls at the picket.
  • Aldith, one of the four clerks Constano saves at Fairport. She is his confidential secretary and has been for eleven years, and she is the one who carries the credit books when his foot gives out.
  • Hob Carrow and Ralf Pennick, two of Seniro's captains. The army knows and nobody says. They share a tent and a mess, and their men cover for them the way soldiers do. In ch. 22 Seniro gives them the causeway rearguard together, deliberately, because they hold better side by side. Both survive the book.
  • Dame Ysolt Harrow, Lyra's mentor, has kept house with the same woman for thirty years. The court calls her "the Dame's companion" and has for so long that nobody hears the phrase any more. It comes out in one line, when Lyra is asked to supper there. It explains nothing about Ysolt's politics.

Gatherings beyond the official world. Valora has more than one room the Crown doesn't see. Jaennis's company is the most exclusive. There is also a riverside house (prov.: the Lantern), where players, guild men, junior officers and a few people of rank meet under an easy rule: what happens there isn't mentioned outside. Lyra goes once, in ch. 23, as a friend's guest. She enjoys it and nobody there is spying. Constano's old friends in Fairport had a looser equivalent, which is lost with the quays. These rooms are where the open secrets live, and the book treats them as places people are glad of, not dens.

Jaennis. She comes closest to the salacious, because her appetite is the engine of her story and it keeps growing. Keep it in her conduct and choices: whom she seats, whom she delays, whom she releases. Don't narrate bodies. Her story is not "the queer woman is dangerous." Her story is that power lets anyone arrange the world around their wants. Ysolt, Hob and Ralf, Fayd, and Ysabeau stand beside her so that she is not the book's only answer to desire.

Thematic rhyme, never stated

Anyone who has read The Weight You Carry will see the Belworth War repeat, in human form, what Kalrantha did to the lost country:

  • each emergency answer works;
  • each one is a little larger than the last;
  • each one draws requests toward the one place that is answering.

Fayd's office is that place. Keep the pattern structural. Nobody in this book knows about the lost country, and nobody draws the comparison. Chapters 9, 12, 14, 19, 23 and 33 should each show one more kind of request moving to Fayd, and in each case the move is the sensible thing to do.

The one-name dispatch

This is the book in small. An ensemble wins the war: lords, commanders, scouts, nurses, engineers and clerks. The record gives the credit to one person, and she accepts it. It is not vanity. In the Matron's sense it is true, because she ordered the plan and the dying and will not put either hand down. The same pattern runs through the book. The war, the dispatch, the office and the marriage are all instruments. People work through institutions, and people become instruments. Instruments write the record, and the record simplifies. Underneath all of it is one question, and the book never asks it aloud: can any of these be held without being owned?

Register: the tint, and what each viewpoint notices

The tint. The Riarhin books are dry because Riarhin is. She reads a room as a problem, and the prose follows her. That dryness carries into The Belworth War as its base register, but this book is told through seven people, none of them analysts, and the prose should lean a little toward description: rooms, weather, light, textiles, food, the look of ground. Let a scene be seen before it is used. This is a tint, not a change of voice.

  • The measure. Most scenes open with one painted passage of a few sentences that fixes place, season and light, and then the prose gets on with the business. A set-piece (the court welcome, the sack of Fairport, the causeways at dawn, the celebration) may take a full paragraph or two. Nothing is described twice.
  • Description is always somebody's. Each viewpoint notices different things (below), and the painting comes through those things. A room at court is a different room to Elyas, Jaennis and Lyra. If a detail could have come from any of them, cut it or give it to one of them.
  • Concrete over ornate. Texture comes from exact nouns and verbs: the lime-wash flaking off a toll-house, the smell of wet fleece in a shepherds' hall, the tar-and-cinnamon of a Fairport warehouse. Allow one image per paragraph at most, and let it come from the viewpoint's own life. No stacked adjectives, no abstractions dressed as images, no weather standing in for emotion.
  • The season is the book's clock. The action runs from late Haying to Salting (see "Time and messages"): cut hay and dust on the valley roads, then stubble and gleaners, then leaf-fall and early dark in the flint hills, then the first hard frosts and salting-tubs at the Hollin causeways. Let each chapter's light and air say where in the year it stands, so no date is needed.
  • The north is painted differently. North of the valley the Dolmis gradient applies. Description there is starker and less explained: cairns, sheep-tracks, peat smoke, weather that arrives before anyone sees it coming. The places carry the unease. The prose does not comment on it. Anything in the north that might be a portent is described exactly and left alone (see "Veralyn, Myrmis, and Shadowsteel").
  • What does not change. `PROSE_STYLE.md` still governs. Richer description does not license a wink, a narrative-vocabulary word, a tacked-on portent, or the kill-list words. Richness is in the seeing, not the adverbs.

Tylan, and the art of war

Tylan Vayle has two chapters of his own (7 and 24). Everywhere else his way of seeing reaches the page through what he says and writes, and through what Veralyn and the others notice him noticing. He sees strategically, not tactically. He cares about where a battle is fought and whether the army can still eat the week after, not about where the pikes stand. His thinking should be recognisably the thinking of The Art of War expansion (`expansions/art_of_war.tex`), adapted into his own plain speech and never quoted.

  • The commander decides. Tylan does not give Veralyn advice about what she should want. He gives her the cost of each thing she might want, then waits. ("A commander does not fight. A commander decides," from the expansion's ch. 9, is the principle behind his silence in ch. 2 and ch. 12.)
  • Grain and exits. He looks at a position by what feeds it and how it can be left. On the empty field in ch. 2 he checks the earls' line of retreat before he looks at their stakes. At the Hollin table in ch. 28 three of his four moves are aimed at Valdais's food and his road home, not at his soldiers. This is the expansion's picture of a general who counts "the weight of grain in a granary, the exit routes from a mountain pass."
  • Every arrow is a meal. He turns supplies into days and days into men. Four hundred horses in ch. 25 is not a figure to him but a march that will not happen. Remounts and fodder at relay stations are his idea of courage.
  • Morale is a supply. He speaks of an army's spirit the way he speaks of its bread, as a thing that runs down and has to be replaced. He notices when a company has not been paid, and when it has stopped singing.
  • Friction and fog. He plans for the messenger who does not arrive, the ford that has risen, the lord who is a day late. His position sheets record only what he has seen or had confirmed, which is why the one entry about the sword's position in ch. 5 disturbs him.
  • The campaign, not the battle. He sees the war as a sequence: opening moves, manoeuvre, one deciding day, then what follows. In ch. 31 and after, he is already thinking about pursuit and winter quarters while others are still counting the dead.
  • Aftermath. He is the one who knows victory has a price the dispatch will not print. He does not say so. He sees to the wounded horses.

On the page. Tylan's sheets appear verbatim in short excerpts: position, strength, food in days, state of mounts, and nothing else. His speech is brief and exact. When a scene is seen through someone who is watching him, give it one detail of how he looks at ground: he walks a field's exits first, he picks up a handful of a granary's grain, he asks the depth of a ford before he asks who holds it. The description in his scenes should show terrain the way a strategist sees it, as routes, cover, water, fodder and distance. That is his tint. In his own chapters the same eye does the painting.

Style reconciliations (from the tone notes)

  • Caesar's clarity comes from the ensemble, not the narrator. Build the plan across chapters (Tylan's counts, Seniro's roads, Fayd's named lords) so the reader understands it without a lecture. Inside any single chapter, keep Tolstoy's partial view.
  • Dread comes from concrete things. An absent barge, a message reaching an abandoned house, a watch lifted for good reasons. No tacked-on ominous line. If a portent appears, a character acts on it or reports it. In the north, portents may stay subtextual and unexplained, at the level of A Roll of the Fallen, and every one must survive a skeptical reading. See "Veralyn, Myrmis, and Shadowsteel." `PROSE_STYLE.md` governs.
  • The documentary quality is in what characters handle: dispatches, terms, dockets and lists. No historian-narrator. Six principal close-third voices, and Tylan's two chapters.
  • The simplification of the victory is shown, not stated. The herald's line in 32. A song in 34, if it's wanted. Never let a character say the thing about authorship.
  • Spare base, painted surface. The dry register of the Riarhin books is the base, not the whole. This book leans toward description of scenes and settings through each viewpoint's lens. See "Register: the tint, and what each viewpoint notices."
  • Names follow the speaker. Conlin from people who knew him before; Constano on documents. Wymund the Red from soldiers; Seniro in the rolls.
