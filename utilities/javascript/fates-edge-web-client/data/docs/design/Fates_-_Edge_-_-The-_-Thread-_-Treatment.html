The Thread

A working treatment of the second Riarhin book

What this is. The working plan for The Thread, sequel to The Last Blade and, in one particular, to No Bruises. The manuscript is `the_thread.md`.

Viewpoint. Close third-person limited, Riarhin throughout, with one exception: the prologue belongs to Farrick and ends with him. No cutaways into Aryka, Na'ar, Serafine or Lena. What the reader knows about Aryka's operation, the reader learns when Riarhin does — which is the whole mechanism of Act III and must not be leaked earlier by a stray line of narration.

Two years have passed since the parting on the steppe at the end of The Last Blade. Riarhin has been in Acasia and the hills; Na'ar has been with her a year and four months.

PART ZEROWhat It Is Built On

Kalrantha is an object and nobody knows what kind. Green glass in a brass frame, out of the Himadri, one repeated northern paragraph, a wish with a clause folded into it. Riarhin does not know what it is, Kargath never found out, and the book must not resolve it. By the opening of this book, the lead she carried out of the first has become four pieces of paper: the copied catalogue entry, a dealer's name and a forwarding house on the southern coast, an eighteen-month-old refusal from that house, and six Oshiiran names of which four are dead and one never existed. All of it has gone cold. That is why she is in the hills killing Rothari and not on the coast.

Kargath is the reason and not quite the reason. He wanted it, spent years and money on it, came away with a rumour and minded. That is a recommendation, not a reason, and Aryka says so. Beneath it: Riarhin has been moving for years, is very good at leaving, and the road has begun to feel like standing still. Kalrantha is not a road. It is a thing to find. She never examines this and no narration explains it for her.

The blade ledger continues from the first book. Every killing names the hand and nobody ever remarks on the pattern. In Act I the sword is drawn exactly once, on Farrick, and only after she has made him pick his own up. Tacen at his ledger, Parrin against the rock, both men in the loft, the runner in the mud, the child-shaped thing — all Toothpick. Do not let the sword drift into the quiet work.

The thread is a real object and it is not the creature's. The witch spins it out of a bowl of soup and eleven words, and Riarhin walks into it with her eyes open because she is tired. Riarhin feels it at the throat when the witch dies, and it slackens rather than snapping when the promise is discharged — a line goes slack when the other end is let go of, which is not the same as being free of the line. The creature says so explicitly and takes care not to be blamed for somebody else's work.

Morag's grammar, never named. Hospitality, exact wording, courtesy, promises, thread. The creature does not lie; that would be crude. It offers what is needed and lets people discover afterwards what they agreed to pay. The book never names the patron and never explains the rules.

Silkstrand is not a homecoming and Riarhin insists on the distinction often enough that it starts to sound like one. Her body knows the city before she does: the shutters going thin, jasmine over a drain, counting the rings on a stranger's hand and estimating how easily each would come off. Silkstrand teaches her nothing. It explains where the old skills came from.

Aryka Everblood is a warrior who has spent thirty years doing politics, and that order matters. Politics gave her patience, indirection, logistics and the strategic value of letting other people believe they are in control; it did not touch the underlying impulse, which is to identify what must be done and then remove whatever prevents it. Once she has decided, not doing it becomes nearly unnatural. Her brother Wyllmot had the same engine with worse brakes and could mistake certainty for righteousness. Aryka learned the thing he did not: wanting something done does not make you right. It does mean she is probably going to do it.

She was exiled to Acasia. The instrument said appointed; it appointed her to nothing, which was the elegance of it. That is why she has a writ on four Rothari, eight months in hills nobody visits twice, two men who are not for the hills, and a house in Kolmar she never had to ask the way to — and Riarhin deduces the whole of it off those tells, which is the one time in Act I she gets out in front of her. The queendom is not a frontier she chose. It is the place they put her to be rid of her, and she is going back to it with money. For the first time in decades the ambition is hers rather than an argument inherited from dead men.

She is telling the truth about being finished. Viterra, Vhasia, Veralyn, Aleric, the throne, thirty years of somebody else's chair. I am done is a status report, not a confession, and she never argues the point and never lies about it. Riarhin hears wounded pride, which is a reasonable error, because a woman who means it and a woman who has stopped arguing look identical from outside. Every observation Riarhin makes is accurate; only the story is wrong — and the story is Riarhin's own difficulty fitted onto somebody else, because it was the only shape she had on her to fit.

She does not secretly orchestrate everything, and must not be written as though she does. She does not know what Riarhin will find, has not predicted Serafine's moves, and has not arranged the heist's failure. The heist fails because it genuinely exists independently of her operation. What she does is select capable people, create options, move resources, accept incomplete information, and act decisively once the situation resolves. Her intelligence is more impressive for not requiring certainty.

Never confuse Aryka being outside her specialty with Aryka being incompetent. If Lena beats her through an alley, Lena knows the alley. If Riarhin spots the pickpocket and she does not, Riarhin set the trap. If Serafine owns the conversation, Serafine owns the terrain. Let her lose those exchanges; she does not need to win everything to stay formidable, and she can tolerate being the joke when nothing important is being lost, which is precisely what separates her from Wyllmot. Riarhin wins moments. Serafine wins rooms. Lena wins streets. Aryka wins campaigns. And when she cannot win the room she may set it on fire — not the elegant thief's answer but the soldier's: change the conditions until a different game is being played.

Her blind spot is the interesting future trouble. She is very good at deciding what must be done and then making reality accommodate the decision. What she is out of practice at asking is who gave her the right to decide. Institutions and rival powers used to ask it for her; a queendom will not. It is touched exactly once, at the parting: Who tells you no, in Acasia, once it's yours? — and she considers it, and does not consider it for very long, and that is the answer, and neither of them hears it as one.

Riarhin is not Aryka's scout. She is Aryka's advance element, and she does not know it. Aryka learned in the hills that Riarhin manufactures situations rather than collecting intelligence: give her an objective and freedom and the environment gives up its hidden structures. Aryka does not want to correct this. She wants to see where it goes, and to be standing somewhere useful when it gets there.

Lena must come out of this alive and portable. She appears later in the Brass Gate material and in the GM guide's mini-adventure, so the sack of Silkstrand needs to leave her mobile, solvent enough and with a reason to travel. Do not kill her and do not tie her to the city.

Four people are playing four different games and only two of them know it.

Riarhin wants to have already won before she arrives. Her preference is the plan that needs nobody to be fought and nothing to be improvised: understand everyone so thoroughly that the evening arrives at the conclusion she built for it and the whole thing has the shape of a thing that happened by itself. She likes it when they do it themselves, because then it is not her. Silkstrand refuses her this for a month. Every clean plan dies — the clerk who was always going to go to Bell Street does not go; the copied seal meets a countersignature that exists for no reason — and each time she has eleven seconds and builds another. She is far better at the improvising than at the planning, has always been, and has spent forty years arranging her life so as not to notice that the thing she is worst at wanting is the thing she is best at. Nobody ever says any of this to her.

Serafine is not answering her. That is the whole of it and it must be got right: there is no counter-move, no watcher on Riarhin, no duel of wits. Serafine has spent nineteen years arranging a house that cannot be planned against — rotations changed in seasons when nobody was coming, dockets countersigned for no reason, the laundry hour moved a quarter twice a year — and Riarhin's plans keep dying because she is not playing a woman, she is walking into weather. Every branch of every evening already serves Serafine. She is beaten in Act III by arson, which is not on the board, and by the fact that a building with people in it cannot be held.

Aryka is doing exactly what Riarhin is doing, unobserved, and better. She hires, moves money, positions transport, establishes command and prepares extraction in a city where all four of those things are ordinary daylight commerce, and she improvises the whole of it as the opportunity moves. The reader is given every piece: mercenaries who belong to nobody, carts riding low, a man touching two fingers to his brow in a street where he has been told not to, dust on her boots, what needs be done. Every observation Riarhin makes is accurate. Only the story she files them under is wrong, and the story is one Aryka never told her. When her twenty-men suggestion is refused she does not argue, and the reason is not that she has finally understood why force will not work; it is that she has just been told when it will.

Lena is not playing at all. Luck is a loan: you are given it, you give it back, and then it is nobody's. A clean theft leaves nobody holding anything. She will take wages at the end and nothing in advance and no favour in either direction, because in that city everybody holds a piece of everybody and the pieces are why nothing ever moves. She cannot be retained, bedded in the same house, or told where she sleeps, and she does not need to be pinned down — only watched. In an hour and a half of the first meeting she never once asks Riarhin why she wants the papers, and has not forgotten to; she simply declines to pick it up, which is the one thing nobody else in the book can manage.

The reader should believe Lena is edging Aryka out of the story. By the end of Act II this has to look settled: Lena has the house, the hours, the roofs and nine children, argues properly and is right about a third of the time, and told Riarhin to stop making plans and was correct. Aryka has a purse, two men, one ridiculous suggestion and somewhere to be in the afternoons. Riarhin stops consulting her, and it is not a decision — it happens the way a path happens. The last Tally has the two of them working a table for three hours while Aryka sits at the end of it cleaning a sword she has not drawn in six weeks. Na'ar watches that and says nothing, because nobody has pointed at anybody yet.

Lena is one of the eight who got out. Ten years ago Serafine had the roof of the Silk Coin's guildhouse fired in the Dye Yards, and eight children escaped it, and she wrote them down as eight people beyond her reach who would remember. Lena was eight or nine. She never says so and Riarhin never asks. It is assembled out of things nobody said: that she goes round the site rather than across it, the way Riarhin goes round a ford she does not intend to use; that her crew are children she feeds and does not own; that a bowl of food went on being eaten in a precisely ordinary manner when Serafine's name came up. Riarhin does the arithmetic, declines to say any part of it aloud, and notices with some surprise that she is doing for Lena what Na'ar does for her. It is also the reason Serafine does not entirely own her, and the reason the Act III rhyme belongs to her as much as to anybody.

ACT I — THE HUNT

Farrick and twenty Rothari boys collect dues from a hamlet that cannot pay and take what they want anyway. One a day, for a tenday, down to seven in an abandoned mill, praying for wind. Riarhin comes through the thatch. Farrick yields and asks for mercy; she tells him to pick the sword up, because he is beyond it, and kills him when he does.

She carries the head to Kolmar in a wet sack, sits at the bar with whiskey she has not paid for, buys a drink for three Rothari who are Farrick's kin, and tells them to drink or join him. Aryka Everblood walks in behind a trail of bodies, is genuinely delighted, and hires her: two hundred for the man who gave the order, fifty a head for the three hands. Riarhin makes it three hundred and seventy-five, half in advance, because Aryka asked in the Acasi way with a figure, like a woman buying a horse. Aryka changes the terms — the principal first, the hands frightened before they die — and Riarhin takes it.

The road north establishes all three of them. Aryka rides hard, sleeps rough, checks her own feet for hot places, and decides immediately; she is a warrior who has spent thirty years having to do politics, not a noble who learned enough to look convincing. Na'ar requires no explanation and asks Riarhin nothing, which is most of why she travels with him. Riarhin lies on a slope for six hours because waiting is not the absence of the work, refuses to be commanded, and delivers.

Tacen dies at his own ledger with a page torn out of it — he had raised four men's wages two weeks after the hamlet. Riarhin takes the stable boy out on the hay cart. The hands take nineteen days, nine of them spent on nothing: an opened gate, four straps cut halfway through from the inside, a fire on a ridge. One walks out alone and is allowed to finish talking first. The other two are bad work in a loft. Aryka watches the nothing and understands that it is what she paid for.

Aryka pays in full, loudly, in front of the barkeep — and does not leave.

Riarhin lays out her four sheets of paper and hears how little they are. There is a fifth she does not put on the table: three forwarded lines, two years old, saying the Duke of the Belworth's condition is unchanged. She knows he is dead and keeps the earlier letter that says unchanged. She will not dwell on what followed it.

The witch at Ossen Fold is dying, knows four of Riarhin's names, and has read her papers well enough to sell her where Kalrantha is not. She asks Riarhin to save her daughter. Riarhin offers to save her, and is told to have some soup, and eats it; and then, asked for the words, she asks two questions she does not need the answers to and offers the old woman a different verb — you want me to find her — and the old woman takes it. Find her. Yes. Find her. One word late, with something crossing her face that has nothing to do with dying, and she does not correct it, because she has wanted that verb for twenty-two years and a woman two days from the end cannot want two things at once. Riarhin then binds herself, out loud, to find. The witch smiles. Riarhin does not. The wording is exact and given four times: the counting house, in Silkstrand — but you must go by the counting house. When the witch dies, something closes around Riarhin's throat.

Two days north, at walls and fords, Aryka's history arrives in pieces: the height Veralyn always had and the strength Aryka had at twenty that Viterra would not write down; Wyllmot, who was not cruel but could not hold two shapes in his head; Arwyn the drunk, thirty years old in his brother's shadow after Charau; Aleric struck from the succession by Brightstride for making the Fenwood fiction impossible not to answer; Brightstride dead the year after, and the expedient becoming the settlement. She says she is done. Riarhin tells her she is not. Aryka does not argue, and Riarhin files that wrongly.

The hill is under quarantine. Four men from the hamlet — a shepherd, his son, a cooper, and an old man who does the talking — sit at a fire with two billhooks and a boar spear at the head of the last enclosed ground, and have done in shifts for six years, and will not say why beyond not up there. Riarhin cannot decide on the climb whether that is a cordon or a fence: what is up there is theirs and is going to stay theirs, or somebody long dead told them that the day a stranger walks up that track is the hamlet's last good day. The old man does not argue with her. He recognises something and stands out of the path, which she likes least of all. Aryka's colour comes up for the first time in the book — nobody posts a watch on a story, the village has been right about this for six years, and Farrick kept riders in a valley with no tolls and no ford and nothing worth burning for eight months and she had assumed he was hunting her. The Rothari may have been looking for this. Nobody confirms it.

The shieling above the burnt wood holds a clean, combed child on a stone bench where there has been no water and no one for six years. It tells her what it is — what was asked for by a grieving woman who said anything out loud in a place where saying it is an opening — and it points out that it has not lied once, and that the thread at Riarhin's throat is the old woman's work and not its own, and that Riarhin is going to keep her promise. It gives her the counting house and admits it does not know what Kalrantha is either. Wondering is most of what it does.

Then it looks past her at Aryka and says it smells the hunger in her. Riches: nothing. Glory: nothing. No. You seek pow— and Riarhin puts Toothpick through the base of its skull mid-word. The thread slackens. Aryka is furious, and not about the child: I wanted to hear the rest.

There is a third one, and they never meet her. By Na'ar's fire afterwards Riarhin does the other sum: a woman who has not left one bed in a year, and yet the story arrives down the whole valley in order — a woman in Sedris, a sister married in Cair, a carter, a boy off a hay cart, and a hill woman taught a Lethai word she has never heard spoken. Stories travel badly; this one has been carried. Which wants somebody going up and down that country for years in whatever shape the next kitchen will listen to — pedlar, midwife, woman off a hay cart, pilgrim with a bad foot — keeping a road open and a village frightened, because a thing on a hill nobody can reach is only a thing on a hill: what it needs is a rumour and eventually somebody with a sword and a conscience walking up to let it out. She is right. She has no name, no description and nothing to do about it, and the book never produces her. Somewhere off the page a woman hears that the shieling is empty and takes it well and begins again somewhere with better roads.

They bury the witch — Aryka digs, because digging is what she does instead of saying things — and ride south. The watch on the track goes out that evening and the turf hut is pulled down by morning, tidily, the way men put away a thing they never intend to explain. Aryka says twice that she meant it. Along the way the Rothari try Kolmar once more and Na'ar is let off the leash for forty seconds, which is the only time in Act I that anybody sees what he is. On the sea wall the night before the city he mentions his four brothers, and Kargath's rise, and that a man with nothing to be done about it is free; and Riarhin recognises the sentence, and says nothing, because he has not asked her anything.

ACT II — SILKSTRAND

They obey the wording: they go by the counting house, not into it, and watch until a man comes out who is interesting enough to follow. He leads to the Velvet Court and to Madam Serafine.

Serafine's power is not Aryka's. No banners, no formation — obligations, information, introductions, debts, secrets, favours, and people who know exactly what disappointing her would cost. She serves Livaea. She recognises Riarhin as someone trained to survive rooms like hers, and Riarhin recognises the shape of her power before she understands its details. Both women find this interesting.

Aryka is markedly less impressive here, and it begins to be funny. Riarhin knows the forms; Serafine knows the city; Na'ar is large enough to stay relevant under any social conditions; and Aryka Everblood, warlord and former power of another court, keeps being told to wait. She does not enjoy waiting. She asks no further questions, which should read as defeat and is not.

The Kalrantha account cannot be requested. Whatever survived is behind intermediaries, old transactions and forwarding records belonging to interests that do not want strangers reading them. It will have to be stolen, which means a thief.

The thief is found by using the city's theft network as a routing protocol rather than by identifying anybody. A child brushes them; Riarhin slaps its hand off her purse; the child successfully robs Aryka instead, and during the brush Riarhin plants a note that begins To whom it may concern. Aryka runs and does not catch it. The message travels upstream and reaches Quick Lena.

Lena is young and not a novice: children who know streets adults overlook, roofs respectable people never see, keys they should not have, and loyalties Serafine does not entirely own. Her relationship with debt is the opposite of Serafine's — Serafine's world makes obligations durable; Lena's holds that a clean theft should leave nobody attached to anybody. Riarhin and Lena circle each other and then work.

Riarhin puts a stone in the pond and learns nothing she can use. On the twenty-second day, with four versions on a table and no way to know whether Serafine is watching her or is simply weather, she goes up and does the one experiment that answers it: she brings Serafine a true gift — three enquiries about a Himadri account, which Riarhin arranged herself for nine crowns — and is thanked, and is told precisely what has just happened to her, which is that she is now a very small creditor and will be cleared at an hour that suits the house. Then she tries the three things she was taught in a room with a mirror by a woman who did hair: a deliberate error to be corrected, an eleven-second silence, and a question with a false floor. She is agreed with at length about nothing, complimented on the length of the silence, and handed a description of the city's drainage so exact that she takes two useful facts out of it and only realises on the stair that neither is about the account. She spends her last instrument, rudeness — you pay something once a year and your own people go quiet three days beforehand — and gets a plain yes with no content in it, because a denial would have had a shape. They call me the Velvet Touch because I never leave bruises. A bruise is a record. Riarhin then watches the house for two days and it does nothing whatever, and on the third day one woman is sent to Marcott with a box, which is either the whole answer or a woman going to Marcott. The result: the house never moves first, and moves all at once afterwards — which she already knew and has now paid for. Lena hears nothing and is pleased, and Riarhin has to sit down to see why. Serafine is not to be outmanoeuvred in a room. She breathes this the way other people breathe.

And this is where Aryka begins disappearing. Where are you going? — What needs be done. Riarhin stops asking, because there is enough to manage. She sees mercenaries, which is unremarkable in Silkstrand. She sees carts riding low. Once she sees a man acknowledge Aryka with the economy of somebody used to taking orders from her, and Aryka sees her see it, and neither of them speaks.

ACT III — THE HEIST

The job begins well, which is essential. The plan is good, Lena's people are good, the preparation pays where it should — and they get to Kalrantha, or to what generations have left of it.

The records look useless and then look worse than useless. Consignments without contents, payments through unrelated accounts, a forwarding record that terminates where another should begin, descriptions that change between hands, dates without cargo, payments without destinations. Age does that. So do fire, theft and ordinary decay. But the damage has shape: somebody removed information, and then somebody else did, in different archives by different methods, possibly not in concert. The troubling possibility is that they were not working together — that people kept finding out about Kalrantha and independently deciding the next person should know less. Nobody can say why. Kargath could not. This is as far as anyone has got.

Destruction is rarely perfect. Freight costs money, journeys take time, caravans need provisions, and accountants record distance indirectly in fodder, wages, water, insurance, guards and days. The remnants converge — not on a vault or a city or a tomb, but on a region. Ashaan. Then narrower, and the narrowing is partly an accident of names: in the accounts Ashaan means the country and in four other places it means the city of the same name, and no clerk in eighty years troubled to say which. A good deal of what Riarhin took for concealment turns out to be that. Past the city, off any route a sensible foreign traveller would pick, deep into the Red Wastes. Riarhin has spent the book pursuing a thing she cannot name in order to learn that it is somewhere in an enormous and famously lethal desert. It is terrible information and she feels better than she has in years, and does not notice.

Then it goes wrong — not to anybody's hidden plan, just wrong. Riarhin adapts, Lena adapts, the second failure compounds the first, Na'ar finally gets to hit someone and his evening improves. And then Serafine closes the trap, because it is her city, her house, her people and her information, and she has watched the disturbance propagate through networks she understands better than the people disturbing them. Assumptions stop working, escape routes acquire guards, neutral parties turn out to have obligations. The heist actually fails. There is no secret final move, no forgotten tunnel, and Na'ar cannot kill fast enough to change it.

Then the least useful person in the room speaks. I have money. I have men. — Where's your money? — With the men. — And where are your men? — Surrounding the building. And, when Serafine mentions hostages and ways of being persuasive: Yes, well. That's why I told them to set fire to this pillow house.

Only now does Riarhin understand the operation. Aryka never became her sidekick; Riarhin became Aryka's advance element. Aryka came to Silkstrand, saw enormous concentrations of portable wealth protected by arrangements that hold only because everyone with force has reasons not to disturb them, and needed a disturbance to fund a queendom in Acasia. She hired men, moved money, positioned transport, established command and prepared extraction, all in plain sight, because Silkstrand is full of mercenaries and carts and armed strangers. She told Riarhin the truth the whole time. Riarhin had decided the truth was sour grapes and read accurate observations through the wrong story.

The fire destroys Serafine's control of space rather than her power: she cannot hold every door while patrons and workers flee, cannot preserve sight lines through smoke, cannot let the building burn with people in it she actually cares about. Doors open. Riarhin gets Lena and the thieves out. Aryka's men come into the chaos and the operation expands — patrons fleeing the Velvet Court discover the soldiers outside are not rescuers, and an armed force with wagons and insufficient supervision develops an expansive view of its objectives. What began as exploiting a heist becomes something close to a localised sack of downtown Silkstrand.

There is an older rhyme underneath it. Serafine once helped destroy the Silk Coin with force and fire; now her own house burns while thieves go out through the doors. Nobody explains this. The wheel turns.

Ending — The Wheel

The book ends on the morning after the fire, while the sack continues. Riarhin has counted the nine leaves, briefly begun planning the southern journey, and slept in the alley she first used at twelve. Aryka organises the wagons; Riarhin pays Lena in the fish market and sees Serafine directing the salvage of the Velvet Court. Lena has wages and a possible road toward the Brass Gate. Serafine remains dangerous. Riarhin's tooth is growing back; Serafine's will not.

Riarhin and Na'ar walk uphill. Aryka's column has not departed. The last line is Put down that marble bust, Keegain—, followed by THE END. There is no quay scene, sailing scene or Red Wastes coda. The voyage and the southern route belong to `the_red_wastes_notes.md` and the next book; neither is an event already narrated in The Thread.

APPENDIX

Working Rules

On the epigraph. The soup is always free. The price never is. is the same saying that opens The Widow's Hand, and the attribution is deliberately different: Adrian there, an ancient proverb here. It is older and more widely known than Vhasia thinks it is, and Vhasia attributes it to Adria. Do not reconcile the two attributions; the discrepancy is the point. In this book the proverb is also a plot instruction, and the witch's bowl of soup is the first time it is paid.

On the soup. Riarhin knows exactly what a pot on a fire in a witch's house is worth, knows four ways to be certain of it, has used all four in other rooms, and eats anyway because she is tired. The scene must not play as her being fooled. She is not fooled. She is spent, and the old woman has correctly judged that a woman who has been moving for years will take a bowl of soup from somebody who is dying.

On what the creature is owed. It never lies, it is not clever and not cruel, and it is attentive. It is careful to disclaim the thread it did not spin. It admits ignorance about Kalrantha and means it. Riarhin believes it, and that is the worst thing that happens to her that day. It is killed mid-word, and the word is never completed anywhere in the book.

On Aryka's competence gradient. She must be visibly superb in Act I and visibly peripheral in Act II, and both must be true rather than a trick. She is a warrior in a country that does not care who she is, and then a warlord in a city where force is the one instrument that cannot be used. Her suggestions in Act II are genuinely bad suggestions for a heist and genuinely good ones for what she is actually preparing. Let the reader laugh at her. She is humble enough to allow it and occasionally leans in.

On the worldbook. It is a story generator, not a lore supply, and almost everything in it is better implied than stated. A fact earns the page only where somebody notices it, needs it, or trips over it. The Archivolt, the Matron, the Ragman and the Tide Father are all in that city and not one of them is introduced, because Riarhin has no errand with any of them. The dyewater runs red and two women on a quay remark on it in the tone people use about weather and neither of them says the word Vhasia. Ashaan arrives as four accounts in nine days that do not agree with each other, because four disagreeing accounts is what she actually has; the Sisters come off a ship's cook who then asks whether she wants the fish. When a detail starts wanting a paragraph of explanation it is in the wrong place — move it to where it costs somebody something, or cut it.

On the calendars. Three are in use and the book uses two of them on purpose, because which one a person reaches for says who they are. The Civil Count is the calendar of paperwork — Aryka's writ, the date on a file, the twelfth of Solaris — and Riarhin thinks in it when she is writing something down. The Old Count is the calendar of work, and it is the one the whole of Silkstrand actually runs on: ten named days to a tenday, three tendays to a month, each day named for what is done on it. Linen goes out on Hearth. Carts come on Road. Accounts are read on Tally, and nothing may be distrained on Still.

Serafine's defence is that she has broken the Old Count inside her own house. Linen on Stone, books read on Yoking or Water or whichever morning she woke wanting to, deliveries taken on Still. It is not unlawful and it is very nearly obscene, and it means her own people cannot tell you what day a thing happens on — they wait to be told. Nineteen years of it. That is why Riarhin's plans die and why there is nothing to out-think: the house has no rhythm to learn. They go in on Still, when nothing is owed and the pillow houses are full, because a full house is one where everybody who works in it is downstairs.

The Fenwood chancery at Tarlington has heard its accounts on Tally for four hundred years. Riarhin spent four years in that house and never once thought of it as a decision anybody had made. One line, never returned to.

And the ninth day is a space. Dhaharan caravan houses keep the Old Count for wages and write their tally-sheets with nine of the ten days named and the ninth left blank; a clerk once told her it was a scribal convention, in the voice of a man who had been asked before. On four of the sheets in Serafine's press somebody has written in the space — a mark, four times, in four hands, across sixty years, and no two of the four the same. Riarhin looks at it longer than at anything else in the file and then sets it aside, because it is not what she came for. The book does not come back to this. It pulls toward the Ninth and never arrives, which is the standing rule.

On the veils, seeded early. The Ashaani veil grammar must not arrive all at once at the end. Riarhin spends forty days reading a woman in a veil, so the contrast belongs in that room: Serafine's is velvet and Silkstrandi and means nothing except that you cannot be sure where she is looking, and Riarhin has seen the other kind on the wharves, on Ashaani crews, where the colours mean things and the meanings are not optional — told to her once by a man who lowered his voice to do it and could not say afterwards why he had. The coda then needs only the one line with a consequence in it.

On Lena and the nine. They are never introduced and never counted aloud except twice, and both times it is Lena doing it with her hands. The second is in the kitchen court after the fire, touching each head. The first is earlier and quieter: Riarhin comes up onto a roof before she is expected and finds Lena against a chimney with three of them asleep on her and a fourth eating out of a bowl she is holding, and Lena does not move, because moving would wake them, and does not apologise either — and Riarhin goes back down, waits a quarter of an hour, and comes up again noisily. Neither of them ever mentions it. That is the whole of what the fire is later risking, and it is the only tenderness in the book that nobody pays for.

On the Sisters and The Daughter of Ahakar. That book opens with one of the three seen close and from a child's eye, and it is horrifying. Nothing in The Thread may undercut it. Here they stay at rumour distance and arrive third-hand, off a ship's cook who will not finish the sentence, which is the correct altitude for a book whose heroine has not met one yet. Do not let Riarhin form a picture.

On what she remembers. Perfect recall, and not a machine. Her attention is a lamp and not a lit room: she keeps, exactly and in order and for sixty-nine years, whatever she spent herself on, and keeps nothing whatever of what she did not. She can give the wear on the third step and the colour of a courier's coat-lining eleven days later; she walks past an armed man sitting alone on a laden cart at the eleventh hour, spends three seconds on the cart and none on the man, and keeps cart, man, paid. In the long room, cornered, she reaches for his face and finds a clean ordinary blank, and that blank is the hinge of the book. People who know what she is assume she remembers everything, and it is near enough true to be worth letting stand. Never write her recalling on demand. Write the lamp.

On what the peoples are called. Lethai-al, Lethai-ar, Lethai-thora, Aeler, Aelaerem, Aelinnel, Ykrul are the names. Elf, dwarf, halfling, gnome, orc exist in the language and are used by the lazy, the stupid, and by people who mean them as slurs, and the book uses them only in those three mouths and never in narration. Farrick thinks elf-touched in the mill and it is exactly right for a Rothari brigand at the end of a bad tenday. A hill woman relaying a dying witch's message says the Lethai woman and gets the word a little wrong, because the witch used the proper one and she does not own it. Nobody who respects Riarhin ever uses the short word about her, and Riarhin never uses it about herself.

On Riarhin as a scout. She is a terrible soldier: she resists orders, changes plans without permission, and treats instructions as descriptions of desired outcomes. Give her an objective and freedom and she prods the environment until its hidden structures show themselves — finds a person who knows a person, provokes a response, makes an enemy move too early, recruits someone unexpected, and opens something that did not exist when she started. Everyone in the book who understands this finds a use for it. She has never once understood it about herself.

On Braedyn. He is dead by this book, and Riarhin knows it. His death remains undated. She keeps the three forwarded lines that said unchanged while he was alive; retaining that earlier letter is avoidance, not uncertainty about his fate. Do not supply a deathbed scene or make her explain her grief. One touch per act at most.

On Na'ar. Ykrul to the core, past forty-five, a brute with no apology in him, and not comic relief. He is the Conan of the stories rather than the poster: unhurried, worldly, wholly without pretence, and a good deal cleverer than any room assumes — the assumption being the useful part. For most of a book he is drinking and looking frightening and being taken for somebody's hired muscle, and he finds that funny and never corrects it. When swords come out he is rapturous and quiet about it, because a big man is supposed to roar and he does not. He fights with his feet and his reach: in the open, with room, he would beat Riarhin, and both of them know it and it has never once come up.

His tactical judgement is real and stays in its lane — ground for a fight, where a man will come through, how many he can hold, what the shape of a fire-scar on a hillside means. Outside that lane he is content to be told where they are going and to be handed a cup. And he never asks Riarhin what is wrong, in a year and four months, which is most of why she is still travelling with him.

He found her. She talked to him in a tavern where he was drinking and fighting and doing both extremely well, and he said nice to meet you, I am Na'ar, you seem interesting, I am going to follow you, and she said nothing and left, and he followed her. He tells it in exactly those words and there is nothing underneath it.

He lost four brothers in Kargath's rise — not to Kargath, to men who were becoming his, a distinction he has been told matters and does not accept. Kargath is dead, so there is nothing to be done about it, so he is free. It is a trauma response and the book never once says so. Riarhin recognises the shape of the sentence because she owns several built to the same tolerance, and she says nothing, because he has not asked her anything.

On Ghere, and why Lethai cannot read their grandparents. One mention, on the wall in Act I, hung off the fact that the only man who ever said Charau in Riarhin's hearing stopped both times and talked about the drainage. Her brother. He found her in Silkstrand and took her out of it at sixteen, and for fifteen years turned up in the Valewood two seasons out of five to teach her things and go away again; in between he walked in front of armies under the black banners and called it scouting, and took her along at nineteen for one campaign of seasoning. He wants her to be a Sentinel of the Valewood, which is the family trade, said in the tone that makes a thing a wall rather than an offer. Ghere translates as Prince — anybody with the syntax gets that far — and she does not know whether it is a name or a post, because Lethai does not hold still: the syntax is fixed and the sense drifts, nearly by season, so that she understands what her grandmother said and not one word of what her grandmother wrote, and no Lethai alive reads their grandparents' hand without a human ledger open beside it to say what year the words were in. That is why the thora are obsessed with human bookkeeping and the al are not. Do not explain any more of this than that. She asked him once; he said it's a name and gave her a bow to string.

On the failed plans. Four die and none of them is allowed a scene. Buying a clerk, going in at night and following the courier get a paragraph each in Act II and are dispatched in three sentences. Plan one dies because a man Riarhin had read correctly for eleven days does not do the thing he was always going to do. Plan two dies of a countersigned docket nobody had a reason to countersign. The slowness of Act II is the point, but the failures are quick: the reader should feel the ground going out, not sit through the surveying. Every one of them ends on the same note, which is Riarhin discovering that there is nothing to out-think.

On the eleven heartbeats. In the burning street, with the file inside her coat, Riarhin is already four hundred miles away buying grain — a house that will hold money for a stranger, a translator who belongs to nobody, a road known through two accounts both eighteen months old, water carried and not drawn, and the need for a translator — while a man carries a chair past her elbow and Aryka shouts about wagons. It takes about eleven heartbeats from the kitchen court gate. She does not notice that she has stopped thinking about Kalrantha and started thinking about the road to it, which are not the same illness. That beat, and not the coda, is where the next book actually begins.

On Kalrantha, which the author now knows and the characters do not. It was an attempt to control the element of Fate. It does not control Fate; it creates Fate around whoever wears it, which the makers worked out inside an afternoon and then stopped writing down. Fate is a slope and not a destination — it pulls you toward something and is not the thing you are pulled to — so the amulet tilts the ground under its wearer and they arrive at whatever was nearest and downhill, which is never quite what they asked for and is always close by. That is why the record was gone at four separate times by people who disagreed about what to hide: everyone who understood it reached the same conclusion about what to do with the understanding. The popular reading, held by serious people, is that it is a fragment of Varash the Unmaker. Varash exists, is a demon, is sealed beneath the Himadri, and has nothing to do with it except that the temple and the Ninth Seal Mine are nine miles apart in the same range. Keep the wrong answer in play. From inside, a manufactured slope is indistinguishable from a negotiation: coincidence arrives on cue and tailored, and the only shape a person has for that is that something is attending to them and wants paying. The full entry is `ttrpg/reference/worldbook/kalrantha.md`. Nobody in either novel says any of this.

On Riarhin's biography, gear and the regrowth, all of which now live in one place. `ttrpg/reference/worldbook/riarhin.md` is the reference and it supersedes anything in these treatments that disagrees with it. The load-bearing corrections: she worked out the alley at twelve, Ghere got her out of Silkstrand at sixteen, she was with the Black Banners at nineteen, and the Valewood refused to make her Ghe'hai at about thirty-one — after which she found a dead Ghe'hai in the wood and took Remorse, the hauberk and the cloak off it, which is the whole answer to who made it / I found it and must not be improved on. Her cloak is one colour at a time and the colour is always the country's, and only she remembers it changing; everybody else remembers it as the colour it is now and is not lying. And the regrowth runs the other way from the way it was first written: her teeth come back, slowly, and Serafine's will not — which is the one thing about herself she is embarrassed by, has never told anybody, and checks about nine times a day without deciding to.

On the Dye Yards fire: ten years, not nineteen. Lena is nineteen. The guildhouse burned ten years ago and she was eight or nine when she got out of it, which is the age the book needs her to have been. Serafine's nineteen years are a separate count — that is how long she has run the Velvet Court, and it must not be confused with the fire.

On elevens. The number is a signature and it was being spent like small change. It is now reserved: the eleven words of the witch's oath, the eleven steps at the counting house, Serafine's eleven minutes, the eleventh hour and the man on the cart, eleven days beyond the last watered stage, the eleven men in the lane and the eleven people downstairs. Every one of those is a callback or a load-bearing fact. Anything else that wants a number takes a different one. If a new eleven appears, it has to displace one of the above.

On the ending, final shape. There is no quay and no departure. The book does not put Riarhin on a ship and does not name where she is going. It ends with her walking up a hill in a city that is still being robbed, with Aryka's voice behind her, and the last line is the marble bust. Everything about the road south — the ports, the season, the plateau, the country at the other end — belongs to the next book and is not the business of this one. The single paragraph of her thinking in the burning street stays, because it is characterisation and not setting: she cannot stop working, and she does not notice she has stopped thinking about Kalrantha and started thinking about the road to it. It names no cities.

What the last chapter now has room for. Three things were rushed and are not any more.

Aryka works the aftermath. She has not been to bed. Riarhin finds her at the head of a queue in Cooper's Lane at the second hour with a strip of board and a nail, writing down every man and what he is owed for carrying it, with a boy on a tailgate with a piece of chalk who has never been given a job before. Not that. Glass. It will be sand by the Belworth and you will have carried sand four hundred miles. — It's worth— — It is worth what arrives. Twenty-six hours awake, four streets taken, hair up, boots done, and no performance in it: she is simply not tired, in the way of certain people in their early forties who have spent more than twenty years being handed the parts nobody else wanted to carry and have stopped experiencing that as an imposition. Riarhin watches her do it and understands that the purse was the least interesting thing about her and had been standing in the open the whole time. Aryka does not stop working: Do not say anything improving to me. I have been awake all night and I shall remember it.

Serafine's tooth. The four seconds of looking now have a beat in front of them. She gives an instruction with her mouth open enough to show a front tooth broken off square, an old break worn smooth, and Riarhin puts her tongue against her own gap without deciding to — the loft at Vethric's, a tooth in her hand in the dark, and a rib cut that closed in four days and left a line already hard to find. The wounds close quickly; the tooth comes back slowly. It began about the nineteenth day and will take most of a year. Serafine’s tooth will not return. Then the other mended mouth she knows in this city: fifty years ago, a wet night, a hand turning her the right way up a stair, and a silver tooth, because in Silkstrand a tooth is a thing a woman with money puts money into — and Serafine had not had money that year, or had had it and spent it on something that mattered more, which is the likelier reading and the one Riarhin dislikes, because it makes them similar in a particular she did not consent to. And through the whole four seconds Serafine goes on holding the cart with her attention, which is the most frightening thing Riarhin has seen all night, and the night included a burning building. Riarhin walks away not certain which of them the two years will be worse for, and aware that Serafine is already at work and she is not.

The alley. She gets into it without deciding to, and that is the aside: she did not choose that alley tonight. She chose it at twelve, for the wind and the lamplight and the two ways out, and has been choosing the same alley in eight countries ever since without improving the criteria once. A woman could call that competence. A woman could also notice that she was twelve and hungry when she worked it out and has not required anything new since. Lena is nineteen, has wages coming, has nobody holding a piece of her, and will sleep somewhere she paid for. Riarhin gets nowhere with that and stops. Nothing in the last chapter is there for texture; each of the three is the same book arguing with itself about what a life costs to run.

On the shape of the ending, which has been rebuilt once and should not be rebuilt again. The beats run: Riarhin gets out of the kitchen court; she counts the file before she goes anywhere, because a woman who does not count her papers finds out about it on a ship; standing in a chandler's passage in a burning quarter she goes across the water and starts buying grain four hundred miles away, and does not notice that she has stopped thinking about Kalrantha and started thinking about the road to it; what brings her back is a man going past with a chair she once moved eight inches; the Dye Yards rhyme, said to nobody; and an alley off Bell Street she slept in at twelve, where Na'ar takes the second watch. Then morning: the sack has not stopped, and the Watch is negotiating rather than arresting; Lena in the fish market; Serafine in the road with no veil and four seconds of looking; the hill above the streets, with Aryka's unmoved column still audible below. The book's last line is Put down that marble bust, Keegain— and then THE END. There is no Red Wastes chapter. The Ashaani material that used to sit there belongs to the next book; what survives here is one paragraph of Riarhin's logistics in the burning street, which is all the preview this ending can carry.

On paragraphing, which is a standing fault. The default here is one sentence to a paragraph, and it works for turns, reversals and single facts landing — That would have worked. — and it stops working the moment three of them in a row belong to the same beat. Then it is not urgency, it is fragmentation, and the reader's eye is being stopped four times for one movement. The test is mechanical: consecutive single-sentence paragraphs that describe one continuous action get folded into one paragraph; a single-sentence paragraph earns its white space only when the sentence changes the direction of the scene. Action sequences need this most and get it least, because the temptation is strongest exactly where the writing is fastest. The duel with Kargath in the first book breathes; match that, not the staccato.

On sentence fragments. Fragments are for lists that are genuinely lists — Not a vault. Not a city. Not a tomb. — and for the roll of names, and that is close to the whole of the licence. Three in one paragraph anywhere else is a verbal habit rather than a decision, and it reads as one. When in doubt, restore the verb and keep the comma.

On Aryka's interiority, and why the reveal needs it. The reveal in Act III depends on the reader having been given Aryka's want and denied her method, which is not the same as being told nothing. She is a woman who has never once had a thing that was hers to decide and who says so, plainly, twice: in the stable yard at Kolmar — I do not want a throne; I want a room where the last word in it is mine and there is nobody standing in the doorway — and on the stair the night before the job, when she names the only thing she is afraid of, which is being the person things happen around. Both are true. Neither gives away a single cart. Riarhin files both correctly and under the wrong heading, and the error is specific rather than vague: she is listening for absence and what is actually in the voice is appetite, and the two are indistinguishable from outside for exactly as long as the book needs. I'm not doing anything is a joke told on top of that, and it does not have to carry the weight alone. Never write Aryka as withholding; write her as answering the question she was asked.

On exposition in dialogue. Nobody in this book explains an institution to somebody who already knows it. Where an arrangement has to be understood, the shortest version goes in the mouth and the rest goes into the head of whoever already owns it — the Fenwood fiction is three lines in Riarhin's own recollection because she spent four years inside it answering a bell, not a paragraph of Aryka telling her what her own life was. The rule: if two characters both know a thing, at most one of them may gesture at it, and the reader gets the remainder from the one who paid for it.

On the chase in the Dye Yards. The child wins and the chase is not a joke. Riarhin prices it at twenty yards and gets a hundred and fifty, flat out, in boots, with the shouting stopping after four steps because it costs something. Aryka does not slow for corners; she runs through them wide, taking the loss in distance and refusing to take it in speed, which is what a master-at-arms teaches in a courtyard with the door shut. The child wins on ground and not on legs: corners, then a dozen porters who step aside for a bobbin-runner and close in front of a shouting Vhasian, then a gap under a drying rack no grown woman fits through. She has to abandon her first wall, and she looks back twice, and a runner who has been doing this since she could walk does not look back — that is the tell, and it is the only thing Riarhin takes out of the afternoon. Riarhin, honestly, would not have put money on it at the porters' crossing. Aryka then walks the last thirty yards and spends the time on her hair, her breathing and the decision about what her face will be doing when it comes round the corner, and it is doing it by the time it arrives. Riarhin says Mm. and enjoys the whole four minutes and revises nothing out loud. The chase is evidence for Act III, filed under the wrong heading like everything else about this woman: in her early forties, with more than twenty years of fighting behind her, and in the kind of condition that is going to be relevant in a burning building.

On the gear, which must match The Last Blade. Toothpick lives in her boot, always. Remorse — narrow, straight, double-edged, a jian-like blade in a compact block guard, in a metal that neither brightens nor chips — rides on her hip whenever there is any prospect of needing it, and goes wrapped with the bow in a bundle behind the saddle, or slung across her back as a bundle, only when she is travelling and does not expect to draw. Where a bundle cannot follow, it stays behind: in the thorn above Sedris while she rides up to the tower underneath a cart. There is no back scabbard anywhere in these books. Nobody draws a sword of that length off their own back, and the prose never pretends otherwise: on the back it is cargo, on the hip it is a sword.

Silence. The bow is named and it belongs in this book. Short, strongly recurved, the limbs turning back past the grip, silvery wood in joined layers held with a Lethai composite no Acasi fletcher can name, carving that follows the grain rather than covering it, and a pull that a veteran of more than twenty years gets a third of the way and hands back. Its operation makes no sound; the arrow's arrival makes plenty. Aryka handles it at the ford and asks what it is called, which is the introduction, and it is then used exactly as it should be — across her knees on the tavern stair while Na'ar works, and out of the loft door at Vethric's, where the farmhouse fifty yards off hears a man and never hears a string. That distinction is the whole of what the bow is for, and it is the answer to the three surviving Rothari boys in the prologue who were insistent that there had been no noise.

On Serafine and No Bruises. She is the same woman who wrote that autobiography, and Act II should be read against it before it is written. The Silk Coin, the fire, Kes, the veil and the badly drawn mouth are all hers, and the rhyme in Act III depends on the reader's having them. Do not let her lose. She is beaten by arson and by the limits of holding a building, not by being outwitted.
