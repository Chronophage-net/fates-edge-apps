The Weight You Carry

A working treatment of the third Riarhin book

What this is. A complete working plan for the novel following The Thread. The five-act structure is the governing outline: sea adventure, espionage caper, diplomatic travel serial, expedition preparation, archaeological adventure and horror. Kalrantha supplies the continuing purpose; each act supplies an adventure worth reading on its own.

Viewpoint. Close third-person limited, Riarhin throughout. No prologue explaining the ancient catastrophe, no scene inside an intelligence office she cannot enter, no last-page cutaway to someone feeling the amulet wake. Other people's competence must be visible in what they do and in the consequences she encounters. Their private motives remain deductions until they tell her.

Scale. A substantial novel, provisionally thirty chapters and roughly 100,000–115,000 words. The chapter divisions below are drafting units, not an obligation to give every adventure the same space. Act V takes about a third of the book. Travel occupies weeks and then months; neither a fixed sailing calendar nor an exact year should be imposed until the route has been checked against the setting maps.

New names are working names. Ambassador Sada Alemu, the antiquarian Meret, the expedition master Kebede, and Captain Varek are proposed for this treatment. Their functions and relationships are developed here; their names do not retroactively identify unnamed figures elsewhere in the setting.

What the book is about

Riarhin has spent a long time arranging matters so that she can leave. Finding Kalrantha is the first undertaking in years whose completion she wants more than the freedom to abandon it. She starts by exchanging one service for passage and ends responsible for an expedition whose members have different reasons to come home.

Her usual advantage survives the journey. She can read conduct, manufacture a plausible explanation, enter a guarded room, and remember what she chose to look at. The journey puts her among people who know things she cannot extract from conduct: which animal will survive the ascent, what a provincial seal can actually authorize, how a particular ward behaves when its makers have been dead for centuries. She becomes more effective by giving those people authority. She dislikes how exposed this makes her feel and does it anyway.

The title is allowed to acquire meanings without being explained. There is the captain's outstanding service, the ambassador's office, a pack that has to be light enough to lift, water that must come back unused, and the small object Riarhin finally puts into her pocket. There is also Na'ar, who has been carrying his own belongings beside hers without treating that as a claim on her. No character sums these up.

The book ends with the expedition's immediate purpose fulfilled. They have found the object and got everyone out. Its nature remains unresolved. The reader has enough evidence to fear the apparent success, and the characters have enough evidence to be cautious without having the author's explanation available to them.

What The Thread hands forward

The opening follows the sack of Silkstrand. Riarhin and Na'ar have walked uphill; Aryka's column has not yet departed on the page. There is no unwritten voyage to bridge and no Red Wastes scene to repeat.

Riarhin carries nine leaves from Serafine's fifth press-file. The useful chain is commercial: a chandler in Marcott, a saddler in Oshiira, cargo descriptions deliberately removed, payments for water and fodder, guards hired and discharged before the final stage. The papers establish a journey into the Wastes, including an eleven-day unwatered leg. They do not supply a current route, a reliable total travel time, or a promise that the same water source still exists.

She has Kargath's repeated description: green glass in a brass frame, out of the Himadri, supposedly a wish with a clause attached. She knows that several generations deliberately reduced the record. She does not know whether Kalrantha is useful, dangerous, valuable, or even accurately described. Finding it is an objective she can pursue without naming why she needs an objective.

Na'ar comes because he chooses to. He is Ykrul, past forty-five, worldly, physically formidable, an excellent cook, and amused by being taken for hired muscle. He has never been on salt water. He is not an innocent abroad who needs everything explained, and discovering a nautical limitation must not turn him into comic ballast.

Riarhin's recall preserves attended details, not information she never noticed or languages she never learned. Her age remains approximate because she stopped counting. Braedyn is dead and she knows it; the old letter is something she retains to avoid considering what followed. No new uncertainty about his survival is introduced.

Remorse remains the overt sword. Toothpick does the close, quiet work. Silence makes no sound when loosed; an arrow striking someone is another matter. Her cloak is always the local colour in everyone else's memory. None of these becomes a universal solution to unfamiliar terrain. Her slow regrowth is not combat healing.

The slack thread at her throat may receive one brief touch during the voyage. It neither points toward Kalrantha nor activates the final chamber. Aryka, Lena, Serafine, Ghere, and the unresolved thread remain lives and difficulties beyond this expedition; they are not imported to supply a larger supporting cast.

The route and the evidence

Silkstrand → an Amaranthine shore sanctuary → Mbaro in Oshiira → the southern diplomatic road → Adur-Gah in Taharka → the highland staging route → a northward approach into the Red Wastes → the storm city. The road episode locations are working local sites, not new countries. The final approach goes north from Taharka; it is not an unexplained crossing of Ashaan disguised by a directional phrase.

Ashaan is avoided. Its intelligence reaches foreign ports through people, correspondence, and commercial contacts. It does not acquire jurisdiction over Mbaro or Taharka merely because an Ashaani agent appears there. The Black Hand handles human observation and interference; Esoiti supply magical expertise when required. The Veiled Sister, Masked Sister, and Helmed Sister remain the three Maeri. No plural human order of “Veiled Sisters” appears.

Oshiira has its own interests, earned power, and internal arguments. Its Widow is selected from the canal-mothers and has no hereditary heirs. The political question here is who can authorize supply, inspection, and passage in her name. No lost-heir plot is borrowed from older generator text.

Taharka has remained independent through its passes and institutions. Sada's knowledge comes from serving those institutions, not from being the person who explains an entire continent. The Iron Coast is Taharkan; the Bronze Coast belongs to Dhahara; the Brass Coast remains in the Amaranthine. None is a casual synonym for the shore on this route.

The Himadri problem is a chain of custody, not a change of geography. Kalrantha comes from Dhahara. The Himadri temple held it under negation powered by a piece of Varesh, because it could not be unmade; the temple and its charge were erased from history. The Forgotten Prince raided it, losing three hundred men, and carried the amulet to his kingdom in Ashaan, in what became the Red Wastes. His empire was consumed by uncontrolled Fate colliding in perpetual backlash. Pereshi fire magi stole his heart and erased him from history. These are authorial facts reserved for a future work, not discoveries the expedition makes here. Varesh is the Unmaker catalogued as Varash in existing patron references.

The much later transport recorded in Serafine's file is a custodial journey into the ruined country. Its papers do not date the founding or destruction of that country. An intervening circuit through dealers remains a working reconstruction. Do not invent a second Kalrantha or move the Unmaker out of the Himadri. The storm city remains unnamed on the page.

The company

Riarhin

She is the investigator and, by accumulated decisions, the expedition leader. Her greatest tactical skill is improvisation founded on accurate observation. Her preferred self-description still involves having anticipated everything. The book repeatedly lets her prepare well and then encounter a problem preparation could only partly cover.

Her change is practical rather than confessional. In Mbaro she tells people enough to make them useful. In Taharka she tells the people who will cross the Wastes with her enough to let them refuse. In the city she accepts an abort condition she cannot overrule merely because the object is close. She never announces that she has learned trust.

Na'ar

He carries no newly assigned class or mystical desert qualification. He reads men accustomed to violence, understands the handling of tired animals, notices who actually eats at a camp, and can make an unpleasant evening habitable. He is unusually difficult to hurry into somebody else's fear.

His fighting stays economical and pleasurable to him. There should be one excellent fight in the road act where the reader can enjoy how good he is, and another in which he decides the fight is a waste of their remaining daylight. Neither decision needs a lesson from Riarhin.

He is not jealous of Sada. His important disagreement with Riarhin concerns the distinction between going somewhere with her and accepting a task she has silently assigned him. When he objects, he does so plainly and expects an answer. The objection works because he could leave and usually does not.

Sada Alemu — the ambassador

A Taharkan envoy serving in Mbaro, due home to report on grain access, pack transport, and the handling of border traffic. She has negotiated a useful agreement whose implementation will make someone unpopular. Her journey has a purpose before she meets Riarhin and would occur without her.

Sada is good at distinguishing a legal permission from an arrangement people will carry out. She notices connected activity, remembers who can actually promise what, and can accept a partial victory without pretending it is complete. Her blind spot is confidence that a clearly assigned office will settle a dispute about authority. Riarhin is alert to everything left outside an office.

Their attraction begins with recognition of skill and grows through ordinary proximity: a shared meal after a difficult meeting, an argument continued over saddles, the discovery that neither is obliged to fill a silence. Sada gets to be funny, tired, professionally annoyed, and wrong. She has a household and colleagues in Adur-Gah; Riarhin does not become her first interesting experience.

She joins the expedition under a limited Crown Council commission. A route through a lost borderland and the possible movement of a dangerous relic concern Taharka. She represents that interest openly. She will report findings that affect the passes even if Riarhin would rather keep them private. This is declared before departure and remains a real source of disagreement without becoming a betrayal.

Meret — the antiquarian

A Taharkan scholar of old sanctuaries and waterworks, and a Runekeeper of the Sealed Gate. She has spent more time documenting buildings than entering them under pursuit. Her familiar is a small stone-grey lizard that chooses thresholds as sleeping places; its ordinary care is part of her work. Her Codex records forms of admission, exclusion, and permitted passage in objects she can handle and compare.

Her scholarship and her rites are different skills. She can read a building's alterations, recognize several ritual traditions, and translate a relevant administrative script. None of that lets her read every inscription. A damaged text requires comparison and time. Her patron gives her a precise understanding of boundaries, not a supernatural inventory of everything in a room.

Meret has a reason to come: an incomplete survey has been used for years to support claims about the northern passes. A properly recorded expedition can correct it. She wants evidence that survives her return. Her vulnerability is attachment to a sound classification after new evidence has begun to stretch it. Her strength is that she will eventually amend it in public.

She never says “nothing magical” when she means “nothing my tests identified.” In the last chamber this distinction is essential. She does not become Saikou with another name.

Kebede — the expedition master

A Taharkan route surveyor and transport contractor who has brought people home from failed journeys and been blamed for the failure. He knows animals, water loss, unstable ground, the real capacities of carriers, and the difference between a route surveyed once and a route maintained.

He is hired for judgment, not obedience. His agreement gives him authority to halt the march when water or animal condition falls below specified limits. He insists on a staffed return station, recovery arrangements, and an allowance for someone coming back unable to walk. He rejects Riarhin's first equipment list without admiration for how dangerous she looks.

His own stake is professional: the survey must not become a published invitation for poorly prepared imitators. He wants the conditions attached to the route recorded as carefully as the line itself. Sada supports this; Riarhin initially hears a restriction on what she is buying.

The five-person core is not the whole labour force. Drivers, carriers, guards, translators, hosts, and relief crews have names, wages, and reasons to refuse a job. Most stay with the delegation or at the expedition's stations. Five people enter the inner city because the final approach requires a light party, not because five adventurers can carry a country's worth of supplies.

Varek — the captain who belongs to the first act

A Vilikari smuggler and Captain of Raéyn. He can handle a bad sea, judge a pursuit, and make a crew act together under conditions in which speech is almost useless. On a guarded shore he is a recognizable, valuable target with the wrong kind of expertise.

Raéyn has called in a service: he must personally destroy a particular idol of Khemesh. He has delayed departure until he can do it. Riarhin needs a passage he can provide. Their bargain is direct, with the dangerous parts stated. He completes it, carries her to Mbaro, and leaves to continue his own life. He is not waiting off the desert for the final rescue.

Act I — The Sea: Get Out

Immediate objective: secure passage out of Silkstrand and reach Mbaro. About four chapters; roughly a tenth of the novel.

1. A berth that is not for sale

The port is working after the sack, but badly. Quays change hands; honest berths fill with people who booked before the fires; departure papers issued yesterday answer to nobody today. Riarhin can steal money and documents. She cannot steal the wind or make an overloaded vessel seaworthy.

Varek is leaving on the useful southern run as soon as he has discharged his service. He has a berth for two and no need of their silver. Riarhin inspects his ship before accepting. He inspects the nine-leaf packet only as far as she permits and correctly concludes she is carrying work away from Silkstrand. He does not guess Kalrantha.

He names the shore sanctuary and the thing he must do there. His own people have already attempted reconnaissance; two useful approaches are watched. Riarhin accepts after establishing what “personally destroy” requires. Taking the idol away for him to break later will not satisfy the service. The captain must reach it.

Na'ar hears the terms and agrees for himself. The opening closes on departure for the sanctuary, with the actual passage still to be earned.

2. The dry entrance

The sanctuary is built into a working shore installation: a landing, a cistern, storage, and chambers cut deeper into the rock. The cult does not need to be a friendly village in disguise. It has labourers who require food and fresh water, and the cult keeps account of who enters because it expects attack.

Its custodians know what a Captain of Raéyn can do. The sea entrance is deliberately prepared for him; the idol stands away from open weather; changes in tide and pressure are monitored. A disguised captain simply walking through the service door would be caught.

Riarhin spends the available interval identifying the inspection procedure for cistern repairs. She does not invent a forgotten tunnel. She creates a fault that the sanctuary must address, supplies the contractor an apparently valid reason to bring an additional pair of hands, and arranges for Varek to enter as equipment rather than personnel. The load is checked. She has prepared for the check, not relied on its omission. Na'ar handles the hired transport and learns which overseer changes the work crew when alarm is raised.

The plan gets them through one boundary. It does not get them to the idol. At the inner inspection Riarhin must abandon part of the cover because a custodian notices a discrepancy she could not have known about. The woman is right. Riarhin buys minutes by letting her believe she has discovered a theft.

3. The room beneath the water

The interior horror is pressure and enclosure. Sounds arrive from behind stone. A wet footprint deepens while nobody stands in it. Workers speak with the calm of people who have accommodated something intolerable. Khemesh is felt through the place; he does not appear to negotiate.

The custodian concludes that the apparent theft is cover for a maritime attack and closes the prepared exits. She sends people to Varek's ship rather than putting every defender in the idol room. That sensible division gives Na'ar a problem his experience fits and costs the crew a sail and a wounded hand.

Riarhin reaches the idol first and could damage it. She has to make a way for the captain instead. This is the act's central physical problem: the best available thief is not the person who must finish the job. She holds an inspection gate open from the wrong side while Varek crosses. He breaks the idol himself, with an act specific to his service rather than a general ability to destroy any sacred object.

The breach releases water and makes the lower route unusable. It does not destroy Khemesh or erase his cult. The survivors can still make sound decisions. Riarhin leaves a usable way out for workers who follow, partly because a crowd at the wrong door would trap her and partly because she has no quarrel with them. Neither motive needs sorting aloud.

4. A captain at sea

The shore escape is ugly, timed, and expensive. The custodian tries to seize the launch; Na'ar prevents it without becoming inexplicably adept at boats. Riarhin comes aboard injured, with something important ruined by salt water. Varek gets them away because this is now his work.

Give him the sea sequence: sail reduced before the others understand why, a turn apparently toward danger that strips the pursuer of room, shouted instructions that are few because the crew has practised them. Raéyn's favour is weather, immense and not obliging. It creates an opportunity a competent captain can use, not a calm corridor reserved for the heroine.

Then passage. Na'ar is briefly seasick and remains formidable. The injured sailor needs care, wet papers need drying, and Varek's crew continues to have a ship to run. Riarhin and the captain settle the terms; he owes the agreed destination and delivers it. The act ends at Mbaro with Kalrantha still only a packet of papers and Riarhin already looking at people who know how the wharf works.

What carries forward: Riarhin has paid for movement through another person's expertise. The reader has seen a genuine patron obligation completed and a genuine supernatural response. This establishes the contrast needed when Kalrantha later produces neither.

Act II — Oshiira: Don't Be Noticed

Immediate objective: turn the old transport record into a viable expedition without letting a single observer reconstruct its purpose. Six chapters; about a sixth of the novel.

5. The purchases

Riarhin begins well. She employs an interpreter for defined work, uses a money house with a current local reference, and checks the saddler's successor through trade records rather than announcing an eighty-year-old mystery at the counter. The first setback is ordinary: the useful book was inherited by a married daughter, not by the business carrying the old name.

Her inquiries require visible purchases and appointments. Lamp oil, abrasion-resistant cloth, old route surveys, pack fittings, copies of water rights. Each has a legitimate explanation. Together they imply travel outside the provisioned roads. She knows this. The game becomes controlling the explanation people assemble.

6. Four sensible suspicions

The Vermilion Corps suspects an undeclared survey affecting stores or caravan water. A merchant house thinks she is preparing a competing route. A criminal broker thinks she is moving valuables out of a ruined northern city. An Ashaani commercial contact hears that somebody is tracing an old inland consignment and sends a limited report. None knows the contents of her file.

Riarhin feeds these explanations through separate, ordinary channels. She lets a price be quoted too loudly, pursues one unprofitable purchase long enough to be remembered, and asks a question whose answer matters less than who repeats it. The deceptions must cost time or money. She cannot issue four contradictory identities at one desk and expect the desk not to compare them.

Na'ar notices the interpreter being approached before Riarhin does. The interpreter tells them what was offered and raises her fee for further work. She has correctly priced a change in risk. Riarhin pays it.

7. Six weeks of lamp oil

Sada catches the connection between three activities because they intersect the work she is already doing. Transport allotments and the supply negotiations on her desk make Riarhin's purchases legible. She knows an expedition is being prepared; she does not know its destination or cargo.

Their first exchange can use the lamp-oil joke. It must end in a useful question from Sada, not in admiration of how mysterious Riarhin is. Riarhin answers part of it honestly. Sada returns a piece of information she can afford to give: the supposed easy departure through Ashaani approaches has acquired a new inspection, and the man selling Riarhin that route has not travelled it this season.

Riarhin verifies this. Sada notices her doing so and likes her better for it.

8. The daughter who kept the book

The saddler's daughter has a transport receipt because her father was never paid the disputed final instalment. She will allow an inspection in return for settling a specific family claim, not for a coin theatrically laid on a table. Riarhin establishes the claim independently and pays through the channel the daughter chooses.

The receipt distinguishes packed weight from carried water. It confirms that the last stage required a light party and that the return allowance was unusually generous. Someone expected to come back slowly. A damaged place-name corresponds to an obsolete district rather than to the shrine proposed by the latest relic dealer. The file is improving; it is not turning into a map by magic.

The meeting also gives a watcher enough to rule out ordinary commercial surveying. He revises his theory. Riarhin detects that revision from a changed offer, not because the narration visits his superior.

9. Something larger and legitimate

Sada is returning to Adur-Gah with her report and a disputed supply agreement. Her party already needs an independent assessor of northern contracting practices and travel security. Riarhin has relevant experience and can do that work. She enters under an identity she can sustain; the cover is an actual job, not a title no official is allowed to inspect.

Sada proposes the longer route through Taharka and the highlands. It changes whose authorities must be satisfied and reduces the Ashaani exposure. It does not make the expedition invisible. She will sponsor travel with her delegation; she will not pledge the Crown to an undisclosed desert venture.

Riarhin tells her about the object and the damaged consignment record, including the fact that she cannot substantiate the popular explanation. Sada does not need every private reason. She does need to know what people may pursue her delegation for. The disclosure is the first voluntary risk in their relationship.

10. A correct inspection

The Corps places a hold on Riarhin's desert stores because the stated purpose no longer fits the quantities and route. The hold is justified under the rules she has been exploiting. Meanwhile, an intermediary offers to make it disappear in exchange for a copy of her papers. The offer tells her that staying will narrow her choices.

The climax combines Sada's work and Riarhin's. Sada secures the lawful departure of her delegation and its actual supplies. Riarhin abandons stores she cannot honestly bring within its needs, arranges for a copy of an innocuous survey to be stolen, and identifies the person selling information across the two inquiries. She need not arrest or kill him. Making his trade visible to his competing customers is sufficient to occupy him during departure.

The Corps records who left and why. A merchant has lost a speculation; a criminal broker has bought the wrong information; the Ashaani report now points to a Taharkan diplomatic party but still lacks the artifact's name. Some observers have been misled and others have simply been satisfied.

The act ends with Riarhin leaving in a company she did not organize, under a timetable she cannot casually alter, beside a woman she would have chosen to keep talking to even without a useful road.

Act III — The Road: The Diplomatic Voyage

Immediate objective: get Sada and her mission to Taharka with an agreement that can still be implemented. Six substantial episodes; roughly a fifth of the novel.

There is no hidden Kalrantha agent behind every obstruction. These places have business before the delegation arrives and continue after it leaves. The act may be the most pleasurable stretch of the book. Warmth is allowed to be real.

11. The bridge with two owners

Flood damage has closed a bridge. The people authorized to collect its tolls do not own the adjoining ground, and the proposed temporary crossing would move the problem onto households excluded from the agreement. Armed men on both banks are protecting intelligible claims.

Riarhin identifies a way to get Sada across in darkness. Sada refuses because crossing her party solves nothing for the grain traffic named in her report. She negotiates labour, compensation, and a limited reopening witnessed by both sides. Riarhin helps obtain the missing copy of a boundary award whose possessor benefits from delay. The award supports neither side entirely.

The bridge opens late and at restricted capacity. The settlement is useful. Sada has won the chapter; Riarhin has made a contribution. That evening they disagree about the days it took and keep sitting together after the disagreement ends.

12. The missing envoy

A junior member of the delegation has disappeared with a dispatch case. The obvious fear is defection. Riarhin tracks the departure competently and finds the envoy in a household refusing entry to official visitors: he has gone to collect his sister, whose employer is trying to retain her under a disputed contract. He brought the case because he feared his colleagues would leave without him and because it contains the authority he thought he could borrow.

There is a crime to investigate and a personal decision that remains understandable. Sada recovers the dispatches, disciplines the envoy, and arranges a hearing for the sister with representation she chooses. The party does not cure local labour law in an afternoon. The woman leaves under a provisional agreement that will require further work.

Riarhin had expected Sada to protect the mission by quietly buying a silence. Sada instead records the breach and its resolution. The report will cause her trouble at home. Riarhin begins to understand that Sada is willing to be answerable in ways she herself has spent years avoiding.

13. Supper before the ford

Na'ar becomes the centre of an episode without becoming a different person. Men at a crowded staging camp take him for a guard between contracts and offer him money to stand aside during a theft. He lets them finish. Their plan is competent against the escort they believe they have counted.

He tells Riarhin and Sada. They move vulnerable travellers out of the intended ground, leave a plausible target, and prepare a reception. When the thieves commit, Na'ar gets his fight: room to move, good footing, opponents who realize too late that he has been measuring theirs. Riarhin prevents the second team from reaching the baggage. One thief escapes because stopping him would leave other people exposed.

Afterwards Na'ar cooks with someone whose cart was nearly taken. The scene is allowed to end at supper. No stolen map appears in the robber's boot. Riarhin and Sada hear different parts of his account and discover he has told neither of them a lie.

14. The house with seven doors

A storm holds the delegation at a relay house with more travellers than rooms. The host keeps seven doors in use and a passage closed. Riarhin initially treats that as concealment of another route; closer attention shows water staining and work abandoned for the season. The shut passage is a structural hazard. The household's other customs may remain unexplained. Not every refusal conceals a clue.

The adventure is a night evacuation when part of the bank gives way. Riarhin can reach stranded guests; Sada can make competing escorts release transport; Na'ar moves what needs moving. The host knows where the building will fail and is listened to. They save most of the stores by accepting the loss of the flooded rooms early.

Later, with wet clothing hung everywhere and nothing private except a short stretch of corridor, Sada asks whether Riarhin ever stays after a job is done. Riarhin says something evasive. Sada permits the evasion without mistaking it for an answer. Their first kiss happens here, after the immediate work, because they both want it. It does not settle the question.

15. The inscription in the wrong place

The one road episode that advances Kalrantha begins in ordinary diplomatic business at a sanctuary holding old copies of transit agreements. A reused stone carries part of the obsolete district name from the saddler's receipt. The keeper has no object for sale and no secret passage into the Wastes. She has a record of where the stone was brought from, made because somebody disputed payment for its transport.

Riarhin pays for a copy and the time of someone qualified to read it. She recognizes a repeated mark from the file; its meaning is supplied by another person's work. The material establishes that a route dismissed as a local track once carried an imperial water authority. The thing beyond the last watered stage was reached through inhabited, administered country.

They do not detour into the Wastes. Sada still has a destination and Riarhin still lacks an expedition. For once, a new lead makes her prepare better rather than leave faster.

16. The last ascent

A pass closes ahead of the delegation. Going around would mean missing the hearing at which Sada's agreement must be defended. Forcing passage would damage precisely the authority on which that agreement depends.

Sada secures a small permitted crossing with the papers and essential witnesses; the bulk train stays below until the weather changes. Riarhin scouts the agreed route rather than a forbidden shortcut. A fall injures a carrier, and the limited party must relinquish baggage to bring him across. The documents survive because people agreed in advance which copies could be lost, not because paper is more important than the carrier.

Riarhin is ready to continue into the city before the injured man is safely transferred. Na'ar tells her he is staying until that is done. She starts to give him a task and hears herself. She waits too. There is no argument about loyalty.

At Adur-Gah, Sada is met by colleagues who need her report and have little interest in Riarhin. She goes with them. Their separation at the gate is ordinary, mutually understood, and unexpectedly difficult. The diplomatic voyage is finished. Riarhin has reached the place where her own undertaking can begin.

Act IV — Taharka: Assemble the Expedition

Immediate objective: obtain informed people, lawful access, and enough capacity to return. Four chapters; about a sixth of the novel.

17. Her own rooms

Sada belongs here. She has arguments already in progress, a household accustomed to her absences, and a reputation that Riarhin cannot improve by entering it dramatically. Let Riarhin attend one meal where the work is not hers and the people are kind without being fascinated.

The Crown Council hearing puts the road agreements under scrutiny. Sada answers for concessions she made. Her superiors approve some and require changes to others. Riarhin's assessment is useful testimony, not the speech that rescues Taharkan politics from itself.

Separately, the Wastes inquiry reaches the authorities responsible for the passes. They have a legitimate question: what will happen if an unknown northern company opens a usable route or brings something dangerous back through their communities? Sada recommends a limited survey commission. Its terms include shared route records, truthful declarations of recovered objects, and a review before any relic is sold or exported.

Riarhin can decline and seek another route. She accepts because these terms buy real support and are preferable to the alternatives. Sada is appointed the Council's representative. The appointment follows her expertise and access to the negotiations, not a sudden willingness to abandon her office for a lover.

18. Two people who say no

Meret examines the papers and rejects the confident demon identification. She does not replace it with an equally confident new answer. A green stone described by dealers and a removed cargo line cannot establish a patron relationship. She agrees to come if she can document inscriptions before anything is dismantled and refuse an invocation she believes unsafe.

Kebede examines Riarhin's proposed loads and refuses to quote a price. The historical eleven days are outward travel under unknown conditions. They need the return, delay, animal losses, and an injured passenger. He removes equipment that duplicates a function, changes the animals for the ascent, adds repair materials and spare containers, and specifies a staffed station before the unwatered leg.

Recruitment is a negotiation among capable adults. The expedition hires support workers openly. Water is measured with the actual containers. The skins are tested. A possible route is walked for a day and revised. The planning scenes need a few tactile details, not a shopping list posing as drama.

19. A visitor with another answer

Saikou Ira appears briefly while following the Forgotten Prince and Lost Kingdom through a relic-market lead. Use his mentality in The Witnessed Prey: distrust his own certainty, distinguish witnessed behaviour from a dealer's label, investigate origins, and attend to people endangered by the objects. He can demonstrate that descriptions were copied; he cannot prove the objects had no connection. An old temple plan retains inward-facing wards and an empty socket. A damaged muster retains provisions and reduced returns but lacks the ruler's name. These attract him toward the erased history, not toward claiming or explaining Kalrantha. He remains wary and explicitly declines to call Riarhin's unseen object harmless.

Riarhin can now place a later movement beyond the part of the trail he has followed. He checks her evidence rather than conceding to confidence. His annoyance is professional and dry. He leaves with a copy of one permitted excerpt; she receives a comparison that helps Meret date a custodial seal. Neither has traded away the whole inquiry.

Saikou has work in a different direction. He does not accompany the expedition, send a prophetic warning naming its final danger, or promise to arrive if summoned. His brief presence enlarges the investigation without making Riarhin's company a lesser version of his.

20. Permission to turn back

A commercial concern offers to underwrite the expedition in exchange for first claim on whatever is recovered. An Ashaani-linked intermediary wants one hired specialist placed in the party. A Taharkan office wants unrestricted publication of a route that Kebede considers unsafe for ordinary traffic. These are separate pressures with separate information. Only people to whom Riarhin has disclosed the name know she seeks Kalrantha.

The immediate intrigue concerns a compromised copy of the load schedule. Riarhin discovers it through a change in a supplier's demand, follows the transaction, and exposes the attempt to arrange a forced dependence on a particular depot. The Council has reason to act because the attempt also undermines its commission. Its response is local and effective; no all-powerful spy chief is required.

The expedition leaves after revising its arrangements. Each core member receives the same account of what is known, what is rumour, who has tried to interfere, and what would require turning back. Riarhin withholds the private history that made her want the object. She does not withhold a hazard because somebody might sensibly refuse it.

Before departure, she and Sada have the harder conversation. Sada's report will be complete about public dangers. Riarhin's company is not a promise to settle in Adur-Gah. Neither converts affection into an exemption. They choose to continue together with those facts intact.

The act closes on the first camp beyond the last ordinary traffic. Kebede checks the containers after everyone else has finished checking them. Riarhin notices and lets him.

Act V — The Wastes: Find It

Immediate objective: reach the destination, identify the object as far as possible, and bring the expedition out. Ten chapters; roughly a third of the novel.

The first stages retain the serial's episodic movement. Then encounters with other people become rarer. Intelligence reports stop arriving. There is no pursuit party crossing an unprovisioned desert at the speed of a rumour. The company gradually finds that the landscape contains more human work than the living world remembers.

21. The last watered stage

The last station is inhabited and maintained. Its people are neither mystical guardians nor extras waiting to die for the expedition. They know local wells and recent weather; they do not know the location of an object last described generations ago.

Kebede establishes the return arrangements and checks the farthest proposed cache. The heavy train remains here with a relief crew and the animals that should not attempt the next ground. Named workers know when to begin a search, how far they can safely go, and when they must stop. Riarhin contributes a description of the party and its intended signs. For someone who ordinarily leaves as little trace as possible, arranging to be found is an effort she does not discuss.

An old measurement on a well agrees with the obsolete district record. That is the first confirmation earned by the journey itself. It justifies the next stage, not the whole venture.

22. The road under the dune

After scattered ruins, they find paving below wind-scoured sand. Then a second stretch with drainage on both sides. A mile marker counts toward a place absent from current maps. Kebede distinguishes a road from a processional approach because its working margins were repeatedly repaired.

Na'ar finds a practical use before anyone settles the interpretation: the buried edge offers firmer going for an animal beginning to founder. His experience is with footing and exhaustion. He does not discover an ancestral intuition for this desert.

The route gains a direction and loses the comfort of being an isolated ruin. Too much labour was spent here for one shrine. They follow the road until a sand-filled cutting makes following it the worse choice.

23. Water that has already been spent

They reach a cistern whose masonry appears sound. Kebede refuses to put its remaining water into their stores before testing it. Meret finds maintenance marks showing that the chamber was adapted after its original construction. Two competent readings conflict: a protected emergency reserve, or a settling tank whose outlet has vanished.

They open a small inspection point rather than the main gate. The sediment and mineral crust establish that it is a settling tank. The decision costs a day and preserves their water containers from contamination. The spare repair material Kebede insisted on now restores a damaged closure, allowing them to leave without wasting their own supply to clean it.

The episode is tense because the water calculation is tightening, not because someone has forgotten that desert water can be bad. Riarhin accepts the lost day. Meret records her first interpretation and its correction.

24. A country in the ground

Irrigation divisions appear beyond the cistern. Boundaries cut across the supposed empty space between ruins. There are local repairs, secondary roads, a school room built against a former storehouse, a shrine converted into an administrative office. These places changed use while people lived in them.

Sada identifies the political scale from repeated jurisdictions and the allocation of water between them. An inscription previously read as tribute to a single city names obligations among districts. They have been walking through a country.

The revelation should come across several discoveries and then one restrained conversation. Riarhin stops calling the structures outposts. That is enough. Give the lost inhabitants particular lives: a stair narrowed by a later wall, a child's exercise corrected twice, a threshold worn where the easier entrance was kept shut. Their destruction matters before it becomes evidence about the amulet.

25. Too many successful answers

Records survive unevenly: copies baked hard in a store fire, stone orders, a few sheltered inscriptions. No intact journal conveniently narrates the end. Meret can translate some material and must leave other parts unresolved. Riarhin preserves what she attends to, including shapes she cannot read; this helps comparison, not comprehension.

A sequence begins to form. Emergency orders are followed by strikingly prompt reports of relief. A route is secured and traffic concentrates there until other routes fail. A water allocation answers one shortage by making neighbouring settlements dependent. People elsewhere move toward the place described as safe. The accounts celebrate successful measures just before the next demand becomes larger.

Sada sees a government centralizing under emergency powers. Kebede sees infrastructure driven beyond redundancy. Meret sees attempts to contain a problem using inherited wards. All are reasonable and partly correct. The reader who remembers the language of wishes and consequences may begin to see another pattern. Nobody says that events are becoming a story, that Fate is drawing a protagonist, or that the world is being watched.

26. The storm line

The storm is visible before the city is. It has a persistent boundary and ordinary weather around it. The company spends time observing it from shelter. Approaching once, retreating, and marking what changed are actions, not delay between set pieces.

Kebede finds a former service cutting that can protect a small party during an entry interval. He cannot guarantee another identical interval. Meret identifies a boundary effect that can be held briefly at one crossing; invoking it will cost her and does not control the storm. Riarhin scouts the first reachable interior before the others commit.

They leave animals and supplies in a defensible staging place outside the worst ground, with the support arrangement appropriate to its distance from the main station. The final five carry a light return reserve. Their retreat condition is stated again. Riarhin agrees to it with the city finally visible through the moving dust.

27. The city that was kept

Inside, the city is built to a human scale and defended at an inhuman expense. Some routes are intact because someone deliberately blocked others. Seals are of different dates. The outer work belongs to the city's life; later repairs belong to people who returned after its end. This makes the last consignment intelligible without requiring the ancient country to have fallen only eighty years ago.

The dungeon begins at a service complex adjoining a civic sanctuary. It has an identifiable purpose and an actual layout: intake court, settling galleries, raised processional route, inspection rooms, restricted inner chambers. There is no sequence of unrelated puzzle doors. Routes join, and a decision in one part changes conditions in another.

Riarhin locates the route used by the later custodians through wear, replaced fittings, and the practical question of how a carried case could pass. The obvious stair is quicker. Kebede finds that its supports have been robbed from below. The service route is less impressive and more credible.

28. What the building still does

Give the central dungeon two linked problems rather than a succession of interchangeable traps.

First, an old hydraulic gate still balances a head of trapped water. Opening it would flood the lower gallery and cut off the return. Kebede understands the mechanism; Riarhin can reach its inspection platform; Na'ar can hold a loaded lever steadily while the repair is made. Strength helps because the others have made a viable task for it, not because he can stop an unlimited flood with his shoulders.

Second, a ward admits a category of person whose political meaning has been mistranslated. Meret recognizes how the exclusion operates but will not falsely claim the authority named in it. Sada identifies the inscription as an office of custodianship with a duty to remove dangerous material under specified conditions, rather than a declaration of hereditary ownership. The wording permits an application; it does not automatically appoint Sada or Riarhin to an ancient throne. Meret constructs the limited request the ward can actually evaluate.

That passage is available, but the process closes another way behind them. They knew it might and have kept a second retreat route. This is where conflicting expertise should be most productive: Riarhin wants a faster access; Meret refuses an invocation that might change the object's confinement; Kebede supplies the physical route that allows both concerns to stand.

One failure still costs them. A structure sound enough under a single inspection cannot support the loaded return. Na'ar is hurt bringing someone clear. He remains conscious and useful, but the injury spends the contingency that has been part of their planning since Taharka. The expedition has time for one final examination, not an indefinite search. Riarhin agrees to leave without the object if that limit arrives.

29. Not all prizes

They reach a chamber prepared by the later custodians inside older architecture. The sarcophagus is not a lavish royal tomb. It is a repurposed container placed where it can be left undisturbed. Removing its cover takes observation, a workable lifting arrangement, and time. It does not demand a blood sacrifice or reward a true heart.

The inscription reads:

NOT ALL PRIZES ARE WORTHY.

NOT ALL TREASURES ARE SOUGHT.

Meret supplies the reading after checking the damaged construction that makes the second line easy to get wrong. The warning is plain. It gives no instructions for a wish and does not name a demon.

Inside: green glass, brass, an object small enough to hold in one hand. Its workmanship and a documented physical detail agree with the independent descriptions. The custodial seal provides another link to the last transport. They can reasonably believe this is Kalrantha without discovering its maker's explanation.

Meret examines it within the limits agreed before entry. No response identifying a patron relationship. No detected spirit, invocation, active ward, or familiar form of curse. She does not exhaust herself provoking it to make the ending spectacular. Her conclusion is exact: she has identified none of those things. That is a report of findings, not a certificate of harmlessness.

The removal is a decision. The sarcophagus was a repository; no surviving ward has been shown to rely on the object remaining inside. Leaving it is possible. Sada says removal places it under the commission's declared custody and requires examination on return. Kebede insists that the examination ends now if they are to meet their water limit. Na'ar wants to get out and can still walk with help.

Riarhin wants what she has come for. She can also give reasons the others accept: they have a supported chain of identification, the means to transport a small isolated object, and an agreed procedure for handling unknown finds. The warning weighs against removal without proving a particular hazard. Their decision is cautious by the available models and may still be wrong. Do not make them disregard a known, tested catastrophe in order to manufacture the sequel.

She picks it up.

Nothing happens.

She wraps it separately and puts it in an inside pocket. She does not wear it, invite it to speak, or test the old wish story on Na'ar's injury.

Nothing happens.

The last useful daylight inside the storm is being spent. They leave the chamber.

30. The return weight

The exit uses the routes they preserved. The difficult lift is difficult because Na'ar is injured; the water is sufficient because they carried a return allowance; the ward does what Meret established it would do. There is no new collapse triggered by lifting the necklace. The city's remaining danger belongs to the same neglected works that endangered them on the way in.

Outside, the support party is where its instructions said it would be. Give those workers the relief of seeing all five return and the authority to start treating an injured man without waiting for a heroine's speech. The company gets back to its established water station. This closes the adventure's survival question; a chapter of triumph in Adur-Gah would begin another ending.

Riarhin gives Sada the custody account she promised, including the inscription and Meret's negative findings. She keeps the object temporarily because they have agreed she will carry it, not because she secretly substitutes a stone. Their argument about what follows has a place to occur after they return. She has not promised to stay forever. She does tell Sada she will come back to Adur-Gah with the others rather than leave at the first branching road.

Let the final human beat be small: Sada makes room beside her; Riarhin takes it without finding a reason to stand again. Na'ar objects to the way someone else has made supper and is well enough to take over part of it. Kebede reduces the next day's distance. Meret begins the report with what she could establish.

The necklace does nothing. No warmth, green light, voice, conveniently arriving stranger, change of wind, or coincidence retrospectively attributed to it. The last image returns to its literal size: Riarhin shifts the packet out from under her when she lies down. It is an ordinary discomfort. The book ends there.

What the author knows, and what the book may show

Kalrantha creates Fate around its wearer. It cannot direct Fate toward a chosen result, grant a wish, or be used as a safe source of tactical luck. The popular account mistakes consequential coincidences for a bargain. The Unmaker is real; a piece of him powered the temple's negation of Kalrantha. This connection does not make the amulet a demon or a wish-granter, and the characters do not discover it here.

Kalrantha consumed the Forgotten Prince's empire through accumulating Fate and perpetual backlash. That is authorial knowledge used to arrange the evidence, not a revelation delivered by a translated final paragraph. The historical pattern is convergence: local relief followed by dependence, concentration, and a final answer that destroys the conditions that made an answer useful. The catastrophe also involved human decisions and infrastructure. The object does not replace all of that with a villain pressing a button.

Finding it requires no proof that it has been summoning Riarhin. The events before possession have causes: a passage bargain, a diplomat's report, visible purchases, local disputes, old roads, damaged buildings. Do not retroactively turn the entire novel into Kalrantha steering its future wearer. Doing so would weaken every earned decision in the five acts.

The wearer's role matters. Riarhin pockets the object; she does not put it on. Nothing establishes whether carrying it unworn has any effect. The absence of an observable response is preserved without inventing a definitive inert-state rule. The ending needs no secret activation cue.

Meret's diagnostics are appropriate and limited. Her patrons and scholarly categories describe many dangerous things accurately. Kalrantha failing those tests does not mean expertise is useless. It means the categories do not encompass everything. She says so. The company's containment and transport precautions are prudent under an incomplete model; prudence cannot guarantee a safe result. Riarhin briefly considers leaving it, then sees a small prize after so much work. Do not give her a theory connecting the amulet to the country's destruction. Preserve the final chapter with Sada unchanged.

Information and pursuit

Keep a simple account while drafting of what each interested party has actually learned.

  • The Vermilion Corps sees purchases, local contacts, supply requests, and a departure with a named delegation. It can infer a concealed inland purpose. It cannot read the nine leaves from those observations.
  • Oshiiran merchants see demand, prices, introductions, and likely competition. Their conclusions differ because their businesses differ.
  • The Ashaani contact starts with an old-consignment inquiry and later learns that it concerns a Taharkan-supported venture. Identifying Kalrantha requires a further, earned disclosure. Reports travel and can arrive too late.
  • The Taharkan authorities receive the object-name and uncertain danger because Riarhin accepts their terms. Their concern is an expedition crossing their frontier and returning through their communities.
  • The rival commercial sponsor knows enough to want first claim. An attempt to control a depot is cheaper and more plausible than an army secretly following the party through the Wastes.
  • Saikou has independent evidence about the Himadri end of the trail. He compares it with a bounded part of Riarhin's evidence. He is not a source of answers she has declined to ask for.
  • The core expedition shares the practical hazards before departure. Expertise and personal motives still differ. Those differences provide conflict without concealed mission-critical facts.

No enemy needs to survive every act. The first sanctuary's custodian remains dangerous locally; the Corps continues its own work; commercial rivals can fail to follow a changed route. By the deepest part of the Wastes, the absence of human pursuit should be felt as exposure rather than relief.

The romance and the other relationship

Sada catches a pattern, not a secret soul. Their initial pleasure is in being answered by someone who has noticed the important part. Let attraction survive an inconvenient correct answer. It should not depend on Sada overlooking something that a competent ambassador would inspect.

The road gives them ordinary time. Meals, correspondence, washing, fatigue, a shared joke repeated once too often. Physical intimacy follows mutual choice and does not purchase confidential information. Neither woman needs to be rescued from a conveniently unsuitable previous lover. Their history can exist without an explanatory monologue.

The meaningful separation occurs in Adur-Gah, where Sada has a life Riarhin cannot translate into an assignment. The meaningful commitment occurs before and after the Wastes, when they name what they owe the undertaking and decline to imply more than they mean. Riarhin returning with her is a specific choice. It is not a cure for a century of leaving.

Na'ar's place does not shrink to make room. His companionship is not an undeclared romantic claim. He is permitted to like Sada, disagree with her, spend time elsewhere, and find Riarhin's attempts to make affection look like logistics funny. His refusal at the ascent is his own boundary. The final injury must not reduce him to the proof of what other people feel; he participates in the return and remains himself at the water station.

Drafting controls

Each act must finish its own task. The captain completes his service and delivers passage. Riarhin secures a workable departure from Mbaro. Sada reaches Taharka with a report and an agreement. The expedition forms under terms its members understand. Kalrantha is found and the company gets out. The reader is not asked to accept five long postponements of one scene.

No borrowed stupidity. Before a plan succeeds, establish what the opposition is protecting, what it can observe, and why its response is sensible. A competent adversary may lack information, resources, or jurisdiction. Those limitations are more useful than an unguarded door.

The supporting characters own their expertise. Varek gets the sea escape. Sada wins a diplomatic problem Riarhin cannot steal her way through. Na'ar decides when a fight is worth having. Kebede's return planning works. Meret distinguishes an unsupported diagnosis from a negative result. Riarhin connects these capacities and does the work only she can do.

The geography changes the kind of problem. Oshiiran institutions need purposes and people who can explain them. Taharkan authority is negotiated through its existing offices and local rights. The Wastes lose current institutions gradually and reveal the remains of older ones. Their strangeness need not use the explanatory voice of a living port.

Do not make all care a trap. The relay host wants the guests alive. The interpreter prices dangerous work honestly. The captain keeps his bargain. Affection is real even when it becomes difficult. Horror is stronger when ordinary decency remains a workable fact.

Do not spend the title in dialogue. Literal loads recur because the work requires them. Names and memories matter because they belong to people. Nobody offers Riarhin an account of everything she carries, and she does not produce one herself.

Keep the ending quiet. No boss guarding the necklace, no idol-swap mechanism, no final curse speech, no resurrection, no spectacular wish, no afterword revealing a second green light in the dark. The first act has already demonstrated what a supernatural set piece looks like. The last one earns its absence.

Forward hook, added after drafting. The next novel concerns Veralyn and Valdais's war, the court, and Elyas's return. Riarhin is still away during its main action.
