Giants, Ogres, and Oni

The Witnessed Prey, a Hunter's Guide — Chapter 8

They are not fae. They are not beasts. They are something older — or something newer — depending on which story you believe. The Aeler say they were here before the mountains remembered their names. The Ykrul say they were born from broken oaths. The Tulkani say nothing at all. They just tie another knot and keep walking.—Lerris Fenwood, from a field journal found in the Archivolt

I have seen a giant’s footprint in the high passes of Ubral. It was warm. It was still steaming. I did not follow it. I am not a fool.

I have heard an ogre’s laugh in the deep places beneath the Aelerian peaks. It sounded like rocks grinding together — and like a name I had not spoken in twenty years. I did not investigate. I am not a hero.

I have met an oni. Once. In a dream that was not a dream, in a place that was not a place. It offered me a bargain. I refused. It smiled. I woke up with a new scar on my palm and a debt I do not remember incurring. I am not sure I won that encounter.

A Note on Taxonomy (or Lack Thereof)

The scholar’s instinct is to classify. To name, to measure, to place in a neat hierarchy of size, habitat, and threat level. This instinct is understandable. It is also dangerously wrong.

The tall folk — giants, ogres, oni — do not fit neatly into categories. They are not a single species with regional variants. They are not a coherent family of related creatures. They are, I have come to believe, a consequence. A pressure. A shape that the world takes when it is asked to bear too much weight.

The Aeler say that giants are what happens when a mountain dreams of walking. The Ykrul say that ogres are what happens when a blood-feud is left unpaid for too many generations. The Lethai-ar of the Crimson Valley say that oni are what happens when a promise is broken so badly that the world itself remembers the scar. I do not know which of these is true. I suspect all of them are — and none of them are.

What I do know is this: the tall folk are not monsters in the way that a wolf is a monster. They are not predators in the way that a wyrm is a predator. They are witnesses — or perhaps reckoners. They appear when something has been forgotten, owed, or left unfinished. They do not attack without reason. But their reasons are not always ours to understand.

Giants — The Walking Mountains

Giants do not build. They are built — by the land, by the weight of ages, by the slow accumulation of stone and silence. They do not speak. They do not need to. The earth speaks for them.—Lerris Fenwood

Description

Giants are rare. They are also unmistakable. A giant is not merely a large humanoid — it is a landform that happens to be mobile. The oldest giants are indistinguishable from the hills they stride across; moss and lichen grow on their shoulders, and birds nest in the crevices of their joints. They move slowly, with the patience of glaciers, and they do not seem to notice the small things that scurry around their feet.

I have seen a giant once. It was in the high passes of Ubral, at dusk. It stood on the far ridge, silhouetted against the setting sun, and it did not move for the entire hour I watched it. I am not certain it was alive. I am not certain it was dead. I am certain that when I finally looked away, it was closer than it had been before.

Where They Are Found

Giants are most commonly reported in:

  • Ubral — The high passes and deep glens. The clans avoid certain valleys entirely, calling them “giant’s country.” They do not explain why.
  • Aeler — The deepest under-vaults. Some Aeler whisper of giants that walk in the dark places, far below the lowest mines. They count their breaths in eights when they speak of them.
  • The Valewood — The oldest groves. The Lethai-al say that some of the standing stones are not stones at all — they are giants who stood still for so long they forgot how to move.
  • The Himadri Foothills — The highest passes. The Karak-Aeler leave offerings of butter tea at cairns to keep the giants from waking.
  • The Wilds — Anywhere the land is old and the roads are few.

What They Want

Giants do not want what mortals want. They do not hunger for food or gold or power. They hunger for stillness. They are drawn to places where the world has been disturbed — where oaths have been broken, where thresholds have been crossed without permission, where the balance has been unsettled.

A giant does not attack. It arrives. It stands. It watches. And its watching is a pressure that builds until something breaks — or until the balance is restored.

Mechanical Note: Giant Encounters

A giant encounter is rarely a combat encounter. Treat it as a scene pressure — a visible timer that ticks while the giant is present. The party can resolve the encounter by:

  • Restoring the balance — identifying what disturbance drew the giant and correcting it.
  • Making an offering — a memory, a story, a true name, a sincere apology.
  • Waiting — the giant will eventually lose interest and wander away. This takes 1d6 scenes and ticks the Wilds Timer +1 per scene.
  • Fleeing — giants are slow. A party that runs will escape. But the giant will remember their faces.

Giant Variants

The Stone Giant (Aeler/Ubral)

These giants are made of living rock, their joints grinding like avalanches. They are most common in mountain passes and under-vaults. They do not speak — they rumble. A Stone Giant’s presence causes the ground to tremble; all physical actions are at -1 die while it is within Far range.

Weakness: A Stone Giant can be pacified by a correct count of eight — eight steps, eight breaths, eight stones placed in a row. Count to nine, and it will become hostile.

The Frost Giant (Himadri/Northern Reaches)

These giants are wrapped in permafrost and hoarfrost. They appear during the deepest winters, when the passes are sealed and the sky is the colour of iron. Their breath freezes whatever it touches. A Frost Giant’s presence causes all actions to be Desperate for the scene.

Weakness: A Frost Giant can be driven back by fire — not a torch, but a true fire, a hearth-fire that has been burning for nine generations. Such fires are rare. The Karak-Aeler keep them in sealed braziers.

The Dream Giant (Valewood/Ways Between)

These giants are not physical — they are shapes in the mist, patterns in the dark, the feeling of being watched. They appear in thin places and dreams. They do not attack. They ask questions. The questions are always about what you owe.

Weakness: A Dream Giant can be banished by answering its question truthfully — or by refusing to answer at all. Silence is a kind of payment. The giant will accept it. It will also remember it.

Ogres — The Hungry Reckoning

Ogres are what happens when a wrong is left unanswered for too long. The wrong does not vanish. It grows teeth. It grows hunger. It grows a shape that can walk and eat and remember — and it remembers who owes it.—Lerris Fenwood

Description

Ogres are shorter than giants — only nine or ten feet tall — but they are broader, heavier, and infinitely more present. They have thick, leathery skin the colour of old stone or dried blood, and their eyes are small and bright and never quite still. They move with a deceptive speed, and they are always, always hungry.

I have never seen an ogre eat. I have seen the aftermath of an ogre’s meal — a village that was there one day and gone the next, with nothing left but a single stone marker and a smell like wet iron. The villagers had not been killed. They had been collected. The ogre had taken what it was owed.

Where They Are Found

Ogres are more common than giants, but they are still rare. They are most often found in:

  • Acasia — The Broken Marches are full of abandoned places. Ogres are drawn to ruins and broken promises.
  • The Black Banners — Ogres sometimes attach themselves to mercenary companies, serving as living siege engines. They are paid in food and silence.
  • Ubral — The deep glens and forgotten valleys. The clans have names for ogres that are not spoken aloud.
  • The Mistlands — Ogres are said to dwell in the Direwood, beyond the bell-lines. The wardens do not go there.
  • Zakov — Some say the ogres of the Salt Serpent’s coast are not ogres at all — they are the drowned, reshaped by the Reaping’s hunger.

What They Want

Ogres want payment. They are reckoning made flesh. They appear when a wrong has run too long unanswered — a broken promise, a stolen life, a forgotten oath. They do not negotiate. They collect.

An ogre will not attack without reason. It will first demand — a name, a story, a memory, a life. If the demand is met, the ogre will leave. If it is refused, the ogre will take what it is owed. The taking is never clean.

Mechanical Note: Ogre Encounters

An ogre encounter is a social combat with high stakes. The ogre has a Demand Timer [4]. Each exchange, the party can:

  • Offer payment — a memory, a story, a name, a significant item. This ticks the Demand Timer back by 1.
  • Refuse payment — ticks the Demand Timer forward by 1. If the timer fills, the ogre attacks.
  • Fight — ogres are Cap 3-4 adversaries with high Harm (4-5) and high Armour (Medium to Heavy). They are not to be taken lightly.

Ogre Variants

The Promise-Eater Ogre (Acasia/Ubral)

These ogres are thin, almost gaunt, with skin stretched tight over prominent bones. They move with a jerky, twitching gait. They are drawn to places where promises have been broken. A Promise-Eater Ogre will not eat you — it will take your memory of the promise you broke. You will forget what you owed. The ogre will remember it for you.

Weakness: A Promise-Eater Ogre can be satisfied by speaking the broken promise aloud — even if you do not remember it. The ogre will listen. It will judge. If you speak truly, it will leave. If you lie, it will take more.

The Flesh-Ogre (Black Banners/Zakov)

These ogres are huge, bloated, and perpetually hungry. They are drawn to battlefields and places of violence. They eat the dead — and sometimes the living. A Flesh-Ogre is less a collector and more a scavenger, taking what is already discarded.

Weakness: A Flesh-Ogre can be driven off by fire or by offering it something more appealing — a fresh kill, a live animal, a captured enemy. It is not picky. It is always hungry.

The Stone-Ogre (Aeler/Ubral)

These ogres are covered in a thick, rocky hide that resembles weathered granite. They are slow but nearly impervious to harm. They are drawn to places where the earth has been wounded — mines, quarries, collapsed tunnels. A Stone-Ogre does not demand payment. It guards the wound, waiting for someone to heal it.

Weakness: A Stone-Ogre can be pacified by repairing the wound — shoring up a collapsed tunnel, replanting a scarred hillside, or performing a ritual of apology to the land. The ogre will watch. It will not help. It will leave when the work is done.

Oni — The Broken Oath Made Flesh

The oni are not demons. They are not spirits. They are what happens when a promise is broken so badly that the world itself remembers the crack. They are the crack given shape — and the shape is always hungry.—Lerris Fenwood

Description

Oni are the most dangerous of the tall folk — not because they are the largest or the strongest, but because they are the cleverest. They are humanoid, with skin in shades of red, blue, or green, and they have horns — one, two, or sometimes more. Their eyes are bright and sharp, and their smiles have too many teeth.

I met an oni once. In a dream that was not a dream. It sat across from me at a table that was not a table, and it offered me a cup of sake that was not sake. It said: “Drink, and I will tell you what you owe.” I did not drink. I woke up with a scar on my palm and a debt I do not remember incurring. The oni is still waiting. It is patient. It has all the time in the world.

Where They Are Found

Oni are most commonly encountered in:

  • Nihori — The volcanic isles are thick with oni. The Oni-Eyed daimyo rules the western ash wastes, and the Crater Oni sleeps beneath the caldera. The Lethai-al of Nihori have learned to bargain with them — and to fear them.
  • Ayokha — The river spirits and the oni share a border of mist and memory. Some say the oni were once river-spirits who broke their own oaths.
  • The Eastern Realms — Sihai, Nihori, and Ayokha all have their own names for the oni. The Lethai-thora of Sihai keep sealed scrolls about them. They do not open them.
  • The Valewood — The fae courts and the oni have an old, uneasy relationship. The Hazel Queen does not speak of them. The Alder King is said to have made a bargain with one.
  • The Ways Between — Oni sometimes appear in dreams and thin places. They are not physical — they are shapes of regret, given form by the dreamer’s own guilt.

What They Want

Oni want bargains. They do not collect what is owed the way ogres do — they are tempters. They offer power, knowledge, escape from a difficult situation — always at a price. The price is never clear until it is too late.

An oni will not attack without reason. It will first offer — a deal, a bargain, a gift. If the offer is accepted, the oni will honour its side of the bargain. But the fine print is always hidden. The oni always gets the better end of the deal.

Mechanical Note: Oni Encounters

An oni encounter is a temptation scene. The oni offers something valuable — information, power, a shortcut, a resurrection — in exchange for something seemingly small: a memory, a name, a lock of hair, a promise. The GM should make the offer tempting. The cost should be unclear until later, when it becomes a complication.

  • If the party accepts: The oni grants the benefit. The GM gains a String against the party — a hidden cost that will manifest at the worst possible moment.
  • If the party refuses: The oni will not attack. It will smile. It will say, “Another time, then.” And it will remember. It is patient.

Oni Variants

The Crater Oni (Nihori)

This oni sleeps beneath the caldera of the western ash wastes. It is vast — the size of a mountain — and it dreams of waking. The Oni-Eyed daimyo has made a bargain with it, and the bargain has given him power — and a slow, creeping corruption.

Weakness: The Crater Oni can be resealed by performing the old rites — a pilgrimage to the caldera, a true name spoken at midnight, a sacrifice of memory or blood. The Lethai-al of Nihori know the rites. They do not speak of them.

The River Oni (Ayokha)

These oni dwell in the deep pools and eddies of the great rivers. They are smaller than the Crater Oni — only slightly larger than a man — but they are quick and clever. They offer bargains to travellers who are lost or desperate. The bargains are always about water — a safe crossing, a hidden ford, a fish that will not be caught.

Weakness: A River Oni can be driven off by a true name — its own, or the name of someone it has wronged. The Lethai-ar of Ayokha keep a list of such names. They do not share it freely.

The Dream Oni (Ways Between)

These oni are not physical — they are shapes in dreams, patterns in the mist, the feeling of being watched in a room that should be empty. They offer bargains in sleep. They take memories as payment. They are patient. They can wait years for a promise to be fulfilled.

Weakness: A Dream Oni can be banished by waking — but waking is not always a choice. It can also be banished by a truth spoken aloud, a truth that the dreamer has never told anyone. The oni will listen. It will judge. If the truth is sufficient, it will leave.

The Tall Folk and the Ninth Taboo

The tall folk — giants, ogres, oni — are all bound by the Ninth Taboo in ways that mortals are not. They count in eights. They stop at eight. They never, ever take the ninth step.

I have seen a giant stop mid-stride at the edge of a valley, its foot hovering over the ninth stone. I have seen an ogre refuse a ninth cup of water. I have seen an oni smile when I counted to eight and stop counting.

The tall folk understand the Ninth in a way that we do not. They are not bound by the taboo — they are shaped by it. They are the taboo made visible. They are what happens when the count goes wrong.

Mechanical Note: In any encounter with a tall folk, the Ninth Taboo is in effect. Counting to nine in their presence will draw their attention — and not in a good way. The wise count to eight and stop. The foolish count to nine and learn what the tall folk already know: the ninth number is not a number. It is a door.

The Hollow’s Reckoning

The tall folk are not evil. They are not monsters. They are the price of forgetting what you owe — the Hollow’s reminder that every action has a cost, and every cost must be paid. They will collect. The only question is whether you pay willingly, or whether they take what they are owed.

But beneath that, there is a deeper truth. The tall folk are the Hollow’s children, and the Hollow is the echo of a promise that was never spoken. It covets what you have — your possessions, your memories, your agency. And in its covetousness, it has created creatures that are both more and less than themselves. They are the Hollow’s dream, and in that dream, they are trying to become real.

I will not write more. Some debts are not meant to be spoken of. But I have seen the tall folk’s eyes when they think no one is watching. They are not afraid. They are waiting. For what, I do not know. I do not think I want to find out.Lerris Fenwood
