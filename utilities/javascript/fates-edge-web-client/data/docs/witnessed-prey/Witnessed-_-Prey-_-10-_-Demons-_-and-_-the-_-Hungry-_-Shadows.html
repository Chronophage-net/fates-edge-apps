Demons and the Hungry Shadows

The Witnessed Prey, a Hunter's Guide — Chapter 10

The Church of the Everflame calls them demons. The Ykrul call them the Unbidden. The Tulkani do not name them at all — they tie knots and leave offerings at crossroads, hoping the shadows will pass them by. I have learned to do the same. But I have also learned that the old texts were not entirely wrong. The demons have orders, ranks, and natures — not because they serve a dark king, but because they are what happens when a soul is broken in a particular way.—Saikou Ira, from a case journal recovered in Port Shed

I am not an exorcist. I am not a hunter. I am a witness — and what I have witnessed of the infernal has left me with more questions than answers.

The dead, I understand. The dead were once alive. They had names, faces, debts, loves, regrets. Even the most twisted ghost is still a person, somewhere beneath the hunger and the rage. But demons? Demons were never alive. They do not owe anything. They do not remember anything. They are not the shadow a debt casts — they are the hole where the debt used to be.

I have encountered demons five times. Each time, I survived by luck, by the intervention of others, and by the simple fact that I ran when I should have run. But I have also read — obsessively, desperately — the old texts. The Grimorium of the Amber Gate. The Codices of the Covenant. The forbidden scrolls of the Ninth Coterie. And I have learned that the demons are not random. They are not chaos. They are patterns — broken patterns, yes, but patterns nonetheless.

The Gray Wanderer once told me: “The dead haunt because they cannot leave. The demons hunt because they cannot stop. One is grief. The other is hunger. Do not confuse the two. Grief can be soothed. Hunger cannot. It only grows. And it is always, always watching.”’

This chapter is my attempt to understand that hunger — not to catalogue every demon in existence, but to provide a framework for recognizing them, understanding their natures, and, if necessary, surviving them.

A Note on Infernal Taxonomy

The old texts speak of hierarchies: the Nine Orders of the Abyss, the Seven Princes, the Thirty-Six Legions. I have found these taxonomies to be useful as metaphor but unreliable as fact. Demons do not obey a single hierarchy. They are not soldiers in a dark army. They are more like species — each with its own nature, its own weaknesses, and its own preferred method of predation.

The Church of the Everflame names them by behavior: Tempters, Tormentors, Devourers. The Lethai-thora of Sihai name them by the emotion they embody: Rage, Desire, Despair. The Tulkani name them by the absence they represent: The Empty Cup, The Unlit Candle, The Unanswered Question.

I have found the most useful distinction to be by origin. A demon’s origin tells you how it entered the world — and, often, how it can be sent back.

The Orders of Demons

Drawing from the old texts — the Grimorium of the Amber Gate, the Codices of the Covenant, and the forbidden scrolls recovered from the Astroegro Vaults — I have identified five broad Orders. These are not rigid categories. Many demons blur the lines between Orders. But the framework is useful for investigators.

Order I: The Deceivers

These are the demons of lies, illusions, and false faces. They do not attack directly; they manipulate, deceive, and corrupt. Their power lies in making you trust them — and in making you doubt yourself.

The Rakshasa — The Shape-Eater

I have seen a rakshasa wear the face of a dead child, a grieving mother, a loyal friend. It wore them all with perfect accuracy — right down to the scar on the child’s knee, the mother’s wedding ring, the friend’s favourite joke. The only thing it could not copy was the grief. Grief is not a shape. Grief is a weight. And the rakshasa has no weight of its own.—Saikou Ira

The rakshasa is the most dangerous of the Deceivers. It is a shapeshifter — not in the crude sense of a beast taking human form, but in the terrifying sense of a predator that can become anyone you trust. It does not attack openly. It infiltrates. It manipulates. It turns allies against each other, and it feeds on the chaos that follows.

I encountered a rakshasa once. It wore the face of a Vilikari factor I had known for years. It spoke in his voice, used his mannerisms, knew his secrets. It almost convinced me to sign a contract that would have bound my soul to a debt I could never repay. I only escaped because the Gray Wanderer had warned me: “A rakshasa cannot copy pain. It can mimic the expression, but it cannot feel the wound. If you doubt someone you trust, ask them about a scar you never told them about. If they flinch, they are real. If they smile, run.”’

Weaknesses:

  • True Sight: A rakshasa’s true form is visible in a silvered mirror or to anyone with the gift of true sight. Its reflection shows a beast with backward hands and eyes that burn like coals.
  • Pain: A rakshasa cannot feel pain. It can fake injury, but it cannot genuinely suffer. If you injure it and it does not react with genuine anguish, you are facing a demon.
  • Iron: Cold iron burns rakshasa flesh. A weapon forged from iron that has never been heated in a forge — that is, iron that has only been cold-hammered — will wound them permanently.
  • The Ninth Word: A rakshasa must answer to a name spoken nine times in its presence. If you can learn its true name — the name it bore before it entered the world — you can command it to leave.

Mechanical Note: A rakshasa encounter is a social combat with high stakes. The demon will attempt to manipulate, deceive, and divide the party. Players must rely on Insight, Investigation, and their Bonds to see through the deception. Combat is possible, but the rakshasa will flee rather than fight — it is a predator, not a soldier.

The Imp — The Whisperer

An imp is not a threat. It is a nuisance — a small, clever creature that offers small, clever bargains. It will tell you a secret, hide a document, start a fire. It will ask for a coin, a hair, a name in return. And then it will follow you, always, because it has a piece of you now, and it wants more.—Saikou Ira

Imps are the least of demons — small, clever, and endlessly curious. They are not dangerous in the way that rakshasa are dangerous. They are dangerous in the way that a slow leak is dangerous: they wear you down, piece by piece, until you have nothing left to give.

I have met two imps. The first offered to guide me through a haunted forest in exchange for a lock of my hair. I refused. The second offered to hide a letter in exchange for my true name. I also refused. I am not sure I was right to refuse. The imps might have been useful. They might have been honest. They might have been lying. That is the nature of imps: you never know until it is too late.

Weaknesses:

  • Contracts: Imps are bound by bargains. If you make a deal with an imp, it must honour its side — but it will interpret the terms as loosely as possible.
  • Naming: An imp’s true name is the name it was given by its summoner. If you can learn it, you can command it.
  • Iron: Cold iron burns imps, but they are small and fast. Hitting one is difficult.
  • Attention: Imps do not like being ignored. If you refuse to bargain with them, they will eventually lose interest and leave.

Mechanical Note: Imps are Tier I–II adversaries who prefer social manipulation to combat. They offer information, services, or minor magical assistance in exchange for seemingly small prices. The price is always larger than it appears. Imps can be useful allies, but they are never loyal.

Order II: The Tempters

These are the demons of desire, longing, and surrender. They do not force you to do anything. They offer you what you want — and you take it willingly. The tragedy is that you are never the same afterward.

The Incubus and Succubus — The Desire-Eaters

They are not demons of lust. They are demons of longing. The shape they wear is irrelevant. What matters is that they know what you want more than anything — and they offer it to you, in the exact form that will make you accept. The price is never stated. It is paid in the moment of surrender.—Saikou Ira

The incubus and the succubus are the same creature, wearing different masks. They do not feed on the body — they feed on the soul’s surrender. They offer you what you most desire: love, revenge, safety, power. And when you accept, they take a piece of you that you can never get back.

I have met a man who spent a single night with a succubus. He was never the same. He could still love, but he could not trust. He could still hope, but he could not believe. The succubus had taken his capacity for wonder — and he had given it freely, because she had promised him a perfect night.

She did not lie. It was perfect. That was the tragedy.

Weaknesses:

  • Chastity: Incubi and succubi cannot touch someone who is genuinely content with their life. Their power is in exploiting lack — if you are not hungry, they cannot feed you.
  • Naming: Like the rakshasa, they must answer to a name. Their true name is the name they bore before they became demons — if they ever had one.
  • Exorcism: A properly witnessed exorcism can drive them out, but they will always return to the same host unless the host’s desire is addressed.
  • Desire fulfilled: If you can give yourself what the demon is offering — if you can find love, safety, power, or peace on your own — the demon has nothing to offer and will leave.

Mechanical Note: These demons are Tier III–IV adversaries who avoid direct combat. They prefer to manipulate from a distance, offering bargains and temptations. Players who accept a bargain gain a temporary benefit but also a permanent Obligation to the demon — and the demon will collect.

Order III: The Pursuers

These are the demons of relentless hunger. They do not bargain. They do not negotiate. They hunt. And they will not stop until they catch their prey or are destroyed.

The Hellhound — The Unrelenting Hunter

The hounds do not tire. They do not sleep. They do not eat. They hunt. And when they find you, they will not kill you — they will hold you for their master. I have seen a hellhound track a man across three nations, through storms and cities and sacred places. The man never escaped. He only delayed.—Saikou Ira

Hellhounds are the least intelligent of demons, but they are the most persistent. They are summoned to hunt — a specific person, a specific family, a specific bloodline. They do not bargain. They do not negotiate. They hunt. And they will not stop until they catch their prey or are destroyed.

I have never faced a hellhound. I have seen their tracks — burned claw-marks on stone, faint traces of sulfur in the air — and I have always chosen to be elsewhere. The Gray Wanderer once told me: “If you hear a hellhound, you are already too close. If you see one, you are already dead. Your only chance is to run toward something sacred — and pray the sacred thing is stronger than the hound’s master.”’

Weaknesses:

  • Cold Iron: Hellhounds can be wounded by cold iron, but they will not stop until they are destroyed.
  • Sacred Ground: A hellhound cannot cross sacred ground — but it will circle it, waiting. It is patient. It has nothing else to do.
  • The Hunt’s End: The only way to stop a hellhound permanently is to destroy its summoner. The hound is bound to its master’s will. When the master dies, the hound fades.
  • The Name: A hellhound has no name of its own. It answers to the name of its prey. If you can make it forget your name, it will lose the hunt.

Mechanical Note: Hellhounds are Tier III–IV adversaries with high Speed and high Harm. They cannot be reasoned with. They cannot be bribed. They will pursue the party across multiple sessions, reappearing at the worst possible moments. Resolving a hellhound hunt requires either destroying the summoner, finding a way to break the hunt’s binding, or convincing the demon that the prey is no longer worth hunting.

Order IV: The Inherited

These are the demons of bloodlines, legacies, and generational sin. They are not summoned. They are not born from wounds. They are passed down — through families, through cursed objects, through the weight of unacknowledged crimes. An Inherited demon does not attack directly. It erodes. It waits. It turns the bearer’s own nature against them.

The Blood-Demon — The Ancestral Hunger

I have seen an Inherited demon pass from mother to daughter for nine generations. Each bearer believed they were simply unlucky. They were not unlucky. They were collateral. The demon did not hate them. It did not even know them. It was simply there, a debt that had gone unanswered for so long that no one remembered how it began.—Saikou Ira

The Inherited demon is the most insidious of the infernal Orders. It does not possess. It does not tempt. It inhabits — not the body, but the bloodline. It is the accumulated weight of a family’s sins, given form and will. It manifests not as a single entity but as a pattern: the same tragedy repeating across generations, the same failure, the same loss.

I have investigated three Inherited demon cases. The first was a merchant family in Ecktoria who had lost every firstborn son to suicide for seven generations. The second was a Lethai-ar lineage in the Crimson Valley where every daughter, without exception, married badly and died young. The third was a Vilikari clan whose members could not keep a promise — any promise — for more than a year.

In each case, the family had a secret. A crime. A betrayal. A debt that was never paid. The demon was not the punishment. It was what the debt became.

Manifestation:

An Inherited demon manifests differently in each generation. It adapts. It learns. It finds the weakness of the current bearer and exploits it. Common manifestations include:

  • Recurring tragedy: The same death, the same betrayal, the same failure, repeated in each generation. The details change, but the pattern remains.
  • Compulsive behaviour: Bearers feel an inexplicable urge to repeat the actions of their ancestors — to make the same mistake, to trust the same kind of person, to walk into the same trap.
  • Memory gaps: Bearers cannot remember the family’s history. The gaps are where the demon hides. The truth is always there, just out of reach.
  • Inherited guilt: Bearers feel responsible for crimes they did not commit. The guilt is the demon’s anchor — the weight that keeps it bound to the bloodline.
  • The Ninth Generation: In the ninth generation, the demon manifests fully. The bearer becomes a vessel, and the demon walks freely in the world. This is the crisis point — the moment when the debt comes due.

Investigation Notes:

An Inherited demon cannot be exorcised in the conventional sense. It is not possessing a single host. It is bound to the bloodline itself. To remove it, you must:

  1. Identify the original sin: What crime, betrayal, or broken promise created the debt? This may require patient genealogical work, conversations with family elders, and a close reading of old family records.
  2. Find the anchor: The demon is bound to a physical object — a ring, a sword, a book, a piece of jewelry. The anchor was created at the moment of the original sin and has been passed down with the demon.
  3. Break the chain: The demon can be severed from the bloodline by destroying the anchor — but this must be done with witnesses. The demon will fight. It will offer bargains. It will lie. The witnesses are the only protection.
  4. Acknowledge the debt: The original sin must be acknowledged by a living descendant. The acknowledgement must be public, witnessed, and sincere. This is not punishment — it is truth.

Weaknesses:

  • The Anchor: The demon is bound to a physical object. Destroying the anchor releases the demon from the bloodline — but it will try to find a new anchor. The anchor must be destroyed completely, and the remains must be scattered in separate bodies of water.
  • The Truth: The demon cannot survive the truth of the original sin. If the crime is acknowledged and the debt is paid, the demon loses its hold. But the truth must be spoken aloud, publicly, and with witnesses.
  • The Ninth Generation: In the ninth generation, the demon becomes vulnerable. It is no longer a pattern; it is a single entity, and it can be confronted. But it is also at its strongest. The ninth generation is the crisis point — the moment of reckoning.
  • Sacrifice: A living descendant may choose to accept the demon into themselves, containing it through sheer will. This is not a cure; it is a holding action. The bearer will be tormented for the rest of their life, but they will save their descendants.

Mechanical Note: An Inherited demon is best handled as a Campaign Arc rather than a single adversary. It manifests as a series of complications — bad luck, failed relationships, sudden violence — that escalate over time. Resolving it requires the party to:

  • Research the family’s history (Investigation, Lore, Interrogation)
  • Identify the original sin and the anchor (Lore, Insight, historical research)
  • Find the anchor and destroy it (Craft, Combat, or Ritual)
  • Speak the truth of the original sin (Social, witnessed by a third party)

The demon will resist every step. It will send nightmares, temptations, and, if necessary, physical threats. But it is bound to the bloodline — and if the bloodline is set free, the demon has no anchor.

The Inherited Demon Through Generations: A Case Study

I documented a case in the Vilikari marches that illustrates the pattern. The O’Creagh clan had a reputation for misfortune — broken betrothals, failed harvests, sudden deaths. The family blamed a curse. I found a demon.

Generation 1: The Crime A clan chief, Cormac O’Creagh, betrayed his sworn brother for a bride-price. The brother was exiled; the bride was taken; the debt was unpaid.

Generation 2: The Pattern Cormac’s son, the firstborn, died in a riding accident on his wedding day. The same accident, the same day, repeated for three generations.

Generation 3: The Guilt The family began to remember. They did not know what they remembered — only that they were guilty. They gave away land, wealth, and children to atone for a sin they could not name.

Generation 4: The Anchor A family heirloom — a ring that had belonged to the betrayed brother — was passed down. The ring was warm. It glowed when someone lied.

Generation 5: The Denial The family stopped speaking of the past. The ring was hidden. The pattern continued.

Generation 6–8: The Erosion The demon fed on the family’s denial. The tragedies grew worse. The family began to fracture — branches estranged, alliances broken, secrets buried.

Generation 9: The Reckoning The current clan chief, a woman named Sorcha, learned the truth. She found the ring. She brought it to me.

We broke the anchor in a circle of witnesses: a Lethai-ar silk-warden, a Tulkani knot-keeper, and a Vilikari hearth-mother. Sorcha spoke her ancestor’s crime aloud. The demon screamed. The ring shattered.

The family is not healed — some wounds are too deep — but the pattern is broken. The debt is paid. It no longer grows in the dark.

Order V: The Unbidden

These are the demons of wounds — the shape that suffering takes when it becomes too great to contain. They are not summoned. They are not inherited. They are born from places of mass death, unresolved grief, and forgotten atrocity.

The Wound-Demon — The Shape of Suffering

I have seen a wound-demon in the ruins of a village that was burned in a Vhasian chevauchée. It did not attack me. It simply was — a shape of smoke and ash that wept and burned and wept again. It was not evil. It was pain given form. And I could not help it. I could only witness.—Saikou Ira

The Unbidden are the most tragic of demons. They are not malevolent in the way that Deceivers or Tempters are malevolent. They are wounds. They exist because something terrible happened and was never acknowledged, never mourned, never healed. They are the pain of the world, given shape.

A wound-demon does not attack. It presses. It makes you feel what it feels — grief, rage, despair, terror. It does not want to hurt you. It wants to be seen. If you can witness it, truly witness it, and acknowledge the pain that created it, it will fade.

But it will always return to the same place. The wound is still there.

Weaknesses:

  • Witness: A wound-demon requires a witness — someone who will acknowledge the pain it embodies. If you can name the atrocity that created it, it will weaken. If you can speak the names of the dead, it will fade.
  • Healing: The only permanent cure for a wound-demon is to heal the wound that created it. This may require a ritual, a memorial, or an act of justice.
  • The Ninth Word: The ninth name spoken in a wound-demon’s presence forces it to manifest fully. If you can bear the vision, you can understand its pain. If you cannot, it will follow you.

Signs of Demonic Presence

I have learned to read the signs of the infernal. They are not always obvious — a demon can hide in plain sight for years — but they are always present, if you know where to look.

Physical Signs

  • Sulfur or burnt clove: The smell of the infernal is unmistakable. It is not the smell of burning — it is the smell of something that has already burned and is still smouldering.
  • Cold spots: Demons draw heat from their surroundings. A room that is inexplicably cold, even in summer, is a room that has been visited.
  • Mirrors: A demon’s reflection is always wrong. It may be delayed, distorted, or absent entirely. A silvered mirror will show a demon’s true form — if you dare to look.
  • Shadows: A demon’s shadow moves independently of its body. It may move in the wrong direction, or it may move at the wrong time.
  • Markings: Demons leave marks — scorch marks, claw marks, symbols that were not there before. The marks are always in a pattern of eight, never nine.

Behavioral Signs

  • Disproportionate emotion: Demons feed on strong emotions. A person who seems too happy, too angry, or too afraid may be under demonic influence.
  • Sudden skill: A demon can grant its host talents they never had — languages, combat abilities, knowledge of forbidden lore. If someone suddenly knows something they should not, something is wrong.
  • Compulsive behaviour: Demons compel their hosts to act in specific ways — to perform rituals, to seek out certain people, to avoid certain places. The compulsion is always stronger at night.
  • Memory loss: Demons erase memories to hide their presence. If someone cannot remember large stretches of time, or if they have forgotten things they used to know, a demon may be involved.
  • Unusual interest in thresholds: Demons are drawn to boundaries — doors, windows, crossroads, the edges of settlements. A person who seems obsessed with thresholds may be a demon, or may be possessed by one.

Protecting Yourself Against Demons

I have learned a few things that work. I do not claim to understand why they work. I only know that they have saved my life.

Cold Iron

Cold iron — iron that has never been heated in a forge — burns demons. A knife forged from a horseshoe that was never shod, a nail pulled from a ship that was never launched, a coin that was never minted: these are weapons that demons fear.

The Gray Wanderer carries a cold iron nail in her pocket at all times. She has never used it. She says that just having it is enough.

Salt

A line of salt will bar most demons. Not all — the stronger demons can cross it with effort — but all demons are slowed by it. Sea salt is best for demons of the coast; rock salt is best for demons of the mountains; salt blessed by any faith is best for all.

I carry a pouch of salt from the Mistslands. It has saved my life three times. I do not know why it works. I do not care.

Silver

Silver does not hurt demons in the way it hurts the undead. It does, however, force them to reveal their true form. A silvered mirror will show a demon’s reflection; a silver coin placed on a demon’s tongue will force it to speak truthfully, at least for a moment.

My silver mirror is cracked. I do not know when it cracked. It still works.

Witness

A demon cannot act freely in the presence of a witness. It can still act — but it cannot lie. A demon that is seen cannot deny what it is. If you can get three witnesses to look at a demon and refuse to look away, the demon must either flee or reveal its true nature.

The Gray Wanderer says: “The first witness is courage. The second is strength. The third is the truth. Two witnesses can be overcome. Three cannot. The demon knows this. That is why it always tries to separate you.”’

Naming

A demon must answer to a name. If you know its true name — the name it bore before it entered the world — you can command it. The name is never obvious. It may be hidden in a contract, a lineage, a curse, or a scar.

I once learned a demon’s true name by reading the margin of a book that had been burned. The ink was ash. The name was a debt. I never used it. I was afraid of what would happen if I did.

The Ninth Measure

Demons cannot abide the Ninth. They count in eights. They stop at eight. If you can force a demon to count to nine, it must reveal its true name — or flee. I have never seen this work. I have heard of it working. I am not sure I want to test it.

Final Thought

Demons are not the dead. They are not the Hollow. They are not the Hunger Beneath. They are something else — something that was never alive, never loved, never mourned. They are the shape of the world’s wounds, and they are always hungry.

I do not know how to defeat a demon permanently. I know how to survive one. I know how to run. I know how to hide. I know how to bargain — but I do not trust my bargains. The demon always gets the better end.

The Gray Wanderer once told me: “You cannot kill a demon. You can only refuse to feed it. The demon is hunger. If you stop being afraid, it will starve. But you will not stop being afraid. That is why the demons are still here. That is why they will always be here.”’

I will not write more. Some things are not meant to be recorded. But I have seen the demons’ eyes when they think no one is watching. They are not afraid. They are waiting. For what, I do not know. I do not think I want to find out.Saikou Ira

The Inherited Demon: Procedures for GMs

For GMs wishing to use an Inherited demon as a campaign arc, the following procedure provides a framework.

Phase 1: The Pattern

The party encounters a family or bloodline with a recurring tragedy. The pattern should be specific but not obvious. Examples:

  • Firstborn children die before their twentieth birthday.
  • Every marriage in the family ends in betrayal.
  • The family loses its fortune every generation through the same kind of bad deal.
  • A specific type of death — drowning, fire, fall — recurs in each generation.

Phase 2: The Investigation

The party must research the family’s history. This involves:

  • Interviewing family members (many of whom are in denial)
  • Examining family records (which may be incomplete or falsified)
  • Locating the original sin (often a betrayal, a murder, or a broken oath)
  • Finding the anchor (the object to which the demon is bound)

Phase 3: The Confrontation

Once the original sin is known, the party must:

  • Identify and destroy the anchor (this may require a quest to find it)
  • Speak the truth of the original sin in the presence of witnesses
  • Break the demon’s hold on the bloodline

Phase 4: The Resolution

If successful, the demon is severed from the bloodline. The family is not healed, but the pattern is broken. If unsuccessful, the demon may manifest fully — often in the ninth generation.

Mechanical Hooks for GMs

  • Clocks: The Inherited demon can be tracked with a Blood-Demon’s Patience [6] clock. It ticks when the party fails to make progress, when a family member dies, or when the GM spends 2+ Story Beats on family-related complications.
  • Resistance: The demon may attack the party directly — through nightmares, temptations, or physical manifestations — to prevent them from uncovering the truth.
  • Bargains: The demon will offer bargains to the party. It will offer to spare a family member, to provide information, or to grant power — in exchange for the party’s silence or complicity.
  • Success Conditions: The demon is defeated when the anchor is destroyed and the truth is spoken in the presence of at least three witnesses.

The Exorcist’s Debt

Every exorcism costs me something. Not fatigue — I can sleep. Not obligation — I owe the Sentinel nothing.

It costs me witness.

After I seal a demon, I lose a memory — not a skill, not a name, but a moment of my life. The demon’s shard takes it as toll. I do not know what I have forgotten until I try to remember.

I know I have a daughter. I cannot recall her face. I know I was a marine. I cannot recall my ship’s name. I know I have been doing this for forty years. I cannot recall why I started.

The Witness does not take these memories. The demon does. The hole that the demon crawled through is also the hole where the past goes. However, demon never takes the memory of an exorcism. They want you to remember. They want you to lie awake at three in the morning with the sound of your own chanting, the weight of the shattering mirror, the face of the host as the demon left their eyes.

That memory is a hook. They can pull it whenever they wish.

One day I will forget my own name. That will be the last exorcism.

But not today.

The ink is dry. The mirror is clean. The debt is never closed.Saikou Ira
