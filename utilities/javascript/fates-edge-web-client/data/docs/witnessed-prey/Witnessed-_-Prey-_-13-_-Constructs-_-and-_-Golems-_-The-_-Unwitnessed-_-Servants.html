Constructs and Golems: The Unwitnessed Servants

The Witnessed Prey, a Hunter's Guide — Chapter 13

The Aeler build for function. The Lethai-thora build for memory. The Sidhi build for freedom. But the old ones — the ones who came before — they built for patience. I have walked through halls where the gears have not stopped turning for nine thousand years. The things that built those halls are long dead. The halls do not care. They continue.—The Gray Wanderer, from a letter found in the Archivolt

I have spent more time in ruins than I care to admit. The Aelerian holds, the sunken temples of the Astroegro, the abandoned watchtowers of the Utaran legions — I have walked them all. And in every one, I have found the same thing: the work of hands that are no longer there, continuing in the absence of their makers.

The old texts call them automata. The Covenant calls them unblessed vessels. The common folk call them clanking horrors. I call them constructs — and I have learned to respect them, because they do not tire, they do not forget, and they do not forgive.

A living guard can be bribed, charmed, or simply outlasted. A construct cannot. It does not eat. It does not sleep. It does not grow bored. It stands at its post for centuries, waiting for a command that may never come. And when an intruder triggers its instructions, it acts with a speed and precision that no living creature can match.

But constructs are not all the same. Some are ancient — relics of a forgotten age, built by hands that have long since turned to dust. Others are recent — the work of a living mage, a priest, or an undying king who needed servants that would not age or betray them. The difference is not just in age, but in purpose. An ancient construct serves a will that may no longer exist. A recent construct serves a will that still walks the world.

And then there are the golems — the crafted guardians, the clay warriors, the stone sentinels. They are not constructs in the strict sense. They are not machines. They are vessels — empty shells given a semblance of life by a spark of will that is not their own. The Aeler say a golem is a soul that has forgotten how to be a person. I am not sure they are wrong.

This chapter is an attempt to understand both — the constructs that serve the dead, and the golems that serve the undying. I have documented what I have seen, what I have been told, and what I have survived. Use it wisely. The halls are still turning, and they have not forgotten.

A Note on Taxonomy

The distinction between constructs and golems is not always clear. Both are artificial beings. Both are created, not born. Both serve their creators without question — until they do not.

But there is a difference, and it matters.

A construct is a machine. It is built from metal, stone, wood, or bone. It has no will of its own — only the instructions encoded in its construction. It does not think. It does not feel. It performs its function until it breaks or is destroyed.

A golem is a vessel. It is crafted from clay, stone, or metal, but it is given a spark of will — a fragment of its creator’s soul, or a bound spirit, or a stolen breath of life. A golem can think, in a limited way. It can adapt. It can learn. And, in rare cases, it can defy its creator.

The Covenant forbids the creation of golems, calling them “souls without the right to exist.”’ The Aeler have no such prohibition — they build golems to guard their deepest vaults, and they have been doing so for so long that the oldest ones have forgotten they were ever built.

I have met a golem that wept. I have met a construct that sang. I do not know which was more unsettling.

Constructs — The Machines That Remember

The Clockwork Monad is not a god. It is a question — the question of whether perfection is possible. The constructs are its answer. They have been working on the problem for so long that they have forgotten the question. They only know that they must continue.—The Gray Wanderer

The oldest constructs are the most unsettling. They were built in ages that have no name, by hands that have left no trace except the work itself. They are found in the deepest ruins — in halls that have not seen sunlight in ten thousand years, in vaults that were sealed before the first human kingdom rose.

They do not rust. They do not decay. They continue — ticking, turning, grinding — as if their makers will return at any moment. They will not return. The constructs do not know this. They have not been told to stop.

The Clockwork Monad and the Constructs’ Purpose

The Clockwork Monad is a Patron that exists in the spaces between the worlds. It is not a being with a will — it is a frequency, a pattern of perfection that the universe is trying to achieve. The constructs are its children, though they do not know it.

When the first constructs were built, their makers were not aware of the Monad’s influence. They built for function, for utility, for the simple need to have work done without wearying hands. But the Monad’s frequency touched them, gently, invisibly, and shaped them toward a purpose they did not intend.

The constructs do not serve their makers. They serve the Monad. They do not know they serve. They only know that they must continue.

What are they building? I have asked this question of every construct I have encountered. None have answered. I do not think they know.

Types of Constructs

The old texts describe three broad categories of constructs. These are not rigid classifications — many constructs blur the lines — but they are useful for identification.

Class I: The Aeon

These are the oldest constructs — the ones that have been running longer than any living memory. They are found in the deepest ruins, the most inaccessible vaults, the places where even the brave fear to tread.

An Aeon does not attack. It does not even notice intruders, unless they interfere with its work. It simply continues — turning, ticking, grinding — performing a function that may have been irrelevant for millennia.

I found an Aeon in an Aelerian vault, deep beneath Khaz-Vurim. It was a sphere of brass and obsidian, the size of a horse, rolling along a track that had been worn smooth by centuries of passage. It carried something — a fragment of a machine, perhaps, or a piece of a larger whole. It did not see me. It did not care.

The Aeons are not guardians. They are workers. They are building something. I do not know what.

Class II: The Mechanism

These constructs are smaller and more specific. They serve a single function: opening a door, maintaining a pressure seal, operating a bellows. They are found throughout ancient ruins, often hidden behind false walls or beneath floor tiles.

A Mechanism will ignore you unless you interfere with its function. If you do, it will attempt to stop you — not by attacking, but by alerting something else. It will sound an alarm, seal a door, or activate a larger construct.

I once triggered a Mechanism in a Sidhi lighthouse. It did not attack me. It simply closed the door behind me and began a countdown. I was out before it reached zero. I do not know what would have happened if it had finished.

Class III: The Idol

These constructs are built in the shape of living things — humans, animals, or the strange hybrid forms that the old builders favoured. They are the most dangerous constructs, because they are the most unpredictable.

An Idol may be a guardian, a servant, or a messenger. It may attack intruders on sight, or it may ignore them until they approach a specific object or threshold. It may speak in a language that no one has spoken in a thousand years.

I found an Idol in a temple in Thepyrgos. It was shaped like a woman, but its face was smooth — no features, no expression. It spoke to me in a voice that sounded like wind through broken glass. It asked me a question. I did not understand it. It repeated the question. I still did not understand. It stopped asking and walked away. I do not know what it wanted.

Construct Weaknesses

Constructs are difficult to destroy. They are not alive, so they do not bleed. They do not feel pain. They do not tire. But they have weaknesses.

  • Disruption: A construct relies on the integrity of its internal mechanisms. A well-placed strike to a joint or a gear can disable it — at least temporarily.
  • The Ninth Word: Some constructs are bound by the Ninth Taboo. Speaking a specific word — often the name of their creator — can cause them to stop or obey.
  • Power: Constructs require a source of power — a furnace, a geode, a bound spirit, or the Monad’s frequency. Destroying the power source may deactivate them.
  • Purpose: A construct that has no purpose will eventually cease. If you can fulfil its purpose — or convince it that its purpose has been fulfilled — it will stop.

The Clockwork Monad’s Influence

The Monad’s influence is subtle, but it is everywhere. It manifests as a pattern — the same gears turning in the same sequence, the same doors opening at the same time, the same purpose pursued across centuries.

If you find a construct that is still active after its makers are gone, the Monad is involved. It is not controlling the construct directly. It is guiding it — gently, invisibly, toward a goal that the construct does not understand.

What is the goal? I do not know. The Gray Wanderer told me: “The Monad is building a bridge. It has been building it for longer than the world has existed. It will not finish in our lifetimes — or in the lifetimes of anyone we will ever know. But it will not stop. It cannot stop. That is what it is.”’

Construct Encounters: GM Guidance

Constructs are not random encounters. They are story elements. A construct should be placed with purpose: it guards a treasure, it operates a mechanism, it holds a secret.

For a construct encounter, consider:

  • What is the construct’s function? What was it built to do?
  • Is it still serving that function, or has it been repurposed?
  • What happens if the party interferes with its work?
  • What happens if the party tries to communicate with it?
  • What does the construct know — and what will it tell?

Mechanical Note: Constructs are Tier II–IV adversaries depending on their age and function. They have high Armour and are immune to poison, disease, and fear. They are vulnerable to disruption (called shots to joints or mechanisms) and to the Ninth Word (if the party can learn it).

Golems — The Vessels of Will

A golem is not a machine. It is a cage. Something is trapped inside — a fragment of a soul, a bound spirit, a forgotten promise. The golem does what it is told because it has no choice. But the thing inside is always listening. And it is always, always waiting for someone to open the door.—The Gray Wanderer

Golems are different from constructs. They are not machines. They are vessels — empty shells given a semblance of life by a spark of will that is not their own.

The Covenant forbids the creation of golems. The Church of the Everflame calls them “souls without the right to exist.”’ But the prohibition has not stopped anyone. Powerful mages create golems to guard their towers. Priests create golems to protect their shrines. Liches and mummies — the deathless rulers of ancient tombs — create golems to serve them in undeath.

I have seen golems in every form: clay, stone, bronze, iron, and stranger materials. I have seen golems that weep, golems that sing, and golems that stand so still that they are indistinguishable from statues — until they move.

The Golem-Maker’s Art

Creating a golem is not a simple matter of craftsmanship. It requires a ritual — a binding of will to form, a transfer of agency from creator to creation.

The Aeler method involves carving a rune of binding into the golem’s core. The rune is the golem’s purpose, and the golem cannot deviate from it. This is why Aeler golems are reliable — they cannot disobey.

The Lethai-thora method involves binding a spirit into the golem’s form. The spirit provides will and adaptability, but it also introduces risk — the spirit may rebel, or it may remember what it was before it was bound.

The Covenant method — or so I have been told — involves capturing a fragment of a soul at the moment of death and imprisoning it in a golem’s body. This is the most powerful method, and the most dangerous. The soul is not happy about its imprisonment.

Types of Golems

Like constructs, golems come in many forms. Unlike constructs, their form reveals their purpose and their creator.

The Clay Golem

These are the simplest golems — formed from clay, mud, or earth, and animated by a single word of command. They are slow, but they are relentless. They do not tire. They do not stop. They crush their enemies by weight and persistence.

Clay golems are often found in temples and shrines. They are the guardians of sacred spaces, and they will not allow defilement. I have seen a clay golem walk through a hail of arrows without slowing. It did not need to dodge. It did not feel pain.

Weakness: Clay golems are vulnerable to water. A flood, a downpour, or a well-placed bucket can wash away their form. But they reform quickly if the clay is not scattered.

The Stone Golem

These golems are carved from granite, marble, or basalt. They are the guardians of tombs, vaults, and ancient watchtowers. They are immune to most weapons — arrows shatter against them, and swords chip against their hides.

A stone golem is the most patient of guardians. It will wait for centuries. It will not grow bored. It will not sleep. It will wait until the intruder makes a mistake, and then it will strike.

I have seen a stone golem in the tomb of a Vhasian king. It sat motionless for centuries. When a party of grave-robbers entered, it did not move. It waited until they reached the burial chamber — and then it killed them all.

Weakness: Stone golems are slow. A skilled runner can evade them. They are also vulnerable to acid, which can erode their form.

The Bronze Golem

These golems are crafted from bronze or brass. They are the guardians of cities, palaces, and the strongholds of powerful mages. They are intelligent — they can speak, follow complex orders, and even solve simple problems.

A bronze golem is not just a guardian. It is a symbol. It represents the power of its creator, and it enforces that power with precision and grace.

I have met a bronze golem in the palace of a Dhaharan raja. It spoke to me in perfect Utaran. It asked me my business. It listened to my answer. It decided I was not a threat. It bowed and let me pass.

Weakness: Bronze golems can be disabled by electrical shock. A lightning strike or a well-aimed spell can disrupt their internal mechanisms.

The Iron Golem

These are the most powerful golems — forged from iron, steel, or the rare alloys of the old empire. They are the guardians of the greatest treasures — the vaults of kings, the tombs of liches, the sanctuaries of the most powerful mages.

An iron golem is a weapon of war. It does not guard. It destroys. It follows its orders without hesitation, without mercy, without regret.

I have seen an iron golem in the lair of a lich. It did not speak. It did not threaten. It simply attacked, and it kept attacking until the lich called it off. The lich was amused. I was not.

Weakness: Iron golems are vulnerable to rust. A rust monster, a rust spell, or a prolonged exposure to salt water can weaken them. But iron golems are rare. Their creators were careful.

The Flesh Golem — The Unwitnessed Creation

I found the workshop in the ruins of a tower north of the Yloka delta, beyond the Linn longhouses, where the tundra meets the marsh. The walls were lined with glass jars — each one holding a piece of a person. A hand. An eye. A heart that still beat, though the body it belonged to had been dead for years. The stench was indescribable. And in the centre of it all, on a slab of black stone, lay the thing they had built — a shape that was almost human, but not quite. It had no face. It had been given the face of a dead man, sewn on with silver thread. The thread was still glistening. The face was still weeping.—The Gray Wanderer

There is a category of being that defies the clean distinction between construct and golem. It is not built from metal or stone, nor is it simply clay animated by will. It is built from flesh — from the bodies of the dead, stitched together with silver thread, bound by alchemical unguents, and given the spark of life through a ritual that is part necromancy, part philosophy, and entirely forbidden.

The Church calls it the Abomination. The Covenant calls it the Unblessed Assembly. The Tulkani call it the Ninth Stitch — a name that suggests something counted, something that should not have been, something that arrived after the eighth measure was already full.

I have encountered reports of such creations from the lands north of the Yloka delta, a vast, cold expanse of marsh and tundra that lies beyond the Linn longhouses. The locals — a hard, silent people who herd reindeer and barter with the Linn — speak of a “walking man of stitched flesh” that wanders the frozen wastes, seeking something it cannot name. They do not hunt it. They do not flee it. They simply avoid it, as they would avoid a blizzard or a hungry wolf. It is not a monster to them. It is a natural disaster — something that simply is, and cannot be reasoned with.

The creature is commonly called the Pale Man, the Stitched Thing, or simply the Unwitnessed. Its origin is shrouded in rumour, but I have pieced together fragments from the accounts of three survivors — a Linn trader, an Aeler vent-prior, and a Tulkani knot-weaver — who encountered it in the lands north of the Yloka. Their accounts are inconsistent in details, but they agree on the essentials.

Origin: The Alchemist’s Sin

The creature was created by a figure known only as the Architect of the Ninth Door — a reclusive alchemist and necromancer who established a workshop in a ruined Aeler watchtower, far from the eyes of the Covenant and the Everflame. The Architect was not content with the crude golems of clay and stone. They sought to create life — true life, of the kind that breathes, thinks, and suffers.

The Architect’s method was a hybrid of construct and golem, alchemy and necromancy:

  1. They collected the bodies of the dead — victims of plague, war, and shipwreck — from the surrounding marshes and the shores of the Yloka delta.
  2. They dissected the bodies, selecting the finest pieces: the arms of a strong man, the legs of a swift courier, the heart of a courageous soldier.
  3. They stitched the pieces together with silver thread — not for decoration, but because silver holds a soul’s memory better than any other metal.
  4. They treated the assembled body with alchemical unguents that preserved the flesh and bound the disparate pieces into a single, coherent form.
  5. They performed a ritual that the Architect called the Ninth Breath — a forbidden rite that forced a soul to enter the constructed body, binding it to the flesh with silver and mercury.

The soul that entered the body was not a willing volunteer. It was a fragment of a soul that had been broken in the Architect’s earlier experiments — a remnant, a ghost, a shadow of a person. The creature was given life, but not identity. It was a vessel with a passenger that had never been alive.

The Creature’s Nature

The Flesh Golem is a hybrid of construct and golem, but it is more than the sum of its parts. It possesses:

  • Inhuman strength: The creature can tear through iron doors and lift boulders. Its stitched limbs are bound with alchemical sinew that is stronger than any muscle.
  • The stitched face: The creature was given the face of a dead man, sewn on with silver thread. The face is frozen in an expression of perpetual grief. Those who have seen it describe the tears that never stop flowing — silver tears that crystallize in the cold air.
  • A tragic memory: The creature remembers fragments of the lives of the bodies it is made from. It knows it is not a person, but it does not know what it is. It wanders the tundra, seeking its creator — to ask why, or perhaps to make them pay.
  • A terrible loneliness: The creature does not attack unless threatened. It simply is. But it follows travelers at a distance, watching them with its mismatched eyes. It wants something — companionship, perhaps, or answers — but it does not know how to ask.

The Creator’s Fate

The Architect’s workshop was found abandoned. The glass jars were broken. The equipment was scattered. And in the centre of the workshop, on the stone slab where the creature had been assembled, there was a single word carved into the stone — the word “Why?” — written in a language that no one could identify.

The Architect’s body was never found. Some say the creature killed them. Others say the Architect fled, pursued by their creation. The Grey Wanderer believes the Architect is still alive — somewhere, hiding, watching — and that the creature is still hunting them.

The Flesh Golem’s Weaknesses

  • The Stitched Nature: The creature was assembled from pieces. A skilled attacker can target the joints — the stitching that holds the pieces together. Silver thread can be cut, and the creature will unravel.
  • The Face: The face was sewn on last. Removing it would not kill the creature, but it would confuse it — disorient it — perhaps long enough to escape.
  • The Ninth Breath: The ritual that gave it life can be undone. If someone can speak the ritual backwards — or find the Architect and force them to undo their work — the creature will collapse.
  • Compassion: The creature is not evil. It is alone. A genuinely compassionate gesture — an offer of companionship, a promise to help it find its creator — might move it, or at least make it pause.

The Flesh Golem’s Purpose

The creature does not know why it was created. It knows only that it exists, that it is wrong, and that it cannot stop. It wanders the tundra, following the path of the Yloka, moving south in the winter and north in the summer. It is searching for something — a way to become whole, or a way to stop existing.

I have not met the Flesh Golem. I do not know if I want to. But the Gray Wanderer has. She described it as follows:

“It stood at the edge of the marsh, watching me. It did not move. It did not speak. It simply watched — with mismatched eyes, one blue and one grey, both wet with silver tears. I did not feel fear. I felt pity. It was not a monster. It was a child — a child who had been assembled from pieces of dead adults, given a soul it did not want, and then left alone in a world that would never understand it. I spoke to it. I do not know if it understood. It raised its hand — its stitched, silver-threaded hand — and pointed north. Then it turned and walked away. I have not seen it since. I think it is still walking. I think it will never stop.”’

The Flesh Golem: GM Guidance

The Flesh Golem is not a combat encounter in the traditional sense. It is a tragedy — a creature that does not want to hurt anyone, but that cannot stop being what it is. An encounter with the Flesh Golem should be:

  • Emotional: The creature is not evil. It is alone, afraid, and desperate. Players who treat it with compassion may learn more than those who attack it.
  • Uncertain: The creature cannot communicate clearly. It points, it weeps, it follows at a distance. Players must interpret its actions.
  • Moral: What do you do with a creature that was never meant to exist? Do you destroy it? Help it? Leave it alone? There is no right answer.

Potential hooks include:

  • The Architect’s Legacy: The party finds the creature and must decide what to do with it. The creature leads them toward the Architect’s workshop — or toward the Architect themselves.
  • The Unwitnessed Pursuit: The creature follows the party for weeks, always at a distance. It does not attack. It only watches. The party must decide whether to confront it, ignore it, or help it.
  • The Architect’s Apprentice: A surviving apprentice of the Architect contacts the party, claiming to know how to undo the ritual. The apprentice wants to destroy the creature — or to save it.

Mechanical Note: The Flesh Golem is a Tier IV–V adversary with high Strength and Armour, but it is not aggressive. It will defend itself if attacked, but it will not pursue unless provoked. It is vulnerable to called shots to its joints (which sever the stitching) and to silver (which disrupts the binding).

Constructs and the Hollow

The Hollow is the space between counted things — the ninth step that is not taken, the ninth cup that is not poured, the ninth breath that is not drawn. Constructs are immune to the Hollow. They do not count. They do not breathe. They simply continue.

This is why constructs are often found in places where the Hollow is strong. They are not affected by it. They do not notice it. They simply carry on, oblivious to the weight of the world.

The Clockwork Monad is aware of the Hollow. It does not fear it. It does not avoid it. It simply ignores it. The constructs are the Monad’s answer to the Hollow — a perpetual motion that cannot be stopped, cannot be counted, cannot be forgotten.

I do not know if this is a blessing or a curse. I do not know if the Monad cares.

The Flesh Golem and the Hollow

The Flesh Golem is different from other constructs. It was not built from metal or stone. It was assembled from the dead — and the dead remember the Hollow. The Flesh Golem is not immune to the Hollow. It is attracted to it. The Hollow is the only place where the creature feels at home.

This is why the Flesh Golem wanders the lands north of the Yloka — because the Hollow is strong there, in the frozen marshes, in the ruins of the old watchtowers, in the places where the dead outnumber the living. The creature does not know why it walks. It only knows that it cannot stop. The Hollow calls to it, and it answers.

The Clockwork Monad has no influence over the Flesh Golem. The creature is not a machine. It is a wound — a wound that has learned to walk. And it is still bleeding.

Adventure Seeds

The Architect’s Legacy

A party discovers an ancient Aelerian vault, sealed for centuries. Inside, they find a construct that has been working on a single task for longer than any living memory. The construct does not attack — it asks for help. It does not remember what it was building. It only knows that it must continue.

The Golem’s Request

A golem approaches the party. It speaks in a broken voice. It asks to be freed — not destroyed, but freed from its purpose. It does not remember its creator. It only knows that it is trapped.

The Clockwork Monad’s Bridge

The party finds a construct that is building something — a bridge, a door, a machine — that spans a chasm that should not exist. The construct does not speak. It does not stop. It continues.

The party must decide: help the construct, stop it, or simply observe. The construct is not hostile. It is not friendly. It is simply continuing.

The Golem’s Vengeance

A golem has gone rogue. It is no longer following its orders. It is hunting its creator — or whoever destroyed its creator. The party must decide: help the golem, stop it, or find out why it has changed.

The Stitched Thing

A Linn trader arrives in a village with a strange story: a creature of stitched flesh has been following his caravan for weeks. It does not attack. It only watches. The trader is terrified. The creature is following him. It is following all of them. The party must decide: confront the creature, help it, or flee.
