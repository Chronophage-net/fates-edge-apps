A Note on Two Faiths

Two gods haunt this chronicle, and neither is named often, because both are dangerous to name.

Myrmis
The older claim on this family — a goddess of bargains kept in blood, who answers prayers by taking as much as she gives, and whose favored instrument is a blade that does not choose its wielder so much as it accepts them.
Canray, called Kon'reh in the old Aeler tongue
Not a god at all but a game, a discipline, a way of thinking that the Fenwoods carried out of their exile: patience, clauses, the slow multiplication of small advantages, the belief that every debt can be resolved if you are willing to wait long enough and write it down correctly.

Myrmis is the fire that answers a scream. Canray is the ledger that answers a silence. Every figure in this chronicle chooses one, or is chosen by one, or spends their life trying to hold both at once and is torn for it. Where the histories below feel haunted — a hound sacrificed, a slate left unfinished, a blade that will not dull — that is the shape of the choice underneath. Watch for it. It does not announce itself.

A Note on Women and the Law

In both Vhasia and Viterra, women may inherit lands and designate their property to whomever they wish. A man cannot wrest land from his wife by right of marriage alone. Titles come with land and vassalage, but a landed woman may own more than her lord husband. Women warriors are rare but not unheard of. Neither Vhasia nor Viterra, however, will knight a woman. This legal paradox — the right to rule without the right to bear arms — shapes every conflict in the chronicles below. Viterra practices male-preference primogeniture for titles (equal inheritance for plain property); Vhasia practices agnatic-cognatic succession for titles, with the same equal rule for property.

The Baldvyn Dynasty — A Clarification

To avoid confusion: there are three Baldvyns in this chronicle, spanning four generations.

BaldvynIdentity
Baldvyn the Good (King Baldvyn II)Braedyn Fenwood's sworn brother and one-time bedmate, second son of King Wymund IV. Married Elianor of House Longblood, his father's widow, six months after his elder brother Harald IV's death. Father of Wyllmot. Died of a wasting stomach cancer in 834 AR.
Baldvyn "Brightstride" (King Baldvyn III)Son of Wyllmot and Queen Alaysha, grandson of Baldvyn the Good, brother of Veralyn. The beloved young king, poisoned in 838 AR.
Baldvyn MaesonA Fenwood cousin, twelve years old in 838 AR, named for the old king out of love rather than descent. Mentioned here only so no reader mistakes him for a claimant.

The tangled relationship between Baldvyn the Good and Elianor — widow of his own father, wife by his own choosing — is the seed of the Longblood Defiance and the root of the current succession crisis. Elianor's children by her first marriage (to King Wymund IV) and her second (to Baldvyn the Good) are, in the official record, cleanly divided: Valdais and his full siblings by Wymund; Wyllmot, Aryka, and Arwyn by Baldvyn. Privately, it is whispered from Charau to Coldmorrow that Baldvyn's affair with his father's young queen began well before Wymund's death, and that Valdais is Baldvyn's son in every way but the seal on his birth writ. No one alive can prove it. That uncertainty is the weapon that has been sharpened for three generations, and it is the reason Valdais Longblood has spent his life being both the rightful heir of a smiling king and the illegitimate shame of a good one, depending entirely on who is speaking.

A Note on Queen Alaysha

Queen Alaysha is Wyllmot's wife and the mother of Baldvyn Brightstride and Veralyn Demon-Eye. She is a Renard by birth — freakishly tall, even beside her husband, with the pale coloring and colorless eyes of old Vhasia — and her family's secret devotion to Myrmis would prove disastrous for the crown. She is not to be confused with Alayse Everblood, Baldvyn the Good's youngest sister, whose rape by Branton Thornhill began the Thornhill Rebellion three decades before Alaysha ever came to court; nor with Veralyn Renard, a third and separate ancestor, three generations removed, who married Harald III and won the Renards their long forbearance of Myrmis worship. Vhasians consider it an ill omen that the names recur so closely. They say Myrmis borrows names the way a moneylender reuses a ledger page — crossing out the old debtor, writing the new one in the same hand.

On the eve of her marriage to Wyllmot, Alaysha prayed to Myrmis for two great warriors and three lovely daughters, sacrificing her favorite hound on a cold altar within sight of the Belworth. She got two children, not five: a son and a daughter, both warriors, no daughters to speak of beyond Veralyn herself. She has spent every year since wondering whether the goddess shorted the bargain out of spite, or fulfilled it exactly, and simply priced a warrior-daughter as worth three ordinary ones. Her prayer set in motion the events that would claim her husband's life at Eryas and poison her son's legacy long before any assassin's hand was needed.

The Chronicle, Year by Year

790 AR Year -44

The Province War

A war between the Viterran-aligned provinces of Istovar and Aberiel and the loyalist provinces of Ethor, Adria, Ylerea, and Lence. A decisive victory for King Wymund IV “the Smiling King,” who crushed the rebels and solidified the Everblood hold on the eastern marches. It set the table for the Thornhill Rebellion fourteen years later; many of the grievances that erupted in the Longblood Defiance and the Sack of Lence were seeded here, including the enduring Everblood-Longblood rivalry over Ethor and Adria.

Key Figures: King Wymund IV (Viterra) — victorious, but exhausted; Duke Oswyn Everblood (Aberiel) — loyalist commander; the Bloods of Lence — fought for the crown; the Ethor Marchers — brought to heel.

804 AR Year -34

The Thornhill Rebellion

The Thornhills, a Linnic house in northwestern Viterra (Brigh), rose against the crown after Lord Branton Thornhill raped his ward, Alayse Everblood — the youngest sister of Wymund IV's sons. King Wymund IV died avenging the rape, falling at the Battle of Ubbin Falls. His heir, Harald IV “the Bold”, reigned exactly ten days before an arrow took his eye at the Siege of Brigh — the origin of his crueler epithet, the Tenday King. Baldvyn — the second son, the unexpected king — was crowned a fortnight later and rode out at once to storm Brigh, slaying Branton in single combat. House Thornhill was extinguished; its survivors scattered. Braedyn Fenwood, then a young knight and Baldvyn's sworn brother, caught the new king's hand as he drew a dagger to kill the Thornhill heir: “He is a child. Prune him gently, or the root will spread.” The child was spared. Thirty years later, the root would be pulled from Coldmorrow — by the mace of Baldvyn's own granddaughter.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the unexpected king; Branton Thornhill — rapist and rebel, slain in single combat; Duke Oswyn Everblood — defender of Aberiel; Logan Thornhill — the child spared, the debt that would come due.

821 AR Year -13

The Longblood Defiance

After the death of Arwyn of Adria, Queen Elianor and King Baldvyn the Good had a falling out. The queen rallied Adria, her cousins in Ethor, and the Bloods of Lence against her husband. Valdais Longblood — Elianor's son by Wymund IV, or, as the court whispered even then, by Baldvyn himself — sided with his mother; Wyllmot, the king's undisputed son, sided with his father. It ended at the Battle of Charau in a decisive victory for Baldvyn, who incarcerated his wife but pardoned Valdais — a mercy later remembered, and resented. Braedyn Fenwood, who commanded the left flank and captured Valdais himself, told the king: “A debt unpaid is a leash.” The leash held for thirteen years. After Baldvyn's death, it would snap.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the victor; Queen Elianor — the rebel, the exile; Valdais Longblood — the pardoned claimant; Wyllmot — the loyal son, the future king.

832 AR Year -6

The Sack of Lence

The Vhasian king rebuked House Fenwood for holding lands in Vhasia while refusing to pay homage. Duke Braedyn Fenwood “rebelled” — a convenient fiction — and invaded Vhasia, burning Lence to the ground; the major houses of Istovar joined him, and the Everbloods of Aberiel followed under Wymund, son of Oswyn. Lence was sacked, pillaged, the royal Blood family killed. Princess Veralyn Everblood — fifteen, dressed as a page, hidden in a supply wagon — squired for Seniro Fenwood against her father's express wishes. After the sack, she tracked Logan Thornhill — the child spared thirty years before — to a village in the royal domain: Coldmorrow. He wounded her cousin Lyonel; she killed him with a mace, and burned the village in her rage. She was sixteen. Logan's own infant daughter, Ylena, survived; raised by strangers, trained as a courtesan, she would return to Veralyn's own court a generation later, wearing another woman's household as a disguise.

Key Figures: Duke Braedyn Fenwood — architect of the invasion; Seniro Fenwood — Veralyn's mentor and protector; Princess Veralyn Everblood — the young killer; Lyonel Vayle — the wounded cousin; Logan Thornhill — the debt paid in blood.

834 AR Year -4

The Death of King Baldvyn the Good

King Baldvyn the Good, feeling himself dying, pardoned Queen Elianor, ending her exile. Before he died he threw a grand tourney — a final celebration of chivalry. Princess Veralyn, though not a knight, was permitted to compete: she unhorsed Sir Arin Tymber, Sir Lerris Fenwood, and Sir Ouen Longblood (the last insulted her; she killed his horse in the tilt), was defeated by her friend and cousin Sir Tylan Vayle, who was then defeated by her brother, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride. The old king watched and wept — not from sadness, but from pride.

836 AR Year -2

The Seven Week Uprising

A fanatical sect of Myrmis worshippers rose against King Wyllmot “the Warrior,” assassinating nobles in retaliation for the destruction of their holy sites. Wyllmot besieged their mountain fortress, Eryas Citadel, and died attempting to storm it. Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn Demoneye crushed the rebellion in seven weeks — Veralyn, fighting the sect's champion, her distant cousin Gelfrey Renard, disarmed and killed him on an altar to Myrmis with his own dagger. A blade appeared beside the altar afterward that no one remembers placing there: perfectly balanced, unmarked, edge unchipped. She has not set it down since. The court calls it Shadowsteel. Many Renards were secretly Myrmis worshippers, including Queen Alaysha; some at court presume she engineered the Uprising herself, to appease a goddess she felt had been shorted. There is no proof, only the arithmetic of who benefited and who did not sleep for seven weeks.

Key Figures: King Wyllmot — killed at Eryas; Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn — who crushed the rebellion; Gelfrey Renard — the Shadow-Court's champion, slain on the altar; Queen Alaysha — whose secret faith was exposed.

837 AR Year -1

The Incident of Aleric

Arwyn's son, Aleric, was removed from the Viterran line of succession for plotting against his cousin, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride, with his aunt Aryka. Both were exiled to Acasia — not the Petty Kingdoms as some later, garbled retellings claim, a distinction that matters, because Acasia keeps its own account of exiles, and its own opinions about what Viterra owes them. Aleric's claim was broken, but he did not forget.

Key Figures: Prince Baldvyn Brightstride — the intended victim; Aleric Renard — the exiled claimant; Aryka Renard — the conspirator.

838 AR Year 0

The Death of Baldvyn Brightstride

Prince Baldvyn Brightstride fell ill and died suddenly; the kingdom mourned the loss of their beloved young king. He was suspected of being poisoned — likely by someone seeking revenge for Lence or the Seven Week Uprising. The poison was subtle, slow-acting, and untraceable, administered weeks before symptoms appeared. The prime suspect was Jaennis Fenwood, his young queen, Braedyn's granddaughter — small and waifish, married to Baldvyn barely a season before his death, rumored to have taken abortifacients in secret rather than bear his child. The evidence was circumstantial, and the accusation would have meant war with the Fenwoods. No one spoke the suspicion aloud.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn Brightstride — the beloved, the poisoned; Queen Jaennis Fenwood — the suspected poisoner; Princess Veralyn Demoneye — the heir, the warrior.

The Current Crisis (838 AR)

Veralyn is the acknowledged Duchess of Aberiel and the named heir of King Baldvyn Brightstride. Her grandmother, Elianor, and her uncle — or half-brother — Valdais seek to expel the Everbloods from Vhasia. House Fenwood is propping her up. Her cousin Aleric, exiled to Acasia, presses his claim as the eldest male of the major line. Adria and Ethor are for Valdais; Aberiel and Lence are for Veralyn; Istovar is split.

Veralyn is unsure of being anything more than a killer. Her challenges: learning to be a leader, not just a soldier; learning diplomacy, the art of the table rather than the blade; learning that a ruler has no friends, only allies and debts; learning who to trust, and knowing everyone has a price; carrying the trauma of her father's rejection and the Temple's misogyny; confronting the self-hatred for what she did at Coldmorrow.

A Note on What Follows

This chronicle ends at the open wound of 838 AR, with the crown unclaimed and the factions still circling. It does not resolve here. Within the Demon's Eye picks up the thread and carries it through Veralyn's coronation as Queen of Viterra, and on to Duke Braedyn Fenwood's death shortly after, in 840 AR — a death recorded in his own words in The Old Duke's Wars. Two much older Fenwood adventures reach back further still. The Reckoning Bridge, set two centuries earlier (640 AR), records the Linns' arrival at the Tarlington Gap and the founding oaths first sworn between the Fenwoods and the Everbloods-to-be. The Exile Road, its own prequel, is set a further three centuries back (337 AR) — a full century before the empire's own final fall, when the Valvano cadet branch first fled Acasia and the Tarling dux who sheltered them was only beginning to style himself King of Viterra. The names recur. The debts compound. That is the point.

GM Guidance: The Shadow of the Past

Every event in this timeline is a thread the party may pull. Consider player characters as: survivors of Lence seeking justice or vengeance; Fenwood loyalists who have seen too much; Veralyn's companions, the only people she trusts; agents of the Shadow-Court manipulating the crisis for Myrmis's ends; Temple inquisitors seeking to expose the heresy that brought the crown low; or Acasian exiles, allies of Aleric Renard.

The timeline is not a cage; it is a map of debts. Every event is a debt compounding interest. The party may find the interest unpaid and still growing, or find the principal, and decide what to do with it. Two threads run under all of it: the goddess who answers prayers by taking as much as she gives, and the discipline of clauses and patience that the Fenwoods learned in exile. A campaign built on this chronicle should let the party feel both pulling at once — the blade that wants blood, and the ledger that wants time.
