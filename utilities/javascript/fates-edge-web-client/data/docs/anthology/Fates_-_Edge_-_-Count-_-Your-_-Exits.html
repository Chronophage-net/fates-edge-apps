Count Your Exits

A Novella of the Belworth

Being an account of Veralyn Everblood, called Demoneye and Blackrose, from her eleventh year to her coronation; reconstructed from two folders of notation, a tourney roll, and the things people were willing to say afterward.

Third of three. A book of the Belworth.

Every ounce of gold is an ounce of grain. Opportunity is always cost.

— Sgt. Rekedim Many-Scars, Canray by Convoy

A note on the sources. This was put together twenty years after the last event in it, out of Rekedim Many-Scars's papers, which came to the crown in nine boxes and are the fullest record of a single player's development in either kingdom; the tourney roll of 834; the Parlement's own minute of Lavius 840; a Fenwood clerk's inventory taken in the ash at Coldmorrow in 832; and the recollections of about thirty people, of whom eleven were principals and four would not speak.

Which is why some rooms in this book are reported to the minute and others are not reported at all. Where a scene is given whole, somebody who was in it said so. Where a thing is attributed to a person's mind, a reader should assume the reconstruction and discount accordingly. Two of the four who would not speak are still living.

A note on the board. Kon'reh is the game of encirclement and escape. The Ykrul play it before they learn to hunt and the Vhas play it badly and the Viterrans mostly do not play it at all, which is one of the several reasons Viterra keeps losing arguments to people it has beaten in the field.

It has pieces and it has a ring and it has four squares in the middle that everyone wants and that are worth less than they look. Its whole literature comes down to three sentences that a steppe child has by six years old: count your exits before you enter; never commit to a single path; an ally untested is a blade without a sheath.

The last of those is about people, and it is the one this book is about, and it is the one the Fenwoods have built a duchy on.

There is a fourth sentence, but it is not in the literature, because it is not a move. It has no notation. Two people in this account are known to have played it and one of them wrote afterward, in a manual, in ink, that it must never be done.

MOVEMENT IMobilization

Camp is a board. Count roads, not bread.

Tarlington, in the Belworth. 828–830 AR.

i.

She came up the Belworth road in Octis with nine in the party, a led horse, a wooden sword she had been told to leave at Valora, and instructions from her father that had been read aloud to the Duke of the Belworth's steward and that amounted to: keep her, correct her, and do not indulge the nonsense.

She was eleven. She had been told it was a punishment. Everybody had been told it was a punishment; her father had said so at his own table in front of forty people, in the voice he used when he had decided a thing and wanted the deciding witnessed.

Braedyn Fenwood read the letter standing in his own yard with his hat still on, took about four seconds over it, and understood exactly what it was.

He said nothing whatever. A Fenwood does not.

Tarlington is not a castle. That is the first thing everyone gets wrong about the Fenwoods and they have never corrected anybody, because a house that is underestimated for four hundred years does not start correcting people in the fifth century.

It is a seat: a great sprawling terraced thing on the last rise above the Fens, with four courts and a counting-house bigger than the chapel, and vineyards on the south slope, and below it four hundred square miles of drained black ground that was marsh when the Valvano came out of Ecktoria in their wagons and is now the richest farmland on either bank of the river. There is a canal. There are eleven miles of dyke maintained by a schedule that is two hundred years old and has never once been allowed to lapse. There is a bell that is rung when the water is wrong, and it has been rung nine times in forty years, and every time, within an hour, the duke has been standing on the dyke in the rain with his sleeves rolled.

The Fenwoods are the richest house in Viterra and they came from farmers and they will tell you so before you have got your cloak off.

A man came out into the yard on her second morning to watch the new one at the pells, because that is what everybody did, and stood at the rail with his arms folded and said nothing for about an hour and eleven minutes. Steppe face, steppe hands. Nobody told her who he was and she did not ask, because she was eleven and he was plainly nobody: no chain, no colours, no office she could see.

On the third morning he came to the rail again and this time, at the end, he said:

"You are not slow."

Veralyn, who was eleven, and who had been told she was very good by everybody at Valora for four years, said, "Thank you."

"That was not praise," said Rekedim. "It is a measurement. You are not slow, you are strong for your frame, and your left hand does not know what your right is doing, which will be an advantage until you meet somebody who has noticed. Come and sit down."

And he took her into the long room off the counting-house where there was a board, and he did not let her touch a piece for eleven days.

She did not learn his name for a fortnight.

ii.

There were four wards at Tarlington in her first year and she did not understand for a long time that they were a set.

A house, four wants, and nine years. He had never in his life acquired anything he could not have explained as a kindness.

Jaennis Fenwood was nine, and the duke's granddaughter, and small, and had been ill so often that her household had stopped reporting it upward. She had a face that made people carry things for her. She read enormously. She was in the muniment room more than the yard and she was in the yard more than anybody expected.

Tylan Vayle was fourteen and the second son of a house that had been nothing a generation before and was now a county, which is a sentence that sounds like good fortune and is in fact a description of a man standing on ice. He was already the size of a grown man and already better than the master-at-arms wanted to admit, and he was the most prepared person Veralyn had ever met — he carried a spare bowstring at eleven; he knew where the keys were; he had never in his life walked into a room he had not first worked out the shape of. She took it for a temperament for about six years.

Elyas Everblood was twelve and was everybody's brother inside a fortnight. He was the youngest son of the Duke of Istovar. He was funny, and generous, and would carry your things, and got a laugh out of a room the way his father had at the same age; and he was a hostage, and every adult in that building knew it, and not one of them ever said the word.

Oswyn Everblood's armies sat across the Belworth from the Fens. They had sat there for thirty years. They were not aimed at anybody and Oswyn had never in his life broken a word — and Braedyn Fenwood, who had watched an entire generation of that family and had buried most of it, held the man's youngest son in his house and fed him well and had him taught, and the arrangement was called a wardship because that is what it is called.

Elyas knew. He had known since he was nine. And he had decided, at nine, with the whole of his considerable intelligence, that the correct response to being collateral was to become the single most beloved person in the building — and he had done it, and it had worked, and it was going to go on working for another twenty-five years, and he never once let it show except twice, and the second time was at the end of this book.

iii.

The man at the rail was called Rekedim, and he was not a ward and was not a servant and had no office anybody could name, and he was the most consequential person in Veralyn Everblood's life.

He was eight-and-twenty. He had the steppe face and the steppe hands and not one word of the steppe language, because he had been born in the Fens.

His father had been a war-leader of the Ykrul who came down on the wrong side of Kargath — Kargath the Last Blade, the one who ate the east for nine years and whom Braedyn Fenwood and Baldvyn Everblood had met at twenty as two second sons in the Black Banners and had never afterward discussed. Rekedim's people had lost, and run, and come four hundred miles west with what they could carry; and a young Fenwood who had seen them from the other side of a river had taken them in and put them on ground he had drained himself.

Rekedim was born in the third year after that. He grew up on Fenwood ground eating Fenwood grain and went for a soldier at seventeen into the same company his patron had served in, and came back at six-and-twenty a sergeant of the Red Bill Cadre with the scars that gave him the only name anybody uses.

He was Elyas Everblood's bodyguard. That was his office. It is written in the household roll and it was true.

It was also the neatest single arrangement Braedyn Fenwood ever made, and it took Veralyn nine years to see it: the Duke of the Belworth had put a permanent armed man at the shoulder of his most valuable hostage, and the man was somebody who owed the Fenwoods his family's continued existence — which meant Elyas was protected absolutely, and watched absolutely, by the same body, forever, and neither of them could ever raise it, and both of them liked each other enormously.

That is Fenwood work. That is what they do. They do not build a wall and a gate; they build one thing that is both, and then they are generous about it for thirty years.

iv.

He gave her the names before he gave her the rules, and she did not understand for six years why he bothered.

Kon'reh is the steppe's, and the steppe set the first board. It is played in dirt with pebbles and sheep-bones. It is given to children before they are permitted to hunt. And it has produced, in six hundred years, the only body of doctrine any of them possesses that was written down by people who die of being wrong.

The Aelers have it on slate in the under-vaults and call it K'thra, and do not, on the whole, play it for pleasure. They arbitrate with it. Two factors with a disputed toll sit down over a board and are finished by evening, and the settlement binds, and each of them walks out having agreed to a thing he would not have signed at a table — which is either the most civilised procedure in the world or the most alarming, and Rekedim, asked which, said that he had been in the under-vaults twice and had not cared for the light.

The Empire collected it. Aqyl of Thepyrgos went up and down the Amaranthine in the year 538 writing down every form of the game he could find and every people who played it, and the thing appears in the Vhasian rolls as the Corpus Canré; and fifteen years after he finished it, it was banned as contrary to the health of the empire. Set that beside the book itself, which is a book about counting the ways out of a position. An empire looked at that and issued an instrument. Vhasia has what survived, and has the court word out of it — Canray — which is what a river house means when it says that two men are playing.

The Vilikari call it Kanry and carry it on the road, where the object is not to win the game but to be the only man at the table who knows that one is in progress.

And in Dhahara they call it Kanrāy, and there is a line in a caravan copy that Jaennis found at thirteen and brought down to the long room and read out:

I asked my master, "Who created Kanrāy?" He smiled. "Who created the stones with which we play? Who created the river that serenades us, the sun that shines upon us, the birds who are our audience?" He gathered the stones into his pouch. "Who invented the world?"

She wanted to know what it meant.

Rekedim said, "That is not a question about the game," and set the pieces, and would not be moved — not that day, and not in the sixteen years afterward, in which she asked him three more times, because she was Jaennis and that is what she did with a closed door. Tylan wrote the passage into the back of his own sheets anyway. He wrote down everything nobody would explain. He had done it since he was fourteen and he never stopped.

So: five names, and a dozen more that do not matter, and Rekedim Many-Scars had all of them and standing in none. A Ykrul who had never seen the steppe. Taught by a father who had lost. Living out his life on drained ground belonging to a house that files things, teaching four Vhasian children in the Vhasian word. When he set his own manual down at four-and-thirty he put Canray on the cover, because it was for Vhasian soldiers, and he was an honest man about audiences.

He had not one word of the steppe language.

He had one. It was not a name for the game. It was the name for a particular kind of position, and he said it aloud in front of her exactly once, and she was eleven, and she did not ask.

By the time she was old enough to ask, she was old enough not to.

What he taught her, for nine years, can be written on one side of a sheet, and he did in fact write it on one side of a sheet, eight years later, in a field, for soldiers.

Count to five. Every plan is a Reforge plan. If taking a thing today cannot be held for five of their turns, it is not a victory, it is a trap you have laid for yourself and walked into first.

Own the ring. The four squares in the middle are a lever. The ring is the road. Boys want the middle. Quartermasters want the ring. The middle is where you are seen; the ring is where you are supplied, and a man who is seen and not supplied is a story.

Weld in two plies. One piece to close the file, one piece to print a fork two moves ahead. Do that twice and a runner drowns.

Seed is a depot, not a daring.

Exits before enemies. A closed line cuts on their turn, which is the half of the game that beginners never see. Count exits. Spend captures only where exits survive.

And respect stagger — which is the tenet nobody remembers, and which means: a piece that has just spent its second special is rooted, and if that piece is yours you planned badly, and if it is theirs you planned well, and the whole difference between the two sentences is whether you did the counting before or after.

She lost every game for two years.

She has said since that those two years were the happiest of her childhood and everyone assumes she is being wry.

v.

He was not kind to her and he was not unkind; he was the first adult in her life who was simply indifferent to her rank, and she was eleven, and it went through her like weather.

At Valora she had been a problem. At Valora she had been a girl who wanted the pells, which her father could not look at, and a girl with her mother's face, which he could look at even less; and the whole apparatus of that court had been organised, kindly and constantly, around managing her.

At Tarlington a Ykrul sergeant with no lands and no name beyond a nickname looked at her across a board and said that is a bad move and here is why in exactly the tone he used on Tylan and on Elyas and on a corporal, and did not adjust it by one degree in nine years, and never once in that time asked her how she was.

She would have followed him into the sea.

vi.

The first lesson he ever gave her he gave secondhand, and he told her so, which is the other reason she loved him.

She had asked him — at twelve, over a board, losing — who had taught him.

"A supply sergeant," said Rekedim. "Vhasian. Dead now. I was seventeen and I had two hundred men's bread to move on one road and I was very frightened and very loud about it."

"What did he say?"

"He said: camp is a board. Count roads, not bread." Rekedim moved a piece and took something of hers that she had not been looking at. "Then he taught me the whole of it in about a quarter of an hour, under a canvas awning, on oat-sacks, with chalk on his knuckles. I led a piece to brush the middle and he grunted at me. That is a cut — he said — now do not chase. Fork the far mouth. Two exits, both priced."

"And then?"

"And then," said Rekedim, "my hand went to the piece that wins games, and he caught my wrist."

He looked up.

"Rent the middle," he said. "Do not buy it."

She was twelve. She did not understand it, and she said so, and he said that was correct and that she would understand it at about twenty and that he would remind her.

He reminded her twice. The second time was the last conversation they had before the end of this book.

vii.

And they were happy.

Not in an idyll and not without quarrels; Tylan and Elyas did not speak for a fortnight in the second year over something neither of them would later admit the substance of, and Jaennis could be cold in a way that took two days to notice and four to forgive. But for nine years, on ground that had been marsh and was now the richest in Viterra, four children who each had a reason to be frightened were put in one house and given horses and books and a Ykrul with a board, and they were happy, and every one of them knew it at the time, which is rare.

They had names for each other that nobody outside used. They had a place on the dyke. They stole wine at fourteen and were caught by Rekedim, who confiscated it, drank it himself in front of them over the following hour while explaining the correct way to steal wine, and then gave them a game each and beat all four in under forty moves.

Veralyn Everblood was eleven when she came to Tarlington and twenty when she left it for the last time, and she has never in her life been at home anywhere else.

That is the position at the start. Four pieces, well placed, on good ground, with a road behind them.

Count the exits.

MOVEMENT IIThe Seed

Seed is a depot, not a daring. Seed only when the index is safe.

Tarlington. 830–836 AR.

i.

Jaennis Fenwood was nine when Veralyn came and she was the only one of the four who had not been sent.

She lived there. Her father was the duke's heir and her father's foot had been born turned in, and Braedyn Fenwood had straightened it himself out of a Thepyrgan text when four masters said it could not be done, and Conlin Fenwood walked with a cane for the rest of his life and ran the duchy better than anybody but his father. Jaennis grew up in that house among people who took a difficulty as a specification.

She was ill a great deal as a child. Fevers; a thing in the chest at six that nearly had her; a whole winter at seven when she was carried everywhere. By nine the household had stopped reporting it upward, in the way households do, and by eleven she had understood the mechanism of that and had begun using it, and by thirteen she was the only person at Tarlington who could disappear for four days and have nobody look.

She was not a liar. It is important to be exact. In nineteen years nobody has produced a lie of hers. She is simply the finest living practitioner of a thing that is not lying, which is the arrangement of a room such that the true thing you are about to say will be understood as something else.

Her grandfather saw it in her at about twelve and never told her so, and never told anybody else, and made one remark on the subject in his life, to his son, at a window, which Conlin wrote down:

"I have been given dutiful sons and clever daughters, and I am grateful. And I have been given one who is neither, and she is the best of us, and God help whoever is standing next to her when she finds out what she is for."

ii.

They were sisters first and it was real.

That has to be said flatly and defended, because everything afterward has been read backward by people with an interest, and because the alternative reading — that it was strategy from the first afternoon — is both more convenient and wrong.

A sickly girl of nine and a hard-staring girl of eleven were put in the same wing of the same house and the older one could not sit still and the younger one could not go out. So the older one brought things in. Frogs, at first, and then, when that was discouraged, news; and then, when the news ran out, the games.

Veralyn taught Jaennis Kon'reh in the second year because she wanted somebody to lose to her and Elyas would not take it seriously and Tylan would not lose.

Jaennis beat her in eleven weeks and then, quite deliberately, stopped beating her for about a year and a half.

Nobody knows this. There is no record of it anywhere. It is in this book because Jaennis Fenwood told one person, at thirty, in a bad hour, and that person wrote it down; and it is the earliest dated instance in the whole of her life of the thing she does.

iii.

At thirteen Jaennis Fenwood understood two facts about herself and put them together.

The first was that she had been ill since infancy and was small and narrow and that the women of that household talked, over her head, as women do, about who was built for what.

The second was that she was the granddaughter of a duke in a house that had doubled its holdings in two generations through marriages, and that her entire value — her whole value, the only column she appeared in — was as a carrier.

She did the arithmetic at thirteen and arrived at a certainty that has never left her: that the thing she was for would kill her.

She has never said this aloud to a living soul. She said it once to a physician in Faemley, at sixteen, in the guise of asking about a cousin; and what she took away from the interview was not comfort but a list, and she has been quietly, carefully, expensively obtaining items from that list for eleven years.

That is the secret. It is not adultery and it is not murder and it is not treason. It is a small locked box of dried preparations, and the certain knowledge that if it were ever opened in front of her grandfather she would go from being the cleverest asset in the Belworth to being a defective one overnight.

Every single thing Jaennis Fenwood has ever done has been done by a woman standing on that box.

iv.

The lane opened over four years.

It went like this.

At fourteen and twelve they shared a bed in winter because the east wing was cold and everybody in that house did, and nobody thought about it, including them.

At fifteen and thirteen Jaennis began being unwell in a particular way — not falsely; she was unwell; she was simply unwell on days that were arranged — and Veralyn began sitting with her, which she had not done before, and which she was extremely bad at, and which Jaennis praised her for.

At sixteen and fourteen there was a quarrel, the only real one, about nothing — about a horse — which ran nine days and ended when Jaennis came out to the yard in the rain, which she was not allowed to do and had not done in four years, and said one sentence that Veralyn has never repeated to anybody and has never forgotten.

At seventeen and fifteen Veralyn went to Lence in a wagon and came back with a village behind her, and she did not speak for eleven days, and Jaennis was the only person she let into the room.

And at eighteen and sixteen, in the spring, it was something else, and neither of them could have told you which week.

The house does not manufacture. It arranges, and the material it arranges is generally real.

Veralyn Everblood experienced the whole of it as having been chosen.

She has never, in twelve years, asked who opened the lane.

v.

Rekedim knew.

He knew by the third year and he said nothing for six, because it was not his office, and because he had one rule about the four of them that he never broke, which was that he would teach them the board and he would not play them.

He broke it once and it was not about this.

But there is a moment, recorded in nobody's account except his own notation, which he kept the way he kept everything, and which survives because his papers came to the crown with the rest.

In the sixth year he set Veralyn a problem. It is on the sheet in his hand and the sheet is dated. It is a standard tightening position, four moves from a weld, of the kind she could do in her head by then.

And in the margin, beside her solution, which is correct and fast and brutal and takes the far mouth in three, he has written four words.

She never checks behind.

vi.

Brightstride knew too, eventually, and later, and the book will come to it. He was her brother and they were strangers until they were twenty and he worked it out inside a month of having her at court, because he was not clever but he was decent, which is a different instrument and sometimes a sharper one.

And he decided that a rumour is a thing that only exists once somebody official has looked at it, and that he was the only official person in the kingdom, and that he was therefore not going to look.

He held to that for two years and it cost him more than anybody knew, and it is on the short list of things that killed him.

vii.

One more thing about the Seed, and then the book will go to Lence.

In the tenets it is the fourth, and it is the one that sounds least like the others, because it is not a warning against the enemy. It is a warning against yourself.

Seed is a depot, not a daring. You put a thing down on the board because you will need supply there later, when the fighting has moved, and the whole discipline of it is that you must do it on a quiet turn, when nothing is happening, when it is not exciting, when you have checked the index and your enemy's strongest piece is two moves off and out of tricks.

If you plant because it feels bold, it is not a depot. It is a burned wagon nine turns from now.

Rekedim taught that to four children in a long room off a counting-house and every one of them learned it, and three of them applied it to the board only.

Jaennis Fenwood applied it to a person, at nine years old, without being taught, and checked the index every single time for eleven years.

She has never once planted on a loud turn.

MOVEMENT IIIThe Lane

Own the ring. The four squares are a lever; the ring is the road.

Lence, and a village in the royal domain. 832 AR.

i.

The Sack of Lence is taught in two ways and both of them are wrong.

In Viterra it is taught as an outrage that got out of hand: a proud house insulted, a quarrel over homage, a punitive ride that went further than anybody meant. In Vhasia it is taught as the end of the world, which is closer, and is also a description of the weather rather than of the cause.

It was a Kon'reh position, and it was played by one man over thirty years, and the only thing about it that was not planned was the mirrors.

Set it out the way Rekedim set it out afterward for four students, on a board, in a long room, in the winter of the same year. He used the actual pieces. It took him an hour and eleven minutes and nobody interrupted.

The ring first. For thirty years the Fenwoods had not fought anybody. They had bought crossings. They had married Tirens and Ubrals and one Silkstrand girl with a shipping interest. They had taken the maintenance of roads nobody wanted the maintenance of, and had then, quietly, been the only people who knew where those roads went. By 830 you could not move a wagon from Aeler to the Vhasian grain country without paying a Fenwood twice, and half the men who paid did not know whom they were paying, because the Fenwoods had never once put their name on a toll-board.

The ring is the road. That is not a metaphor in the Belworth. It is a description of an account.

Then the lever. The Vhasian crown — which was in the last useful decade of the Sun Court and did not know it — rebuked House Fenwood for holding land in Vhasia and declining homage. The rebuke was correct. That is the second time in this trilogy that a crown has been entirely right about a tenure and has come off worst for it, and it will not be the last.

Braedyn Fenwood had been waiting for that rebuke, in the specific sense that he had arranged the circumstances that made it inevitable and had then declined, for six years, to make it easy to withdraw.

Then the weld, in two plies. Istovar's great houses came in — they had been in the ring a decade and they were bought, and there is no shame in that and no mystery about it either.

And then Istovar's duke came in, which is a different thing entirely. Oswyn Everblood did not march; he was sixty and had renounced a kingdom to be left alone. He sent his eldest. Wymund Everblood, son of Oswyn, five-and-thirty, took the Istovar levy across on his father's word, and made the second ply, and was the piece nobody had counted, and was the one that made the position unanswerable.

He came because Braedyn Fenwood had spent thirty years being the best friend that family ever had and had never once asked them for anything — which is what that sentence is for, and which is what it costs when it is finally spent.

Wymund Everblood died at the Ford of Ashes. He was not the only man who did. He is the only one of them this book has to keep returning to, because eight years later his father declined to move nine thousand men off a road and nobody could establish why, and the answer was standing in a stall at Charel with a name on it.

And King Baldvyn did not call the banners of Viterra.

He was four-and-sixty and dying of a thing in his stomach and he had loved Braedyn Fenwood since they were two second sons in the Black Banners with the same scar across the same knuckle, and he did not call the banners, and he has never been forgiven for it in Vhasia and never been asked about it in Viterra.

Then they killed the Blue.

The Ford of Ashes, and the Sun King's army ground into the mud, and Lence, and the royal family dead — the last Blood King and every known cousin — and the great hall burning so hot that the mirrors ran down the walls and made the one image out of that year that everybody has.

"Now," said Rekedim, to four students, in the winter, with the pieces where he had put them. "What is the mistake?"

Tylan said there wasn't one.

"There is always one," said Rekedim. "Tenet the first."

And Jaennis, who was thirteen, said: "He cannot hold it for five."

"Go on."

"He has killed their Blue and he has no claim," she said. "He does not want their throne and he has not got a man who does. So the board reforges, and it reforges around a hole, and everybody with a banner runs for the shortest lane at once." She put one finger on the board without moving anything. "He has not won Vhasia. He has opened it. And he is two-and-sixty."

Rekedim looked at her for a moment.

"That is correct," he said, "and it is not a mistake, and you will understand the difference when you are older." He began putting the pieces back in the box. "He is not playing to hold. He is playing to reforge, because the board as it stood could not be won and he knew that at thirty. He has taken a game he was losing slowly and turned it into a game where nobody is winning. Then he will be the best-supplied man in the wreck."

"That is monstrous," said Elyas, cheerfully.

"It is quartermastery," said Rekedim. "Which is monstrous. Yes."

ii.

Veralyn was not in the long room for that lesson. She was, but she was not; she had come back nine weeks before and she sat at the end with her hands in her lap and did not say one word for the whole hour and eleven minutes, and Rekedim did not ask her to.

Because she had been there.

iii.

She went in a supply wagon. She was fifteen. She dressed as a page and she went with the Fenwood train because the Fenwood train was the only train anybody at Tarlington could get into, and she squired for Wymund the Red, who had known exactly who she was from the first morning and had taken one look at her and said nothing whatever, because he is his father's son.

Lyonel Vayle went with her, squiring for Braedyn the Younger, and Lyonel Vayle went because Lyonel Vayle would go anywhere, loudly.

The marshal of that army was the duke's second son, who is entered in every roll ever drawn as Seniro Fenwood and who has not been called it by a soldier since Lence. The army calls him Wymund the Red. The man whose name that was is buried at the Ford of Ashes, and nobody in either family has ever explained the borrowing, and nobody has ever objected to it either.

Her father had expressly forbidden it. That is in three chronicles and it is true and it is also not the reason she went.

She went because she was fifteen and had been drilled for four years by three masters and had never once been allowed to find out whether any of it was real, and because everybody she loved was going, and because the alternative was a winter at Tarlington being the girl who had not gone.

There is no more mystery in it than that. The mystery is all afterward.

iv.

The sack is not in this book.

That is a decision and it is worth stating. There are four hundred pages of Lence in the other sources and every one of them is a man describing a city he was in for nine days, and there is nothing this novella can add to the burning of a hall except one more hand.

What is in this book is what a girl of fifteen did with what she saw, and the answer is that for the first six days she did it very well.

She was steady. She carried what she was told to carry. She was in the second rank at the Ford and was not blooded there and was not disappointed about it, which Wymund the Red remarked on afterward, twice, because it is not what fifteen usually does. She held horses. On the fourth night she sat up with a man of the Red Bill who was dying slowly of a gut wound and did it properly, without talking, for six hours, and he was forty and she was fifteen and she has never mentioned it to anybody.

And on the seventh day the city went, and she was in it, and she came out of it at the end of the ninth day.

She is reported to have killed two men in Lence. She has never confirmed or denied that and the book will leave it where she left it.

v.

Coldmorrow was a fortnight later and four days' ride, in the royal domain, and it was not part of the war.

Somebody at the camp fire said a name.

That is all it took. It was not a Fenwood captain, though the pamphlets have said so for twenty years.

It was a scout called Riarhin, who had found the village herself four days earlier doing the work she was paid to do, and had reported it where she was meant to report it — to the marshal, in the marshal's tent, to one man and nobody else. She had done the thing correctly. Then there was a sack, and a fire, and she was very drunk, and she said, in the way people do after a sack when they are telling each other who is who, that the last of the Thornhills was alive, and was a cooper, and was in a village in the royal domain about four days off, and that the old king had spared him in a yard at Brigh as an infant with his dagger already out.

She did not know the girl was at that fire. Nine people have sworn to it and there is no reason to doubt any of them, and it makes no difference, and she has never once offered it in her own defence.

She is in the rolls for eleven years after and then she is not. She carried a blade she never drew in anybody's hearing and would not discuss. The men who served with her say she was the best scout in that army and the worst company after dark, and that those were the same fact.

They also put her at six-and-twenty, every one of them, in every deposition, for eleven years running. She was seven-and-fifty at Lence. She is Lethai-al on her mother's side out of Silkstrand and the army did not know it and would not have cared, and it means she was a grown woman with a trade before Braedyn Fenwood was born, and it means she is almost certainly still alive, and it means that when this book says she has never offered it in her own defence, the tense is not a literary one.

He is a child. Prune him gently, or the root will spread.

Veralyn Everblood was fifteen years old and had been taught for four years by a Ykrul logician that an account left open is a liability, and she had been taught for eleven years before that, at her father's court, by everybody, that the Thornhills had killed her family's kings.

She took nine men and she went.

Logan Thornhill was a cooper of about five-and-thirty. He had a wife and a daughter of two. He had never in his life done anything to anybody. He had been spared in a yard before he could hold his head up, by a king, in front of four thousand people, as an act of mercy that the whole of Viterra could still quote.

He fought badly and with everything he had, which is the only thing about him that anybody wrote down.

He wounded Lyonel Vayle, who was seventeen and was showing off and had not counted anything.

And Veralyn Everblood killed him with a mace, in single combat, in front of nine men, and it was clean and it was fast and she has always been able to describe it.

And then she burned the village.

The whole of it. Eleven houses, a barn, a rope-walk and a mill. It took most of the afternoon. The people got out; there is no account anywhere of a death at Coldmorrow other than Logan's, and men who were there have said so under oath four separate times, and the book accepts it.

But she burned it, at fifteen, for no reason that any of the nine men could afterward give, and one of them — the youngest, who was nineteen and who wept when he was asked about it years later — said the only thing anybody has ever said about her state:

"She was not in a rage. Everyone says she was in a rage. She was giving orders."

vi.

She cannot remember it.

This is the fact the whole of the rest of this book turns on and the book is going to be exact about it, because the pamphlets have made it a wickedness and the apologists have made it a swoon and it is neither.

She can remember Lence. She can remember the man on the fourth night and the gut wound and what he asked for. She can remember the ride, and the camp fire, and the name being said. She can remember Logan Thornhill's face and his hands and the way he came at her and exactly what she did and how long it took and what it sounded like, and she has described that, in daylight, to three different people, without a tremor.

And then there is the afternoon, and there is nothing in it.

Not a blank that frightens her. That would be manageable. What is in the place where the afternoon should be is a sentence, and it is always the same sentence, and it is not in her own cadence, and she has produced it verbatim for eight years whenever anybody raises the name:

"Seems I do recall Coldmorrow. It was a village near Lence. We burned it."

Seems. We.

She is the most direct person in two kingdoms. She has never in her life used seems about anything else, and she has never in her life hidden behind a plural.

Somebody at some point wrote her a line, or she wrote it for herself at fifteen in a wagon on the way home, and she has been reading it off ever since, and she does not know she is reading.

vii.

She came back to Tarlington in the autumn and did not speak for eleven days.

Her father sent a letter. It is the coldest document in a family that produces cold documents and it does not mention Coldmorrow at all, which is the cruellest thing in it, because it means he had heard and had decided the offence was the disobedience.

Braedyn Fenwood said nothing. Rekedim said nothing. Tylan Vayle, who was eighteen and had not been allowed to go and had been quietly furious about it for four months, took one look at her in the yard and went and sat on the dyke with her for an entire afternoon without saying a word, which is the single most useful thing anybody did for her that year and which she has never forgotten and has never repaid.

Elyas made her laugh on the fourth day. It took him four days, which for Elyas Everblood is a catastrophic failure, and he was frightened by it, and he went away and worked at it and came back and managed it.

And Jaennis Fenwood was the only person she let into the room.

For eleven days, in the east wing, with the door shut. And whatever was said in there has never been reported by either of them.

But this is what it did, and Jaennis did not plan it and could not have and has never claimed otherwise:

It made her the safe place. Permanently. At the exact hour when a girl of fifteen came home with an afternoon missing out of her head, one person was inside the room and everybody else was outside it, and that arrangement has never since been altered by anything, including everything.

Seed is a depot, not a daring. You do not plant on a loud turn.

It was the loudest turn of her life, and Jaennis Fenwood did not plant anything at all, and that is precisely why it held.

Veralyn was seventeen and had not been to court in six years.

She asked to enter. She did it correctly: through the Earl Marshal, in writing, at the proper interval, in a letter Rekedim made her rewrite twice, the second time for being too clever.

The Earl Marshal took it to the king because there was no rule to apply. The rule had never been written because the situation had never arisen, and a kingdom which has not written a rule has two choices, and Viterra's Parlement would have taken nine months over it.

Baldvyn Everblood did not need to think about it.

Nine people in that room assumed he was being indulgent with his granddaughter. He wanted to find out. That is all. He had heard about the girl for six years from a duke who does not exaggerate, and he was dying, and he wanted a good week, and the best entertainment available to a dying king is to open a door and see what comes through it.

ii.

She unhorsed Sir Arin Tymber, who was a good lance and twenty-four and took it beautifully and has dined on it for six years.

She unhorsed Sir Lerris Fenwood, who was bright and charming and shallow and who expected little and of whom little was expected, and who afterward described the experience in a phrase that got around: "I did not lose to a woman. I lost to a Fenwood education, which is worse, because I had one."

And she unhorsed Sir Ouen Longblood — Valdais's eldest, two-and-twenty, out of Adria — who had come past her in the lists on the second morning and said, not quite quietly enough, something about the look of Vhas in her face and what her mother's people were known for in the hills.

She put him down in the second course and then killed his horse.

It is the ugliest thing she did in public in her life and she has never explained it and never apologised for it and never once pretended it was an accident. The horse was worth nine marks and she paid for it out of her own purse before the afternoon was over and had the receipt entered, which several people found colder than the act.

Braedyn Fenwood, watching from the gallery, said one word, to nobody, which his granddaughter heard and repeated to exactly one person twenty years later.

He said: "Ah."

She was beaten in the fifth course by Tylan Vayle, who was twenty, four inches taller and two stone heavier, and who has never in twenty years let one single man say that he beat her on anything but reach — including once, at a table in Valora, to a lord who persisted, with a knife laid flat on the board between them and a silence of about nine seconds.

They embraced in the lists. Everybody saw it. Everybody read it as chivalry.

It was two children out of the same house, one of whom had just done a job.

iii.

And then her father came down into the yard.

Wyllmot Everblood, eight-and-thirty, King of Viterra in all but the crown his father was still wearing, who had exiled his daughter at eleven for wanting the pells and had not written her a warm sentence in six years — came down off the gallery in front of four thousand people, and walked the length of the lists, and took her head in both his hands, and put his forehead against hers.

And said something nobody heard.

She has never told anybody what it was. Not Jaennis. Not Rekedim. She was asked, once, at the worst possible moment, by somebody entitled to ask, and what she said was: "It was between him and me, and he is dead, and it is still between him and me."

They were reconciled from that hour.

It was two years. She did not know it was two years.

Baldvyn the Good died nine weeks later.

He was six-and-sixty and he had been king for seven-and-thirty years and had done exactly one thing that anybody remembers doing and it was a mercy in a yard at Brigh, and the root spread, and it was pulled out of the ground at Coldmorrow by his own granddaughter five-and-thirty years later, and he lived long enough to know that and never once mentioned it to her.

iv.

The Seven Week Uprising began in the spring of the thirty-sixth year in the Aelerian foothills.

There is a whole other book about why. It is in the other book. What the men at Valora knew, and all they knew, was that a sect of Myrmis-worshippers had begun killing nobility, that eleven shrine-houses had been granted out and their nails drawn on the king's ruling over the preceding four years, and that the sect had a champion who was a Renard of the Kaerndal line, third cousin to the queen.

Wyllmot Everblood, who had come back from Galalina with God at eight-and-twenty and had been patient about it for six years, which for him was superhuman, was not patient about this.

He took the field in nine days.

Braedyn Fenwood counselled him to starve the citadel. It was a mountain fort in bad country with one road and a cistern; the Chancellor of Viterra, who had been ending sieges with a counting-house and two clerks since before the king was born, told him it would take eleven weeks and no assault at all.

The king said that eleven weeks would be eleven weeks of the north watching him wait.

And Braedyn Fenwood — who despised him, and had left a field and been sick behind a wall over his work at Charau fifteen years before, and who has never denied either — said the last thing he ever said to him, which is recorded because there were nine people in the tent:

"Your Grace, the mountain does not care how you look."

Wyllmot went in on the fourth day, ahead of his own household, because the alternative was to stand in a field and be looked at.

He was the third man of that line to do it and it is the third time in three generations, and all three of them were right about something else at the time.

v.

Veralyn was nineteen and she was not there.

She was at Valora holding the west road with two hundred horse, which was a real command and a correct one, and she got the word at the second hour and rode two hundred and forty miles in four days and came into a camp that had been leaderless for three of them and had begun, quietly, to come apart.

What she did in the next nine weeks is the reason anybody in Viterra took her seriously afterward, and it is almost entirely logistics, and nobody sings about it.

She did not assault the citadel. She sat down in front of it with her brother — who arrived on the sixth day, twenty-one years old, crowned in the field, and who had the good sense, which was considerable, to say in front of the whole camp my sister has the dispositions and then keep his word — and she starved it exactly as Braedyn Fenwood had said, in eleven weeks, less two days.

And while it starved she took the foothills apart. Not with fire; she had done fire at fifteen and something in her would not go near it. She did it with the ring: the four passes, the two fords, the market at Cair, the dwarf-road tolls. She closed the lanes and she priced the exits and she let the sect's own people walk out of it in ones and twos over two months, and by the end of it the thing that had begun with nine murders in a spring was four hundred hungry men in a rock with no road.

Her own account of it, to Rekedim, in a letter, is one line and it is the proudest thing she ever wrote:

I did not chase. I forked the far mouth.

vi.

On the last day she went into the citadel with a dozen men and then, at the inner sanctum, with none.

That is the fact that everybody knows and that everybody has read as swagger. It was not swagger and it was not madness. She had been in the place for two hours by then and it was over; the fighting had stopped; men were sitting down in corridors. She went the last forty feet alone because the man at the end of it was her cousin and because she had worked out — correctly, on the stair, on no information at all — that he would not draw if she came alone.

He did not draw.

Gelfrey Renard was three-and-thirty and devout and had been dull his entire life until sixteen years ago when somebody said eleven words to him in a passage, and he stood at his altar in his own sanctum with four hundred men starving behind him and he put his hands at his sides.

She killed him with his own dagger, on the altar, through the heart.

She has been asked about that a great many times, because it is the one thing in her life that looks like cruelty and reads like ritual. And what she has said, every time, in the same words, is that he had a dagger at his belt and she had come in without a weapon in her hand to make him not use it, and that when she got to him he would not take it out, and that somebody had to.

Nobody has ever been able to find a fault in that answer. It is completely coherent. It accounts for every observed fact.

It is also not what a person says about a thing they chose. It is what a person says about a thing that was arranged.

vii.

And a sword was found beside the altar.

Unmarked. Perfectly balanced. Light, and very sharp, and it will not chip. Nobody remembers carrying it in. Nobody has ever claimed it. The inventory of the citadel, which was taken in the proper form by a Fenwood clerk over three days because the Fenwoods inventory everything, does not list it, and the clerk was asked twice and said twice that he could not have missed it and that he must have.

She picked it up because it was there and it was good and hers had gone into a man's ribs and stuck.

She has not been more than a few feet from it since.

It has never done anything.

That has to be said plainly and it will be said again at the end of this book. In four years that sword has not glowed, moved, spoken, warmed, sung, resisted, or turned in her hand. It has never once been observed to do a thing that a very good sword would not do. Four hundred people have seen it. Two armourers have handled it. A Thepyrgan who does not believe in anything examined it for an hour at her own request and pronounced it the finest piece of work he had ever been allowed to hold and entirely ordinary.

Nobody knows it is anything.

Including her.

viii.

One anomaly, in its proper place.

Rekedim Many-Scars had kept her games since she was eleven. Not out of sentiment — he kept everybody's; he kept his own; his papers came to the crown in nine boxes and are the most complete record of a single player's development that exists in either kingdom. He kept them because he is a logician and because a man who does not write it down is guessing.

In the winter after Eryas he did what he did every winter, which was to go through the year's sheets.

And the shape had changed.

Not the quality. Her play in 837 is not better than her play in 835; he has said so in writing, twice, and he was not a man who softened things. The positions she reaches are the positions she would have reached. The judgment is identical. He went over it for a month looking for a decline and did not find one and was, by his own account, relieved.

What changed is the interval.

He had been noting her deliberation times since she was thirteen, in the margin, in a shorthand of his own, because tempo is half the game and he wanted her to see her own habits. And for six years the notation shows the same profile: fast on the ring, fast on trades, and then, before the middle, a pause — three or four beats, sometimes longer, every time, without exception, the interval in which a player who has been properly taught counts her exits.

After Eryas the pause is not there.

Not shortened. Gone. She enters the middle at the same speed she posts a screen.

And her moves are the same moves. That is the part he could not get past, and that is why there are four sheets in his hand where he has worked the same position eleven times looking for an error, and there is no error, and at the foot of the fourth sheet he has written, in the only piece of unprofessional prose in nine boxes:

She does not need the pause. Or she has stopped being able to take it. These are not the same and I cannot tell them apart from the outside.

He raised it with her once, that winter, flatly, over a board, in about four sentences.

She said she had got faster.

He said, "Yes," and put the pieces away, and never raised it again in his life.

i.

They were strangers.

That is the fact people cannot hold on to, because by the end they were the closest thing either of them had, and because the songs have them as devoted from the cradle. They were not. They were fostered apart at eleven and nine and did not spend a consecutive month in the same building for ten years, and when Baldvyn Brightstride was crowned in a camp in the Aelerian foothills at one-and-twenty he was meeting his sister for the fourth or fifth time.

He grew up beloved at Valora. She grew up useful at Tarlington. Those are two entirely different educations and they produce two people who cannot, at first, find anything at all to say to each other after the first quarter of an hour.

What saved them was the siege.

Nine weeks in front of a rock with nothing to do but the work, and a young king who had the enormous good sense to say my sister has the dispositions in front of the whole camp and then never once, in nine weeks, interfere with them. She had expected to have to fight him for it. She had drawn up, on the ride, a whole argument with exits in it.

He gave it to her in a sentence and went and sat with the wounded, which he was better at than she was, which she noticed, which annoyed her, which was the beginning.

ii.

He was Harald.

Nobody at that court said so, because nobody at that court had known Harald the Bold, who reigned ten days and took an arrow at a gate nine-and-thirty years before anyone in the room was born. iii.

Her office had no name. In the rolls she is the Duchess of Aberiel from 837 and nothing else, and what she actually was, for two years, was the thing a decent king needs and cannot be.

And she closed things. That is what she was for.

A quarrel gated here. A marriage held up there. A grant not refused but not signed. A pension continued at the old rate. A wardship "still under consideration" for four years. Nineteen knots.

iv.

He knew.

And he sat with it for eleven days.

"A rumour is not a thing until an official person has examined it."

"Your Grace."

He kept that for the rest of his life.

It cost him more than anyone knew. Jaycen Wraith, who was furniture for four kings, said at the end of his life that the young one aged a year in that fortnight and never got it back.

And it is on the short list of things that killed him — not because keeping it was wrong, but because a man who has decided not to look at one thing has to decide, every single day, not to look at it, and he had a dozen other things to look at, and there were only so many hours.

vi.

iii.

She was not sure she was anything but a killer.

She said one thing about it, to Rekedim, in the week before the coronation, in the long room at Valora with the board out, which he wrote down because he wrote everything down:

"They are going to make me queen and I am a mace."

iv.

Two Coldmorrow tenants, a cooper's apprentice and a woman who had been eleven years old on the day.

Nobody has called her Blackrose since that afternoon.

the Warrior Queen.

It was not a change of character.

The two halves closed.

For nine years there had been a girl at Tarlington who tied knots — patient, ringwise, welding files two plies ahead, nineteen open matters and not one of them raised above a murmur — and a girl who entered without counting, who went in at Lence at fifteen and up the last forty feet at Eryas alone and killed a man with his own dagger because somebody had to.

Everybody who knew her thought one of them was the real one. They disagreed about which. Her father thought the second and could not look at it. Her grandfather-in-law thought the first and invested a duchy in it. Rekedim Many-Scars, who was the only person who ever had both sets of numbers, thought they were the same girl and could not prove it and said so exactly once.

Not in a rage. Not in a swoon. Giving orders, which is the word a nineteen-year-old used about a fifteen-year-old in an afternoon she could not remember, and which is the word for what she did at three-and-twenty in an afternoon she never afterward forgot a second of.

The knot and the cut, in the same day, by the same hand, with the same face on.

That is the Warrior Queen, and the north was right to be frightened of the phrase it had invented, and the north sent its levies in the autumn.

ix.

Two more things and then the last movement.

The first. She never asked Jaennis.

Jaennis Fenwood has never been asked, under oath or otherwise, what she knew about her own maid, by anybody with the standing to make her answer. There is one person in the world with that standing.

The second. Rekedim went through the year's sheets that winter, as he did every winter.

The pause did not come back.

iii.

If she does not go north, she loses the north, and with it any claim to be queen of a country that begins at Dornhall; and Valdais crosses in the spring against a realm that has already shown it will not defend its own earls.

If she goes north, she is committed four hundred miles from the Belworth with the whole of her levy on a bad road in winter, and Valdais crosses behind her into Aberiel — which is hers, which is the family's beachhead, which is the seam — and takes it in a fortnight against garrisons that have not been reinforced because the reinforcements are in the north.

If she splits, she does neither thing and loses both, and every doctrine ever written says so.

iv.

He looked at it for a long time.

v.

Nobody gave it to her. It is not in the Field Manual — it is the thing the Field Manual forbids, in ink, in the fifth tenet, in the hand of the man sitting across the board. There is no notation for it. The steppe has played this game for six hundred years and has no symbol for what she did; neither have the under-vaults, who have been at it longer and who write everything down twice; and the reason is that it is not, in the technical sense, a move at all. It is a refusal to make one.

She went north with nothing behind her, on purpose, knowing.

And Valdais let them hang.

He did not come. He wrote to them twice more on the road — warm, generous, entirely true letters, both of which were produced afterward and both of which exonerate him completely — and he did not move one man north, and by the first week of Decis the earls of Viterra understood, in a hall at Dornhall, all at once, what they had been for.

Two of them came over to her that night. Two more sent sons. The document with eleven seals on it was worth nothing by Winter's Crest and four of the signatories died in the next eighteen months and not one of them died fighting her.

He came up out of Adria through Ethor in the last week of Nonis and through Istovar in the first week of Decis, and Istovar did not move.

That wants a line of its own. Oswyn Everblood was eighty years old and had held that canton for three-and-forty years by the terms of a clause he had signed himself, and nine thousand men walked the length of it, and he did not send to Valora and he did not come out and he did not treat.

Nobody knows why. He was asked afterward, by two people entitled to ask, and gave the same pleasant unhurried answer both times, which is that he is an old man and the roads are the crown's business and he was not consulted.

There is an answer available. It has been available for eight years and he has never once given it, which is either the most disciplined thing in this book or the most frightened, and there is no way to tell them apart from the outside. He sent his eldest son over that river for Braedyn Fenwood's tenure dispute and got him back in a cart, and has spent the eight years since making a canton safe for a second son who did not want it and is not built for it — Harald, careful, courteous, thirty-one, who checks a figure four times and has never in his life wanted anything badly enough to be seen wanting it.

And his third son was on the Belworth that week, holding it for the Queen.

Elyas Everblood was at the ford below Tarlington when the rider came down with the news that nine thousand men had walked the length of Istovar and that Istovar had not moved.

He was eight-and-twenty. He had been the most beloved man in that valley for nineteen years and he had done it on purpose, and everybody who was in the yard that morning has given the same account of it, because there were eleven of them and they all noticed, and men who notice a thing like that remember the light.

He read it. He handed it back to the rider, which is not what you do with a dispatch, and then took it again. He said, "Thank you, that is very clear," which is a thing he says. And then he stood in the yard with it for rather longer than a man stands anywhere, looking at the water, and one of the eleven — a Vayle serjeant, in a deposition given nine years later about something else entirely — put down that the Duke of Istovar's youngest son looked, for that while, exactly like a man doing arithmetic he already knew the answer to.

Then he was pleasant to everybody for the rest of the day.

That is the second time. The first was at nine, at Charel, three years before he ever saw the Belworth, on the day he worked out what he was; it is not in any record; he told one person about it, once, a great many years later, and she is the only reason this book knows it happened at all.

And on the fourteenth of Decis, with the Queen of Viterra three hundred and ninety miles away on a frozen road in the north, Valdais Longblood crossed the Scar at the Vhasian fords below Ravier with nine thousand men and every canton of the west behind him, and went into Aberiel, and took the seam in eleven days.

Then he crossed the Belworth, which nobody had expected him to do at all, and turned east along the Viterran arm of the Scar, south of Tarlington, and sat down across the only road between the north of that kingdom and its capital.

The cry was Vhasia for Vhasians, and revenge for Lence.

It is a very good cry. It is the best cry anybody has raised in that country in two hundred years; it has a real grievance in it and a real corpse under it and it will carry a hundred thousand men who have never met him.

And it is possibly not the reason.

vii.

Her grandmother's people would have asked what Valdais Longblood wants and would have gone round the houses about it for forty years.

She was taught otherwise. You do not need a man's mind if you have his board. Read what he spent.

He spent the earls. Four provinces of somebody else's rebellion, raised, warmed, written to, and left standing in the north to be reduced at the crown's leisure. They cost him postage.

He did not spend Istovar. Nine thousand men went the length of Oswyn Everblood's canton and not one field was burned in it, which is either a courtesy or an arrangement and is certainly not an accident.

He bypassed the Fens entirely. That is the one that will not come out. His cry is revenge for Lence — and the man who sacked Lence is six-and-sixty in a wagon on the north road with two clerks and the tolls, and his duchy was open, and Valdais Longblood went past the mouth of it and turned north over the Scar for Aberiel.

A man avenging Lence marches on Tarlington. Whatever this is, the proclamation it is being fought under is contradicted by the first eleven days of it, and nine thousand men shouting it have not noticed and will not.

And he provisioned to hold. Fourteen months of grain across four factors, bought at the wrong season, paid out of a coin-house at Fairport. You do not buy five turns' supply for a raid, or for a revenge, or for a throne you intend to take in the spring and sit on. You buy it when you mean to be somewhere specific in a year's time and still be there.

So: not the earls, not Fenwood, not Lence, and not quickly.

He crossed at the one place on eight hundred miles of border where a Viterran king stops being a Vhasian duke, and he took it, and he sat down in it with the grain.

Which is consistent with wanting Vhasia clean of Viterra. It is equally consistent with wanting the crown of Vhasia without a Viterran claim standing behind him when he takes it. It is consistent with a locked house and a guard on a stair, and with four words in a good hand that he has had six-and-twenty years to think about, and there is no move on that board that separates the four of them.

She could tell you what he did. She could tell you within a fortnight what he would do next, and did, twice, correctly.

She could not tell you why, and neither can anybody else, and he has been asked directly twice and answered both times in a way that would have made his mother proud and told the asker nothing.

viii.

And the last question, which this book has been carrying since a girl of eleven came up the Belworth road with a wooden sword she had been told to leave behind, is not about him at all.

Is the Queen of Viterra playing, or being played?

Nineteen knots tied in two years without her voice once going up. A rebellion read out of a grain return. A trap identified, priced, and walked into as an instrument.

Against which: a lane opened at nine and walked for twelve years; four children with debts put in one house by a man who has never been wrong about a return; two hundred moves made by a maid before anybody knew a board was out; and a girl of fifteen on cobbles in a stable yard asking for warriors.

And a sword that has never done anything, in the hand of a woman who no longer takes the pause.

Her grandmother asked which hand answered her and spent eight-and-twenty years on it with a thread and never got past six letters. Her great-grandmother asked what it cost and could not produce a figure.

Veralyn Everblood has never asked.

ix.

The last thing.

On the morning of the nineteenth of Nonis, in the yard at Valora, with four provinces on the road and the whole of it already in motion, Rekedim Many-Scars held her stirrup, which was his office, and looked up at her and said the only unprofessional thing he ever said to her in nine-and-twenty years.

He said: "Count them for me. Once. Out loud. Even if it is nought."

And Veralyn Everblood, three-and-twenty, Duchess of Aberiel, Queen of Viterra, with a black sword at her hip that has never done anything, looked north up a road she had already decided to take.

"No," she said.

And went.

Here ends Count Your Exits, and the three books of the Belworth.

He began at fourteen and never stopped. Nine years at Tarlington, eleven at court, and the whole of it in the same hand: what he saw, where people were, and how long they were there. No judgements. He kept no diary and wrote no letters worth keeping. These came to the crown in two boxes and have never been published, for reasons that will be obvious.

The notation is his own. The dates are the Old Count, which is what a household runs on.

i. Greening, 829.

J. in the yard at the second hour. Not permitted, being ill since Ashing. Nobody sent her back. R. saw her and did not report it. Fourth time since Thawing.

ii. Leafturn, 832.

V. returned on the ninth. Did not speak.

Day one, yard, did not go in. Day two, dyke. Day three, dyke. Day four, dyke, I sat. Days five through eleven, dyke. Spoke on the twelfth at supper, about a horse.

E. came to the yard on day four and went away again. Did not come to the dyke. Went to the counting-house and asked the steward what the ride had cost. Steward told me because he thought it was funny. E. asked for the number twice.

iii. Kindling, 833. The long room.

One hour and eleven minutes. Nobody interrupted.

V. at the end of the row, hands in her lap, no word the whole hour.

R. did not ask her anything. R. looked at her twice.

iv. Planting, 838. The wedding.

Nine hours.

V. in cloth of silver, four feet from the bride, delighted, visibly, the whole nine hours. Did not sit at the second remove when the others sat. E. sat.

J. looked at the door eleven times. I did not begin counting until the second hour.

v. Haying, 838.

The king took ill on the third and died on the ninth.

Household over those six days: physician twice, confessor once, the queen present continuously excepting the sixth hour of the seventh day, when she was in the muniment room.

The steward's boy saw her there. I have written down that he saw her there. I have also written down that he is nine years old.

Board out. R. already in the room at the tenth hour. V. entered at the eleventh. Door shut.

Two hours and forty minutes.

Van formed at first light. I commanded it.

FROM THE ROLLS OF THE DUCHY OF ADRIA

Valdais Longblood held Adria for four-and-twenty years and ran it well, which is not a thing the pamphlets on either side have ever found a use for. The rolls survive entire. They are in his own hand for nineteen of the twenty-four years and in a clerk's for the rest, and the clerk's hand is worse.

817. The ford road above Avimont, repaired to the water and eleven perches beyond it, the carts having broken there four times since Leafturn. Charged to the duchy. The ford is mine and so are the carts.

823. The mill tolls at Roulen remitted for the year, the barley having failed. Charged to me, not to the duchy. Entered so that it may be found.

828. Petition of the widow Careno as to the boundary stone. Allowed. The stone was moved and the man who moved it knows he moved it and has been told that I know.

831. Bridge at the second ford, rebuilt in stone. Four hundred and eleven marks. It will outlast the argument.

836. The Adrian levy mustered, counted, paid and sent home within the tenday, per custom. Nothing owing.

An archivist's note, appended in 862 by a clerk of the Queen's chancery who was given the rolls to catalogue and gave them back three weeks late.

These are the best-kept ducal rolls I have handled and I have handled a great many. I record the following because I do not know what to do with it.

In four-and-twenty years of entries there is no complaint. There is no petition to the crown of Viterra, no appeal, no protest, no memorandum of grievance, no reservation of right. The word Viterra occurs eleven times and every one of them is a place that goods come from.

There is no reference to his mother. There is no reference to the Right by which he was acknowledged. There is no reference to the tenth clause of the settlement of 814, by which the said Right was renounced on his behalf and he was made a Vhasian and nothing else forever, which is the single most consequential document of his life and which he appears never to have written down.

Every roll of this duchy carries a price column. Every entry in four-and-twenty years is priced.

One is not. It is dated the eleventh of Floralis, 814, in the month the settlement was sealed. The column is ruled and left empty. The entry reads, entire:

Item, to my mother, the road as far as the water.

The hand does not change before it or after it.

A LEAF OF ELYAS EVERBLOOD'S

Found among his papers at his death in 865, folded, with nothing else in the fold. Unsent. Undated; internally the winter of 840.

Father,

I hope the winter is not hard at Charel. Harald will have written that the levy is out and I will not repeat him.

I am holding the river below Tarlington with a good deal less than everybody believes I have, which is a thing you taught me and which I have not forgotten, and I find I am good at it, which surprises nobody but me.

Nine thousand men went the length of your country and you did not send to Valora. I have been asked about it eleven times in eleven days by men who expect me to be angry, and I have been very pleasant to every one of them, which is the thing I am actually good at.

I have tried four times to write that I understand it. I cannot get the sentence to be true, so I will not put it down.

Wymund would have understood it. That is the whole of what is wrong and I am sorry to have written it.

I am well. I am liked. Tell Harald the tolls at the second ford have been wrong since Lavius and that I will send him the figures, because he will not ask for them.

Your son,

E.

He kept it five-and-twenty years. He did not send it, and he did not burn it, and he did not add to it, and there is no second draft anywhere in the papers, which in a man who drafted everything four times is the only remarkable thing about the document.
