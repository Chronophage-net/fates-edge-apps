Tales of the Threshold Regions — Set down by Dusana of the Raven Road

Anthology 22 Tales Folklore & GM Flavor Author: Nicholas A. Gasper

A story settles nothing. It is not meant to. But it warms the hands, and it keeps, and a knot in a cord will hold it for sixty years without anyone having to write it down.

You travel. You sit by fires. Someone speaks. You write. The ash on your cuff becomes ink. That is all. That is enough. These tales were told by midwives, bell-wardens, hedge-mothers, cord-weavers, herders, and ferrymen, gathered on the road from one threshold to the next — one tale for nearly every region and people I have crossed, kept as they were told, or as close to it as ink allows.

What follows is not the whole of what I carried home. It is the part worth keeping — the tales that still hold their heat when the fire has gone to coals. A gamemaster may use them as they were given to me: read aloud for flavor when the party arrives somewhere new, as a cold open before a session, or as the seed of a night's trouble in whatever region the road has brought your table to. Read them. Or do not. The hearth waits either way.

How this book is arranged

The contents above run by region, because that is how a book gets used at a table: the party arrives somewhere, you turn to that somewhere, you read. The order to read the book in is a different order, and it is printed below.

Every tale carries two notes at its head. The first is how long it runs spoken aloud, at the pace of someone who is not hurrying — scan the durations and pick what fits the gap you have. The second is the tale's object: the ring, the bowl, the coin, the loose knot. The object is the hook. Put it on a table and the tale becomes a session.

Most tales close with a line from the Witch. It is not the moral. It is the rule the tale enacts — something an old woman in your game can say to the party in a doorway, and mean. Where a tale ends better without one, there is none, and that silence is deliberate.

This is the performable book of the six: every tale in it was chosen because it survives being spoken, and none of them runs long enough to need a break. The long tales — the ones written for the page rather than the mouth — are in The Weight of the Unpaid and Tales of the Threshold, and are marked as such where they stand.

One more thing, since it is the kind of thing a book ought to say about itself rather than leave to be discovered or not. These were arranged, not merely collected. Objects recur. Tellers turn up in each other's tales without being introduced. More than one closing is answered by a tale three sections away that appears to be about something else entirely, and at least one of them is answered by a tale in a different volume. None of that is necessary to enjoy any single piece, which is why none of it is pointed out where it happens. It is there for the second reading, and the second reading is the one the book was actually built for.

On the retellings, and where the voices come from

From Dusana's frame: I have kept these as they were told me. Where I have retold rather than kept — where a teller gave me a shape and I gave it back its words — I have marked the retelling in the byline, and you may weigh it accordingly. Three tales here are retellings by that measure. One I have marked twice, because I could not decide.

From the author: The tellers in this book are written in borrowed cadences, and the borrowing should be named rather than absorbed. Old Orra's clipped underground count is built from Welsh and Cornish mining talk. Temur's tale follows the shape of Mongolian and Turkic steppe epic, where the lesson is delivered by an elder in the last third and the audience is expected to have seen it coming. Rusti's swerve is a Romani and Balkan trickster tale in its bones. Granary-keeper Oris and the Oshiiran grain country draw on Swahili and wider Bantu oral practice — the proverb laid down before the story, the teller's pause where a listening room is supposed to answer. Hag Morag stands at the crossing of Slavic and Gaelic hearth-hags, and owes a debt to both. The Trow are Orcadian and Shetlandic. The Mistlands are Scots. Vhasia is medieval French, Ashaan is Persian and Mughal court storytelling, Dhahara is Indo-Persian river country, and the Lethai courts borrow their register from Sanskrit serpent-lore.

These are cadences, not translations. Nothing here is a specific traditional tale carried over and renamed; where a real form is used — the call-and-response, the proverb-first opening, the saga's flat refusal to editorialize — it is used because it does something a flatter telling cannot, and it is named here so that a reader, or a gamemaster about to perform one of these aloud, knows whose rhythm they are standing in. Departures are deliberate. Errors are mine.

The High Country

The first hearths are hard to reach. Wind and stone determine how close people sit and how long a visitor may stay.

The Vent That Whispered

Aeler — the Aelerian Range

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A trimmed lamp. A count of breaths. A shadow that takes a week to come back.

Old Orra keeps the lamps on the Third Stack and has not gone below in thirty years. She trims a wick in four movements and has never once needed a fifth. She still counts her breaths before sleep. She gave me this one in the dark and would not let me light a candle for it, and twice in the telling she stopped and put her hand flat on the wall, the way a woman does who is listening to a house she does not trust.

You ever walk the Deep Galleries alone? No. Good. Don't start.

The air down there has a measure. Eight breaths to a corridor. Sixteen to a chamber. Thirty-two, and you've gone too far to matter. Breathe, count, turn. Never answer the ninth.

I was young. Lamp trimmed, chalk dry, walking from the Third Stack to the old sump — an easy two-hour walk. At the junction — you know the one, three tunnels, the middle one always drips — I stopped. Something was wrong. The drip had stopped. The echo had changed. The stone felt soft under my boot. And then I heard it.

Not with my ears. Inside. Like a memory that wasn't mine, someone else's grief pressed into my skull like a thumb into clay.

"Count the ninth breath."

I didn't. I walked faster. The voice followed.

"The ninth holds the name of the miner who died here. The year the pillar cracked. The road to the lost sump. The name of the man who forgot to turn back."

My lamp flickered — burned blue for a heartbeat. I didn't stop to trim it. I kept counting. Eight in, eight out, lips moving. The voice couldn't find the crack.

When I reached the surface, the lamp was dry and my shadow was gone. The sun was setting and I cast nothing. The other miners stared. They did not ask.

The next morning I looked in a mirror — real brass, not a puddle — and the reflection smiled first.

It took a week for the shadow to come back, and another for my left hand to stop writing in a script I couldn't read. The Vent-Prior said the deep had spoken and I hadn't answered, and that was mercy — that the mountain respects the ones who know where the door is.

She made me lamp-keeper after that. I never went down again. I tend the wicks. I count the hours. I do not breathe in rhythm.

The count of eight is the first thing I was handed on this road and very nearly the last thing I came to trust. Orra has it underground; the halflings have it at the well; the north has a whole grammar for it that I have set down separately, in the frost country. Viterra says it is a fiction invented to keep children off the ice. Dhahara pays a man a wage to hold the ninth place and thanks him by name. I have put those two in this book on purpose, and not next to each other.

"Stone remembers. So does the space between breaths. Answer neither. And if you must walk the deep, count to eight — then start over. The ninth is not for you."— the Witch's Closing

The Warrior Who Refused to Call the Runner

Ykrul — the steppe clans

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A kon'reh board. A War-Chief left standing free. A braid cut off at the fire.

Temur Many-Scars wears his braid to the belt and taught me the board before he told me the tale. He was clear that the order mattered: you do not hear it until you have lost at it. The game is kon'reh that night. I lost every match, and after the fourth he set the pieces back himself without being asked, which is how a steppe teller tells you the lesson is coming and you are to stop talking now.

The clan-mother called a muster. We were young, hungry, and stupid — the kind of stupid that gets carved into the archive as a lesson for grandchildren. My cousin, bold as a winter wolf and half as patient, wanted glory. He pushed his War-Chief forward across the board without counting exits. He just pushed.

The enemy stepped aside. Let him pass. Let him think he was winning. That's the oldest trick on the steppe: give a fool enough rope and he'll hang himself. Give a warrior enough board and he'll forget the Well.

By the time he reached the Well, his cut was spent. He looked at the far Well. Empty. He could Call a Runner — but his War-Chief would be Rooted for a breath. Fixed. A target the size of a tent flap.

I said, "Don't. Their War-Chief still has a cut. They're two breaths from boxing you in. Count the shadow before you move."

He laughed. "They won't risk it. Cowards. They haven't moved in three beats."

He called.

The enemy hopped a single stone — the smallest move on the board, the kind you learn on your first day and forget by your second — landed on the Well, and knocked his War-Chief clean off it. The Five-Breath Race started ticking. Five beats to plant his banner or lose the match, the tribute, and the clan's good opinion.

He didn't. He couldn't. The enemy had already welded the approach. Every lane ended in the shadow of a stone. The race was dead before it started. The banner never touched ground.

We lost. The clan paid tribute for a year — silver, horses, three strings of good beads my grandmother had woven. The clan-mother made us watch as she handed them over.

She demoted him to herd-watcher. Took his braid. Took his seat at the fire. "You forgot the first number," she said. "Not the second, not the third. The first. Count exits before you count glory. Call only when the geometry is yours. The board does not forgive. Neither do I."

My cousin watched the sheep for three winters. He learned to count. He never played kon'reh again.

"A free War-Chief is a noose waiting for a neck. Call only when the shadow is yours. Always keep one cut for the door. The board is patient. It will wait for you to forget."— the Witch's Closing

The Peddler's Swerve

Vilikari — the border ford

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A tar-sealed barrel of mountain air — and one kept back, empty.

Rusti the Factor is short one finger and has sold the same horse three times without at any point ceasing to own it. She does not call this theft. She calls it velocity, and she did not laugh when she said it. She tells the way her people tell: the ending first, so you know where you are going, and then the pleasure is entirely in the route.
Retold. She gave it to me out of order and at speed, and I have straightened it. Something has certainly been lost in the straightening.

A peddler came to a border ford. The toll was high. She had no coin. She had a cart full of empty barrels — iron-hooped, tar-sealed, the kind that once held brandy from the southern islands.

"Air," she told the toll-keeper. "Fresh mountain air. The valley pays handsomely. They say the fog down there is thick enough to drown in. My customers prefer to breathe."

He laughed — a thick, wet sound. "Air? I will let you pass for a taste."

She uncorked a barrel with ceremony. Held it to his nose. Pine and snow and the particular cold that lives in high places, the kind that makes you remember a childhood you never had.

He waved her through, still laughing.

The next day, a rival told him the barrels were empty. Furious — not at the loss, at the insult — he rode after her.

She had already sold the air.

Not as air. As "breathing lessons for the rich." Copper per lungful. Copper per desperate merchant who could not afford another winter of valley lung. The barrels were full of gold now, and the air was gone.

He cornered her at the next ford. "You cheated me."

She did not flinch. "Air is free. The mountain gives it to anyone who climbs. You chose not to sell it. I did. That is not cheating. That is seeing what is in front of your face."

She paid double the original toll — from the gold — and asked, "Do you want a lesson in breathing?"

He did not answer. But the next year, he sold fog to the lowlands. Called it "cloud silk." Made a fortune.

The peddler? She kept one barrel empty. Just in case.

"Sell the hour, not the horse. If a toll-keeper asks what is in the barrel, open it and let him smell. Name the need before you name the price, and he will pay for air."— the Witch's Closing

The Goat, the Toll, and the Other Goat

Vilikari — the same ford, some years later

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A goat, a rope, and three coppers, two of which change hands twice.

A man came down to the ford with a goat. The toll was two coppers for a man and one for a goat.

"Three," said the toll-keeper.

"I have two."

"Then leave the goat."

So the man left the goat on the near bank and paid his two and crossed. On the far side he turned round and cupped his hands and shouted, "Whose goat is that?"

"Yours," said the toll-keeper.

"Then it is standing on your side of the river and it has not paid."

The toll-keeper looked at the goat. The goat looked at the toll-keeper. It is worth saying here that this toll-keeper had held the post eleven years and had never yet lost an argument to a man on foot.

"One copper," he said.

"I have none. I gave you the two."

The toll-keeper paid the goat's toll himself out of his own purse and sent it across, because the alternative was a goat living at his gate at his expense for the rest of its life, and he had done that arithmetic faster than I am doing it for you.

The man caught the goat by the rope, thanked him very civilly, and walked on. Halfway to the village he met his brother coming the other way with two goats and three coppers, who asked him how the crossing was.

"Cheap," he said.

Here the teller stopped to laugh and did not start again.

I have kept this one and I will say why, since it does nothing else for the book. It made me laugh. It was the ninth story of that night and I had not the strength for another sad one. And I did not catch who told it — he was three places ahead of me in a queue at a toll-house and telling it to someone else entirely, and by the time I had my ink out he had paid and gone over. So there is no byline. There is no rule at the end. There is a man, and a goat, and a toll-keeper who lost eleven years of form in a single morning. Some of what is told at fords is like this and I have been leaving it out, which I now think was a kind of lie about what the road sounds like.

The Hollow Bride

Aelaerem — the mill villages

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  The ninth bucket. A wedding dress out of the cedar chest. A red door.

Mabs worked the grindstone through the whole of this, so some of it arrived under the noise and I have guessed at it. She says the Hollow talks to her at night. She would not tell me what it says, and was very pleasant about not telling me — she offered me more bread twice, both times at the exact place in the tale where I had opened my mouth to ask.

You know about the ninth cup, don't you? Course you do. Every child knows: you pour eight. You never pour the ninth. The ninth is for the Hollow.

But children forget. Or they get curious. Or they think the rules are for other people. That's how it always starts.

Little Kesta — seven, maybe eight — went to the well at dusk while the grown folk were at the harvest feast, the kind where the ale flows and the children run wild and no one watches the threshold. She was meant to draw water for the morning bread. She'd done it a hundred times.

She counted the buckets. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight. She pulled them up, set them in a row. The water was clear. The moon wasn't up yet. The well was just a well.

Then she thought: what's one more? Just to see.

She drew the ninth bucket. The rope was cold. The water was black — not dark, black, like ink poured into the deep and stirred with a bone. And something climbed out.

It wore her mother's wedding dress, the one that hung in the cedar chest untouched since the plague year. It smiled with her mother's mouth, the tired smile her mother wore when she was pretending not to be tired. But its eyes were the color of a drowned moon — gray, swollen, watching.

"Hollow Bride," Kesta whispered. That's what the old folk call it. You don't name it. You don't speak its name. You don't give it that kind of hold. She named it anyway.

The Bride followed her home, three paces behind, silent as a held breath. It stood outside the red door. It knocked — three knocks, a pause, three more. The rhythm of a heart that stopped. The rhythm of someone counting to eight and waiting.

Kesta's mother opened the door. She saw the dress. She saw the mouth. She did not see the eyes — she looked at the threshold, not the face. "Come in," she said. "You must be cold." That is the law of hospitality. A red door means bread, salt, and one safe night. You cannot refuse. The door remembers.

The Hollow Bride stepped inside. It sat at the table. It did not eat. It did not drink. It watched — watched Kesta braid her hair, watched the mother bank the fire, watched the father pretend not to see.

At midnight it spoke, its voice the sound of water dripping in a well gone dry. "You poured the ninth cup. You named me. Now I am your guest."

The mother understood then. She cut a loaf with a blessed knife. She set salt on the table with her left hand, palm up. She said: "Guest-right lasts one night. At dawn, you leave."

The Hollow Bride smiled — the mother's smile, worn wrong. "I will leave," it said. "But I will return. The ninth cup is an invitation. The threshold remembers. And I have a long memory for small slights."

Kesta is an old woman now. She still sets eight cups on her table and leaves the ninth empty. She still checks the red door at dusk and at dawn, and every harvest, when the lanterns burn blue at the ford and the sheep won't cross the bridge, she walks the perimeter and touches every stone.

So far, it's held. The Bride hasn't returned. But the miller says the well water tasted of salt last spring, and the sheep still won't drink.

"Hospitality is a thread. Tie it right, and it binds the guest. Tie it wrong, and it binds you. Never pour the ninth — not even to see what happens. Something always does."— the Witch's Closing

The Grain That Spoke

Oshiira — the grain country

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  One warm kernel, pocketed. A ledger with three names struck through and marked paid.

Granary-keeper Oris is a heavy man with a gentle voice and a count that has never once come out right. He began, as they do in the grain country, by laying down a proverb and waiting: the full basket does not explain itself. There was nobody in the room to answer him and he waited anyway, the way you would for a congregation, and then went on. He says the silo talks to him when the wheat runs low, and that it does not whisper, it instructs. He is no longer certain there is a difference. He also says — and he was firm on this, and I record it because he was firm — that the ninth sack has never been anything but a blessing, and that the Witch who taught me otherwise never keepered a granary in her life.
Retold. He gave me the shape of it over two evenings and the words are mostly mine. I have marked it twice, here and in the contents, because I am not confident I have kept his meaning as well as his order.

A clerk was tallying grain in the silo, late, the lamp dying, his fingers black with dust. He counted aloud to stay awake: one sack, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight.

"Ninth," whispered the grain.

Not a voice, exactly — a rustle, a shift, the sound of one kernel settling against another in a way that formed a word. He'd been taught: never count the ninth. The ninth isn't grain. The ninth is a door.

He stopped, and looked. The grain had shifted, just a finger's width, leaving a small hollow. In the hollow, one kernel lay apart from the rest, warm as breath. He picked it up. It sat in his palm like a living thing. He pocketed it without deciding to. His hand simply moved.

That night he dreamed of debtors — not strangers, people he knew. Farmers who'd borrowed seed and not repaid. Widows who owed for flour. A fisherman who'd taken grain against a catch that never came. Their names were scratched into his ledger, each one a small wound in the page. The grain whispered each name, slow as a tide: "You could. You should. You will not."

He woke, walked to the ledger, and forgave three names — drew a line through each, wrote "paid" in a hand that wasn't quite his own. The ink was warm. The page did not stain.

The next day the grain was heavier. He could feel it climbing the silo steps, a weight pushing back against his feet. He counted again — eight sacks, and the ninth full of a pale, grain-colored glow that came from somewhere deep, somewhere that hadn't seen the sky in years.

He forgave another debtor. The grain stayed full. He forgave another. It rose higher.

For a year he forgave one debtor a night — not the ones who owed the most, but the ones the grain named. The ones it seemed to love.

The granary never emptied. The villagers called it a miracle. The priest called it suspicious. The clerk said nothing. What could he say — that the grain spoke to him, told him who to spare, and that he listened because he was afraid of what would happen if he didn't?

When he died — in the silo, at midnight, a kernel in his hand — they found the grain had gone still. Not empty. Still, as if it had been holding its breath for a year, waiting for the next clerk. The kernel in his hand had sprouted a small green shoot, pale as bone, fragile as a promise. They planted it by the silo. It did not take root. It withered by morning.

The grain never spoke again. Or perhaps it did, and the next clerk simply wasn't listening. The villagers couldn't tell. The grain was heavy. The grain was quiet. The grain was just grain.

"Forgive the debtor the ledger tells you to collect, and the grain will stand higher in the morning. Forgive the one you were going to forgive anyway, and it will not. Count the sacks aloud and stop at eight. Whatever the granary tells you it is offering, it is asking."— the Witch's Closing

The Eight That Was Not Enough

Viterra — the mill villages under the Scar

About 7 minutes read aloud  ·  A ninth cup, poured. A pair of boot-laces tied together at the knot.

Told by Gerte Halbmann, who is sixty-one and keeps the upper mill, and by her brother Willem, who is fifty-eight and keeps the lower. They do not agree about this and have not agreed since the winter in question. I asked each of them alone. Neither knew I had asked the other until I read it back to them together, which I should probably not have done. Viterra tells in the flat mill-country manner — dates, weights, whose field, whose cow — and the uncanny discussed in the same voice as a tax assessment, which is the whole reason this one is worth having.

As Gerte tells it.

You want to hear about the ninth cup. Fine. In Viterra we pour it.

Not as a dare. As a cup. My grandmother poured nine at every wake she ever sat and so did hers, and the reason is that there were nine of them at the first one anybody remembers and one of them was dead, and you do not leave the dead out of the count at their own wake. That is all it is. It is not a door. It is arithmetic and manners.

They will tell you otherwise up the pass. I have heard the Aeler version, the one with the breathing. I have heard the halfling one, with the well and the girl and the dress. The dress is a nice touch. My grandmother would have liked the dress.

Here is what happened the winter our uncle Marek died, because that is the winter everyone means when they ask. It was the eleventh of the Cold Month. There was ice on the race and we had lost two ewes to it the week before, which is why I remember the date. Fourteen of us in the room. I was nineteen.

The priest from over the ridge had come down for it — an outside priest, a pass priest, full of the count — and when my mother set out the cups he stopped her hand at the eighth and said the ninth was not for us.

And my mother, who had buried two children and a husband and did not require instruction on what was and was not for her, said: "It is for Marek."

And he said: "It is for the Hollow."

And she said, "Then the Hollow may come and sit with Marek, and I will pour a tenth."

She poured it. I watched her do it. I was nineteen and I will tell you I was frightened, because I had been told the same stories as every child on this road, and a person does not stop being frightened just because her mother is not.

Nothing happened.

Nothing happened that night. Nothing happened that week. Nothing happened that spring, and the barley came in heavy, and the ewes we had left lambed clean, and the priest went back over the ridge and did not write to us.

My mother died eleven years later of a growth in her belly, in her own bed, at seventy-four, which where I come from is not a curse, it is a good run.

So I will tell you what I think the ninth is, and you may write it down or not. The ninth is a tax. It is levied by the old on the young, and it is paid in fear, and the old collect it because it is the only thing they still have to sell. My grandmother did not fear the ninth cup. Somewhere between her and that priest, somebody worked out that a frightened village is an obedient one, and put the fear in and called it a rule.

Pour the ninth. It is a cup. Someone at the table is not drinking and they should still be counted.

As Willem tells it.

My sister is not lying to you. I want that written down first because she will read this. She is not lying. She is telling you the eleventh of the Cold Month and stopping there, and you are letting her.

Everything she said about that night is true. Fourteen in the room, the pass priest, the ninth cup, our mother's hand, nothing happened. I was sixteen and I was there and nothing happened.

Three winters after, our cousin Ansel walked into the millrace at the lower mill on a clear morning with his boots tied together at the laces.

Now Gerte will tell you — she will tell you before I am done — that Ansel had been ill in his mind for nine years and everyone in both villages knew it, and that his mother had been the same, and that he had tried it twice before with the river and once with a rope. All of that is also true. I am not a fool and I am not a priest.

I am only saying: he tied the laces. A man who means to go in does not tie his laces together, because it is more work. He does it when he has decided he must not be able to change his mind. And I have asked myself for thirty-nine years what a man is arguing with, that hard, on a clear morning, on the far side of a room where a cup was poured that nobody drank.

I pour eight. My children pour eight. My grandchildren pour eight, and they think I am a superstitious old man, and I let them think it, because it costs them nothing and I have seen what the other arithmetic costs and I did not like the look of it.

"Pour the ninth. Count the one who is not drinking. A house that will not name its dead at table will find them named somewhere less convenient."— the Witch's Closing, as given at the upper mill
"Pour eight. If someone at your table insists on the ninth, let them pour it and watch which of you thinks about it again in three years. It is always the one who did not pour."— the Witch's Closing, as given at the lower mill

I have printed both because I could not choose and because I do not think I am owed the choice. I will say only this: of everywhere I went, this is the one country where I was told to my face that the count is a fiction, and it is also the only country where two people sat me down separately and each spent an hour on it. Nobody argues that hard about a thing nobody believes. Make of that what you like. I have made three different things of it and expect to make a fourth.

The Silk Witness

Lethai-al — the Valewood

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A white silk thread strung across a river. A garden of mud and rushes under a bridge.

A merchant came to the Valewood, full of himself and his ledgers. He needed a bridge. He had coin. He did not have courtesy.

He spoke to the Lethai-al wardens. "Name your price," he said. "Double if you build it by autumn."

The merchant laughed and stepped over. The thread broke.

The Vigil nodded. "You have refused witness. Build your bridge. It will hold. But every plank will remember your rudeness. No one will sing its name. No child will skip across it. No lover will meet at its center."

He built it. It held — stone and good timber, no curse in the mortar. But the workers called it "the Rudeness." Travelers spat on its railings. Mothers pulled their children to the other bank.

The Vigil picked a single strand of silk from the dust and held it to the light. "Say this: 'I am sorry for the courtesy I broke.' Say it once, and mean it. Then build a garden under the bridge, for the eels to hide in. Soft places. Mud. Roots. Let the water slow."

"Step over a Vigil's thread and your bridge will stand, and no one will use it. To mend it: say the apology once, in your own mouth, and then spend a year making a soft place under the thing you built. Mud. Roots. Somewhere for the eels. The saying is the cheap half."— the Witch's Closing

The Bargain of the Shed Skin

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A shed skin. A child with no shadow. A favour agreed without limit.

"Without limit."

The Selkie's Oath

Linn — the fisher coast

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A sealskin, cut into bandages with the husband's own gutting knife.

He hid hers under the hearthstone. Ten years. Children. Mended nets. The quiet arithmetic of storms survived.

Then the youngest coughed blood into the porridge.

The nearest healer was a day's sail. The fisherman weighed the distance against the skin under the stone. His wife watched him do it.

She did not speak. She reached under the hearthstone, pulled out the skin, and cut it into strips with his own gutting knife.

"Bandages," she said. "The child's breath is worth more than my freedom."

She wrapped the small chest. The fever broke before dawn. The skin was ruined — salt-stiff, chopped, fit only for the tide.

"That is not an answer."

The fisherman never asked again.

The Witch had a closing for this one. Captain Rana would not let me write it down. She said the tale was finished where the fisherman stopped asking, and that anything after was a woman from the shore explaining the sea. I have left it out. I think she was right, and I am still a little sorry about it.

The Lantern That Remembered

Kahfagia — the headland coast

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A lantern turned one degree east. A great-grandmother's oil jug.

Pilot Seref has one eye and the coastline of three nations by heart. She runs a cutter called the Red Wake. Says the sea is a poor liar but an excellent witness.

A young pilot lit a beacon on a headland with too many names to agree on. His father had lit it before him. His grandfather had built it. The stone was warm even in winter, and the wind never blew the same direction twice.

It was his first night alone. His father had died the week before — not at sea, but in his bed, with salt on his lips and a question he'd never finished asking.

He trimmed the lamp, checked the mirror, poured oil from his great-grandmother's jug. The flame burned steady, small as a child's fist, throwing light across the channel like a net.

At midnight, the beacon spoke. Not in words — in a pressure behind the eyes, a warmth at the base of the skull, the sense of something old turning its attention toward him the way a tide turns toward a gap in the rocks.

"I have seen this coast for three hundred years," it said. "I remember every ship that broke. I remember every pilot who listened. I remember the ones who did not."

The pilot's hands did not tremble. His father had taught him that much. "The light does not fear the dark," his father used to say. "The dark is just the light's older sister. They argue. They always make up."

"What do you want?" the pilot asked.

The flame flickered — not from wind, but from something inside the glass. "I want someone to listen. Then I want someone to change the light. The channel shifts. The wrecks move. I cannot move myself. I am stone. I am patient. I am also tired."

The pilot listened — to wrecks he'd never seen, crews that had drowned singing, storms that came from nowhere. Then he climbed the cold ladder, reached the lantern, and turned it one degree east.

The beacon fell silent — not an angry silence, a satisfied one, the silence of a stone finally settled after a long rain.

The next morning, a ship that would have wrecked passed safely. He didn't wave. He didn't cheer. He simply listened to the silence, which was not empty, which had never been empty.

He was promoted to a higher tower and a better lamp. He never told anyone about the voice. But every time he lit a new beacon, for a new generation of sailors who would never know his name, he spoke to it first.

"I am listening," he said. "Tell me when to move. Tell me what you remember. Tell me the names of the ones who did not listen, so I do not become one of them."

And the beacons — some of them — answered.

"Speak to a beacon before you light it, and say aloud that you are listening. Then turn it when it tells you to, one degree, no more. A light you never consult will make its argument to the sea instead, and the sea will side with the older voice."— the Witch's Closing

The Coin That Came Back

Zakov — the free waters

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A silver coin taken from a dead man's eye, stamped with a ship that has no name.

Old Salt has drowned twice. The sea returned him both times and kept his left ear the second time, which he considers a fair settlement and will explain at length if you let him.

A smuggler found a coin resting in a dead man's eye — not pressed into the socket, resting there, warm as a fresh egg, as if the dead man had been saving it for someone. Silver, stamped with a ship that had no name, sails furled, masts bare. He pried it loose with two fingers. The eye did not close. The dead man did not stop looking.

He took it. A smuggler who won't take a coin from a corpse has never been hungry.

That night, the coin bought him safe passage past a patrol. The watchman glanced at it and waved him through without a word, without a toll, without remembering the ship had ever passed at all.

The next day, the dead man's ghost sat cross-legged in his cargo hold, hands folded, eye sockets empty but somehow still watching. "You took my toll," it said, its voice the sound of water finding a crack in a hull. "Now you owe me passage."

The smuggler tried to sell the coin. No one would take it. Merchants paled at the sight of it. A priest of the sea held it a long moment and handed it back, hand shaking.

He threw it overboard. It rose straight up through the water like a bubble and landed on the deck at his feet with a sound like a bell.

He gave it to a beggar. The beggar returned it at dawn, pale as milk. "It followed me," was all he'd say. "It knows your name."

The smuggler sailed to a drowned shrine and set the coin on the altar. The ghost appeared again, legs dangling off the edge like a child waiting for a parent. "Not enough. The shrine is not where I died. You must carry me to the water that took me. You must carry me home."

He sailed to a reef of broken ships and bleached coral, water the color of bruises, and dropped the coin in. It did not float back. It sank, slow as a dying star. The ghost smiled — thin, relieved — took the coin, and vanished.

The smuggler sailed home with a hold full of fish, more than his nets should have held, species he'd never seen, scales the color of sunset. He sold them. He grew rich. He never picked up a coin from a dead man's eye again.

But sometimes, on still nights, he hears a coin spinning on the deck. He does not look. He knows better. A coin that has been returned is not a coin that has been forgotten. It is a coin that is waiting.

I heard this coin again, much later and much further south, in a city where it runs the other way: there it is found in a gutter rather than an eye, and it buys a hungry woman a meal instead of buying a rich man a ghost, and if she fails to spend it by sundown it spends her. I have set that version down in the Dust Cycle, among the tales of the uncounted. Two tellers, two coins, the same rule underneath: a coin that arrives free has already been made out to somebody.

"A coin off a dead man's eye is a ticket, and the ticket is made out to him. Carry it to the water that drowned him and drop it there. Not a shrine. Not a beggar. Not the deep. The water that did it. Anywhere else and it comes back, and it will keep coming back long after you have stopped looking down."— the Witch's Closing

The Merry Crew of the Laughing Eel

Zakov — Port Shed and the Drowned Quarter

About 5 minutes read aloud  ·  A warm coin the size of a palm, half skull and half king. One thrown back every year.

A Sidhi deckhand, the last of a crew that once numbered twelve, told me this while coiling rope and did not stop coiling to tell it.

You want a story about Zakov? Fine. But don't say I didn't warn you.

Three seasons back I shipped with Captain Mira "the Lucky" aboard the Laughing Eel. She was a Sidhi woman with a smile that could sell sand to a djinn and a debt to every dockmaster in Port Shed. Her crew was a dozen misfits, deserters, and dreamers who could not hold a real job because they were too busy pretending the world was funny. We laughed at everything — a snapped anchor chain, a stew that grew legs, a tax collector we fed expired rum until he signed our manifest as "inspection passed." It was merry. It was stupid. It was the best time I ever had.

Then we found the coin.

It was a calm night in the Drowned Quarter, where the tide covers the streets twice a day and the buildings lean like drunk uncles. We were offloading cargo that wasn't ours to sell when our eel-girl, Fen, dove off the pier to cool off. She came up screaming — not in pain, in wonder. "The bottom of the slip is all silver," she said. "Coins. Hundreds. Maybe thousands." We dove. It was true. We filled our pockets, our boots, our mouths, laughing the whole time, water in our lungs and silver in our fists. Captain Mira held up one coin, big as a palm, stamped with a face half skull and half king. "This is going to buy us a new ship," she said. The coin was warm. None of us noticed.

No one argued — not out of bravery, but because the coin in her palm was whispering our names.

The sea turned strange. Waters that should have run green ran black. Gulls stood on the rigging with eyes too human to look away from. Fen stopped eating, said the food tasted like memory, and hummed a tune none of us recognized. That night the Drowned Prince came aboard, crowned in barnacles, his voice the sound of a ship breaking on rocks. "You took my salt. My coin. My silence. I have waited three hundred years for someone to remember me." Captain Mira stepped forward, shaking, still Captain Mira the Lucky. "We'll give it back," she said. "All of it. With interest." The prince laughed — the most terrible sound I have ever heard — and said he liked that word.

He let us live, on one condition: every year, on the same night, we return to that reef and throw in a coin that has been paid for a life — a coin that bought someone's freedom, a coin that has seen blood. If we forget, he will come again, and next time he will not ask for interest. We have kept the bargain every year since. But Fen — Fen who dreamed of drowning, who hummed the nameless tune — took a boat out alone last spring and did not return. The coin on her pillow was warm. She had paid the interest for all of us.

Now we laugh quieter. Now we check the water before we sail. Now we remember that Zakov is not a city of merry pirates. It is a city of debts that live under the waves, waiting for you to forget.

"A crew that laughs at everything has simply not yet met the one debt that laughs back. The sea keeps no count of its own. It borrows the Prince's, and the Prince has never once mislaid a name."— the Witch's Closing

The City-States

The city offers another sort of shelter: a mask, a stair, a marriage. Each has a door that someone must keep.

The Reckoner's Mask

Ecktoria — the assembly cities

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A bronze mask that will not come off, and a hammer that does not help.

An old clerk feeds the pigeons by the plaza fountain. Children dare each other to ask him about the mask, and he always answers, which is how you know he was the worst of them.

The Reckoner of Ecktoria wears a mask — bronze, old, eyeholes dark as a well at noon. It does not cover the face. It replaces it. He wears it only at the Assizes, when the city brings its petitions and its lies.

One year a merchant offered a bribe. A bag of silver. The Reckoner refused. The merchant doubled it. Refused again.

"Name your price," the merchant whispered.

The Reckoner removed the mask.

The merchant screamed. There was no face beneath. Only a hollow space, and in it, a whisper: I have seen your ledgers. Every page. Every missing grain.

The merchant fled. The Reckoner put the mask back on. He wore it again at the next Assizes.

But that night he dreamed of his own lies — the grain he'd skimmed from the public store, the witness he'd ignored because the bribe was smaller but sweeter. The mask had seen him too. The mask saw everyone. It simply waited.

He tried to remove it. The straps would not loosen. He cut them. The mask stayed. He heated the bronze. It cooled around his jaw. He broke a piece off with a hammer. By morning it had grown back.

For a year he wore it. He could not eat. He could not drink. He did not starve — the mask fed on his guilt, and he felt the hunger but could not die from it, suspended between confession and silence like a fly in amber.

At the next Assizes, he walked to the plaza and knelt before the assembly. "I am unworthy," he said. "I have stolen. I have lied. I have worn this mask as armor when it was meant to be a mirror. Judge me."

The mask fell off.

His face beneath was not old. It was young. His missing eye had grown back. The brand on his cheek was gone, as if the skin had forgotten the iron.

He served thirty more years — the most honest Reckoner the city ever had. He never wore the mask again. He didn't need to. It had taught him where to look.

The Stair That Was Not

Thepyrgos — the Collegium of Keys

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A thesis returned with corrections in a hand nobody recognises. A warm piece of chalk.

Scholar Lyra does not leave the library and reads by candle. She holds that there is a ninth aisle nobody has found. She has been looking for forty years. The candles are getting shorter and she has noticed.

A student climbed a stair in the Collegium of Keys. He had counted the steps: one through eight. Then the ninth. There was no ninth. The stair continued anyway.

He should have stopped. A stair that does not know how many steps it has is a stair unsure it exists. But he was young, his thesis was due, and he'd been awake three nights. The body forgets to be afraid when the mind is drowning.

He climbed until his legs ached. The walls changed from stone to vellum to glass — old foundations, unbound manuscripts, a window looking out on a courtyard long since burned away. The steps became sentences. The banisters became arguments.

At the top, a desk. No chair, no lamp. Just the desk, the dust, and a single page — his own thesis, submitted three days before, the one his advisor had nodded at and said nothing about.

It was covered in corrections. Not his advisor's hand — a hand he didn't recognize, letters small as a gnat's wing. But he could read them. That was the worst part. They were right. Every crossed-out line, every margin note that said this is where you lied to yourself, and you knew this was wrong when you wrote it.

He had not been wrong about the data. He had been wrong about the question. He'd asked the wrong thing. The stair showed him what he should have asked instead.

He wept — not from sadness, but because the corrections were kind. Whoever had written them had taken the time to explain, had drawn small diagrams in the margins, had crossed out a paragraph and written, in letters no larger than a whisper, "Try this instead. It will hurt less when you read it in the morning."

When he looked up, the desk was gone. The stair was gone. He was standing in the basement, holding a piece of chalk. It was warm. It had been used recently.

He never told anyone what the corrections said. His next thesis frightened the examiners into silence. The archivists bound it in leather and set it on the highest shelf, where the dust falls upward and the light does not reach.

He never climbed a stair again without counting twice. He never stopped writing, either. Every page he wrote after that night carried a small mark in the corner — a dot so tiny only he could see it. A reminder that somewhere, in a room that existed and did not exist, someone was still reading. Still taking notes. Still being kind.

"Count a stair on the way up. If there is no ninth step and the stair goes on regardless, you may climb it — but only if you are willing to be corrected, and only if you have brought something of your own to be corrected. Arrive empty-handed and it will find something else of yours to mark up."— the Witch's Closing

The Bride of Nine Knots

Vhasia — the red towers

About 2 minutes read aloud  ·  A dowry rope tied with nine knots. A silver locket. A cracked yellow tooth.

A bride tied her dowry rope with nine knots — one for each year she'd waited for her betrothed while he fought in the southern passes. The priest warned her: "Eight is custom. Nine is a door. Do not tie what you cannot untie."

She tied it anyway.

The morning of the wedding, her rival — who had also loved the groom, who had also waited, whose own dowry rope held only seven knots — stepped forward and cut the ninth with a blade of bone.

The groom forgot his vows. Not slowly. Not gently. He stood at the altar, mouth half open, hands empty, eyes the color of a winter river. He simply stopped.

The bride did not weep. She stepped around him, took his cold hand, and spoke for him. "I give this ring. I take this name. I close this door. He is not here to refuse. That is not my problem. That is yours."

The bride kept the severed knot in a silver locket. She wore it fifty years. She never opened it. She never needed to.

On her deathbed she gave the locket to her daughter. "If you ever need to silence a liar," she said, "cut a knot. But be sure you know whose tongue will still speak afterward. The blade does not ask whose blood it spills."

The daughter opened the locket. The knot was gone. Inside was a single tooth — the rival's, yellowed, cracked.

The daughter closed the locket. She did not ask.

"The ninth knot is the thread that holds the story closed. Cut it, and the story unravels — but not always in the direction you expect. The blade remembers who swung it."— the Witch's Closing

The Veil and the Cradle

Ashaan — the palace cities

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  A pale blue veil, later a shroud. A poisoned blade. A cedar chest kept shut.

The Chancellor's widow keeps the mask on indoors and speaks through a curtain. Nobody has seen her face in twenty years. She says the one underneath is not the one she was born with. She does not say how it changed.

Pity in that household was a dangerous thing. It was also the only honest thing.

"Who are you?" he asked.

She opened her mouth. After three years of silence she'd almost forgotten how to shape words. But she found them. "The one who was never there."

Then she died. The poison was fast. He did not have time to call a healer. He did not have time to learn her name.

The magistrate had the veil woven into a shroud for her — the same silk that had hidden her, now the only thing to touch her skin. He kept it the rest of his life, folded in a cedar chest, never opened. He never married again. He told his advisors he had seen the face of mercy, and mercy had not worn silk. The assassin was never found. His enemies remembered the veil differently afterward: a hiding place, certainly, but also a cradle, and sometimes a witness no one has thought to silence.

"Give a veil to someone you mean to protect and understand what else you have given her: a place to stand where no one counts her. She will see everything. She will be able to say nothing. The Ford That Moved

Dhahara — the river roads

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  A single grain of wheat. A name paid aloud once a year. An empty seat at the stern.

Ferryman Dav has poled the same ford for fifty years. The river has changed course three times in that span and he can no longer say with confidence which change was which, though he can tell you what it cost each time. He keeps the bench at the stern of his boat clear, always, and greets it first when he pushes off — before the passengers, before the water. When I asked who sat there he said, pleasantly, that in Dhahara one does not leave a guest unaddressed merely because the others cannot see the guest, and that the courtesy costs a man nothing and has, on two occasions he would rather not detail, cost him considerably less than the alternative. No other ferryman on my road did this. Several thought it funny that anyone would.

A caravan master needed to cross a ford. The river ran high, the stones slick, the current muttering to itself like breaking glass. He'd crossed it a hundred times. This time felt different. This time the water was watching.

He knelt and prayed to the ford itself — not the river, not any god of rivers, but the place where water met stone and let travelers pass. He offered a single grain of wheat.

"I will stay still for one crossing," the ford whispered, the voice coming from everywhere and nowhere. "Then you will owe me a name."

He crossed. The ford held. He reached the far shore dry-booted.

The next night he returned. The river was deeper, swifter, darker. The ford had moved — the crossing now a hundred paces downstream, the old path a whirlpool wide enough to drown a horse.

"Name a price," he said.

"You have already named it," the ford replied, softer now, almost kind. "The name of your firstborn child. The name you whisper when you think no one is listening. The name you have kept folded in your chest like a letter you are afraid to send."

The merchant had no child. His wife had died that winter. He'd buried her with his own hands and not wept then. He wept now, and the sobs echoed off the water and came back twisted, mocking.

The ford softened. The current slowed. The stones rose just enough to offer a path.

"Then give me the name you would have given them. The name that died in your throat when your wife died. Give me that, and every year, on this night, return and speak it again. That is the toll. That is always the toll."

He knelt, pressed his forehead to the wet stones, and whispered a name no one had ever heard — a name that existed only between his heartbeat and his breath.

Dav stopped the telling here to say something the tale does not need and I have kept anyway. In Dhahara, he said, a crossing party is counted before it enters the water and counted again on the far bank, and both counts are said aloud, and both counts are one higher than the number of people standing there. This is not a mistake. It is a post, and the man who holds it is paid: a ferryman who keeps the ninth place is given a measure of grain at the year's turn by every household whose people he has carried, and nobody in the district considers this charity. Elsewhere on my road the ninth is a thing you count around, or step over, or do not say. On the river roads it is a thing you keep a seat for and thank by name. Dav could not tell me why his country alone does this and everyone else's does not. He seemed to think the question was the wrong way round.

The ford calmed. He crossed safely.

He returned every year and spoke the name. The ford drank it whole. His wife did not come back. But some years later he remarried, and his new wife bore a daughter, and he named her the same name. The ford never troubled him again. But the daughter sometimes woke from dreams she could not remember, salt on her lips and water in her ears. She never learned to swim. She never wanted to.

"Pray to the ford, not to the river. They are not the same and the ford knows it. Name the toll out loud before you step in — all of it, in words you would be ashamed to have overheard — and then come back every year on that night and say it again. A grain of wheat opens the conversation. It does not close it."— the Witch's Closing

The Bell That Never Rang

The Mistlands — the levee towers

About 3 minutes read aloud  ·  A bell that has never been rung, and a clapper held in a dead man's hands.

Bell-warden Hollis has soot under the nails and a cough that does not finish. He tells it flat, the way they do on that levee — no grandeur, nothing made larger than it was, and the worst thing in the tale delivered in the same voice as the weather. Asked why he keeps no assistant, he said: "The last one asked too many questions. The bell answered one. Neither of us liked the answer."

A bell was cast for a tower in the Mistlands — bronze, etched with ward-runes so dense the metal seemed to hold its breath. The smith who forged it lost his hearing the day of the casting, not to the noise, but to the silence that followed.

"Ring it once," the smith said, "and the fog retreats to the edges of the valley. Ring it twice, and the dead listen — they do not obey, but sometimes listening is enough. Ring it thrice, and the deep wood speaks. I cannot tell you what it says. I was not brave enough to stay for the third ring."

The warden hung the bell, polished it, oiled the clapper, and counted the hours without sound. The villagers grew used to the silence. They forgot the bell was there.

When the fog rose from the river, thick and gray and smelling of wet ash, the villagers pleaded. "Ring the bell!"

He refused. "It is not time. The fog is testing us. If we ring too soon, it will learn."

When a child wandered into the mist chasing a fox that was not a fox, they begged at the tower door. "Ring it! The dead will help us find her."

He refused. "The dead do not help. They collect. If I ring twice, they will come. They will find her. But they will not bring her back. They will ask what she is worth. I do not know how to answer that."

The child was never found. The villagers never forgot, but they did not stop pleading.

When three gray wraiths came, tall as pines and silent as the bottom of a well, the villagers fled with their children and livestock. They did not look back.

The warden stayed. He climbed the tower, cough following him step by step, and stood beside the bell with his arms spread, saying nothing. The wraiths climbed after him. They did not touch him. They circled the bell and waited. He waited longer.

At dawn the wraiths withdrew. The fog lifted. The villagers returned and found him dead, hands clasped around the clapper, knuckles white, mouth open in a silence that matched the bell's own. He had kept it silent even as his breath left him, even as the dead girl's voice called from the mist, asking him to ring it just once so she could find her way home.

He had not answered her. That was the part the villagers did not forgive.

They buried him under the tower. The bell still hangs. No one has rung it. The dead girl's voice still calls on certain nights, when the fog rises and the fox runs. She does not ask for the bell anymore. She asks for the warden.

The Bargain of the Unlit Lamp

Trow — the crossroads folk

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  A left-hand glove folded small as a coin. A brass lamp that burns without oil.

A Trow gave me this and gave no name, smoking a pipe with no ember and no stem, in the corner of a tavern where its hat drank the light. It told it the way the crossroads folk tell anything — in trade terms, item by item, as though reading out a receipt. I wrote down what I heard. By morning the ink had gone and I set it down again from a memory that was not entirely mine.
Retold, and of the three retellings here this is the one I trust least. I do not think the words are mine. I am no longer sure they are its, either.

A traveler came to a crossroads at midnight — not the kind where two roads simply meet, but the older kind, where the path between the living and the hollow crosses itself, where the shadows lean the wrong way. A figure waited there in a gray robe and a hat that drank the starlight.

"I am a Trow," it said. "We trade in absence. We deal in the spaces where things used to be. What do you lack?"

The traveler lacked nothing. He was wealthy, healthy, happy, loved — a wife who smiled when he entered a room, children who ran to the gate, a dog that slept on his feet, a house with a door that swung true.

"Then sell me something you do not need," said the Trow. "A memory you have not used in years. A breath you were going to waste. A name you have never spoken aloud."

The traveler laughed, thin and small. He pulled off his left-hand glove — a good glove, but he had another to match — and held it out. The Trow took it, folded it small as a coin, and tucked it into a sleeve that did not bulge.

In return it gave him a lamp — small, brass, unlit, a wick that had never seen flame. "The light will not fade," it said. "It will burn forever. It will never need oil. But you will forget why you needed it. The light remembers. You will not."

The traveler took the lamp and walked home. Its light guided him, steady and warm, showing every root and stone. But at his gate he paused. The paint was the right color, but the shape was wrong. He opened it anyway. Inside, a woman looked up from the hearth, her face kind, her hair gray. He did not know her.

"Who are you?" he asked.

"I am your wife," she said.

"I do not have a wife." He meant it. He could not remember ever being loved. The lamp burned steadily in his hand, warm as a heartbeat, patient as stone.

He turned back to the crossroads. The Trow was gone. Only his glove lay in the dust, unfolded, full-sized, as if dropped by someone in a hurry. He put it on. The lamp went out. The dark was absolute. He sat in it a long time, remembering — his wife's face, her name, the way she laughed when he tripped over the dog, the dog itself. Everything the lamp had taken. And that he had given it willingly, and laughed while he did.

He left the lamp at the crossroads and walked home in the dark, not looking back. It was still there the next morning, and the next. No one took it. The Trow did not return.

He never bought from a Trow again. But on winter nights, when the wind was right, he would stand at his gate and look toward the crossroads. He could not see the lamp. It was too far. But he could feel it — waiting, burning, remembering everything he had been willing to forget.

"Trow trade in gaps. The gap that light fills is the gap where memory used to be. Do not buy a lamp from a creature that wears a hat made of starlight. The seller does not need your glove. The seller needs your forgetting."— the Witch's Closing

The Soup That Was Free

Hag Morag — the dry well

About 4 minutes read aloud  ·  A kettle with no fire under it. A borrowed breath, repayable at your own hearth.

The one who calls herself Hag Morag sits by a dry well stirring a pot with no fire under it. The soup is hot. The well is dry. She says the pot has been stirring itself longer than the village has stood. She does not explain further. She asked me three times whether I was hungry, in the way those hags ask three times, and I said no three times, which I understand to have been the correct number.

A starving family came to a cottage that was not their own — they had lost their own to fire, or to a tax collector, or to a winter that would not end; the story changed depending on who told it. What did not change was the hunger.

A crone answered the door, eyes the color of old milk, hands the color of bark. "I have soup," she said. "It is free. The first bowl is always free. I did not make the rule. The rule was here before me."

They ate — rich, meaty, warmer than any soup they had known. They did not ask what was in it. Hungry people do not ask. Hungry people eat.

The crone watched, unblinking. "The soup is free. The first bowl is free. But the seconds will cost you."

The father spread his hands. "We have no coin. What could we possibly have that you would want?"

"I do not need coin. I am not a merchant. I am a cook. I need a breath — one from each of you. Not your last. I am not the Pale Shepherd. I take only what you have to spare. I will give it back when you next share a meal at your own hearth, cooked with your own hands."

They agreed. They breathed into her kettle, one long exhalation each, watching the fog curl and vanish into her shawl. The kettle steamed. The crone nodded.

That night they dreamed they were drowning — not in water, but in something thicker, tasting of salt and old meat and the memory of hunger. They woke gasping, breath shorter than the day before.

They returned. "The debt is not due yet," the crone said. "Eat again. The second bowl costs another breath. The third costs two. The fourth costs four. The rule is clear. The bowl does not lie."

They ate anyway — the father from fear, the mother from exhaustion, the children because the soup was warm and the night was cold. For a year they ate free soup once a week, and each time the crone took a breath, and each time the sleep grew deeper and the dreams more vivid.

On the night of the forty-eighth bowl, with the father's breath coming in rasps and the children sleeping sixteen hours at a stretch, he finally said: "We cannot pay. We have no breath left. What happens when we have nothing left to give?"

The crone's eyes softened for the first time. "Good. There you are. The soup opened my door. Your borrowed breaths kept it open. Truth is what lets you leave. Go home. Cook your own meals. Breathe your own air. The kettle will remember you. It will not follow. It does not need to. You know where it lives."

They went home lighter — not the lightness of a full belly, but of a held note finally sung. Their breath returned. Their sleep cleared.

They never ate free soup again. But on certain winter nights, when the wind was right, they could smell it drifting from the direction of the dry well. They did not follow the smell. They had learned. The soup was free. The seconds were not.

The soup is the oldest thing in this book and the most widely travelled. Hag Morag is not the only one stirring: the hearth-menders of the burnings say it over a pot to a girl with a new name, as a welcome, and the Daughters of the Cord say it to a woman in labour, as a term of business. Same eight words, three hands. I have printed the other two where I found them. I do not know which of the three said it first and I have stopped expecting to.

"Free soup is a hook. The hook is not the soup. The hook is the second bowl — the one you eat when you are not hungry, because you are afraid of being hungry again. She does not need your coin. She needs your breath. And your breath, once given, is never quite your own again."— the Witch's Closing
A last note from the Raven Road

I set these out in order last week, to see what I had, and found I had gathered a book of things deliberately kept empty.

A barrel a peddler keeps with nothing in it, just in case. A ninth cup poured and not drunk, and in the mill country a ninth cup poured and meant, and nobody able to tell me which is the older practice. A well with no water and a pot with no fire under it, both of them hot. A seat at the stern of a ferry that a man greets before he greets his passengers. A bell that has never once been rung, in a tower built for the ringing. A locket worn fifty years and never opened. A scroll that is blank. A lamp with no oil that will burn for ever, bought with a glove that had a pair.

I do not know whether that is what the road is like, or what I am like. I have been asking people about doors for eleven years and it did not once occur to me to ask why. A second time through this book is not the same book, is all I will say about it. Go back to the ford, and to the peddler, and see whether they are still about what you thought they were about.

— D.

Colophon

The tales gathered here are drawn chiefly from the fuller Hearth Cycle — a longer collection of one folk tale per region I crossed on the road, twenty-four in all — with one exception noted below. What follows was chosen for how well each one stands alone at a table: for the heat it still holds once the fire has burned to coals, and for the spread of the telling, so that no one corner of the map crowds out the rest. Each has had a light hand run over it — a loose thread trimmed, a stumbled word set right, a name made consistent with itself — but no tale has been rebuilt. The voices that told them are still the ones you hear.

Regions represented: Aeler, Ykrul, and Vilikari (twice over) of the high country; Aelaerem, Oshiira, and Viterra of the hearth and harvest lands; the Lethai Dominion's al, ar, and thora branches; Linn, Kahfagia, and Zakov (twice over) of the coast; Ecktoria, Thepyrgos, and Vhasia among the city-states; Ashaan and Dhahara of the southern courts; and the Mistlands, Trow, and Hag Morag of the threshold places.

Five regions from the fuller cycle were left out of this collection for reasons of pacing and repetition rather than quality — among them a second telling from Vhasia's neighboring hall, which had wandered so close to the tale already told here that keeping both would have cost the book more than it gave. The second Zakov tale, "The Merry Crew of the Laughing Eel," is the one exception to the Hearth Cycle's authorship: it is drawn instead from the Salt Prince expansion, and earned its place here because a coin that curses one smuggler and a debt that drowns a whole crew are the same warning, told at two different scales. Readers who want the complete cycle, notes and all, should look to the full Hearth Cycle expansion.

On what was changed in this edition. Four tales lost their closing line and were left to end where they end: the selkie at the fisherman's silence, the Reckoner at the last thing the mask taught him, the bell-warden at the girl who now asks for him by name, and — a different case — the ford-side joke, which never had one. Where a closing restated what the tale had already shown, it has been replaced with the rule the tale enacts, on the principle that a gamemaster can hand a rule to an NPC and can do nothing at all with a moral. Two tales were added: Viterra's dispute, which is the only account in the book that says the count of eight is a fiction, and which is printed in both its versions because its two tellers are siblings who will not concede; and the goat, which is here for no reason except that it is funny and the road is not only sad. Three tales are marked as retellings in their bylines. One of those is marked twice. The rest are as they were given.
