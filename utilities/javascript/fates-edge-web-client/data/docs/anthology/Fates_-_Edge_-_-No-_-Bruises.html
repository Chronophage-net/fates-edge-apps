No Bruises

A Novella of Silkstrand

The autobiography of Madam Serafine, Mistress of the Velvet Court.

A word before we undress

Put my name on it large enough to be read across a shop.

The portrait may be the old one. The mouth is badly drawn, but the painter is dead and his widow has learned to charge accordingly. Leave the mouth. People who remember me before the veil will enjoy knowing better. People who do not will imagine something expensive.

I have been advised to omit the unpleasant parts.

By a man whose wife sent me a ham last winter, in gratitude for an unpleasant part I performed on her behalf. He ate most of it. There was a note about the rind.

You see the difficulty.

I would rather tell you myself. I know the names. I know where the second door was. When somebody says a woman did a terrible thing with a smile, I can usually tell you whether she was smiling, or holding a pin between her teeth.

There will be deaths. I am sorry about several. There will be women who should have known better, men who did know better, and a respectable quantity of other people's money. You may dislike me. A great many people do, particularly before they have met me.

Have something to eat while you decide. The little pastries are eel. Everyone asks whether they are eel after eating three.

And let us have one understanding. If you came for the night I gave Livaea, you will leave disappointed. I cannot remember it. I have sold three accounts of it, all different, all excellent, and the most expensive was the least plausible. This one costs you the price printed on the cover. You may have what I know.

Bring the lamp closer. I do not like talking to a man I cannot see.

PART IThe girl under the stall

The first purse was yellow goatskin. There were two white stitches in the bottom where the leather had split. I put my finger through one and felt a coin.

I was seven. He smelled of cloves. When he laughed he leaned backward and the purse came forward, swinging from the belt beneath his belly. He was laughing at a boy who had dropped a tray of cups. The boy was trying to gather the pieces before the stallkeeper hit him again.

I got the purse off with a mussel shell.

That is the part people improve. They give me a little knife. I had no knife. A knife was a possession to be taken from you by someone who had two. I had a shell with a good edge and a piece of sack tied around my waist. The shell cut through the cord, then through the side of my finger. I held the purse under my arm and walked away sucking blood.

He never stopped laughing.

I bought bread, smoked sprats and a pear. The pear was a mistake. I had never bought one before and did not know you were allowed to touch it. One side was brown. I ate that side first so the good half would remain.

Behind the dyers' steps I was sick into the canal. The bread went first. I tried to hold the sprats down with my hand over my mouth.

Go on, take another pastry. This is a childhood. They are disagreeable up close.

There were two of us under the stall that autumn. Me and a girl called Tessa, who was a year older and had a whistle through a broken front tooth. She could do a gull with it. I could do the stallkeeper's wife. We performed for each other when the rain came through the boards and there was no prospect of sleep.

The stall sold onions. In the morning we came out smelling of onions and the woman paid us in bruised ones to move the sacks. She knew we slept there. Her husband did not. She would leave a sack hanging over the front when the Watch came down, then shout at us for vermin as soon as they were gone.

Tessa took half the purse money.

Not because she had earned it. Because I showed it to her, and she put her arms around me, and I wanted her to do that again.

We bought a blanket. She chose a red one that leaked fluff. At night we picked the lice out together and crushed them against a stone. She gave each one the name of somebody important. A harbour judge died under her thumbnail. We laughed so hard that the stallkeeper woke up and kicked the boards.

I have slept in beds that took six men to carry upstairs. I remember that blanket.

I remember wanting it to be mine, too.

There was a collector called Barto. He had the right to collect for the stalls, which he had obtained by breaking the previous collector's jaw. The stallkeepers considered this an irregularity for about a week. Afterward they complained that he came too early.

Barto found the purse because Tessa bought shoes.

You must forgive her. They were red. There were buttons down the side. She came back with her old feet poking out of them, all sores and dirt above this astonishing leather, and stood with one foot turned outward like a lady at a fountain.

I wanted to hit her. I wanted the shoes. I wanted her to give me something because she had something. None of these feelings made me less pleased to see her pleased.

Barto lifted her by the ankle and the shoe came off in his hand. She went down hard. I can still hear the little noise of her chin against the step.

He looked inside it, as though the rest of the money might be there.

Then he looked at me.

I pointed at the onion woman's drawer.

It was an excellent drawer. Her husband kept the stall money in a box under the scales. The drawer held what she did not tell him about. I knew because she sometimes gave us a copper from it when the onions had sold well. She had a cough that made her stop in the middle of laughing. She was putting money away to see a physician across the bridge.

I told Barto all of that. Rapidly. Children are very thorough when they are frightened.

He let Tessa go.

He went through the stall. The woman stood with both hands full of onions. When her husband came she put them down. He listened to Barto, opened the drawer, and looked at her for a long time.

We did not sleep beneath the stall again.

Tessa said I should have kept quiet. I asked whether she would rather have lost her other shoe. We had our first proper quarrel, which is to say she hit me and I bit her and afterward we went looking for somewhere dry together.

The woman died that winter. The stall was open the following morning. Her husband sold onions from the physician's drawer.

I did not cause the cough. I have never claimed otherwise about the money.

For years, whenever someone complained that I had ruined them, I remembered her standing with the onions and felt a sharp little irritation. Not grief. Irritation. She had been kind to me. It made a nuisance of remembering her.

I can tell you that now. It has been a very long time. There is no one left to return the money to, and I have endowed two wards at Agatha's hospice. The second has better windows.

Old Kes found us behind a fish smokehouse, trying to dry the blanket over a stolen brazier.

He took the brazier. It belonged to a customer of his. Then he gave us bread and a task, which was to carry a parcel across the Dye Yards without opening it.

Tessa opened it while I watched the alley.

Buttons. Mother-of-pearl. Hundreds. We put them back, all except one that had fallen into a crack. She wanted to leave it. I got it out with the shell.

Kes counted them in front of us. Every button. His left hand was short three fingers, and the two that remained moved with the neatness of a pair of tongs.

He gave us another parcel.

That was my apprenticeship. There was no touching speech. He had parcels and we could run. On wet days he let us sit inside the warehouse where the cheap cloth was kept. Once a month he paid someone to cut our hair. Tessa cried the first time. I stole the hairdresser's comb.

Kes made me give it back.

"He'll come here again," he said.

I thought that a poor reason until the next month, when the man cut around a sore on my scalp so gently that I scarcely felt it. I kept my hands on my knees throughout. It required effort.

Kes taught us to look at the person who wasn't talking. To wrap a broken thing before trying to sell it. To count what went into a warehouse as well as what came out. He would buy stolen goods, but not a dead worker's tools. He called certain men thieves in a tone that suggested an entirely different profession from ours.

He did not make a habit of telling us he loved us. He kept bread where we could reach it.

When I was twelve, a customs man put a hook through a runner's cheek for swallowing a pass. Kes paid to have it taken out. He sat beside the bed and fed the boy broth through the gap on the other side. I watched him do it. I wanted somebody to do that for me if it became necessary.

I also noticed how the other runners watched him.

There. You want the beginning of the woman? It is both those things. You may put your finger on the second if it pleases you. The first was there.

Tessa went to work in a bindery. At fourteen she had wages, a place in the attic, and hands permanently red from the dye used on cheap covers. She called it honest work and laughed when she said honest. The binder took a portion for the bed, another for meals, and charged her for a sheet of paper if she spoiled it. She stole the paper instead.

I sold it for her.

That is where I met ink. Until then a written thing was something you carried without knowing whom it would hurt. Tessa showed me a debt notice and read it aloud while I followed her finger. Her nails were bitten almost to the quick. She made the words sound like something foul she had found in a shoe.

I learned my name first.

Yes, Serafine. It was mine before I could write it. No priest bestowed it on me. No dead lady left it conveniently unattended. My mother had taste, whoever she was. You need not recover her from a cellar to explain me.

Tessa wrote the letters on an offcut. I copied them until the curves stopped collapsing. We drank small beer from the same cup. By morning I could make a name that looked as if it belonged to someone with a bedroom.

The binder caught us before dawn.

He hit Tessa for using the lamp. I had the shell in my pocket. By then I owned a knife, but I kept the shell. I went for him and he caught my wrist and bent it backward until my knees gave.

He said he would call the Watch.

I told him about the sheets Tessa had been selling. Who bought them. What I had been paid. Where the money was hidden.

He loosened his hand.

It was becoming a habit.

Tessa lost the attic and two teeth. I got out with my wrist intact and a piece of paper on which I had written my name eleven times.

She would not speak to me for a year.

When we met again she had learned to make teeth out of bone. Not for herself; she could not afford her own work. She was doing quite well, she said, and asked if I knew a lady who wanted a comb.

I found her six.

PART IIA better class of dirt

At nineteen I could write three sorts of hand, pick an ordinary lock, and eat a meal without bending an elbow over the plate to defend it. That last accomplishment cost the most.

I worked for a notary's copyist called Pel, who owned one good coat and treated it as a senior partner. He wore it to meet clients. When there were no clients he hung it where he could see it, then sat in his shirtsleeves complaining about the weather.

Pel paid me to copy inventories. I paid Tessa to alter the order in which the copies were bound. A page put between the wrong covers could spend years out of reach of the person who wanted it. We charged extra for making it turn up promptly afterward.

She had forgiven me. Or she wanted the work. We did not discuss the distinction.

I had a room above a poulterer. In summer the heat brought feathers through the floorboards. They stuck to my wet stockings. I would peel one away while dressing for dinner and wonder whether people born above a certain height ever had to do such things.

There was a woman at those dinners called Irena Voss. No relation to any Voss you know. She corrected people who assumed one, which I thought wasteful. Her father had left her a ribbon shop and enough resentment to run it profitably.

She was twenty-six. I was twenty-two when she first put her hand on my knee beneath the table and asked whether I meant to spend the entire evening looking at her.

"Is it bothering you?"

"Your restraint is beginning to."

She wore dark green. I remember because I could never find the exact shade afterward, though I asked several dyers to make it and made a nuisance of myself. The dress was plain at the throat. When she leaned to refill my glass I saw a small pale mark just below her ear, where an earring had caught.

We left separately and met around the corner. She kissed me against the shutter of a closed bakery. I had prepared something clever to say and lost it when she laughed against my mouth.

At her house she asked whether I wanted to stay. I said yes too quickly. She seemed to like that.

There was hot water. I cannot give you a proper account of my first great love without the hot water. A servant brought it, covered, and went away; it remained ours to waste. I washed slowly. Irena sat on the bed pulling the pins from her hair. She had no hurry in her at all.

In the morning I stole a little silver spoon.

I did not need it. I had wages in my room. I had eaten until I was pleasantly uncomfortable. She was asleep with one hand open on the sheet, and there was the spoon beside the coffee, and I put it in my stocking.

You have been waiting for me to confess something I cannot explain. There it is. I liked the spoon. I liked her. I took it.

Pel got into trouble over a ship that had arrived without the cargo entered on its manifest.

It was my manifest. A handsome piece of work. I had allowed for differences in the inks and rubbed the folds with a little soot. The ship's owner would have sailed comfortably through the inspection if his nephew had not tried to sell a case of the missing cargo to the inspector's brother.

The nephew fled. The owner declared himself deceived. Pel put on the good coat and went to explain.

He came back without the coat. There was blood in his ear. He sat down on the floor of the office and leaned against a cupboard, and I could hear his breath snagging on something inside him.

"They want the hand," he said.

I packed my things while he watched.

Irena let me in. I told her I needed somewhere for one night. She did not ask why until I had eaten. I appreciated that; I have found it worth remembering in my own houses.

When I told her, she said, "You can stay."

I began with the reasons it would be dangerous for her. I was very eloquent. Then I noticed she was not enjoying my account of her courage as much as I was, and stopped.

"What do they have?" she asked.

"Pel."

"What does Pel have?"

"My name. My hand. The little room."

"Then tonight won't be enough."

She knew a clerk at the counting-house of Dey and Sorn. He owed her for ribbons supplied to a lady whose husband believed her expenses were smaller. The clerk could get me out through the harbour in a grain boat. A berth, papers, enough money to start elsewhere.

I asked what Dey and Sorn had to do with the missing cargo.

She went very still.

"You are leaving," she said.

"They insure it."

"Serafine."

"The owner gets paid twice. Once for what he sold. Once for what he says he lost."

"Yes. Now put on the brown coat."

I had been copying their forms for two years. I knew the sort of books they kept. The owner's claim would be there. His real payments might be there too, if he was stupid enough to use the same house for both. He was a man who had employed his nephew. I thought the risk reasonable.

Irena sat down on the edge of the bed.

"You could be safe tomorrow," she said.

I can hear her still. Not pleading. Trying to understand why she was having to say it twice.

I put on the brown coat.

I went to Dey and Sorn.

The door at the back had a new lock.

That is how most disasters begin. Something cheap has been replaced by something better. The man who ordered it goes home satisfied and never learns what an inconvenience he has caused.

I could open it. I needed time. The watchman crossed the yard every few minutes, and there was a cook asleep beyond the kitchen door who snored until she stopped, at which point I stopped as well and waited for her to begin again.

Rain slid down the back of my collar. I could smell the drains and yesterday's onions. Somewhere above me a woman was singing one of the songs you hear in pleasure houses when the good clients have gone and the staff are washing up.

I hummed the space after the refrain.

Kes had taught me that much of Ikasha. You give the silence its place. You do not push a tune through it because you are nervous. I held the pick still. The watchman's lamp crossed the end of the passage, stopped, and moved on.

Then the pick broke.

I had half a piece of iron in the lock, half in my hand, and a boat waiting on the ebb that I had already missed.

The singing stopped.

"That was a very pretty little prayer," a woman said. "Did it get you in?"

She stood in the kitchen doorway, barefoot, with an apple in her hand. There had been a sleeping cook behind that door. There might still have been. The woman bit the apple. Juice ran over her thumb.

"I would like to leave," I said.

"Would you?"

She held out the apple. I did not take it.

"Your friend has a room ready," she said. "There is a boat. There was a boat. You could still catch it, if somebody were kind."

I looked at the broken pick.

"But there are books in here," she said, "and you know how to read them."

She had a bit of apple skin caught between two teeth. I remember wanting to remove it. I remember the wish with an unpleasant clarity. The door, the prison, Pel on the floor, all of them had become less urgent than the small red strip against the white tooth.

She smiled and it was gone.

"One night," she said.

"For what?"

"Everything you have come for."

"Which night?"

"You will know until you don't."

The kitchen smelled of bread. I had eaten at Irena's less than an hour before. My stomach hurt with wanting it.

"Will you get me out?"

"Yes."

"And the books?"

"Yes."

I asked for Pel. I want that included. I did ask.

She looked amused.

"You may have enough to fetch him. What you do with enough will be your affair."

I took the apple.

There is a taste in the world I have never found again. I have employed cooks from five coasts. They are all still employed. It would be unjust to punish a cook because an apple tasted of something he has never wanted.

The next thing I remember is washing my hands at a fountain.

It was morning. My clothes were dry. There was wax under my nails and a small unmarked coin in my mouth. I spat it into my palm. Warm. I could not tell whether the warmth was mine.

Beside me stood a porter with a handcart full of account books. He asked where I wanted them.

He had asked before. There was impatience in his voice.

"My house," I said.

"Which?"

That was new.

He showed me a paper. There were two properties on it, both conveyed in a hand I knew. My hand. Beneath the name, a seal. I had wax beneath my nails the same colour.

I chose the nearer address because my feet hurt.

By noon I owned a counting-house. By dusk the shipowner was paying me to manage the accounts he had tried to have me imprisoned for altering. I had his second set of books. I had a letter to his nephew. I had several things whose value I did not yet understand, but understood well enough to keep.

Pel was dead.

The gaoler said it had happened early that morning. There was a fee to release the body. I paid it. There was a separate fee for his clothes. I paid that too, before remembering that they had taken his coat.

Irena came to the counting-house three days later.

She brought the spoon.

"You left this in your other stocking," she said.

I thought she would be impressed by the room. It had a window on the canal and a rug that was a little too large, so that one corner curled against the wall. I had bought flowers.

She looked at the flowers, then at me.

"Come home," she said.

I said I was home.

She put the spoon down.

The porter was waiting outside. The shipowner had sent a clerk. Someone needed a signature. Irena stood between me and the door, and I kissed her until she stopped looking at the room.

It worked. That afternoon it worked.

I was young. I had nearly died. I had a great deal to do.

You can understand it.

PART IIIThe house with the yellow door

The woman who sold me my first pleasure house tried to include the staff in the sale.

I made her strike them out.

It is a pleasing incident. I have told it at dinners. The guests approve, the younger women look at me with a little more interest, and the man who has been trying to get me alone discovers a reason to admire my principles.

What happened next is less often requested.

I stood in the downstairs parlour and told the staff they were free to go. Four women went. A man who sang in the evenings asked whether his last month's wages would be paid. A laundress put on her coat, took it off again, and asked whether freedom included the rooms upstairs.

I had not thought about the rooms.

The woman who had sold me the house was sitting by the fireplace, counting my money. She made a small sound. I wanted to put the poker through her face. Instead I told the laundress she could stay until she found somewhere.

"At what rent?" she asked.

I gave a figure. The old owner made the sound again.

There were eleven people in the house that evening. I had offended six, frightened three, and acquired a cook who wished to know whether we were still feeding the other two.

It was a useful first day.

The place stood beside a canal where the dyers emptied their vats. On red days the water looked like something slaughtered upstream. On blue days it looked clean enough to tempt a stranger. The cellar walls sweated all year. There were mushrooms behind a cabinet in the dining room.

I called it the Yellow Door because the door was yellow and I had spent my invention on the purchase.

Irena came to see it. She walked through the rooms with her skirts gathered above the dirty boards. In the best bedroom she lifted a corner of the mattress, looked underneath, and lowered it carefully.

"Burn that," she said.

We burned six.

She supplied ribbons for the bed curtains at cost. I paid her cost and added a little, secretly, to the next order. When she discovered it she came down to the counting-house and threw the account at me.

"You cannot bear to owe me anything," she said.

"I owe you a spoon."

She laughed. I had expected her to. I stood and put my hands on her waist. She let me kiss her once, then stepped back.

"You do that when you want to stop hearing me."

"I do it when I want to kiss you."

"Yes," she said. "That is why I keep letting you."

It was becoming difficult to win an argument without losing the evening.

We opened in rain. A musician arrived drunk, a customer fell on the steps, and the cook discovered that the fishmonger had supplied something with the eyes of a much older fish.

The evening was saved by a woman called Celise, who had stayed through the sale because she was owed money by three men who knew the address. She had worked in houses in Zakov and along the Brass Coast. She could speak to a drunken man without letting him believe they were having the same conversation.

Celise sent the musician to bed. She put the fallen customer beside the fire, gave him dry socks, and told his friends he had saved her from doing exactly the same thing. Then she sang without accompaniment while I took the fishmonger into the kitchen and explained the knives.

By midnight we had paid for the fish twice over. I asked Celise to run the parlour.

She named a wage. I offered less. She went upstairs.

I followed her.

In her room she took off one earring and put it on the dresser. She had heard me come in but did not turn around. The back of her neck shone with perspiration. There was a strip of dark lace caught in the clasp of her necklace.

"Your door," she said.

I closed it.

"Outside."

I paid what she asked. We did very well together.

I liked the house.

You will expect me to have endured it until I could afford a cleaner business. I liked it from the first evening the floor had been scrubbed and the lamps were trimmed and someone upstairs laughed with no effort to make it carry.

I liked the preparation: stockings drying over a rail, powder on a dressing table, a man's careful shave before someone he wished to impress. I liked the tired kitchen talk when a woman came down in her shift and ate cold chicken from the bone. I liked how a room changed when the right person entered it.

There was pleasure there. Ordinary pleasure, bought and given, sometimes disappointing, sometimes much better than either person had expected. Adults came to us because they wanted to touch and be touched. We asked what they liked. We listened to the answer. We put clean water beside the bed.

Some nights that was all that happened.

Celise had a client who wept whenever she sang a particular song. He paid her not to sing it, then asked for it before he left. Another wanted to sleep while someone sat in the room sewing. He woke at the slightest silence. The woman who sat with him learned to pull the thread through a little noisily.

We kept a note so she would not have to explain it to a substitute.

That was the first book of preferences. A practical book. Allergies, names not to use, a warning about a man who would be pleasant until the drink caught up with him. Celise kept it locked because it concerned her staff's safety.

I had a key because it was my house.

The man who wanted sewing was a clerk at the harbour court. When an inspection threatened the Yellow Door, I asked him to postpone it. He refused. His hands were shaking. I could hear Celise upstairs singing the song for the man who paid not to hear it.

I said I would hate to explain to the inspector why a court clerk spent so many nights in a house he had never inspected.

He looked at the locked drawer.

The inspection was postponed.

Celise came into my office the next morning and emptied the book into the fire. She tore out the pages in handfuls. I watched her because I had already copied the useful ones.

"He was sleeping," she said.

"I know."

"He wasn't doing anything to anyone."

"I know."

"Then why him?"

She was usually more intelligent than that.

There were rooms upstairs. Wages due. The drains needed work. A postponement had allowed us to continue earning while I found someone to do it. I could have explained all this, but Celise had seen the accounts. She knew.

I let her finish burning the book. Paper is cheap when you know where to get it.

The first shrine was behind the linen cupboard.

I had not authorised it. There was a little mirror, a lamp, and a silk ribbon tied around the stem of a glass. Someone had left a peach cut open on a dish. The flesh was already darkening where the knife had gone through.

I asked who had put it there.

Celise said several people.

"For what?"

"What do you think?"

I thought of the apple in the kitchen doorway. I had been thinking of it all week. Sometimes, after a very good supper, I caught myself licking the inside of my wrist as if the taste might have got there somehow.

That night I brought a better lamp.

Do not laugh. People have entered religions over less.

When I knelt, a woman in the mirror was already kneeling. I saw the pale oval of her face and the line of her mouth before I realised it was mine. I had expected another face. I do not know why.

I wanted the room to love me. There was no other word for it. The entire room: the woman stripping a bed next door, the man waiting below, Celise counting the linen, Irena in her own house deciding whether to come. I wanted them all to turn when I entered. I wanted them pleased. I wanted their wanting to continue after I left.

My knees hurt against the boards. I could smell the peach.

The lamp burned until there was no oil left.

In the morning Celise asked whether I had slept. I asked how many bookings we had.

Tessa made the first sign for the house. She had learned gilding by then. She put a small bird above the yellow door, carrying a ribbon in its beak.

"You should have had a bird," she said. "People remember pictures."

I told her it was beautiful. It was. Her hands were red still, but she wore gloves when she went out, very cheap ones with pearl buttons I had sent her from Kes's warehouse.

She would not come in.

I thought she disapproved of the business. She said she disapproved of the stairs; her knees were bad. I offered to have supper brought down.

"Another night."

I watched her go along the canal, stopping once to bend her leg before taking the bridge steps. I wanted to call her back and put her in a better coat and have everyone see how well I treated old friends. She would have hated the coat. That was part of the difficulty with her.

She sent a bill. I paid it at once.

I did not notice until years later that she had stopped asking me to find her customers.

PART IVWhat the city wanted

By twenty-seven I owned three warehouses, two gambling rooms and the house. I have written that elsewhere. It sounds sufficient when put in a line.

Every morning I woke with something missing.

A week without Irena was obvious. A week with her was worse. She would be beside me, warm and drowsy, her hair caught beneath my shoulder, and I would look toward the door because someone might be waiting with news.

I learned to take a moment before getting up. To kiss her forehead. To make the going look reluctant. Sometimes it was reluctant. I wanted to remain and to leave and to have her waiting when I came back.

Livaea did not arrive at breakfast and demand a fresh atrocity. There was breakfast. There was coffee. There was a day full of things I might obtain. I did not feel cursed. I felt unusually well equipped.

The Watch Captain was a man called Orren Dask. He had a round face that looked kindly until he smiled. The smile showed too much of the lower teeth.

He came to the Yellow Door for money. He came again because Celise made him feel witty. The third time he wanted to stay until morning.

I had instructed the staff that they could refuse anyone. Celise refused him. He sat very still, looking at the cup in his hand.

"She is tired," I said.

Celise looked at me.

Dask asked for her the following night.

You see what I had done. A small courtesy to a powerful customer. It took three words to make her refusal into a postponement.

She came to the office and shut the door so hard that a glass fell off a shelf.

"Tell him," she said.

I told him.

He inspected us the next morning. Six men came with him. They opened chests, pulled the beds apart, tipped a jar of powder onto the wet floor. One held the cook against a wall while another went through her pockets. The cook had a son at sea. She had a letter from him folded in her apron. When the man was finished, the letter lay in the spilled powder.

They found no offence that would stand in a hearing. They closed us pending one.

Dask came back that evening, alone.

"A misunderstanding," he said.

He touched my cheek with the back of a finger. I did not move away. He took that as encouragement and told me what would make the misunderstanding disappear.

I did not supply Celise.

Write that accurately. I have done enough that you need not give me this as well.

I supplied the Captain with a promotion.

It took five weeks. He had a superior, a deputy, and a cousin who sold lamp oil to the Watch at a price the superior had begun to question. The cousin had been paying for bad oil and supplying worse. A watch-house had burned the previous winter. Two men had died in their beds.

I bought the carter who had delivered the oil. I bought his debt, I mean. He had pledged his mule to a moneylender and could not work without it. I returned the mule. He cried against its neck. I was embarrassed for him and waited until he was finished.

Then I asked him to remember that the superior had ordered the substitution.

He had not.

The carter understood the request. He did not give me a speech about honesty. He looked at the mule, then at me, then at the man waiting in my doorway.

He said he remembered.

I found two further witnesses and a clerk who could put their recollections in a convincing order. The clerk belonged to the Archivist, though at the time I did not know that. He had a dry little cough and charged extra for dates.

The superior was dismissed, tried and hanged. Dask took his place. Celise watched the procession from behind the curtains. I was in the kitchen when she came down.

"Does he know?" she asked.

"He knows enough."

"I meant the one they hanged."

The cook was skinning onions. There were papery scraps all over the table. I picked one off my sleeve.

"He signed the supply orders," I said.

"For oil."

"Yes."

She left me with that distinction, which did the dead man no good at all.

I had guests expected later and had forgotten to cancel them. When the maid reminded me, I told her to receive them in the lower parlour and send up if there was anything she could not manage.

I changed my dress.

It was a black one with sleeves that made my hands look delicate. There was a pocket inside large enough for a folded book. I put the unmarked coin in it, because I had begun keeping the coin with me, and could feel its small warmth through the cloth.

I remember the dress more clearly than the first report. There is no virtue in pretending otherwise.

A fire in the Dye Yards does not begin red.

It begins as a thicker darkness over the roofs. Then the smoke catches what light there is, and people look up from the canal because the air has changed. If there is oil inside, the first flame goes high and narrow. If there are dry beams, it spreads sideways. By the time the neighbouring roofs show clearly, someone is trying to get a child through a window.

I knew all that. I had lived there.

From my upper room I could see only the light. The city stood between us: chimneys, bridge towers, a bell-house. I opened the window and heard nothing I could separate from the ordinary night.

The Silken Blade sent a runner. The upper floor had caught. The defenders were still at the stairwell. There were fewer of them. They were holding it anyway.

Kes did not survive the night.

There are accounts of how he died. Some are more useful than others. All agree that he had given the children what help he could and was dead before the business was finished. I did not require a more exact account. You may think that generous. You may think I preferred not to have one.

The children got out.

Eight, by the best count. The report was uncertain at first. There were bodies in the stairwell, bodies near the entrance, and a cellar the searchers could not reach until the beams had cooled. They found a passage to the canal. By then the people who had used it were beyond the net.

Someone had guided them. The description was of a hooded figure, no name, seen briefly. I was given three names afterward and paid for none. A good witness says when he does not know. A witness who needs supper gives you a name.

I did not let the eight escape. I did not look away. Nobody was carrying a secret instruction to spare them.

The people on the stairwell held long enough.

It annoyed me more than the loss of the minor books, more than Kes's last kindness, more than the expense of the men who had to be paid for being hurt. The guildhouse was gone. The Silk Coin could not operate in the city. I had obtained what I had set out to obtain, and there were eight people beyond my reach who would remember it in a way I could not arrange.

I ordered the roads watched.

When that failed, I ordered the names taken off the guild rolls. The Archivist dealt with the legal residue. Rooms were let to other tenants. Credit was withdrawn. Employers were advised that certain names would complicate their own affairs.

Friends forgot faces. Some genuinely, I am told. Some had families and decided that forgetting was a thing they could learn.

I do not know where courtesy ended and Livaea began in all of that. I knew which offices to send to. I sent.

I did not ruin her. She worked for years afterward. You can find the bindings if you know what to look for: a little ridge at the foot of the spine, where her thumb always pressed too hard.

They brought me the jar from Kes's warehouse.

The fingers had gone pale in the spirits. The stopper was stuck. I sat with it for a while and remembered the bread he kept within reach, the boy with the hook wound, the care with which he counted the buttons we had carried.

Then I remembered the money pushed across the table, the open door, the people listening while he told me no.

You will want one memory to defeat the other. They took turns. They still do, when I let them.

I sent the jar to his daughter through Celise. I do not know whether she kept it. I enclosed no letter. Some offerings are easier without your handwriting on them.

That afternoon the creditor who had put his knife on my table returned. He thanked me for settling the matter. The others sent messages. There was a gift of wine, an invitation from a house that had previously been careful about receiving me, and a warm folded note with no signature.

It has been twenty years.

The city has new bridges. The dyes are no cleaner. Dask is dead and his successor comes to the house on the same afternoon of the week, though I have never mentioned that to him. The Yellow Door has been painted twice and remains yellow. I own a larger share of the Pillow than I did then. Celise no longer runs its rooms. She has people who do that for her.

I have not shown my face in public since the war. A porcelain half-mask for certain occasions. Velvet for others. You may choose a reason. Age, vanity, a rite, a scar, a woman seen in a doorway wearing the face I thought was safely upstairs. I have heard all of them. The last has cost people money to repeat convincingly.

I do not spend more than three nights in the same bedroom.

The servants find it inconvenient. I pay them well. A fresh room is a small indulgence for someone who has had to sleep beneath onions.

There are nights when I wake wanting Irena with such force that for a moment I think she is in the next room. I know where she lives. I know the name of the woman who lives with her. I have not sent for either of them.

I have sent customers to the shop. Fewer than I used to. She charges them full price and no one complains.
