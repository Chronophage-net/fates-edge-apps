The Widow's Walk

A Tale of the Grain Covenant, told in five nights

A note on the sources. This is the telling of Obi Diallo, who had it of his mother, who had it of Sekou Diallo her uncle, who was a boy carrying water at the weigh-house at Mbaro in the year the barges did not move. Four tellings of it are written down. They do not agree. This is the longest and it is not the kindest, and it is the one in which the old woman is permitted to interrupt.

Where she interrupts, it is set down. Where the teller refuses, that is set down too.

NIGHT ONEThe Gift That Came Down With the Monsoon

Sit. Sit, the fire is not for looking at.

You have paid, Chidi Toure, and you will be praised, and the praise will be true, which is more than most men get for their money. Your grandfather's grandfather weighed grain honestly at the Mudflat station for thirty years and was never once written up, and a man whose people were never written up may sit closest to the fire on the first night. That is the whole of the praise. If you want more you may buy it from a worse man than me.

Now.

I am Obi Diallo. My mother was Nia Diallo who carried this word. Her uncle was Sekou Diallo who was a boy with a water-jar in the weigh-house at Mbaro in the year the barges did not move, and who saw two of the things in this tale with his own eyes and heard the rest from a woman who washed for the Corps.

I say that first because you should know what you are buying. Two things seen. The rest carried.

Hear me.

She came down with the monsoon.

That is how they say it and it is true the way a proverb is true, which is to say it is short. She came down in the month the coffee goes east, on the fourth caravan, with forty mules and eleven drivers and her father's factor and her father's word.

She came down with a scale in her baggage.

That is the part they leave out, and I will not leave it out, because the whole of what follows is in that box. A woman of eighteen, going to a husband she had never seen, in a country she had never walked, packed a merchant's balance with her own hands. Not her mother's beads. Not her grandmother's cloth, though she brought those also and they are in the inventory and you may go and read it.

A balance. Brass. Taharkan pattern, with the long tongue.

You do not pack a scale to go to a wedding. You pack a scale to go to work.

Her name was Zawadi, of the house of Gebre, and her father was Dawit Gebre whom they called the Monsoon-Watcher, who had four hundred mules and a share in nine ships and a way of speaking that made men feel they had thought of it themselves.

Zawadi. You know what it means. It means gift.

I will say this once and then I will let it alone, because a teller who keeps poking the same place is a man with one tool: her father named her Gift when she was born and gave her as one when she was eighteen, and there is no evidence anywhere that he ever once noticed. He was not a cruel man. He was a trader. To a trader a gift is a thing you give in order to receive, and he received: a grain lane, a tariff remission, and the Salt Syndicate off his back for a generation.

He got a very good price. Everybody says so. Even she said so.

She said it with the flat voice of a woman reading out a figure that is correct.

FATOU KAMARA, from the dark, where she sits every night: He wept when she went.

Obi Diallo: Mother of the fire, he may have wept.

He wept. My grandmother was in that house.

He may have wept and it may have been a good price. Those are not two stories, mother. Those are one story, and it is the commonest story on this earth, and if we had to stop the telling every time a man wept over a thing he did anyway, we would be here until the rains.

Set it down that he wept.

— It is set down.

Now, Taharka.

You people of the flat country think the highlands are a place where it is cold and there are horses. That is not what the highlands are. The highlands are a place where the weather is a government.

The monsoon comes up out of the sea and hits the ridge and it rains on one side and it does not rain on the other, and which side you were born on decided what your grandmother ate. Every Taharkan of any standing spends their life reading a sky that has already decided. They are not fatalists. They are the opposite. They are the most prepared people on this earth, because they know exactly how much of their life is not theirs, and they have spent four hundred years arranging the rest of it around that hole.

A Taharkan trader's daughter is taught three things before she is taught to write.

She is taught to read a sky. She is taught to read a road — which pass is open, which is closed, what a closed pass does to a price four hundred miles away. And she is taught to read a face across a table, because in the highlands the goods are far away and the man is in front of you, and you are not buying grain, you are buying what he says about the grain.

Zawadi Gebre was very good at all three.

Now put that woman on a barge in the canal country, where it rains when it rains, where the road is water, and where the man across the table is not permitted to lie to you because there is a book.

You see the trouble already.

Mbaro.

I must do Mbaro or the rest makes no sense, and I will be quick, and Chidi Toure has paid for a long night so do not sigh at me.

Mbaro is a city built on the principle that hunger is the only thing a thousand tribes will agree about.

Not famine. Control. Any fool can promise you food after the harvest. The Covenant promises you food after the bad harvest, and it does it by the simplest arrangement ever written and the hardest one to keep: you put in when you have, you take out when you have not, and the taking-out is not a favour and cannot be refused and does not have to be asked for twice.

That is it. That is the whole thing. Hundreds of peoples, a thousand tongues, four wars with the marble country fought to a standstill — and underneath all of it, one sentence about a granary.

In the middle of the city stands the Tower. You know it. You have seen its shadow on your own table at the third hour, wherever you keep your table, because that is the joke about the Granary Tower and it is not entirely a joke.

And around the top of the Tower, under the overhang, there is a gallery. A walkway. Wide enough for two, with a rail, going all the way round, and from it you can see the bay and the canals and the barge-lanes and the roofs of the four great weigh-houses.

The Holder of the Covenant walks it every morning at first light. Every morning. Since the beginning.

Not to be seen. Not for ceremony.

To count the barges.

Because a Holder who can be told how many barges came in is a Holder who can be lied to, and a Holder who has counted them himself cannot. That is the Covenant in one habit, and it is worth more than the Tower it is built on.

They call it the Walk.

The Holder in that year was Idriss Keita, and I am going to make you angry now, because I am going to tell you he was a good man, and you have all heard how this ends.

He was a good man.

Hear me. I am not a fool and neither was my mother's uncle. I know what the fourth night holds. I am telling you that on the first night, in the year she came down, Idriss Keita was the best Holder in nine generations and everybody in the confederacy knew it, including the people who later spat when they said his name, including her.

He was forty-one. He had been Holder for eleven years. He had come up through the weigh-houses, which is not usual — Holders are generally made out of the great clans and educated to it — and he had done six years as a canal factor before that, standing in the mud, arguing about moisture content, and he never lost it. He could still put his hand in a sack to the wrist and tell you what it had been stored on.

Three things about him, and then we will get to the wedding, mother, I see you.

He had no praise-name that he liked. They had tried several on him. Keita of the Full Granary. The Fourth Wall. He would not answer to any of them, and after a while people stopped, and his name stayed his name, which in a country of praise-names is itself a kind of praise and he knew it and that is why he did it.

He was famous for one act. In the ninth year of his holding, the Sow country south of the bay lost its harvest to a rot and asked for the take-out, and there was a question, because the Sow had put in short for three years running and the rules are the rules. He came down himself, with the Tally, and he stood in their weigh-house and read out their own three short years to their faces, aloud, so that nobody could say afterward that it had been hidden.

And then he gave them the full take-out. Full. Not reduced.

And then he wrote in the Tally, in his own hand, given full, the shortfall of three years standing, so that it would be in the book forever that they had been carried.

That is the act everybody praises. And you should notice — because she noticed, on the second night, and it is why she came to love him — that it is two acts. The mercy and the record. He gave them everything and he wrote down that he had.

He did not do that to shame them. He did it because a mercy that is not in the book is a mercy that can be denied later, by a worse man, in a worse year.

And the third thing. He did not want to marry her. Not for one hour. He had buried a wife at thirty and had not wanted another, and the Salt Syndicate and the whole eastern council had spent two years explaining to him what a Taharkan lane was worth, and he had agreed the way a man agrees to a tooth being pulled.

So understand what walked into that room.

A woman who had been given as a gift by a father who wept, and a man who had agreed to receive her the way you agree to a necessary injury.

Both of them entirely certain the other was delighted.

They were married in the fourth week, in the Tower, under the Tally, which is how it is done.

And I will tell you the truth about the wedding, which is that nobody remembers it, and the reason nobody remembers it is that nothing happened at it. Forty days of feasting is what the song says. It was nine days and it rained for three of them.

What everybody remembers is the morning after.

She came up to the gallery at first light.

He was already there. He was always already there; he had walked it eleven years in every weather and the men at the weigh-houses set their own morning by the moment his shape went past the eastern rail.

She came up the stair in a plain wrap with her hair tied like a woman going to work, and she stood at the rail beside him, and neither of them said good morning, because neither of them had decided to be pleasant yet.

He counted. She let him count.

Then she said: "Forty-one."

He did not turn his head. He said, "Thirty-nine."

"Forty-one," she said. "Two came in behind the reed spit while you were looking at the western lane. They are low in the water and they are not ours."

Silence on that gallery for a while.

Then Idriss Keita said — and my mother's uncle had this from a woman who had it from the man who carried the Holder's water, so weigh it as you like — he said:

"Show me."

And she showed him. She put her hand on the rail and walked him along the eastern arc and showed him the reed spit and the shadow under it and the two hulls, and explained, in the flat voice of a woman reading out a correct figure, that in her father's country you learn to count what is behind a ridge because everything of value in the highlands is behind a ridge.

And he looked for a long time.

And then he said the worst possible thing he could have said, and he said it with pleasure, because he was a weigh-house man and he had been handed a true thing:

"Then I have been walking this gallery for eleven years standing in the wrong place."

FATOU KAMARA: This is where you make them fall in love. Everyone makes them fall in love here.

Obi Diallo: I am not making them fall in love, mother.

You are. The scale and the two barges and the man saying show me. I have heard it forty times.

Obi Diallo: They detested each other for two more years.

Good.

Obi Diallo: She thought he was a peasant with a book who had been handed a country he was not equal to, and who hid behind the Tally the way a small man hides behind a big brother. She said so. It is written down; she said it to her father's factor in a letter and the factor was a gossip and the letter went east.

Good. Say the rest.

Obi Diallo: And he thought she was a merchant's daughter who had been taught that everything on this earth is a price, and who would find out, the first bad year, that a confederacy is not a trade.

Say the rest, Obi Diallo.

Obi Diallo: ...And they were both correct, mother. That is the rest. Each of them had the other exactly right, and two years later each of them was wrong about it, and neither of them ever changed.

That is the thing about that marriage and I will say it once. They did not become different people. They stopped being wrong about the same two facts.

Now I will tell you what the Corps is, and then you may go to your beds, and on the second night we will do the year the rot came up the bay.

The Vermilion Corps is not an army. Put that out of your head. Oshiira has armies, and they are somebody else's business, and they have never in four wars decided anything.

The Corps is two thousand people in red, and what they guard is the book.

Not the grain. Anyone can guard grain; you put a wall round it and men on the wall. The Corps guards the tally — the weighing, the entering, the copying, the carrying, the keeping. A warden stands in the weigh-house and watches a scale. A warden rides the barge with the sack. A warden sits in the room while the clerk copies the day's figures, and a second warden, who does not know the first, reads the copy in a different room that evening.

They are not cruel. You must understand this or the fifth night will make no sense to you. In my whole life I have never heard of a Vermilion warden beating a man. They do not need to and it would spoil the instrument.

They are thorough. They are the most thorough people in the world, and they have been thorough for four hundred years without a break, and the entire confederacy — a thousand tribes who agree about nothing — has agreed to be watched by them, forever, because the alternative is a grain book that can be edited and a promise that can be broken in a bad year by whoever is strongest that season.

They keep vigil without cruelty. That is their own phrase and they are proud of it and it is true.

And here is what I want in your heads when you lie down tonight, because it is the hinge of everything and I will not say it again on the fourth night when you would rather I did:

The Corps does not serve the Holder.

The Corps serves the Covenant.

Those have been the same thing for four hundred years. Everybody says them in the same breath. In four hundred years nobody has had to find out what happens when they come apart.

Sleep. Come back tomorrow. Bring your own fire-wood; Chidi Toure has paid for the telling, not for the burning.

NIGHT TWOThe Year the Rot Came Up the Bay

You came back. Good. Sit where you sat; a fire remembers where people sat and it is bad luck to teach it otherwise.

Last night I left them on a gallery being correct at each other. Two years passed. I am going to spend them in four breaths, because a teller who gives you every year gives you no years.

The first year she learned the canals and he let her, because he could not think of a reason to stop her and because a man who has agreed to a tooth being pulled does not then take an interest in the tooth.

The second year she began to be seen in the weigh-houses. Not sent. Seen. Standing at the back with her arms folded while a factor argued moisture content, saying nothing, and then one question at the end, and the question was always the one nobody had asked.

The weigh-house men gave her a name in the second year and it is the first name she ever earned in that country. They called her Bibi of the Two Barges, and they meant it half as a joke and half as a warning to each other, which is the only honest kind of praise-name there is.

One scene out of those two years, so that you do not think I am hurrying past them because there was nothing in them.

In the second year there was a dispute at the Toure weigh-house about damp millet — a factor had taken in a barge at a moisture the scale-master would not sign, and the factor was a large man in the eastern lane and the scale-master was twenty-four.

The Holder came down himself, because he always came down himself, and the whole eastern lane came to watch a big man be reduced.

And Idriss Keita heard it out, and looked at the sacks, and put his hand in one to the wrist, and then he did not reduce anybody. He signed it at a lower grade, wrote the grade in the Tally, wrote the factor's name beside it, and said that the barge would be re-weighed at Mudflat at the factor's own cost, and went home.

Merciful. Recorded. Exactly the man.

And she went after him into the passage and said — in front of two clerks, which is the part everybody remembers — "You have taught four hundred men in that room that a large man gets a lower grade and a small one gets a refusal."

And he said: "I have taught them that the grade is written down."

And she said: "You have taught them that you are written down. There is no rule in that room. There is only you, and your hand, and your excellent memory, and when you are dead there will be a man in your chair who is not excellent, and the room will have forty years of your judgements and no rule at all."

And he said, very coldly, that the Covenant had held for four hundred years on the judgement of Holders.

And she said that her father had held a house for forty years on his own judgement, and that when he died it would go to her brother, and that she had spent her whole childhood watching a great trading house arrange itself entirely around the contents of one man's head, and that she could tell him exactly what such a house is worth on the day the head stops.

And then she said the thing that was unforgivable, which is that she said: "You are not building a covenant. You are being one."

He did not speak to her for nine weeks, and in the ninth week he brought in the grading schedule — fixed bands, published, applied by the scale-master without reference upward — which you have all weighed grain under your whole lives, and which is called the Keita bands, and which was her argument given back to her with his name on it and no acknowledgement anywhere.

That is the marriage. You may have the rest of the two years; there is nothing in them you have not now got.

And in the third year the rot came up the bay.

I want you to understand the rot, because you were all raised on the songs and the songs make it a monster.

It was not a monster. It was a wet season and a fungus and a bad three weeks, and it went through the low country along the bay — the Sow lands again, and the Diallo flats, which is my own people, and I will not pretend to you that I tell this part from a great height.

It took the standing crop in nine districts.

Now. That is not a catastrophe. Hear me — that is not a catastrophe, and if you think it is then you have never understood what you live inside. Nine districts is what the Covenant is for. The granaries were full. The Tally was clean. The take-out was owed, the take-out was available, and Idriss Keita had the barges moving within four days, which is fast, and nobody praised him for it because that is the job.

The catastrophe was the road.

Here is what happened, and it is arithmetic, and I am sorry, and it is also the whole of the tale so you will sit still for it.

Grain moves out of the great granaries by canal. The canals go where the canals go. To reach the nine districts, the take-out had to come down the eastern lane and then transfer to mule at Mudflat, because the last forty miles are mud in a wet season and always have been.

Forty miles of mule.

And the mules were not there.

Not stolen. Not withheld. Nobody's fault, and this is the part that makes people angry, because we all want it to have been somebody's fault. The eastern mule-trains had been contracted three months earlier — before anyone knew — for the coffee lift, because the coffee comes down out of Taharka in that month and it always has, and a mule that is under the coffee is not under the millet.

Four hundred mules in the wrong valley, under a legal contract, for a good reason.

And in the weigh-house at Mudflat they stood and looked at each other and did the sum, and the sum said: nine districts, eleven days, and grain in a tower a hundred and forty miles away that might as well have been in the moon.

They brought it to the Tower on the third evening.

Every senior factor in the eastern lane in one room, and the Holder, and the Corps, and a map, and eleven days.

And they did what men in a room always do, which is that they began to be excellent at the wrong question. How many mules can be bought. How many can be seized — and there was a lawyer for that, there is always a lawyer for that. Whether the contract could be broken and what it would cost and who would be owed.

Three hours. Three hours on how to get four hundred mules.

And Zawadi Gebre, who was not on the council and had not been asked and was standing at the back with her arms folded, said the thing she always said, which was the one question nobody had asked.

She said: "Whose coffee is it?"

Silence in the room.

Because the answer, which everybody knew and nobody had been holding in the same hand as the problem, was: it is Taharkan coffee, contracted by four Taharkan houses, of which one is the house of Gebre, and it is going east to Ashaan and to the Brass Coast, and it is going there because that is where it goes, and nobody in that room had thought about the coffee at all except as a thing occupying mules.

And she said — and she said it in the flat voice, and my mother's uncle was in the passage with a water-jar and heard it through the door, and this is one of the two things he saw or heard with his own body, so mark it —

She said: "You are trying to move the mules. Move the coffee."

Hear it.

The coffee does not have to be in Ashaan in eleven days. The coffee does not have to be anywhere in eleven days; coffee keeps, coffee is not hungry. The coffee has to be east before the rains close the high pass, which is a different date and it is thirty-one days away.

Unload the coffee at Mudflat. Put it under the reed awnings, under Covenant seal, with a Vermilion warden on it, which means it cannot be touched and every house in Taharka knows it cannot be touched. Free four hundred mules in one afternoon. Run the millet. Then re-load the coffee and run it east on the same mules with nine days to spare.

The cost is a transfer, a storage, an insurance, and thirty days of four Taharkan houses being furious.

And the reason nobody in that room could see it — and I want you to be gentle about this, because they were not stupid men — is that they were all Oshiiran, and an Oshiiran looks at a lane and sees what is moving on it, and a Taharkan looks at a lane and sees what has to be somewhere by when.

She had been reading roads and skies since she was seven. She knew, to the day, when the high pass closes. Nobody in that room knew it to the month.

FATOU KAMARA: And they did it.

Obi Diallo: They did it.

And her own father's coffee sat in the mud at Mudflat for thirty days.

Obi Diallo: It did.

Say what it cost her.

Obi Diallo: ...It cost her Taharka, mother. She was never welcome in her father's house again. Not cast out — nothing so clean. Just never sent for. Four houses lost a season's interest and the house of Gebre was the one that had put a daughter in Mbaro and the daughter had unloaded them into the mud, and after that her father's letters came twice a year and were about the weather.

Say the other thing.

Obi Diallo: She never mentioned it. Not once, in the rest of her life, in any account we have. She did not say it in the room and she did not say it after. Her father's name does not appear in her own hand again anywhere.

Now you may have your love story.

He found her on the gallery that night.

Not at first light. At the dead hour, the third watch, when there is nobody on the canals and the Tower is the only lit thing in Mbaro.

She was at the eastern rail, in the place she had shown him two years before, looking at nothing, because there was nothing to look at.

And he stood a little way off and he said: "Your father's houses will make you pay for that."

And she said: "Yes."

And he said: "Did you know that when you said it?"

And she said: "I knew it before I said it. I did the sum in the passage. I stood in the passage for a while."

Now.

Here is the moment, and I am going to do it slowly, because every teller before me has done it fast and made it a sweetness, and it is not a sweetness.

Idriss Keita did not thank her.

He did not praise her, he did not touch her, he did not say any of the eleven things a man says. He stood at the rail in the dark for a long time.

And then he went and got the Tally.

He went down the stair himself at the third watch and came back with the day's leaf and a lamp and a reed pen, and he set it on the rail in front of her, and he wrote — in his own hand, in the book, where it is forever and where every warden and factor and clerk in the confederacy would read it within a season:

The lane was opened on the counsel of Zawadi of Gebre, who saw it when the council did not, and who bore the cost of it in her own house. Written so that it is not forgotten.

And then he gave her the pen and said, "Put your mark on it, so that it is not me saying it."

That is what she fell in love with.

Not the man. The instrument. A country where a thing you did in a passage at the back of a room gets written into the book that outlives everybody, by a man who could as easily have taken the counsel and the credit and been praised in nine districts for it, and who instead went down a stair at the third watch to get a pen.

She said, much later, to the only person she ever said anything to — and I will get to that woman on the fourth night, and no, mother, I know you know, let them hear it in its place —

She said: I had spent my whole life among people who kept their word. I had never before lived in a place where the word was kept for you when you were not in the room.

FATOU KAMARA: And him? What did he fall in love with?

Obi Diallo: Mother, I do not know.

You always say that here.

Obi Diallo: I always say it here because it is always true here. We have her word for what she loved, in a letter, in her own hand. We have nothing from him. Nothing. The man wrote four thousand pages in his life and every one of them is about grain.

Then guess. You are a Diallo. Guess.

Obi Diallo: ...I think he had been Holder for thirteen years and in thirteen years nobody had ever told him he was standing in the wrong place.

I think that is all it was. I think he was a man who had been agreed with, respectfully, by excellent people, every day, for thirteen years, and that it had been slowly killing something in him that he did not have a name for. And then a foreign woman with a brass scale said thirty-nine is wrong on a cold gallery and did not apologise for it.

I think he loved her the way a man loves the first true weight after a year of bad scales.

I do not know. Write it down that I do not know.

Four years after that, and I will close the night on this.

They were not a soft couple. I have heard tellings where they are soft and I do not believe those tellings. They argued in front of councils, which scandalised everybody. She kept her own factor and her own accounts and her own name, and signed as Zawadi of Gebre until the day she died, and never once as Keita.

But this is in the household roll, and my mother's uncle saw it twice with his own eyes, and it is the second of the two things he saw:

She began walking the gallery with him.

Every morning. First light. Two shapes going past the eastern rail instead of one, and the weigh-house men set their morning by the pair of them for nine years.

He counted the western lane. She counted the east, because of the reed spit, because he could not see behind it and she could.

Nine years of two people counting barges in the dark.

And on the fourth night I will tell you what she saw from the eastern rail in the tenth year, and you will wish I had stopped here.

Go. Come back.

NIGHT THREEThe Iron Country

The mountain people came in the tenth year.

Now, you have all seen an Aeler. You have seen the factors at the Brass Gate and the ones who buy the low-grade millet for their under-farms, and you think you know what they are.

You do not. Those are their traders. What came down to Mbaro in the tenth year was a True Mason, and a True Mason is a different animal, and I will tell you the one thing about them that matters for this tale.

They do not lie. Ever. Not as a virtue — as a method.

An Aeler hold runs on a rule that a child down there has before it has letters: every square pays, or it closes. Every chamber, every gallery, every arrangement, must show a return or be sealed and abandoned. They have been doing this for so long that they have become the most honest people on this earth, because in a world where every square must pay, a lie is a square that pays once and then closes, and they have the arithmetic to prove it.

So they do not deceive you.

They tell you the whole truth about a thing, at length, with the figures, and then they let you walk into it.

And that is far worse, and you will see why.

He was called Orthen Aezenbron, and he came up out of the bay on an Ecktorian coaster with nine chests and a slate, and he was in Mbaro for four months and not one person ever caught him in an untruth, and when he left there were people weeping on the quay.

What he offered was this. I will give it to you plainly because he gave it to them plainly.

The Aeler would cut the canals.

You are laughing. Do not laugh. The Aeler have been cutting rock for two thousand years and the thing that stops Oshiiran grain is not rock, it is water and mud, and the Aeler build locks. Locks, and a cut through the high bar at Mudflat, and an under-passage at the Sow neck, and stone banks on the eastern lane so that it does not silt every third year.

Do you understand what that is? Forty miles of mule becomes eleven miles of barge. The eastern lane runs in the wet season. The transfer at Mudflat — the transfer that nearly killed nine districts in the year of the rot, the transfer that cost Zawadi Gebre her father — does not happen any more, because there is a cut.

It would take thirty years. It would outlive everybody in the room.

And the price was grain.

Not money. Never money; the mountain does not want your money and has more iron than you will ever have. Grain, delivered to the Brass Gate, so much a year, for as long as the works stand.

For as long as the works stand.

I met him once. No — I did not; I am old and I have started to say that. My mother met him, as a girl, carrying figures for her uncle, and she said two things about Orthen Aezenbron that I have never forgotten.

The first is that he was the most courteous man who ever came to Mbaro.

The second is that being in a room with him was like standing near a wall that is being built while you talk.

She carried in the assay figures on a day in the second month and he was at the long table with his slate and he thanked her and asked her name and used it, and then he said — to her, a girl of fourteen with a tray — "Do you know how much your country loses to the silt at the eastern bar each year, in bushels?"

And she said no.

And he told her. Exactly. She can destroy them in a way they can never recover from, slowly, at a desk, with a pen.

They can destroy her in an evening.

Neither can ever do it, because the moment either begins, the other's hand is already on the thing.

So it is an even exchange.

That is not a threat. Nobody in that conversation was threatening anybody. Two people stood on a gallery and calmly described the arrangement that they were both about to be locked inside for the rest of their lives, so that neither would ever be able to say afterward that they had not understood.

It is the most honest sentence ever spoken in Oshiira and I would put it on the Tower.

There was a third thing that could have been said on that gallery, and it was not said, and I am going to say it once, the way my grandmother said it once and then would not say it again.

There are people on the caravan roads north of the Brass Gate, out where the dry wind comes down in the hard months and takes the tracks off the ground in a morning, who will tell you that a book is a chain, and that a chain is a thing that can be scattered. They do not argue with a figure. They do not pay a debt and they do not refuse one. They wait for a wind, and after the wind there is no figure and no leaf and no lane and no debt, and nobody has to end anybody, and nothing at all can be shown to have happened.

That is the offer. It costs nothing and it takes everything, and it is very old, and it is not wicked. A man the book has counted and closed, who owes what he cannot pay and what the book says correctly that he owes, has nothing else left to appeal to, and I will not stand at this fire and call him a fool for listening.

Understand what it is. It is not the other side of the exchange. It is the refusal of the exchange. Those two on the gallery each took a hostage and handed one over. The wind says: put the hostages down, there is no need for them, I will take the gallery.

There was a warden at the back of that gallery whose name is in the roll. She was from the north, and her mother's people tied a strip of white cloth to a thorn before the dry season, and she had heard the offer — heard it properly, the way you hear a thing that is being offered to you, and not the way you hear a story. She stood with her hands behind her and watched a Taharkan trader's daughter take a hostage and hand one over, and she said nothing, and she served thirty years after it and died in the red.

I do not know what she thought. I have asked in four towns, and the roll gives her name and her years and her lane and no more than that.

FATOU KAMARA: She thought the book was worth it.

Obi Diallo: Mother, you were not there either.

No. But I am here, and I am telling you the book is worth it.

Obi Diallo: I did not say it was not.

You said it slowly, Diallo. That is how you say a thing you have not settled.

Obi Diallo: Yes.

She took it.

Not that day. She took it at the end of the second day, and the way she took it was to turn back to the rail and say: "Then who counts the eastern lane?"

And Amara Bangura said, "You do, madame."

And that was the whole of the accession. No oil, no drum, no clan sitting. There is no ceremony for it; there never has been. The Holder of the Covenant is whoever the Corps salutes at first light, and on the eighteenth day at first light two thousand people in red saluted a Taharkan trader's daughter on a gallery, and it was done.

Now the name, and then I have one thing left and it is short.

She would not take a praise-name. They tried; the clans tried hardest, because a clan that lands a praise-name on a Holder has a hand on the Holder's sleeve forever. Keita of the Full Granary had been refused by the last one. They came to her with nine.

She refused all nine. She would not be Keita — she had never once signed it — and she would not be of Gebre in that office either, because Gebre had not answered her letters in four years.

And so for two seasons the most powerful person in the confederacy had no name to be called by in the third person, which is an intolerable state of affairs for a country with a thousand tongues in it, and the country did what a country does.

The weigh-house men had called her Bibi of the Two Barges for eleven years, and they loved her, and they could not use it now, because it was a joke from before and it had his morning in it.

So they used the only other true thing they had.

They said: the widow.

Not as a title. Not at first. The way you say a thing at a counter — take it up to the widow, she is on the walk — and it went from the Mudflat station to the four weigh-houses in a season and out along the canals in a year, and nobody decreed it and nobody could have stopped it.

By the time she died it was carved.

And it is still there. You said it yourselves, tonight, walking here, without thinking: the Widow wants, the Widow's writ, the Widow's Eye. Four hundred years and a hundred women and not one of them a widow, most of them never married at all — and the confederacy calls every one of them by the name of one Taharkan woman's dead husband, because the weigh-house men at Mudflat needed something to call her in the autumn of a bad year.

That is how a country names itself. Not in a chamber. At a counter, in the afternoon, by people who needed a word.

FATOU KAMARA: Now say the part you always say.

Obi Diallo: She walked it for thirty-one years, mother.

Every morning.

Obi Diallo: Every morning. First light. Eastern rail first, because of the reed spit, and then the whole circuit. Through two bad harvests and the fourth Ecktorian war and the year of the low canals. In the last four years they carried her up the stair in a chair and she counted from the chair and she was wrong twice and knew it both times.

And the mountain?

Obi Diallo: Paid. Every year, on the schedule, to the half-bushel, for thirty-one years. She never once tried to break the Aeler instrument — she had signed it, and it was in the book, and a Widow who breaks a sealed instrument is a Widow who has taught the confederacy that instruments can be broken.

She paid it in the two bad harvests too. Out of the reserve. Exactly as she had said in that council, at forty people, and lost.

Say what it cost in the second bad harvest.

Obi Diallo: ...Eleven districts went on the short take-out for a season, mother.

Say the rest.

Obi Diallo: Nobody died. It is in the Tally. Eleven districts, one season, short measure, no deaths recorded.

Because?

Obi Diallo: Because she had spent nine years building the southern granary. Out of the Covenant's own account, voted properly, argued for in council four times before it passed.

...Which is the thing she had imagined he might say, on the gallery, in the eleven days when she did not ask him.

Yes. And you will not say what that means.

Obi Diallo: No, mother. I will not. It has been forty years and I have not got to the bottom of it, and I am not going to pretend at this fire that I have.

One thing left.

Fatou Kamara has come to this fire for thirty years and every year on the fifth night she says the same thing to me, and every year I have no answer, and tonight I am going to let her say it and then something else is going to happen.

Mother. Say your piece.

FATOU KAMARA: The exchange is invented.

"You may end me quickly. For a slip of the tally you may end us slowly." No teller had that from anybody. It is too good. It is the shape a man makes when he wants a tale to close like a door. My grandmother heard this told by Sekou Diallo himself and it was not in it, and I have said so at this fire for thirty years and no Diallo has ever answered me.

Obi Diallo: ...That is true. I have no answer, mother. I have never had one. My mother had none either and she said so.

And then the woman in red, at the back, beyond the light, who has sat here five nights and eaten nothing and asked for nothing, said — without standing, in an ordinary voice, the first and only thing she said:

"It is in the day-book."

Nobody spoke for a while.

And then Fatou Kamara said, "...Which day-book."

And the warden said: "The Commander's. The sixteenth to the eighteenth day. She wrote up the conversation herself, as the procedure requires. It is not sealed. It has never been sealed. Any person may read it at the Tower on any day the Tower is open."

And Fatou Kamara said: "Then why has nobody?"

And the warden said: "People do. Four or five in a year."

That is the end of it.

I am Obi Diallo. My mother was Nia Diallo who carried this word, and her uncle was Sekou Diallo who was a boy with a water-jar at the weigh-house at Mbaro in the year the barges did not move, and who saw two things with his own eyes and carried the rest.

I have told you five nights. Chidi Toure paid for four and got five, and that is between him and me.

Go home. The lane opens at first light and somebody will be on the gallery counting you in.

Here ends The Widow's Walk.

A NOTE ON THE TELLING

Order. This stands alone. It is not of the Belworth and does not touch it.

What it is for. Oshiira's generator already says the Widow has held the confederacy together for centuries, that the Vermilion Corps guards the covenant and not the grain, and that a page of the Great Tally has gone missing and the Corps has hunted it for nine years. This tale is the origin of the first two and deliberately does not touch the third. The missing page is not in here and should not be put here.

The four tellings referred to in the text are not written. Their disagreements are: whether the father wept, whether she loved him, what the private account was for, and whether the exchange was ever said.

At a table. The laying of an information is the handout. Any person may lay it, it must be about a figure and not a man, it is read back, and it cannot be withdrawn by anybody including the person who laid it. Hand a player that rule and a figure that does not reconcile.
