The Weight of the Unpaid

A Fate's Edge Anthology of Oaths and What They Cost

Anthology 22 Pieces Duty, Debt & Confession Author: Nicholas A. Gasper

Every story in this book is about something a person said out loud and then had to live beside. Not always one kept in ink — sometimes it is kept in silver wire and knotted cord, in a gorget that will not unbuckle, in a promise with no legs and no mouth that sits in the town square until someone pays it. Held is held, all the same, and the held things do not get lighter with time.

These are stories about what people owe each other — a life for a life, a name for a night's shelter, a story for a stolen tree, a confession for a soul let go too easily — and what it costs to pay it. Some of these debts are paid in full and the payer is worse for it. Some are refused, and the refusal is its own kind of payment. A few are never settled at all; they are only carried, a little further down the road, by someone who was never asked whether they wanted the weight.

This is quieter, darker material than a session opener or a campfire aside. Read it when your table wants to sit with consequence for a while — after a hard choice, before a reckoning, or whenever a Game Master needs to remind their players that the world was also in the room when they said it, and has a longer memory than they do.

I. Broken Vows

From the fractured cantons of Vhasia, where chivalry survives as a code of armor and isolation — protection that costs connection, mercy that costs the mercy-giver everything.

The Knave's Regret

Sung by Alain of the Cantor's Road, years before he ever carried water for a dying cook — the ballad he learned before he had a story of his own to add to it.

Before there was Corin, there was the knight the Ethorians only ever call the Iron Bard, though no one remembers if he sang or only listened. The story says he served forty years in a canton whose name has been polished off the telling, the way a coin is polished smooth by too many hands. He wore his armor to his own wedding. He wore it to his mother's funeral pyre, standing so close the varnish blistered, and did not feel the heat through the plate.

He was, by every measure his order kept, a perfect knight. He had never been struck through a seam. He had never gone hungry in a siege, because he had never removed his helm to eat in front of an enemy who might see his face and remember it. He was so armored, the Ethorians say, that he forgot his own face — forgot the shape of it, the color of his own eyes, the way his mouth moved when he was afraid. He had not seen himself, skin to open air, in longer than he could count.

He loved a woman of the sacred groves, where the barrow-kings hold their tribute and the trees remember every oath sworn beneath them. She would not come to his hall. The groves do not travel. So at the end of forty years he rode to meet her, the way he had always ridden to meet everything — sealed, plated, correct.

The trees would not part for him.

He rode the tree line three times, and three times the grove closed its paths in front of him like a hand closing around a coin. An old woman selling salt at the grove's edge told him plainly: the trees do not fear steel. They fear what steel is for. A blade is an argument that ends before it begins. The grove does not admit arguments. It only admits people.

He unbuckled the gorget first, because that was the piece nearest his throat and the piece he trusted least to remove. Then the gauntlets, because a bare hand can hold a bare hand and a gauntlet cannot. Then the helm, last, because the helm was the only thing that had ever let him pretend he had no face to lose.

The trees parted.

He walked into the grove bareheaded, in the open air, for the first time since he had been a boy too young to be knighted. He felt the sun on skin that had not felt weather in forty years, and the story says he laughed — not a knight's laugh, not a courtly laugh, but the laugh of someone startled by their own gladness. He found her waiting at the grove's heart, and whatever passed between them there, the ballad does not say, because it was theirs and not the singer's to sell.

An arrow took him on the walk back to the tree line. A raiding party from a rival canton, working the border the way border raiders always work it, saw an unarmored man where armor was expected and did not ask why. He did not have time to remember he had a body worth protecting. He had spent that time, all forty years of it, learning not to feel anything through the steel — and so, when the steel was finally gone, and the arrow found him, the Ethorians insist he did not flinch. He was smiling. He had, at last and only once, felt the sun, and it seems he judged that a fair trade.

His order buried him in his armor anyway, sealed shut, gorget buckled, helm down — the only way they knew how to bury a knight, and the only way, perhaps, that would have let him rest. The grove keeps no marker. It does not need one. Every year on the anniversary, they say, the trees at that one crossing lean very slightly toward the sun, as if remembering a face they only saw the once.

"Protection requires isolation. Honor requires connection. The armor is the wall that makes mercy possible — but you cannot deliver mercy through a wall. You can only open the gate, and hope the moment you are exposed is not the moment the world chooses to collect."

The Ledger of Mercies Refused

A tale of the Order of the Iron Gorget, told in the canton of Ethor by a hedge-priest who keeps their old chapter house swept, though no knight has slept there in a generation.

Dame Guenna of the Iron Gorget had not unbuckled in nineteen years. Not to eat — she had learned to slide bread and broth beneath the lower rim of her helm. Not to sleep — she slept sitting upright, plate and all, in a chair built for exactly that purpose. Not to bathe, which her Order handled with oiled cloths and a vow of no comment. She had taken the Order's furthest promise, the one few knights complete and fewer still survive with their minds unbroken: never once, in any hour of any day, to be caught with the gorget open and the throat bare.

In exchange she kept the Ledger — the canton's true accounting of debts owed and mercies extended, older than any duke currently claiming the sunburst seal. Every ransom paid, every widow's tithe forgiven, every peasant spared the gallows because a lord's mood happened to be generous that morning: all of it, in Guenna's hand, locked in a strongbox she wore chained to her belt so that not even sleep could separate her from it. The Order called this the highest form of protection. The realm's memory, guarded by a woman who could not be moved, bribed, seduced, or frightened, because she could not be reached at all.

When word came that the throne at Lence stood empty — sacked a decade past, its royal line scattered, its bells cracked and its courts gone quiet, leaving every claimant on both sides of the Belworth circling like gulls over a wreck — the border lords began settling old scores under cover of the confusion. A raiding column crossed into Ethor lands at harvest, burning barns for the spite of it, and Guenna's chapter house stood in its path.

She could have opened the strongbox and burned the Ledger herself, denying the raiders any claim they might have pressed from its pages. She could have unbuckled, taken up a plow-hand's pitchfork, and helped drag children out of a granary already catching flame. She did neither. She stood in the chapter house doorway in full plate, gorget sealed, and held the line with her sword while the granary burned, because the Order's law was clear on the order of things: the record must be protected first, the flesh after, and only if time remained.

Time did not remain. Three children died in that granary. Guenna killed eleven raiders defending a strongbox that was never actually threatened — the raiders had wanted grain, not paper — and when the smoke cleared she was found standing over the ash exactly where she had stood the whole night, unmarked, unmoved, the Ledger's chain still looped twice around her wrist.

A Cantor came through a season later, gathering news the way Cantors do, and heard the story from a farmer who had lost a nephew in the granary. He went to the chapter house and asked Dame Guenna, plainly, whether she would do it again the same way. Through the gorget's grille, her voice came out flat as the plate that made it: "The Ledger protects thousands. The granary protected three. I did the arithmetic the Order trained me to do."

The Cantor wrote the song anyway — not as praise, and not quite as condemnation. He called it The Weight of Iron, and it is sung today as a warning to Order novices before they take their final vow: that a code built to protect everyone, followed exactly, will eventually ask you to let three children burn to keep a strongbox safe, and that the strongbox will never once thank you for it. Dame Guenna is said to be Keeper of the Ledger still. No one has seen her face in longer than the song has existed. Some in the Order consider this proof of her discipline. The Cantors consider it the whole point of the song.

"The Armor Closes when knights choose protection over mercy, when gorgets are buckled tight and chivalry curdles into siege mentality. No one rings a bell when it happens. It only closes a little further, one defensible decision at a time, until the thing being protected is smaller than the thing being lost to protect it."

II. What the Cord Holds

The Daughters of the Unbroken Cord trade in deferred mercy — a good death, a quiet birth, a debt that outlives the debtor by generations. The three accounts below are not three separate stories but one, read across a lifetime: a confession, a bargain, and a vision, all belonging to the same two women.

A note on how these came to be gathered. All three pieces below survive because a scholar named Lerris Fenwood went looking for a family secret and found someone else's instead. Sent north to a Baron's holdfast beyond the Belworth to settle an old debt of his own house, Fenwood was handed a lead-lined box by the Baron's inquisitor-valet — payment, of a kind, for a guest's polite request — and told plainly that the price was not his to bear, but that he would carry it anyway. Inside was Sister Aris's confession. Fenwood transcribed it, and what the Cord's own archive later yielded alongside it, and passed both into wider circulation under the same plain acknowledgment the Baron gave him at the door: the price is never free, and someone always ends up carrying it who never agreed to. The vision that closes this trilogy was recovered separately, through a different diviner's rite, and only makes sense once the first two pieces are read.

The Hollowed — A Confession

Recorded by a silenced scribe of the Witness, found in a lead-lined box. Spoken by Sister Aris, Keeper of the Door, to her Hollowed son. Presented here nearly as it was found, with only the lightest polish to the transcription.

The first thing you learn, little one, is that a woman in labor does not see you. She sees the pain. She sees the face of her mother, or the face of her god. She does not see the boy who never blinks, who holds the linens with hands that do not warm.

You are good at linens. I am proud of you.

This was a farmhouse, a good one. Stone floor, thick beams, ash on the hearth. The father was the nervous type. He kept touching his belt where a knife should hang. I told him to boil water. Men need tasks. It keeps them from counting their fears.

The mother was young, maybe eighteen, with the broad hips of a woman built for this. Her name was Ellia. I had delivered her own mother twenty-three years ago, a quick birth, a quiet one. That mother paid with a lock of hair and a promise to name her first daughter after my own mother, who had no name I ever spoke aloud.

That debt is still in the Cord. It will be collected eventually. Maybe by you.

Ellia screamed. Good screams — productive, open-throated. I checked her progress. Eight fingers, then nine. The child was positioned well. I told her to push.

You stood by the washbasin, your hands folded, your eyes open. The father glanced at you once, then again. He could not hold your gaze. No one ever can. They think it's shyness. They think you are simple. They do not see the silver wire, even when it glints in the firelight. That is the miracle of the Cord, little one. No one sees the help.

The first twin came fast. A boy, red-faced, strong, with fists that knew how to hold the air. I tied the cord, passed him to the mother. She laughed and wept at once. The father touched the boy's cheek, and his hand did not shake.

Then the second twin.

He was smaller, paler, and his cord was wrapped around his neck twice, maybe three times. I felt the knot with my fingers. I could have unwrapped him. I have done it before. But I also felt the weight of the thread in my pocket — the thread that would have bound him to a lifetime of coughing, of debts to physicians, of nights when his parents would not be sure if he would wake.

I looked at you. You were watching the father.

He was still smiling at the first boy. He had not yet looked at the second.

So I did not unwrap the cord. I held it tight, just for a moment. I felt his heart slow. It was quick. It was quiet. It was kind.

I wrapped the second boy in a clean cloth and laid him on the table.

"The second one did not make it," I said. My voice was calm. It is always calm. That is why they trust me.

The mother's face did what faces do — the grief tried to arrive. I placed my hand on her forehead. "You have one healthy son. Focus on him. Do not let the shadow of the one you lost touch the one you hold."

She nodded. Her eyelids fluttered. I had not given her anything yet. But the words were enough. The debt of the dead child was already transferring — from her memory to the Cord.

The father looked at the still bundle. "He was so small," he whispered.

"He was weak," I said. "He would have grown sickly, frail. A lifetime of coughing, of debts to physicians, of nights when you are not sure if he will wake. This is easier. Kinder."

He did not argue.

I cut two pieces of the umbilical cord. One for his mother's memory box. One for the Cord.

You took it. You placed it in your pocket, next to your heart that does not beat.

The mother asked for tea. I poured it. Dark, a little milk, a little iron. The father asked if he could have some. I poured a second cup. He drank it without tasting.

As he drank, he looked at you.

He saw you.

Not the shy boy. Not the helper. He saw the thread where your mouth had been sewn. He saw the hollow where your heart should be. He saw the gray cord trailing from your chest, pulsing with the rhythm of his own heartbeat.

His hand went to his throat. His mouth opened.

"Drink," I said. "The tea will help you forget."

He drank.

I told him: "The child was weak. This is better. You have one healthy son. That is enough."

He nodded. He was already forgetting the shape of the dead boy's face.

"The soup is always free. The price never is."

The Bargain at the Broken Well

A second confession from Sister Aris, recovered from the same hidden archive as the first, written on the back of a grain manifest in ink mixed from lampblack and tears. She wrote it, by her own account, only because she would not remember writing it by morning — the Cord takes memories as interest, and she meant to outrun the collection.

Under the lamp, bread and salt. The soup was free. The price was her.

I am Sister Aris, Keeper of the Door, and I am writing this because I will not remember it in the morning. The Cord takes memories as interest. But I have hidden this page, and I have paid a wayfarer's toll to forget where. When the Cord finds it — and it will — I will already be ash.

My Hollowed has no name. I call him little one. He was weak. His mother was dead. A dark, uncertain life lay ahead. I held the cord tight. I did not unwrap it. The Cord demanded a breath or a death; I gave it a death.

He has been my witness for forty years. He does not eat. He does not sleep. He does not age. His eyes are sewn open with silver wire, and he watches every soul I tend, every death I harvest, every thread I tie. I have told myself he is not my son. I have told myself he is a tool.

I have lied.

The Cord does not forbid love. It forbids attachment. Love is a debt; attachment is a loan with no interest. The High Mother weaves attachment into the pattern, but she does not permit us to keep the thread for ourselves.

I have kept the thread.

⁂

I fled the Cord at the dark of the moon, taking only my Hollowed, a skin of water, and a silver coin pressed once into a dead man's eye. The place I walked toward is not on any map. You find it by walking toward the place where the stars forget their names. I walked for nine nights, through waking and through dreams, and on the ninth the walls rose from the dust — black stone, slick with a wet that smelled of honey and copper. The gates were open. The gates are always open.

Inside, the Worn shuffled past me — shamblers, screamers, stalkers, and one who spoke in a cracked and patient voice and asked me for a name. I gave her the name of a Sister who had wronged me. She took it and laughed, and her joints snapped like dry twigs. "She will dream of drowning tonight," she said. "That is the interest." I did not ask for the principal.

At the heart of that place, in a vaulted chamber lit by a single guttering candle, I found what I had come for. She hangs in chains that drink light. Her wings are bound to the stone. Her mouth is sewn shut with silver wire. Her eyes are open, and they are not angry. They are patient.

She spoke through a wound in her throat, her voice the rasp of rust and honey. "You have brought a Hollowed," she said. "And you wish to restore his heart."

I knelt. "I wish to raise him as my son. Not as a witness. Not as a tool. As a child."

"He was dead. He was killed with kindness long ago, letting the breath fade. The Cord accepted that death as payment for another. He is not yours. He is the Cord's."

"Then I will pay the Cord's price. Whatever it is."

"You do not understand, little sister. The Cord does not sell. It transfers. You cannot buy a Hollowed's heart from the High Mother. She does not own it. She merely holds it."

I did not understand. I still do not.

She smiled, and ash fell from her lips. "I can restore their heart. I can make their lungs fill with air, their blood move with warmth, their eyes close at night. They will grow. They will laugh. They will weep. They will be everything you lost when you held that cord."

"And the price?"

"A plague upon the Cord, three generations from this night. Not death — death is mercy. A plague. That is all you need know. The High Mother will forget a thread. A knot will loosen. A debt will be lost. The Cord will fray, just a little, just enough to let one soul slip free."

I thought of my Hollowed — his silver-wire eyes, his hands that never warmed, his heart that did not beat.

"One soul," I said. "You will let one soul slip free from the Cord?"

"One soul. Not yours. Not theirs. But one."

I agreed.

⁂

She reached through her chains. Her fingers were long and cold and smelled of a well gone bitter. She touched my Hollowed's chest. The gray thread that bound the child's heart unraveled — it did not fall, it dissolved, turning to ash that smelled of milk and iron. The ash swirled, then settled into the shape of a heart, small and pink and pulsing.

The Hollowed gasped. Her eyes closed. When they opened, the silver wire was gone. Her eyes were brown — warm, bewildered, alive. And yet the way she tilted her head, the small furrow of her brow — that was his. That was always his. And I loved her for it.

She looked at me. "Mama?" she said.

I wept. I had not wept in forty years. The Cord does not permit tears — tears are a debt unpaid. But I wept.

She watched me weep the way a creditor watches a debtor sign — with the small, satisfied patience of someone who has just written a new clause in fine print. "The plague will come in three generations. You will not see it. But you will have raised a daughter. Is that not enough?"

I held my child. She was warm. She was real. I did not ask what the fine print said.

⁂

We fled that place at dawn. My daughter — I named her Bryn, after my own mother, who had no name I ever spoke aloud — walked beside me. Her hand was small and warm.

We reached the Black River by nightfall. I built a fire. I poured two cups of tea.

As she drank, I saw it. A gray thread, thin as a spider's silk, wrapped loosely around her neck — the way a Daughter ties her witness cord before a binding. The thread trailed behind her, disappearing into the dark. I reached for it. My fingers passed through.

"Mama?" she said. "What's wrong?"

I looked at her. Her eyes were warm. Her heart beat. She was alive.

But she was not free.

The Cord had not released her. The heart had been restored, yes — but the Cord had never owned her heart. The Cord owned the debt that her death had paid. And that debt had not been erased. It had been transferred — from the dead twin to the living child.

Bryn is a Daughter of the Unbroken Cord. She always has been. She always will be.

I did not flee the Cord. I delivered my own daughter into its keeping. My own little sister.

⁂

I have raised her. She does not remember the walled city. She does not remember the well. She does not remember the day her heart began to beat. She wears gray robes. She tends the dying. She collects debts. She is a good Daughter — efficient, precise, and cold.

Sometimes, when she sleeps, the gray thread around her neck glows faintly. I think the High Mother is whispering to her. I have tried to cut the thread. I cannot. I have tried to tie it in knots. It loosens. I have tried to offer the Cord a substitute — a memory, a name, a year of my own life. The Cord does not accept substitutes.

The plague will come in three generations. I will not live to see it. But I have written this confession so that someone, somewhere, will know: the debt-holder keeps her bargains. The Cord does not forgive. And I am the woman who sold her daughter's soul to buy her a heartbeat.

"The soup is always free. The price never is." — Sister Aris, Keeper of the Door, fleeing south. The ink is ash. The thread is gray. The child is Bryn.

Sunset Over the Black River

The third piece of the trilogy: what was seen decades later, through the Rite of the Echoing Vessel — a divination that draws a vision from still water and a lock of the dead's hair. The caster who witnessed this vision reported only what is set down here; the ritual's mechanics have been omitted as they add nothing to the account itself. Read after the two confessions above, there is no ambiguity left in who Aris and Bryn are to each other — only in what, at the very end, Aris chose.

The voice came to Bryn in dreams — not as a memory, but as a presence. A honeyed rasp, sweet as rot, patient as a sealed tomb. She had heard it only once before, in a vaulted chamber far beneath the earth, when she was small enough to fit in her mother's arms. She had not understood the words then. She understood them now. They came to her in the half-dark of dawn, when her mother's breathing was shallow and the Cord lay heavy against her collarbone.

But not for her. Never for her.

Bryn's thumb brushed sweat from Aris's temple. The skin was paper-thin over bone, mapped with old scars like healed cracks in dry earth. "Your time is soon, mother," Bryn said, voice flat as a stone. "Don't worry. I'll do my duty. I'll take your last breath. Your debt to the Cord will be paid."

Aris's eyes flickered — just a shadow crossing water — and she turned her head. Not fear. Sadness. Deep and quiet, like a well long drained. "Help me up," she whispered. "I want to see the sunset over the Black River."

Bryn shifted her weight, ready to lift. They moved slow, Aris leaning hard on Bryn's shoulder, each step a small surrender. The path wound through knee-high grass, silent except for the rasp of Aris's breath and the distant cry of a lone crow. Bryn kept her hands loose at her sides, ready to steady or support. No words came. The Cord's weight against her collarbone was a familiar pressure — steady, expected.

At the river's edge, Aris stopped. She didn't look at Bryn. Her gaze fixed on the water, where the sun bled gold into the valley's green folds. "I tried," Aris said, voice thin as old thread. "Several times. To escape. Every time... they brought me back." A pause. The wind stirred the reeds. "You told them each time."

Bryn's throat tightened. She hadn't meant for Aris to know. Not like this. "The Cord requires honesty," she said. "Secrets weaken the weave."

Aris didn't nod. Just sighed — a sound like fabric tearing — and fell silent. They stood shoulder to shoulder, watching the light thin to amber, then rise. The river whispered over stones, indifferent.

Aris finally spoke, soft as a sigh. "I don't want to go to the Cord. The House Without Doors. Child... I have no friends there." Her hand lifted, trembling, to wipe her cheek. Came away wet. "Everything I ever wanted... everything... is right here."

Bryn's jaw locked. She'd said those words to dying women a hundred times. Duty was the only warmth left. "You know the rules, mother. And no tears. They're... inefficient." Her voice sounded thin even to her own ears. She kept her hands unclenched at her sides, nails digging into her palms.

Aris turned her face fully toward her. The sunset caught the tears tracking through the dust on her cheeks. "You were always a good daughter," she said. Her thumb brushed Bryn's knuckle — barely there, gone before Bryn could flinch. "And... more besides. I know the rules. And I have earned my tears."

Bryn swallowed. The Cord felt tighter, a live thing against her throat. "A breath, or a death, mother. The Cord demands. Nature chooses."

Aris's eyes were suddenly sharp, ancient in their weariness. "No, Bryn." Her voice dropped to a blade's edge. "No tears. And I choose." Then she wasn't leaning. She was falling.

Bryn's arms jerked out — too late, too weak — to catch air. Aris vanished over the edge. The river swallowed the sound of her fall before it could finish.

Bryn crashed to her knees. Stone bit through her robes. She pressed her palms flat to the earth, head down, and tried to break. Tried to let the grief crack her open like rotten wood. Nothing came. No sound. No wet heat on her cheeks. Just the hollow press of her own ribs, and the Cord — tight, insistent — pulsing once, twice, like a second heartbeat where hers should have been.

She stayed there, knuckles white in the dirt, waiting for tears that would not come. The Cord held her silent. The river kept singing. The sun dipped below the hills, and the light went gray.

"The vision ends the way these visions always end — without a verdict. The Cord does not say whether Aris paid her debt or escaped it by falling before it could be collected. It only shows what happened, and lets the living decide what kind of choice a fall like that was."

III. What the Oath Demands

Rivers and forests and roads that were present when it was promised, and do not forget it, no matter how small, and do not care whether the debtor remembers incurring it.

The Oath of the Unsung Dead

Told by the Direwood itself — or so the teller dreamed. A voice of bark and moss and running sap, heard in sleep and written down still half-asleep.

The Direwood lies west of the Mistland, a forest of black trees and gray moss where the sun does not reach the ground. The wraiths of forgotten kings walk between the trunks. The dead do not stay buried there — they rise as mist-shrouded things with hollow voices and colder hands.

A Linn jarl — call him Bjorn — cut down a tree in the Direwood to build a longship. He did not ask permission. He did not leave an offering. He just cut.

The tree fell. The wood was strong. The ship was fast. The jarl raided for forty years and died rich.

But his descendants did not prosper. Their crops failed. Their ships sank. Their children were born with teeth already grown and eyes that did not blink.

A völva came. She touched the jarl's grave. "The Direwood remembers the tree you cut. It wants the tree back. But the tree is a ship, and the ship is at the bottom of the sea."

"Then how do we pay?" the descendants asked.

"With stories. One story for every ring in the tree. The tree was three hundred years old. You owe three hundred stories. But the Direwood also wants the axe. The axe was forged in the Mistland, from iron that belonged to a wraith-lord. The wraith wants it back."

The descendants told stories. They told of battles, births, deaths, feasts, storms, loves, betrayals, and one cat who learned to speak. They told for three years.

On the last night, the Direwood spoke. "I have heard three hundred stories. The tree is paid for. But the axe remains. The wraith-lord is impatient."

The descendants searched. The axe hung in a hall of relics in Ecktoria, mounted on a wall and labeled as a curiosity from a northern people no one there had ever met. They stole it back. They returned it to the Mistland. They buried it in a barrow where the wraith-lord was said to sleep.

That night, the wraith-lord rose. It was a thing of fog and bone, with eyes like frozen stars. It took the axe. It looked at the descendants. It said nothing. Then it sank back into the earth.

The barrow sealed itself. The wraith-lord did not return.

The descendants prospered. But every year, they walk to the edge of the Direwood and leave a single coin — a toll for the next tree they might cut. And every year, they walk to the Mistland and leave a cup of ale for the wraith-lord, who sleeps with his axe across his knees.

"The Direwood remembers. The Mistland remembers. The axe remembers. Do not borrow from the west without asking. The price is paid in stories, and the stories are never cheap."

The Walking Promise

This tale arrived by letter, found tucked in a book's binding, written in a hand no one recognized and dated the same night the teller had left a certain baronet's forest.

A promise walked into a town. It had no legs and no mouth, but it walked. It sat at the town square, and it waited.

The townspeople were afraid. "What does it want?"

An old woman said, "It wants what it is owed."

They brought coins. The promise did not take them. They brought grain. The promise did not take it. They brought a prisoner. The promise did not move.

The old woman said, "The promise is not owed in things. It is owed in stories."

She sat beside the promise. She told a story. The promise listened. When she finished, the promise stood and walked to the next town.

The townspeople were relieved. But the old woman said, "The promise will return. It always returns. A story only adds another thread."

"A promise is a story we tell ourselves to make the world feel fair. The world is not fair. But a story — a story can buy time."

The Dwarf's Bridge

Told by an Aeler tunnel-wright named Vorn, who had built bridges under three mountains. He smelled of stone dust and old beer.

The Hyaar peninsula is split by a river that has no name. The dwarves call it the Vein. The Pereshi call it the Weight. The Sidhi call it the Salt Line.

A bridge was built across the Salt Line by an Aeler engineer — call her Helga. She built it to last a thousand years. She carved her name into the keystone.

The bridge lasted a hundred. Then a flood washed away the approaches. The dwarves came to rebuild.

"The agreement says we built it to last a thousand years," the locals said.

"The agreement says nothing about floods," the dwarves said. "The flood is an act of the river. The river is not a signatory."

A Pereshi judge was called. She listened to both sides. Then she asked, "Where is the keystone?"

They brought the keystone. Helga's name was still carved into it. But underneath, in smaller letters, was a new name: The River.

"The engineer signed on behalf of the river," the judge said. "The river did not consent. Therefore, the agreement is void. Rebuild the bridge. This time, ask the river for permission."

They did not know how to ask a river for permission. So they sent a Sidhi diver down to the riverbed. He came back with a single stone, smooth and black.

"The river says yes," he said. "It also says the toll is one story per crossing."

The dwarves rebuilt the bridge. They left a niche in the arch for a storyteller. And every traveler who crosses must tell a story to the river — or pay double.

"A bridge is a conversation. Speak to the river before you build. It has a longer memory than your agreement."

IV. Small Mercies, Quietly Paid

Not everything owed is collected by a spirit or a court. Some of it is answered in the plain, unglamorous work of showing up for someone who cannot show up for themselves.

The Naming of Stones

A quieter chapter from the road of the storyteller once known as Quinn, now called Thyme — the same fugitive girl whose flight from the Chain-Lanterns is told elsewhere as "The Smell of Thyme." This is what she found, grown, on a road with no name left to give it.

Thyme comes to the village at dusk, when the light turns the dust to gold and the air smells of woodsmoke and wet stone. There is no sign, no gate — only a milestone at the crossroads, its face worn smooth as a river stone by wind and time. Where a name should be carved, there is only a shallow depression, like a held breath.

She dismounts from her borrowed mare and runs her fingers over the hollow. Not erased. Unmade. As if the village chose to forget its own name, or something took it and left only the shape behind.

A woman steps from the tall grass at the field's edge. Her hemp bracelet is frayed to individual threads, each one catching the last light. She carries a basket of dried figs and a waterskin, but her eyes are on Thyme's wrist — where a red thread, tied by a weaver long since left behind, still circles, faded to the color of old blood.

"We don't remember what we were called," the woman says. Her voice is low, like stone grinding on stone. "Only that we are still here."

Thyme nods. "How do you survive it?"

The woman gestures toward the village center, where a standing stone rises from the earth like a broken tooth. Pebbles, bread crusts, a single red thread are tucked into its cracks. "We leave offerings. We don't ask who left them. We take what we need and leave what we can. It's not a cure. It's just... holding."

Thyme touches her own thread. It feels warm from the day's sun. "I'll stay three days," she says. "I walk the roads. If I find anyone out there, I'll bring them in."

The woman's lips twitch — almost a smile. "The roads bring us what we need. Sometimes that's a stranger. Sometimes it's a mirror."

She finds a man on the third loop of an old road, the same broken milestone, the same scorched oak, the same silence where a bird's call should be. He walks with his head down, his lips shaping words that never leave his mouth. She matches his pace without speaking. After a mile he stops at the milestone, his hand trembling as it touches the chipped letters — Regret. Hunger. The Same. He presses his forehead to the stone.

Thyme sits beside him in the dust and takes a piece of white chalk from her pocket. She draws a single vertical line on the stone's face. Not a name. Not a plea. Just a mark, like a stitch holding fabric together. "You don't have to say it aloud," she says. "You can leave it here." He doesn't look at her. But he doesn't walk away, either. His fingers trace the chalk line once, twice. Then he pulls a small knife from his boot and etches a second line beside it — parallel, deliberate.

That night she brings him to the village. She doesn't tell the elder his story. She asks only for a bowl of nettle soup and a space by the fire. Afterward, he takes a scrap of bark from the kindling pile and scratches a single word into it: Sorry. He folds it small and tucks it into the milestone's crack, beside the red thread and a crumbling piece of barley bread. He doesn't speak again for the rest of her stay. But he stops walking the loop.

She finds a mother at the edge of a burned orchard, walking its perimeter, arranging fallen branches into a loose circle at the base of the oldest tree. Thyme kneels and adds a snapped twig to the circle without a word. They work an hour in silence, the circle taking shape — not a door, but a low, wide arch. "It's not a door," the woman finally says, her voice raw. "It's a grave marker." Thyme nods. "Then you know where she is." That evening the woman lights a single candle in an empty room and throws the window wide to the wind. The flame bends but does not go out.

She finds a girl at a triple crossroads, no older than twelve, arranging pebbles in a slow, widening spiral, humming a tune with no clear beginning or end. Thyme sits at the spiral's outer edge and answers with a low drone in the spaces between the girl's notes — not harmony, accompaniment. She sets a smooth river stone where the spiral's next turn might begin, if the girl wishes it to continue. The girl studies it, then nudges it half an inch, just enough to turn the spiral inward. They build it together for twenty minutes, Thyme never leading, only ever answering. When the girl finally stands, she walks to the standing stone and presses her palm flat against it, leaving a single pebble at its base, placed exactly where the noon shadow falls.

On her last morning, Thyme walks to the standing stone before the sun clears the ridge. The offerings are all still there — the folded bark, the candle stub, the pebble in its precise spiral. The elder joins her.

"You didn't cure anyone," the elder says.

"No."

"But they're still here."

"Yes."

The elder touches the milestone where the village's name used to live, her finger tracing the shallow depression where letters once were. "We've forgotten what we were called. But we haven't forgotten how to be together."

Thyme unties the thread from her own wrist, feeling the silk catch on her skin like a memory. She kneels at the stone and ties it around its base — low enough for the girl to reach, high enough that it won't drag in the dirt. It lies atop the girl's pebble, beside the man's bark scrap, beneath the elder's own red thread.

"Then this is your name now," Thyme says. "The place where threads hold."

She stands, and the elder does not try to stop her as she walks back toward the road. Behind her, the stone waits. The threads wait. She does not look back. She knows the thread will hold.

"Some debts are not paid in coin or courage, but in the quiet insistence of showing up — again and again — for the ones who cannot show up for themselves. It is the cheapest currency in the world, and almost no one can afford it."

Colophon

The nine pieces gathered here are drawn from four of Fate's Edge's expansion sourcebooks. Two are new short works, adapted and expanded from the introduction, canton descriptions, and faction lore of the chivalric-fracture material of Vhasia, built to stand alongside — not replace — the siege narrative already told in full elsewhere. Three form a single trilogy pulled from two different sources: a confession and a bargain recorded nearly verbatim from the appendix material of the Daughters of the Unbroken Cord, and a vision recovered from a divination rite recorded in a separate expansion's appendix, its garbled transcription cleaned into readable prose without touching the events it recorded. That bargain was itself recovered secondhand — copied from a Daughter's hidden archive into a horror-focused expansion's own framing narrative, in which a scholar named Lerris Fenwood is handed the same lead-lined box under very different circumstances; the frame around that discovery belongs to that expansion and is only nodded to here, not reprinted. The remaining four pieces are chosen from the wider story-cycles of the northern, southern, and hedge-witch traditions — tales of tolls, thresholds, and quiet obligations that were left unused by this collection's two sibling volumes.

Every piece has had the same light hand run over it: typographic artifacts and stray markup mended, real-world echoes filed off and replaced with the setting's own vocabulary, prose tightened where it wandered — but no plot altered, no voice rewritten, no ending softened. These are dark, quiet stories, and they were left dark and quiet on purpose.

This volume was assembled to complement, not repeat, its two sibling anthologies — Tales of the Threshold and The Hearth Cord. Where those collections gather the wide spread of Fate's Edge's regional folklore for flavor and session color, this one narrows its focus to a single throughline: what is owed, and what it costs to pay it, refuse it, or simply carry it a little further down the road. Readers who want the fuller siege of Corvane, the wider Hearth Cycle, or the region-by-region folk tales should look to those companion volumes; nothing here retells what they have already told.

V. The Dust Cycle: Tales of the Uncounted

The Beggar's Ledger

The Beggar's Court

Told by Little Tilda, who is seven and has already learned that the best place to sleep is under the bakery's vent.

In the back of the Dye District lies a courtyard absent from any map. The buildings lean so close they block the sky. The ground is packed earth, worn smooth by a thousand feet. At dusk the beggars gather there.

They call it the Beggar's Court—not a court, but a circle of the forgotten. The Beggar King sits on a crate at the center. The beggars bring him their grievances: a stolen blanket, a beaten child, a guard who took more than his due. He listens, then asks, “Who here can pay?”

If none can pay, the grievance is heard and noted; he will wait, patient. If someone can pay—a coin, a crust, a piece of gossip—he will act: sending a message, finding a witness, making the problem vanish.

The Watch knows of the Beggar's Court but does not enter. The Beggar King has a deal with the Watch: they leave his people be, and he does not encourage rebellion. The pact has held for forty years.

  • What's true The Beggar's Court exists as a place where the poor air grievances and seek justice. The Beggar King is real, his network wide.
  • What's exaggerated He may not hold a literal court; he may simply be a trusted listener and helper. The story captures the spirit of the place.
  • What the poor believe The Beggar's Court is sacred ground; the Hollow cannot enter. The Beggar King made a pact with the stones themselves—he gave them a name, and they shield the court. Inside, you are safe; outside, you are on your own.

Mechanical Hook: The Beggar's Court is a safe haven. Anyone who enters is under the Beggar King's protection for one night, but the protection bears a cost: you must tell a story or leave a name. The Beggar King keeps a mental ledger of names; the Hollow respects it and does not visit him.

The Laundress's Lament

The Ledger in Water

Told by Mother Yarrow, who washes clothes for three noble houses and has never lost a single stain—except one.

A laundress sees everything: the blood on a lord's shirt after a duel, the wine on a lady's dress after an assignation, the ink on a merchant's cuffs after a night of “worrying about the books.” She does not judge; she washes and she remembers.

One laundress—Anya—served the same house for thirty years. She washed the master's shirts, the mistress's linens, the children's smallclothes. She watched the master’s children grow, the mistress age, the house fall into debt and rise again.

She kept a ledger—not on paper, which could be burned, but in her head, written in the stain patterns of the clothes she washed. A wine stain on a sleeve meant a secret meeting; a bloodstain on a cuff meant a duel; a particular fold in a shirt meant a certain servant had been beaten.

When Anya died, the mistress came to her funeral, weeping. “She knew everything,” she said. “And she never told a soul.”

The other servants nodded; they knew Anya had told some souls, and those souls were still telling.

  • What's true Laundresses are often the best‑informed people in any household, discreet because speaking risks their livelihood.
  • What's exaggerated Anya’s “ledger in water” is likely metaphorical, but it is true that laundresses remember stains and the stories behind them.
  • What the poor believe Anya’s ghost still walks the laundry room, writing her ledger in the steam rising from the washbasins. Gazing into the steam reveals the house’s secrets—though it shows what you fear, not the truth.

Mechanical Hook: A character who works in a household for a season gains access to the household’s secrets. They may roll Wits+Insight (DV 3) to recall a useful piece of gossip. On a miss, the gossip is overheard by the wrong party. On a critical failure, the character gains Exposure +1.

The Stain That Would Not Come Out

Told by a young laundress who wished to remain unnamed—she was still employed by the house in question.

Lady C favored a silk dress the colour of a winter sunset. She wore it to every ball, every dinner, every assignation. One night she did not return from a ball until dawn; her dress bore a dark stain. She said nothing, handed the dress to the laundress, and commanded, “Get this out. Do not ask where it came from.”

The laundress tried cold water, hot water, salt, lemon—nothing worked. The stain was not blood; it was something else, seeming to move when beheld.

She brought the dress to a hedge‑witch. The witch examined the stain and said, “This is not a stain. This is a name. Someone has written a name into the fabric. The name is the stain.”

“Whose name?” the laundress asked.

“The name of someone who was not supposed to die,” the witch replied. “You cannot wash it out. You can only return the dress to the person who stained it.”

The laundress returned the dress to Lady C. Lady C screamed; the stain had spread, now covering the entire garment. Lady C burned the dress. That night her husband died in his sleep. The stain was his name.

  • What's true Some stains cannot be removed; some names cannot be washed away.
  • What's exaggerated The stain may not have been literally magical—perhaps wine, ink, or something worse—but the story captures the idea that the poor know secrets the powerful cannot hide.
  • What the poor believe Certain stains are witnesses; they remember what happened. Trying to wash them away only makes them spread. The only way to remove a witness‑stain is to confess. Lady C did not confess; her husband’s ghost now haunts the laundry room of every house in the district.

Mechanical Hook: A character who discovers a witness‑stain may spend a scene studying it. On a successful Wits+Lore test (DV 4) they learn the name of the person who caused the stain and the crime. On a failure the stain spreads to the character’s own clothing, granting Exposure +1.

The Guttersnipe's Gospel

The Ninth Step

Told by a child who answered only to “Girl.”

Every alley contains a single step—the ninth step. It is neither first nor last. Step on it, and you will see what you ought not to see: a door that was not there, a face like yours but older, a warm coin.

The children know this. They count their steps—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight—then pause, stepping over the ninth. The adults dismiss it as superstition, yet it is the adults who step on the ninth step and disappear.

One child—Pip—did not believe the tales. He stepped on the ninth step, saw a door, and walked through. He returned three days later wearing new shoes, unable to speak. He wrote in the dust: “The ninth step leads to a room. The room is full of coins. The coins are warm. The coins are not for us.” He has not spoken since; he counts his steps each time he walks.

  • What's true Among Silkstrand’s children there is a belief that the ninth step is unlucky; they count to eight, then pause, and if they forget they go back and start again.
  • What's exaggerated The ninth step may not literally open a magic room, but the children are more attuned to the Ninth Taboo than many adults—because the poor have less to shield them from the Hollow.
  • What the poor believe The ninth step is a door through which the Hollow walks. To step on it is to invite the Hollow to follow. Pip stepped on it, and the Hollow followed him. His silence is not muteness; it is continued listening.

Mechanical Hook: In any city scene, a character who counts their steps and stops at eight gains +1 die on their next stealth roll (they have been careful). A character who steps on the ninth step must test Resolve (DV 4) or acquire the Marked by the Hollow condition for one day.

The Coin That Spends Itself

Told by a street urchin paid in bread to tell this story—and who ate the bread while telling it.

There exists a coin that spends itself. It is neither silver nor gold; it bears the hue of a dead man's tooth. If you find it you can buy anything—a loaf of bread, a night’s shelter, a knife, a secret. But the coin bears a rule: you must spend it before sunset. If you do not, it will spend itself, buying something you did not wish to sell.

Mira found the coin in a gutter. She bought a hot meal, spent the coin, and was full. The next day she found another coin, bought a blanket, spent it, and was warm. The third day she bought a knife, spent the coin. She never found another coin. She searched every gutter, alley, and doorstep—nothing.

Then she found a dead beggar clutching a coin of the same colour and weight. She took it, bought a coffin, and buried the beggar. She never found another coin, yet she never went hungry again. The coin had spent itself, purchasing something she did not know she needed: a purpose.

  • What's true Street children tell of “lucky coins”—coins that appear when most needed, often said to be the coins of the dead left to aid the living.
  • What's exaggerated The coin may not be literally magical, but it is true that the poor notice small gifts of luck—a dropped coin, a forgotten loaf, an unlocked door—more keenly than the rich.
  • What the poor believe The coin is a test. Spend it on yourself and it will leave you; spend it on another and it will stay. It is not a blessing but a lesson.

Mechanical Hook: Once per session a lower‑class character may declare they have found a “lucky coin.” They may spend it to gain +1 die on a single roll (the coin is spent on a bribe, a meal, or a distraction). If they do not spend it, the GM gains 1 SB. The coin watches.

The Night‑Soil Man's Truth

The Second Ledger

Told by a night‑soil man who asked to be called “the Stink.”

The night‑soil man carries the city’s waste on his back. He is invisible, despised, yet necessary. He also knows everything.

He sees the back doors of the great houses, knows which merchants receive late‑night visitors, which nobles are in debt, which servants are beaten and which are not. He keeps a ledger—not on paper, which could be burned, but in his head, written in the smells of the city. The scent of wine means a feast; blood means a murder; fear means a secret.

One night the Stink saw a man leaving a house at dawn, carrying a bleeding bundle. He followed the man to the river and watched him toss the bundle into the water, where it sank. The Stink told no one; who would believe a night‑soil man? But he remembered the smell, and he will always remember it.

  • What's true Night‑soil men are among the most invisible and therefore most observant city‑dwellers.
  • What's exaggerated The Stink may not have literally witnessed a murder, but it is true that the poor know the secrets of the powerful.
  • What the poor believe The night‑soil man’s ledger is written in the air. If you can read it, you see the city’s truth. The Stink still walks the alleys, still remembers, and waits for the right person to hear him.

Mechanical Hook: A character who befriends a night‑soil man can gain access to his knowledge. On a successful Presence+Sway test (DV 4) the night‑soil man reveals a secret about a powerful person. On a failure he fears to speak, and the character gains Exposure +1.

The Cart That Sings

Told by a carter who was drunk and may have been lying—but his story was too good not to write down.

There is a cart that sings. It is a night‑soil cart, one of those that carry the city’s waste, but it does not sing like a cart; it sings like a woman.

The carter who drives it—Souris—is deaf. He cannot hear the cart, but he can feel it: a vibration in his bones.

“The cart is sad,” he says. “It has carried the city’s waste for a hundred years. It knows all the city’s secrets. It is singing them to me.”

Other carters laugh at Souris, calling him mad, yet they refuse to ride with him at night, saying the cart’s song is too sad.

One night a young woman—Lina—rode with Souris. She heard the cart singing and wept.

“What did it sing?” Souris asked.

“It sang about a baby thrown into the river,” Lina replied. “It sang about a mother who never stopped looking.”

Lina never rode with Souris again, but she left a flower on the cart each morning. The cart’s singing grew quieter.

  • What's true Many among the lower classes believe old objects can hold memories; a cart that has hauled waste for a century may have absorbed the city’s grief.
  • What's exaggerated The cart may not literally sing, but the poor are more prone to believe in the memory of objects than the rich.
  • What the poor believe The cart is not a cart; it is a witness. It has seen everything the city has thrown away. It still sings—for the dead, for the forgotten, for those never counted.

Mechanical Hook: A character who spends a night riding with the cart may roll Spirit+Lore (DV 4). On a success they learn a secret about the city’s past. On a failure they gain the Haunted condition for one day (they cannot stop thinking about the cart’s song).

The Dust‑Witch's Bargain

The Corner of the Eye

Told by a dust‑witch who lived in a doorway and spoke in a voice that seemed to come from the cracks in the wall.

There is a woman who lives in the dust. She is neither old nor young; she is the dust itself, present in every corner, crack, and space no one sweeps. She is the Dust‑Witch.

She does not ask for coin; she asks for stories. She listens, she remembers, and sometimes she acts.

A merchant lost a shipment of silk. He came to the Dust‑Witch and said, “Find my silk.”

The Dust‑Witch replied, “Tell me a story I have not heard.”

The merchant told a tale—about the day he cheated his partner out of a fortune.

The Dust‑Witch nodded. “Your silk is in the warehouse of your partner. He stole it. You stole from him. The silk is stolen. The truth is stolen. You are even.”

The merchant fumed, but he could not argue; the Dust‑Witch had spoken the truth.

  • What's true The poor often turn to informal judges and advisors not recognized by the authorities—figures who may be cunning, wise, or simply lucky.
  • What's exaggerated The Dust‑Witch may not be literally magical, but she has learned to read people and to use their own stories against them.
  • What the poor believe The Dust‑Witch is older than the city. She was here before the first stone was laid; she will be here after the last stone crumbles. She is the city’s memory. Be honest with her, and she will be honest with you; lie, and she will find your lie in the dust.

Mechanical Hook: A character who seeks the Dust‑Witch must tell a true story. On a successful Presence+Performance test (DV 4) the Dust‑Witch provides useful information. On a failure she knows you have lied and will refuse to help you again.

The Sweeper's Promise

Told by a sweeper who had swept the same street for forty years and had never spoken to anyone—until that day.

There is a sweeper who has swept the same street for forty years. He does not know why; he just sweeps—dust, leaves, scraps of paper.

One day a child asked him, “Why do you sweep the street?”

“Because I made a promise,” the sweeper replied.

“What promise?”

The sweeper did not answer; he kept sweeping.

The child watched him for a year. Every day the sweeper swept; every day the child watched.

One day the sweeper stopped, looked at the child, and said, “I promised my wife I would sweep this street until she came back. She died forty years ago. I am still sweeping.”

The child did not know what to say.

The sweeper smiled. “Do not be sad. I am not sweeping because I am waiting. I am sweeping because I am remembering.”

He kept sweeping.

  • What's true Many perform small, repetitive acts of remembrance—sweeping streets, polishing steps, ringing bells—for the dead.
  • What's exaggerated The sweeper’s story may be metaphorical, but it is true that the poor create their own rituals of remembrance because they lack monuments.
  • What the poor believe The sweeper is not sweeping the street; he is sweeping the dust of the dead. The dust is the city’s memory. If no one swept it, the dust would rise and swallow everything.

Mechanical Hook: A character who helps the sweeper (by sweeping for a day or by telling him a story) gains +1 die on their next roll in the district. The sweeper’s promise is contagious—show kindness, and the city remembers.

The Costermonger's Heart

The Stolen Squab

Told by a costermonger who was not Finch—and who was not entirely honest about his role in the story.

Peck sold pigeons too, but he was no Finch. He was a cheat: he would take a squab from one stall and sell it at another, claim a thin bird was plump, charge double for a crooked‑legged bird.

One day Peck stole a squab from Finch and sold it to a rich man's cook.

The next day Finch’s stall was empty—all the birds were gone.

Peck felt no remorse; he was greedy.

That night he dreamed of thousands of birds pecking at his eyes. He woke blind.

He spent the rest of his life begging. He could not see, could not sell, could not cheat.

The rich man’s cook brought him a squab each day—the same squab he had stolen. The squab had grown old, too thin to eat, but it was the only food Peck could taste.

  • What's true Cheats exist in every market; sometimes they are caught.
  • What's exaggerated Peck may not have literally gone blind, but the story captures the idea that the market has its own justice—often harsher than the law.
  • What the poor believe Peck is still alive, still blind, still eating the squab he stole. He has been eating it for fifty years. The squab does not die; it is the bird that cannot die—because it was stolen.

Mechanical Hook: A character who cheats a costermonger gains Exposure +1. The costermongers have long memories. If they remember you, the market will close to you. If they like you, they will give you discounts—and information.

The Washerwoman's Witness

The Door That Would Not Open

Told by a washerwoman who worked at the river's edge and had seen a thousand secrets float past.

Lira washed clothes at the river every morning. She washed the garments of the wealthy, saw the stains, knew the secrets.

One morning she saw a door. It had not been there the day before. It was a low, iron door set in the riverbank, crusted with rust.

Lira did not open the door. She had seen too many doors in her life; she knew some doors should not be opened.

But the door opened by itself. A pale, cold hand reached out, dropped a coin into Lira’s basket, then withdrew. The door closed—and vanished.

Lira told no one, but she kept the coin. It was warm. It was the colour of a dead man's tooth. It was the coin that spends itself.

She spent the coin on bread; she was full. The next day the door returned, opened again, and gave another coin. She spent it on coal; she was warm. The third day the door returned, opened again, and Lira did not take the coin. She closed the door herself.

The door did not return.

Lira told this story only once—she told it to me. Then she said, “I do not know what was on the other side of the door. But I know that it was waiting. It was waiting for me to open it. I did not. I hope that was the right choice.”

  • What's true Washerwomen are often the first to notice strange things—the river carries secrets as well as laundry.
  • What's exaggerated The door may not have been literally magical, but the story captures the idea that the poor are often closest to the threshold.
  • What the poor believe The door still lies in the riverbank. It opens when the Hollow walks. If you see it, do not open it. But if you do, the Hollow will give you a coin—and that coin will buy you what you need…until it buys you.

Mechanical Hook: A character who encounters the door may choose to open it. On a success (Spirit+Endurance DV 5) they gain a coin that grants +1 die on their next roll. On a failure they gain the Marked by the Hollow condition. On a critical failure the door swallows them—they are lost for one scene.

The River's Memory

Told by a washerwoman who had washed clothes for sixty years and had seen everything—and who was still washing.

The river remembers. It remembers every coin thrown into it, every body dropped, every secret whispered. It carries them all.

Mira could read the river. She would dip her hands into the water and feel the memories. She would know if a body had been thrown in the night before, if a coin had been lost, if a secret had been drowned.

One day Mira found a ring in the river. It was gold, engraved with a name—the same name as a woman who had vanished.

Mira did not keep the ring; she left it on the bank. The river took it back.

The next day the woman appeared. She was not dead, not alive; she was a ghost of the river.

“Thank you,” the woman said. “I was waiting for someone to find it.”

And then she was gone.

Mira never told anyone what the ring looked like or whose name was on it. She simply kept washing. The river remained full of memories.

  • What's true The river does carry memories—bodies, coins, secrets. It is a witness.
  • What's exaggerated The ghost may not have been real, but the story captures the idea that the river is a place where the past lingers.
  • What the poor believe The river is alive. It is the city’s memory. If you throw something into it, the river will keep it for you—until you need it again.

Mechanical Hook: A character who searches the riverbank may find a clue. On a successful Wits+Insight test (DV 4) they find a coin, a ring, or a piece of paper. On a failure the river takes something from them—a coin, a memory, a name.

VI. Running the Uncounted

Superstitions of the Uncounted

The poor have their own relationship with the Ninth. It differs from the rich’s. The rich fear the Ninth because they have something to lose. The poor fear the Ninth because they have nothing else.

  • The Ninth Step (City): In any alley the ninth step is a door. Step on it, and you will see what you ought not to see. The poor count their steps—eight, then a pause.
  • The Ninth Coin: A warm coin is a coin touched by the Hollow. Spend it quickly—or it will spend itself.
  • The Ninth Breath: When you enter a room, count the breaths of the others. If there are eight, you are safe. If there are nine, someone is not breathing—and they are listening.
  • The Ninth Hour: Midnight is the hour of the Hollow, but the poor know the Hollow is also awake at dawn—when night‑soil men work and the wealthy sleep.
  • The Ninth Name: Never speak the name of a dead person while you are eating. The dead might hear you. They might want to taste the food.
  • The Ninth Stone: Every threshold has a stone. If you find it and touch it, you will see who has crossed the threshold before you—and who has not come back.
  • The Ninth Candle: Light nine candles, and the Hollow will see you. Light eight, and it will pass you by.
  • The Ninth Step (Ritual): When you leave a place, walk eight steps and stop. Look back. The ninth step is for the Hollow.
  • The Ninth Child: A child who is the ninth‑born of a ninth‑born is a child of the Hollow. They will never die—but they will never truly live.
  • The Ninth Door: If you see a door that should not be there, do not open it. The door is not for you. It is for the Hollow.

The Dust Cycle Timer

The Dust Cycle Timer [6] tracks the accumulation of stories, secrets, and the weight of the poor. It ticks when:

  • The party learns a story from the Dust Cycle (+1)
  • The party helps a poor person without expecting payment (+1)
  • The party harms a poor person for no reason (+2)
  • The party ignores a poor person's plea (+1)

When the timer fills, a story manifests. Roll on the Dust Cycle Manifestation table:

  1. The Beggar King's Favor: The Beggar King sends a messenger. The party is offered a job—or a warning. If they refuse, the Beggar King becomes an enemy. If they accept, they gain a powerful ally.
  2. The Laundress's Secret: A laundress approaches the party. She has a secret that could destroy a powerful person. She will trade it for protection.
  3. The Guttersnipe's Warning: A street child warns the party of danger. The warning is cryptic, but it is true. The child will not explain further—they are afraid.
  4. The Night‑Soil Man's Truth: A night‑soil man offers the party a truth. It is a secret about a powerful person. It is also a trap—if the party uses it, they will be blamed.
  5. The Costermonger's Gift: A costermonger gives the party a gift—a squab, a loaf of bread, a flower. The gift is a token of trust. If they accept it, the costermonger will help them. If they refuse, they will be remembered.
  6. The Dust‑Witch's Bargain: The Dust‑Witch appears. She offers the party a deal: a story for a story. If the party tells her a true story, she will tell them a secret. If they lie, she will curse them.
  7. The Moneylender's Map: A grizzled figure offers a weather‑worn map that promises hidden riches. Accepting it grants a clue to a lost hoard, but using the wealth to buy favors summons the Hollow’s laughter (see Mechanical Hook above). Refusing the map earns the Beggar King’s respect (+1 to the Ledger of the Uncounted).

The Ledger of the Uncounted

The Dust Cycle introduces a new player‑facing mechanic: The Ledger of the Uncounted. This shared resource tracks the party’s relationship with the lower classes.

  • The Ledger of the Uncounted The party starts at 0. They gain +1 for each act of kindness to a poor person (not for payment—for genuine help). They lose -1 for each act of cruelty or indifference. The Ledger’s level affects social interaction in the city’s lower districts:
    • +3 or higher: The poor actively help the party. They warn of danger, give discounts, and hide them from authorities. They gain +1 die on all social rolls in the lower districts.
    • 0 to +2: The poor are neutral. They will not betray the party, but they will not help them either.
    • -1 to -3: The poor distrust the party. They will not warn of danger. They may actively report them to the authorities. They suffer -1 die on all social rolls in the lower districts.
    • -4 or lower: The poor are hostile. They will actively work against the party—setting traps, spreading rumors, alerting the Watch. The party is no longer safe in any alley or doorway.

The Ledger can be raised by genuine acts of kindness—sharing food, protecting a child, telling a story. It can be lowered by cruelty, indifference, or simply ignoring the poor.

Part IV: The Dust Cycle Generator

The City's Memory

The Dust Cycle is not just a collection of stories—it is the city’s memory. The poor remember what the rich have forgotten. They remember the names of the dead. They remember the secrets of the powerful. They remember every injustice.

If a character spends a scene in the Dust’s Heart (the place where the city’s memory gathers), they may attempt to read the memory of the city. On a successful Spirit+Lore test (DV 5), they learn a secret about the city’s past. On a failure, they gain a memory that is not their own. On a critical failure, they lose a memory of their own.

The Dust‑Witch's Curse

The Dust‑Witch is not evil. But she is old. She is patient. And she is tired of being forgotten.

If a character lies to the Dust‑Witch, she will curse them. The curse is not dramatic—it is subtle. It is the curse of being forgotten. The character will find that doors close in their face, that people do not remember their name, that their coins are always a little light.

The curse can be lifted by telling the Dust‑Witch a true story—a story that she has not heard before. The story must be worth the curse.

The Beggar King's Ledger

The Beggar King keeps a ledger in his head. It is the record of every debt, every promise, every secret in the alleyways. He does not write it down—he remembers.

If the party does a great favor for the Beggar King, he will add them to his ledger. This means they are known. They are watched. They are protected. It also means they are owed.

If they betray him, he will erase them from his ledger. This means they are forgotten. They are not protected. They are not owed. They are nothing.

Epilogue: The Ledger of the Uncounted

I closed the notebook. The fire was low. The alley was empty.

I had written down the stories of the poor. I had listened to their voices. I had done my best to tell them true.

But I knew I had not told all the stories. There were always more. The city was full of them. They were in the dust, in the stains, in the things that no one swept away.

The poor do not need heroes. They need witnesses. They need someone to remember their stories.

I am a witness. I have remembered.

And now, these stories are yours.

The ink is dust. The fire is cold. The road is south. The poor are still counting.

— Dusana of the Raven Road, at the edge of the Dye District, in the hour before the ninth bell

Colophon

This book was written on paper made from rags, in ink cut with ash and the tears of a washerwoman. The binding is leather from a costermonger's donkey.

The ninth story is the one I did not tell. It is the story of the Hollow's own beginning. It is not a story that belongs to the poor. It is a story that belongs to the Hollow itself.

The poor do not speak of it. They do not need to. They already know what it is like to be forgotten.

— Dusana of the Raven Road
