Running Scenarios

Chapter 7 · Running Scenarios

A scenario is a self-contained unit of play with a clear stake, meaningful choices, and visible pressure. Your goal as GM is not to control the outcome, but to shape the pressure so that player decisions create memorable consequences. This chapter distills Fate’s Edge into actionable GM techniques for facilitating scenarios—from preparation to execution—using the system’s core tools: Story Beats, timers, and player agency.

GM Mindset: Co-Creating Drama

Before rolling dice, internalize these principles. They transform rule enforcement into collaborative storytelling.

GM Core Principles

  • Narrative primacy: Mechanics serve the story. When in doubt, choose what creates the most dramatic consequence or interesting choice.

  • Pressure over punishment: Use Story Beats to escalate tension (e.g., “The ritual advances”) not to penalize players (“You lose 2 HP”).

  • Fail forward: Every roll changes the situation. A Miss isn’t “nothing happens”—it’s “the alarm sounds and guards converge.”

  • Player agency first: Offer costs, timers, or complications instead of saying “no.” (“You can try, but it will take time—tick the Ritual Timer +1.”)

  • Your preparation is invisible magic: Between-session work turns random encounters into meaningful episodes. Players never see the strings—only the music.

  • Patrons are resonances, not entities: The Traveler does not decide to guide you. It is guidance. When players invoke a Patron, they are attuning to a frequency, not petitioning a being. Frame intrusions as echoes, not demands.

Preparing a Scenario: The 15-Minute GM Sprint

Effective scenario prep focuses on pressure points, not plots. Do this before each session:

  1. Identify the stake: What is the players’ immediate goal? Write it as a question: “Can they get the Sunstone in time?”

  2. Set 1–3 visible timers: Use the Timer System (see \(\ref{sec:timers}\)) to track progress/opposition. Label them clearly: Ritual Completion [6], Guard Patrols [4].

  3. Prepare 2–3 flexible situations: Not scripts, but pressure cookers with clear stakes and timers. Example:

    • Situation: “The Sunstone is on a pedestal in the collapsing chamber. The ceiling cracks ominously.”

    • Timer: Chamber Collapse [8] (ticks on Partial/Miss inside chamber)

    • Stakes: Succeed = get Sunstone; Fail = take Harm 1 + timer advances.

  4. Seed player hooks: Review Bonds/Complications. Ask: “How does this situation threaten what they care about?”

  5. Check complication debt: Note unresolved Complications from last session—they add +1 SB to the first scene.

Scenario Prep Checklist

  • Stake phrased as a yes/no question

  • 1–3 visible timers named and sized (4/6/8/10)

  • 2–3 flexible situations (stake + timer)

  • At least one player hook per PC

  • Complication debt noted (adds SB to Scene 1)

Zero-Prep Scene Generation

Sometimes you have no prep. Sometimes the players go somewhere you never expected. When that happens, generate a scene at the table using three draws from a standard deck:

  1. Draw a Spade (Place): This is where the scene starts. Describe the immediate environment. (e.g., “A crumbling watchtower on a hill overlooking a cursed bridge.”)

  2. Draw a Heart (Actor): This is the immediate obstacle or NPC. Give them one emotion and one desire. (e.g., “A tired guard who wants to go home to his family.”)

  3. Draw a Club (Pressure): This is what goes wrong right now, or what is already pressing on the scene. (e.g., “A blight is spreading through the ground, corrupting everything it touches.”)

  4. Set a Timer: Use the highest rank from your three draws to set the scene’s primary pressure (4, 6, or 8 segments).1

The Synthesis Question

Ask one question to connect the three draws:

“How does the Pressure (Club) make the Actor (Heart)’s life harder?”

The answer is your scene’s opening hook. Write one sentence.

Example:

  • Draw Spade (Ruined Watchtower), Heart (Sad Guard), Club (Blight).

  • Synthesis: “How does the blight make the guard’s life harder?”

  • Answer: “His wife was taken by the blight-spirits. He guards the tower hoping to find a cure, but he is losing hope.”

  • Result: A concrete, actionable scene with an NPC, a location, and a timer (the blight’s advance), generated in 30 seconds.

If you have more time, draw a Diamond (Leverage) to add a reward or hidden opportunity to the scene.

Running the Scene: The 90-Second Loop

Every meaningful player action follows this GM procedure. Internalize this loop—it’s your engine.

  1. Declare intent and approach: “What do you want to do, and how?” (Player states Attribute+Skill)

  2. Set Position and DV:

    • Position: Dominant (advantage), Controlled (normal), Desperate (high risk). Default: Controlled.

    • DV: 2 (routine) to 5+ (extreme). Default: 3 (pressured). Never inflate DV to “challenge”—use Position instead.

    Flexible Dice Pools

    Let the fiction guide the dice pool. If a player describes a physical action (looming, threatening with a weapon), use a physical Attribute (Body + Melee). If they describe a social action (persuading, lying), use a social Attribute (Presence + Sway). The skill list is a vocabulary, not a cage.

    Action Default Pool Creative Alternative
    Intimidation (physical) Presence + Sway Body + Melee
    Intimidation (verbal) Presence + Sway Presence + Deception
    Lying Presence + Deception Wits + Deception
    Research Wits + Insight Spirit + Lore
    Persuasion Presence + Sway Wits + Sway

    GM Guidance:

    • Ask “How?” — The player must describe how they use that Attribute and Skill together.

    • Maintain Position — A creative pair does not automatically improve Position or reduce DV.

    • Consistency — If you allow Spirit + Craft for visionary work, allow it again in similar circumstances.

    • No Double Dipping — The player cannot swap both Attribute and Skill to max out a single stat.

    Announce: “Controlled, DV 3. On a Miss, the guard hears you.”

  3. Roll and count: Pool = Attribute+Skill d10s. Successes: 6–9 = 1, 10 = 2. Count 1s for Story Beats (SB).

  4. Apply Outcome Matrix:

    • Successes \(\geq\) DV, SB=0: Clean Success—intent achieved, no complication.

    • Successes \(\geq\) DV, SB\(>\)0: Success with SB—intent achieved; GM spends SB.

    • 0 \(<\) Successes \(<\) DV: Partial—progress; player gains 1 Boon.

    • Successes = 0: Miss—no progress; player gains 2 Boons; GM spends SB.

    Remember: A Partial moves the story forward. A Miss always escalates.

  5. Spend Story Beats: Use the SB Spend Menu (see \(\ref{sec:sb-menu}\)) to introduce complications that change the situation. Tie spends to visible outcomes: “I spend 2 SB—the alarm blares and two guards rush in (tick Guard Patrols +2).”

  6. Describe the new situation: End with one sentence stating the new fiction: “The chamber shudders as dust rains from the ceiling—Ritual Completion is now at 5 of 6.”

Between-Session Checklist

  1. Update timers: Tick faction, travel, or campaign timers based on last session’s outcomes.

  2. Check complication debt: Note unresolved player Complications; they add +1 SB to the next session’s first scene.

  3. Prepare 2–3 flexible situations: Not scripts, but situations with a clear stake and a timer.

  4. Review player goals: Look at Bonds, Patron obligations, and personal arcs. Seed at least one hook per player.

  5. Refresh your memory: Re-read the session summary from last time (ask a player to recap if you didn’t write one).

Key GM Techniques

These are the levers you pull to shape pressure. Practice them until they’re instinctive.

The Timer System

Timers are your single most useful GM tool. Use them for:

  • Threats: Reinforcements Arrive [4], Ritual Completes [6]

  • Opportunities: Alliance Forming [6], Weakness Discovered [4]

  • Extended Challenges: Escape to the Docks [6], Research the Relic [4]

  • Travel: Journey to the Pass [8]

  • Social: The Duke’s Patience [4], Rumor Spreads [6]

When to Tick a Timer

  • On a Miss or Partial—even if the player otherwise succeeds.

  • When the GM spends Story Beats (1 SB = 1 tick typically).

  • When the fiction demands it (e.g., “While you argue, the fire spreads”).

  • At the end of a session (for long-term timers like Cult Influence).

Timer Size Guidelines

Segments Typical Use
2–3 Quick challenge, one-scene tension
4 Short-term tension (a patrol arrives, a door breaks)
6 Standard scene timer (a ritual completes, a rival escapes)
8 Multi-scene or session timer (a faction consolidates power)
10+ Campaign timer (a war, a prophecy, a curse)

The Three-Timer Guideline

As a table-speed recommendation, keep no more than three timers active in the shared play space at a time. This is not a rules cap: swap which timers are active between scenes or encounters, and keep unresolved clocks in your notes until they matter again. Do not advance an inactive timer unless an established event calls for it. Advanced options may use more when simultaneous clocks create meaningful choices. Name active timers and tick them aloud where players can see.

  1. Situation Timer: Tracks progress toward the scene’s immediate goal (e.g., Chamber Collapse [8], Persuade the Duke [6]).

  2. Counter-Clock: Tracks opposition pressure (e.g., Guard Patrols [4], Ritual Completion [6]).

  3. Campaign Timer: Tracks long-term front pressure (e.g., Cult Influence [6], Sun’s Fading [10]).

Merge redundant timers (e.g., two “guard arrival” timers become one). Retire timers that resolve naturally.

Progress Timers (For Extended Challenges)

Instead of “3 successes before 2 failures” (which feels arbitrary and often ends too quickly), use a Progress Timer:

Progress Timers

  • Set a timer of 4–6 segments.

  • Clean Success: Tick 2 segments.

  • Partial: Tick 1 segment; player gains Boon.

  • Miss: Tick 0 segments; GM gains 2 SB.

  • GM spends 1 SB: Tick 1 segment.

Example: “You need to escape the auction house. I’m starting a Progress Timer: Escape to the Docks [6]. Every time you make progress, I’ll tick it. Clean Success: 2 segments. Partial: 1 segment. Miss: no segments, and I get SB to complicate things. When the timer fills, you’re out—but every complication along the way matters.”

Why this works:

  • Players see visible progress toward their goal.

  • Even a Miss advances the timer if the GM spends SB, keeping momentum.

  • The challenge feels like a process, not a single pass/fail moment.

  • Clean Successes feel genuinely rewarding.

Story Beats Spend Menu

Spend SB to introduce complications that escalate pressure or reveal truth. Match SB cost to narrative impact—never spend more than needed.

SB Cost Effect
1 Minor pressure: Noise, distraction, tick a 4–6 segment timer +1, worsen Position by one step.
Example: “Your foot scrapes a loose stone—the guard looks up (tick Guard Alert +1).”
2 Moderate setback: Alarm raised, lose cover/advantage, lesser foe appears, split the party briefly.
Example: “The guard calls for backup—two more arrive (new timer: Reinforcements [6]).”
3+ Serious trouble: Reinforcements arrive, key gear breaks, major twist shifts the scene, authority arrives, environment changes dramatically.
Example: “The chamber ceiling groans—a 10-ton block begins to fall (Chamber Collapse now at 7 of 8).”

Pro tip: One strong beat (e.g., 3 SB for reinforcements) creates more drama than three weak beats (1+1+1 SB for noise). Be generous—unused SB is wasted momentum.

Handling Player Creativity

When players propose unexpected solutions:

  • Say “Yes, but” not “no”: Offer a cost, timer, or complication. (“You can try to bribe the guard—but it will take time. Make a Persuade roll, and on a Miss, the guard alerts his captain.”)

  • Use Setup/Assist actions: Let non-specialists contribute meaningfully. (“You can’t pick the lock, but you can hold the torch steady—grant +1 die to the rogue’s roll.”)

  • Embrace the unexpected: If they bypass your planned encounter, let it work—but escalate pressure elsewhere. (“You trick the guards into leaving their post…but the ritual chamber’s seals begin to weaken. Tick Ritual Completion +1.”)

  • Fail forward aggressively: A Miss on their creative solution shouldn’t stop the story—it should change direction. (“You fail to convince the guard…but in your panic, you notice a loose stone in the wall—a possible escape route.”)

Using the Deck of Consequences

The Deck of Consequences is your primary tool for generating answers when you have nothing prepped. Use it for:

  • NPC motivations and secrets (“What does this guard really want?” — draw a Heart)

  • Environmental twists (“What happens when the ritual backfires?” — draw a Club)

  • Divination and prophecy (“What does the future-scrying spell show?” — draw 1–3 cards)

  • Quick scene generation (Spade/Heart/Club - see Section \(\ref{sec:zero-prep}\))

Suit Interpretation for Plot Generation

The suit tells you the nature of the answer:

  • Hearts: The answer involves a relationship, loyalty, or emotion. Who is connected? What bond is at stake?

  • Spades: The answer involves a physical obstacle, harm, or action. What breaks? Who gets hurt? What collapses?

  • Clubs: The answer involves a resource, debt, or cost. What is owed? What runs out? What must be sacrificed?

  • Diamonds: The answer involves a secret, hidden truth, or opportunity. What is hidden? What door opens? What truth is revealed?

The rank tells you the severity (Ace=legendary, 2-5=minor, 6-10=significant, J/Q/K=major).

Interpretation Procedure

When you draw a card for a plot question:

  1. State the literal card: (e.g., “King of Hearts” = Betrayal).

  2. Ask: “Who or what is betraying whom?”

    • If the card answers an NPC motivation, they are betraying an oath, a trust, or a relationship.

    • If the card answers an environmental question, it is a physical betrayal (a structural collapse, a ward failing).

  3. Link it to a Timer: How does this answer advance or tick an existing Timer? (e.g., “The guard betrays his post to save his wife, which means the Ritual Completion timer advances because no one is watching the door.”)

The Three-Card Synthesis Rule

For a complex question (e.g., “What does the future-scrying spell show?”), draw three cards. Force them into one sentence.

Example:

  • Draws: 9 of Hearts (Betrayal), 5 of Clubs (Debt called), Ace of Spades (Scene-shifting harm).

  • Synthesis: “You see the watchtower collapsing…but in the vision, Captain Voss is holding a child, not a sword. He is trying to pay a debt he owes to the blight by offering the child.”

The Golden Rule: If a card makes zero sense after 10 seconds of thought, discard it and draw again. The deck is a tool, not a tyrant.

When to Stop Drawing

  • One draw per scene is usually enough. Draw a single Club to determine the primary complication, then run the rest using dice and SB tables.

  • Stop drawing after 3 cards in a single scene. If you’ve drawn three cards and the scene still isn’t clear, stop drawing. Make a ruling that serves the story.

  • Never draw to resolve a player’s creative solution. If a player says “I want to climb the wall,” just set DV 3 and roll.

Advisory: Environmental & Positioning Maneuvers

The core rules already contain the building blocks for environmental tactics. What follows is advisory—a quick-reference lens that translates creative player descriptions into mechanical terms. Use it when you need to speed up a ruling, not when you want to restrict imagination.

Building and Running Adversaries

An adversary is not a block of numbers. It is a question the players must answer. A ghoul asks: “What are you willing to eat?” A vampire asks: “What are you willing to lose?” An Oath-Keeper asks: “What do you owe?”

The Minimal Stat Block

The Adversary Minimum

  1. Name & Nature: What is it, and what system of the story does it represent?

  2. Stats: Body, Wits, Spirit, Presence, and a Key Skill or two.

  3. Harm & Fatigue: How much punishment can it take?

  4. One Thing That Makes It Interesting: A trait, a timer, or a twist that demands a choice.

Threat Level Scaling

Use Threat Level (TL) to scale an adversary up or down without rebuilding it.

Adversary Moves: SB-Powered Enemy Actions

Instead of rolling for enemies, the GM spends SB to activate adversary moves.

The GM does not roll to hit. This is the part that most often catches GMs coming from other systems, so it is worth stating plainly: an enemy action is not a roll the GM makes against a defense. It is a narrative event the GM pays for out of SB. Where a resistance test is called for, the player rolls it — “the drake breathes frost; everyone in Near tests Body+Athletics (DV 3) or takes Harm 2.” The dice stay in the players’ hands, which keeps them the ones making decisions under pressure rather than watching the GM roll.

The move table below is deliberately broad, because the flat hit is the least interesting thing an SB can buy. Strike at 1 SB — “it hits, Harm 1” — is always available and is almost never the best spend in the room. Press, Grapple, Disarm and Taunt cost the same or nearly the same and each hands the player a decision instead of a subtraction. Reach for those first, and save the flat damage for the moments when the fiction genuinely wants a body blow. See The Art of the Complication in Core Procedures for the general test.

Timers as Adversary Structure

A timer is not a countdown. It is a promise. Use timers to make adversaries feel inevitable without being unfair.

Timer Type Name Effect
Arrival Patrol Arrives [6] Ticks on noise/failed Stealth. When full, 1d4 guards arrive.
Doom Collapse [8] Ticks on failed Body+Athletics. When full, ceiling falls (Harm 2).
Enrage Bloodlust [6] Ticks each time boss takes Harm. When full, boss gains +1 die to attacks, –1 to defense.

Visible Pressure: Making Timers Matter

Timers only create tension if players see and understand them.

  • Name timers descriptively: “Ritual Completion” not “Timer 3”.

  • Tick them aloud: “The cult’s chant grows louder—Ritual Completion advances to 4 of 6.”

  • Link timer advances to fiction: “As the timer hits 5, the air smells of ozone and sulfur—the demon’s presence warps reality nearby.”

  • Place trackers centrally: Use physical tokens where all players see them. Update them during play.

Common Pitfalls and Fixes

Even experienced GMs fall into these traps. Recognize and correct them instantly.

Pitfall Fixes

  • Pitfall: “I’m hoarding SB for a big climax.”
    Fix: Spend SB early and often. Unused SB is wasted momentum.

  • Pitfall: “The players are stuck—they keep failing the same roll.”
    Fix: Ask: “What is your goal?” Then name the simplest obstacle.

  • Pitfall: “I don’t know the rule for X.”
    Fix: Make a ruling that keeps the story moving. Default: DV 3, Controlled Position.

  • Pitfall: “My prep went unused—the players went off-track.”
    Fix: Your prep was about pressure, not specific scenes. If they ignored the ambush, the timer still advances.

  • Pitfall: “I feel like I’m arguing with players about rules.”
    Fix: You are co-creators, not adversaries. When rules conflict with fiction, always side with fiction.

  • Pitfall: “I defaulted to Presence + Sway for intimidation, but the player described a physical action.”
    Fix: Let the fiction guide the dice pool. If a player describes looming, threatening with a weapon, or physical presence, use Body + Melee or Body + Athletics.

  • Pitfall: “The skill challenge felt rushed—3 successes before 2 failures was over in 4 rolls.”
    Fix: Use a Progress Timer instead. Set a 4–6 segment timer. Tick 2 on Clean Success, 1 on Partial, 0 on Miss. Spend SB to tick the timer.

  • Pitfall: “I keep forgetting to use the Ninth Taboo for atmosphere.”
    Fix: Use it as texture, not mechanic. When players break a custom, describe the uncanny result—a bell that rings without being struck, a shadow that moves independently. Give them a chance to make amends. The Ninth is a boundary, not a punishment.

Rookie GM Comfort Dials

If you’re new to Fate’s Edge, start here to build confidence.

Rookie GM Settings

  • Soft SB: For first 2 sessions, cap SB spend per roll at 1–2 (unless it’s a set-piece moment).

  • Visible Timers: Put all active timers on the table. Name them aloud. Tick them visibly.

  • Tag Cards: Print one-liners for frequent tags and hand them out when relevant.

  • One Move, One Sentence: After every ruling, end with one sentence stating the new situation.

  • Default to DV 3: Don’t overthink difficulty. DV 3 works for almost everything.

  • Let the fiction guide pools: If a player describes a physical intimidation, let them roll Body + Melee. The skill list is a vocabulary, not a cage.

What to Do When You Forget

  • Forgot to set a timer? Add it now. “Actually, there’s a timer I should have mentioned: Guard Patrol [4]. It’s at 2 of 4.”

  • Forgot to spend SB? Spend them immediately. “I just remembered I have 2 SB banked. The guard’s backup arrives.”

  • Forgot to use a complication? Introduce it now. “As you’re celebrating, you notice something: the floor is cracked.”

  • Forgot to let a player use a creative dice pool? Let them roll it now. “Actually, that should have been Body + Melee for intimidation. Roll that instead.”

  • Forgot to tick a timer? Tick it now. “I should have ticked this earlier. The timer is now at 4 of 6.”

Scenario Types: Adapting the Loop

Apply the 90-Second Loop to these common scenario frames:

Social Encounters

  • Clean Success: +2 segments to Persuasion Timer

  • Partial: +1 segment; GM may spend SB for complication (“They agree—but you’ll owe a favor”)

  • Miss: No progress; GM ticks Opposition Timer

  • GM Tip: Spend SB to reveal hidden motives or escalate stakes.

Travel Legs

Frame each leg as a scenario with roles:

  • Guide: Wits+Lore to tick Travel Timer +1 (success) or backwards (failure)

  • Scout: Wits+Stealth to avoid surprise on first encounter

  • Quartermaster: Wits+Craft to manage Supply

  • Watch: Presence+Notice for +1 Position on first ambush defense roll

  • GM Tip: On failed role, spend SB to introduce immediate encounter.

Extended Challenges (Progress Timers)

  • Set a Progress Timer [4–6].

  • Each Clean Success ticks 2 segments.

  • Each Partial ticks 1 segment (player gains Boon).

  • Each Miss ticks 0 segments (GM gains 2 SB).

  • GM may spend 1 SB to tick 1 segment.

  • When the timer fills, the challenge is complete—with whatever complications have accumulated.

Ending the Scenario: Leave Hooks, Not Loose Ends

Close scenarios to create momentum for the next:

  1. Resolve the immediate stake: State clearly whether they achieved their goal (yes/no/partial).

  2. Advance relevant timers: Especially Campaign and Faction timers based on outcomes.

  3. Seed 1–2 hooks for next session:

    • “The rival captain escaped—but she dropped this bloodstained sigil.”

    • “The ritual failed…but the cult’s leader whispers your true name as she flees.”

  4. Explicitly note complication debt: “Your Complication remains unresolved—it will add +1 SB to your first action next session.”

  5. Ask one reflective question: “What did you learn about your character tonight?”

The GM’s Sacred Trust

Between sessions:

  • Update timers based on scene outcomes

  • Check complication debt (unresolved = +1 SB next scene)

  • Prepare flexible situations (stake + timer, not scripts)

  • Review player sheets for hooks

  • Refresh your memory on last session’s events

This invisible preparation transforms dice rolls into consequence-rich stories. Let the pressure shape the story. Let the dice sing. And never forget: you are playing the game too.

Quick Reference: Generator Workflow

The Generator Workflow

  1. Starting from nothing? Draw Spade + Heart + Club. Ask: “How does the Pressure make the Actor’s life harder?” Set Timer (rank = 4/6/8).

  2. Need an NPC’s motivation? Draw a Heart. Suit = nature; Rank = severity. State the literal card, then ask “Who or what is whom?”

  3. Need a twist? Draw a Club. Ask: “What just went wrong?” Link to an active Timer.

  4. Need a reward? Draw a Diamond. Ask: “What hidden opportunity is here?”

  5. Need a prophecy? Draw 3 cards. Force them into one sentence.

  6. When to stop: After 3 draws in a single scene, stop. Rely on the dice and Outcome Matrix.

  7. Filter through genre: Interpret every draw through the region’s subgenre.

Morale & Surrender Quick Reference

Morale Timers:

  • Courage [4] — Enemy routs when full.

  • Bloodlust [6] — Enemy gains +1 die to attacks, –1 to defense when full.

  • Desperation [6] — Enemy makes a final stand when full.

  • Retreat [4] — Enemy flees immediately when full.
