Chapter 2: The GM's Job

Fate's Edge GM Guide — Chapter 2

You have been told the GM does everything. You invent the world, conceal the plot, pace the session, arbitrate the rules, voice every NPC, and pretend none of it was your idea.

That is five jobs, and four of them are unnecessary.

Fate's Edge asks you to do one thing: govern. The system invents, conceals, paces, and produces. You read the table, choose where pressure lands, and say the line in the doorway like you mean it. That is a smaller job than being the engine of a story. It is also the only part of the job that was ever fun.

The Five Burdens

Prep.
The lonely work. Hours of invention nobody sees, most of it discarded at the table.
Improv.
Brilliance on demand, in real time, in front of an audience that can tell when you are stalling.
Fairness.
You hold secrets, and you have to manage the players' trust while holding them.
Authorship.
You are expected to invent a story, conceal its shape, and pretend it emerged on its own.
Pacing.
Someone has to decide when a scene has said what it came to say.

Four of these can be handed to the system. The last one cannot. This chapter is about which is which, and what to do with the one you keep.

What the GM Is Not

Not the author. You are not writing a story the players walk through. A story is what the table has afterward, when someone says “remember when.” Nobody can write that in advance, and the attempt is what produces the railroad — not malice, just an author defending a draft.

Not the engine. You are not the source of momentum. If you believe you are, every quiet moment becomes your fault, and you will start manufacturing trouble to cover for a table that was simply thinking. The dice generate pressure. Let them.

Not the concealer. You are not guarding a secret shape. When a draw decides what is behind the door, you find out when the players do — and your surprise is real, which is worth more at a table than any amount of poker face. You still keep things private: an NPC's intention, a timer's face-down segments, what the ledger actually says. That is privacy, not concealment of a plot you already wrote.

Not the entertainer. You are not performing for an audience. The table is not owed a good time by you specifically. Four other people are also responsible for whether tonight is any good, and pretending otherwise is how a GM burns out apologising for other people's disengagement.

What the GM Is

Operator. You run the procedures. Draw the spread, set the DV, start the timer, tick it when it earns a tick. Most of what looks like GM skill from the outside is somebody operating a small number of procedures without flinching.

Governor. You choose where pressure lands and how fast. The economy supplies force; you supply direction. A governor does not generate the engine's power and does not decide the destination alone — it keeps the thing from running away.

Witness. You say what happened. Plainly, and without protecting anyone from it, including yourself. A great deal of GMing is the discipline of narrating the consequence you did not want.

Arbiter. You make the call when the rules and the fiction disagree, and you make it out loud, at the table, in front of everyone. This one never goes away.

The Four Burdens the System Carries

Invention → the decks and the regional generators. A Crown Spread gives you a place, an actor, a pressure and a leverage, each with a regional seed already written. You are not inventing from nothing; you are reading four cards and asking what they are doing in the same room.

Concealment → the draw. What is behind the door is decided by a card neither side has seen. There is nothing to conceal because there is nothing yet to know. A player who suspects you of steering can watch you draw.

Improvisation → the seeded prose. Every generator entry carries a flavour line and a hook. That is your improvisation, pre-written by someone who was not under time pressure. Read it aloud if you like. It is better than what most of us produce in four seconds.

Authorship → the timers and the Story Beat economy. Timers turn player choices into visible, inevitable consequence without anyone plotting it. A front does not need an outline; it needs a clock and a thing it wants.

Pacing Is the Governor's Job

Every other burden in this chapter can be delegated. Pacing cannot. Someone has to decide when a scene has said what it came to say, and that someone is you.

What the system gives you is a trigger, not a schedule. Story Beats are generated by 1s — by the players' own rolls. You do not decide when to escalate. A 1 tells you. You spend, and the world pushes back, and nobody can feel the author's hand because there was not one. The players handed you the pressure themselves.

This is why fail-forward in Fate's Edge is tighter than the usual version. It is not “something happens on a miss.” It is: something happens on every roll, and 1s happen whether you succeed or fail. Success advances. A 1 complicates. Both keep the scene alive. Motion does not depend on failure arriving.

Which means the game does not stall. It can sputter — if your table only goes after easy challenges, easy rolls do not produce 1s, and a quiet table makes a quiet game. But that is a consequence of their choices, not a failure of your entertainment. You are not obligated to jolly anyone along. The players co-author the pressure. Let them.

You are the governor, not the engine. The economy generates momentum. You decide its direction and its pace. Cut a scene that has found its end. Slow down when the table needs a breath. The SB economy will happily produce a fast, loud, incoherent session if nobody steers. Steer.

Synthesis: The One Skill

The system removes invention. It does not remove interpretation.

A draw gives you a domain (MICE), a severity (rank), and a regional seed (prose). Turning that into a scene is a skill, and it is the only skill Fate's Edge actually requires of you. Read the draw. Place it. Trust the economy. If you can do that, you can run this game from a card draw in five minutes.

Reading a draw in three questions
  1. Where does this land? Not “what is it” — what part of the party's current situation does it touch? A complication with nowhere to land is a complication you drew too early. Hold it.
  2. Who already wants this? Attach every draw to somebody's existing appetite. An unattached event is weather. An attached one is a scene.
  3. What does it cost to ignore? If the answer is “nothing,” it is scenery, and scenery does not need a clock. If the answer is a number of segments, start the timer and say so out loud.

Running the Esoteric Draw

Some generator entries resolve themselves. Others hand you a riddle with no stated answer, a price with no stated rate, or a consequence the entry names and never defines — “choose wrong, and you become a child.” Those are not gaps in the text. They are the places where the region expects a ruling, and each region's own chapter now lists its own under Running the Esoteric Entries. The general procedure:

  1. The flavour line is the rule. The italic sentence under an entry is not decoration, it is the instruction. “The riddles have no answer — only a choice” is telling you not to prepare a puzzle.
  2. Ask what it wants. Every esoteric entry is a transaction with an unstated price. Before you narrate it, decide what it is buying and what it is selling. If you cannot answer that, it is scenery.
  3. Price it in the region's own currency. Each region pays in one thing — memory here, oath there, a name, a silence, a count. Take payment in that, not in coin, and the region stays itself.
  4. Never explain it. If a player asks what the ninth is, the honest answer is what it costs, not what it is. A thing that has been explained stops being able to charge.
  5. An undefined consequence is a condition, not damage. Name it, say plainly what it costs the character day to day, and say what would lift it. Then let it sit. Do not roll for it.
  6. Refusing to pay is an answer. It should cost more later than paying would have cost now — and the character should find that out at the worst possible moment, from someone who warned them.

Three Workflows

Full prep — fifteen minutes. Draw a Crown Spread. Name the timer's outcome. Write the three clocks on index cards. That is the session. Two to three hours of invention became fifteen minutes of arrangement.

Commando — five minutes, mid-session. Call a break. Draw one to three cards. Figure out where they go. Resume play before the players finish the chips.

Zero-prep — none. Set DV 3, Position Controlled, and let the die fly. The economy will generate the scene. You are there to govern it, not to build it.

Failure Modes

This chapter has promised you a smaller job. Here is the honest account of what you are still paying for.

The economy guarantees motion, not quality. A session can be eventful and mean nothing. Story Beats will keep producing pressure long after the table has stopped caring what the pressure is about. Motion is not the same as a scene worth having, and only you are in a position to notice the difference.

Synthesis is a skill, and skills can be absent. “Read the draw and place it” is genuinely the whole job — and a GM who cannot yet look at 2♥ + 3♣ and see a room is stuck, not liberated. That GM is stuck in a better place than the one staring at a blank page, but they are still stuck. The cure is repetition, and the three questions above are the drill.

You still arbitrate. The rules will disagree with the fiction in front of your players, tonight, and no card is going to settle it. Every ruling you make is a precedent your table will hold you to. This burden was never delegable and this chapter has not delegated it.

Delegation is not abdication. “The dice decided” is true, and it is also the most tempting sentence in this book. Use it to explain a consequence, never to avoid one. If a draw produces something the table should not have to sit through, you are the one who says so and moves on — see the safety tools in Appendix E. The system's authority is borrowed from you and can be recalled at any time.

What it costs

There is a saying in the anthologies: the soup is always free; the price never is. You have been handed prep, improvisation, concealment and authorship at no charge. What you pay is attention — the whole session, without a break, reading a table you cannot roll dice for. That is a real price and it does not get cheaper with experience. It just gets to be the only thing you are carrying.
