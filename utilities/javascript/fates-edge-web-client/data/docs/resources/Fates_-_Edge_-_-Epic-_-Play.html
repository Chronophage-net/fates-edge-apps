Epic Play

Running campaigns and building a legacy at Tier IV and beyond

Epic characters can win a battle and still have to decide who governs afterward. Their reach brings institutions, promises, communities, and future generations into the consequences of play. Give that reach real effects: an experienced party should solve familiar problems more easily, gain access their younger selves lacked, and change the conditions of the campaign.

What changes—and what stays

Rules compatibility. Use the SRD and extensions in the Player’s and GM Guides as the core. Tier IV and Tier V expand scope, not the numerical ceilings. DV still ends at 5; use the existing rules for attributes, skills, assistance, Harm, armor, Fatigue, Boons, and Story Beats. Continuing a Mythic campaign introduces no automatic sixth tier or unlimited ratings.

This module adds an optional campaign review procedure and supplies the same clarified Legacy procedures published in the Player’s Guide. The Legacy project and crisis tick defaults apply only when that optional framework is chosen. Specific crafting, faction, asset, or adventure procedures take precedence for their own tracks; do not stack two progress engines on one project.

Read the GM Guide for adjudication, the Legacy Engine for character succession, and the SRD for the core loop. The annotated Epic session is an example to discuss, not an additional source of automatic permissions.

Build systemic pressure from causes

A systemic problem persists because several people and structures sustain it. A famine may involve a damaged canal, a grain monopoly, and a border closure. Killing the monopolist can change one part of that situation; it does not automatically repair the canal. Equally, a negotiated agreement that really removes the monopoly should work. Do not declare every problem immune to a decisive action merely to prolong a campaign.

  1. Name the threatened public good. Food, passage, lawful succession, shelter, or the reliability of a magical boundary. Identify who suffers now and who benefits from the current arrangement.
  2. Choose three active stakeholders. Give each a concrete demand, leverage, a limit they will not cross, and a concession they can offer. Include someone the players could plausibly cooperate with.
  3. Draw two or three dependencies. Explain why one success affects another problem: opening the pass restores grain deliveries; exposing the forged charter changes who can authorize repairs.
  4. Prepare no more than three visible pressure timers to begin. For each, name the trigger, review interval, warning sign, and event at completion. Reveal additional pressures when play discovers them rather than presenting a wall of clocks.
  5. Write exits. For every pressure, identify something that can stop, reduce, redirect, or resolve it. These are starting possibilities, not a list of the only permitted solutions.

A timer measures an impending event or unfinished work, not the GM’s desire for tension. Public facts and visible deadlines belong on the table. Secret plans still need observable signs before their consequences become unavoidable.

The campaign turn (optional)

Agree on a review interval such as one mission followed by downtime, a week of negotiations, or a season of travel. Changing the interval changes opportunities as well as pressure; announce that change before the players commit. It is not an extra source of actions or resource awards.

  1. Brief: show current pressures, completed gains, deadlines, obligations due, and choices that can wait. Ask each player what they want to protect or change.
  2. Commit: choose the party’s immediate objective and legitimate delegated tasks. Record who acts, what they can reach, their authority, and their resource costs.
  3. Play: resolve uncertain scenes with the core loop. Set the intent’s scope before rolling. Honor success at that scope, including when it eliminates an entire prepared obstacle.
  4. Maintain: resolve ordinary downtime, upkeep, recovery, and stated project progress. Followers retain their existing capability, assistance, delegation, and action limits.
  5. Review: check each declared scheduled trigger once. The default is one tick per affected pressure per review, not one tick per conversation, player, or ignored plot hook. Use a different rate only when explicitly declared by the chosen scenario or subsystem.
  6. Show the result: state what changed, why it changed, and what can now be done. Close solved pressures. Offer the next decision rather than requiring the same problem to be solved again.

Keep a cause log. Scheduled ticks represent elapsed events; Story Beat spends represent consequences paid for under the normal rules. Neither manufactures SB. Do not charge the same disruption twice under two labels. An unrelated pressure can advance after a Clean Success because its previously announced deadline elapsed; that is not a complication attached to the successful roll.

Escalation without inflation

Increase the reach of decisions, conflicting commitments, and the need for coordination. Do not increase the DV of the same lock because a character improved their sheet. Roll only when uncertainty and consequences matter; a large dice pool is not itself a reason to call for repeated checks.

  • Space: two communities need help at once. Let players choose, delegate, or create a third solution.
  • Authority: the characters can defeat the guard, but need to decide whether force advances their goal of a legitimate peace.
  • Duration: winning a harvest requires a workable supply arrangement afterward. Make the upkeep and handover visible.
  • Values: two allies want incompatible terms. State the disagreement without secretly making every compromise worthless.
  • Exposure: public victories draw attention. A specific foe may respond through established means; success does not automatically earn a punishment.

Split an undertaking into separate scenes only when each resolves a different uncertainty or commitment. “Roll again because this is epic” adds delay, not scale. For an impossible approach, explain the missing permission or capability and let the players change the situation; do not assign DV 6.

Run the infrastructure honestly

Assets, Followers, Bonds, and Obligation give the characters options. They do not replace Boons or abolish individual costs. Use their published activation and upkeep rules. Delegation can move routine work off-screen, but does not grant limitless follower actions, waive a roll’s consequences, or let a follower exceed its capability.

Fatigue may be paid only when a rule or explicitly agreed extension permits that expenditure. There is no general “buy narrative control with Fatigue” rule here. Apply overflow normally. Likewise, owing nothing does not mean a character has no resources: a solvent institution, a loyal ally, and a paid debt are meaningful achievements.

Offer bargains with a named creditor, benefit, price, deadline, and way to discharge the obligation. Players can refuse. A creditor should pursue its interests rather than appear solely to drain a resource track. Distinguish mortal commitments from magical permissions and consequences; sharing a capacity formula does not make every pact interchangeable.

Large pools can change how often particular outcomes occur, but do not guarantee Clean Success or empty resource pools. Count all rolled 1s and apply the outcome and Bond awards actually earned. Do not manufacture failed checks to refill Boons or invent extra SB because the GM wants a climax.

Advice for epic players

  • Declare both the immediate intent and the hoped-for public benefit. “Secure the river pilots’ agreement” is a resolvable step toward “keep the city fed.”
  • Ask what is at risk. Clarify Position, DV, scope, costs, and likely fallout before committing. The GM need not disclose every hidden fact to make the decision intelligible.
  • Give your power a steward. Name who manages an asset, who speaks when you are absent, and who can disagree with you. Use actual follower and asset rules where mechanical benefits are involved.
  • Share decisive scenes. Expertise earns a spotlight, not every spotlight. Invite the player whose relationships or principles matter to lead the next decision.
  • Make commitments legible. Keep a short ledger of promises, their beneficiaries, and deadlines. Negotiate a price you are willing to play through rather than assuming the GM must later forgive it.
  • Choose an ending. Retirement, reform, succession, sacrifice, or a transformed purpose can be a victory. Remaining at the table need not mean endlessly accumulating power.

Advice for epic GMs

Prepare actors and dependencies, not a predetermined chain of setbacks. Begin sessions with a concise state of the campaign and end with the concrete gains the party achieved. Spend SB according to their costs and the fiction, preserving achieved intent on Success & SB. State likely Partial costs without replacing the outcome matrix with a custom success ladder.

Keep one immediate scene playable at a time. Zoom in on a treaty’s disputed clause, a threatened evacuation, or a steward’s choice. Zoom out on routine travel and administration that the party has genuinely secured. Let characters enjoy the institutions they built before asking what those institutions will become.

For Tier V and long-running Mythic play, change the campaign question: who inherits the peace, who gets access to the miracle, or who holds the founders accountable? World-changing power still needs a defined effect and a valid procedure. Revisit boundaries around mass harm, coercion, and irreversible personal sacrifice with the group rather than assuming epic scope authorizes every theme.

Legacy is an institution, not just a replacement sheet

A legacy can be a school, a treaty, a refuge, an archive, or a practice that other people can sustain. Ask who benefits, who performs the work, who has a voice, and what happens when the founder leaves. A successful founder can hand over authority while remaining a PC; that is a transfer of responsibility, not a second character or a free resource refresh.

The shared rules below are also part of the Player’s Guide. Use them for succession at any tier and for institutional projects when the optional timers suit the campaign.

Legacy at the table: the operating procedure

Scope and changes. These procedures clarify the Player’s Guide’s Legacy Engine. Succession remains a choice made through play. The additions specify a handover record, the Legacy Bond’s timing and limit, inheritance boundaries, and optional Growth and Crisis timers. Crisis is the former “Health” timer, renamed because filling it means trouble. No new skill, character tier, or replacement for the core loop is introduced.

1. Write a Legacy record

Record the predecessor, prospective successor, cause, one guiding value, one unresolved burden, and the people who recognize the succession. List each asset, follower, and obligation separately, with its current controller, condition, upkeep, creditor, and any dispute. Name the player who keeps the record. Review it at each succession and campaign downtime.

A Cap 3+ follower is a well-established candidate, not a mandatory purchase. A family member, student, or other suitable character can succeed them. Becoming a PC ends that character’s follower entry: the same person cannot provide follower assistance to themselves, manage assets as an off-screen follower, and act as a PC simultaneously.

2. Set the handover before building the successor

  1. Agree on the replacement budget. Use the campaign’s current replacement-character creation procedure. Follower Cap and the predecessor’s spent XP are not a second XP allowance. If using the existing optional 4–8 XP inherited-training bonus, set the amount consistently for the campaign and grant it once on this successor’s creation; it does not accumulate through a chain of predecessors.
  2. Carry standing, not automatic obedience. Record inherited allies, enemies, and reputation. Identify which offices and relationships recognize the successor and which are contested. Resolve a contested claim through an appropriate scene; a failed trial complicates the claim rather than forbidding the player from playing their new character.
  3. Choose one burden. State who cares about it, what would discharge it, and what foreseeable event makes it urgent. The burden grants no automatic XP and imposes no blanket dice penalty. Existing debts and obligations remain on their own records; choosing one spotlight burden does not erase the others.
  4. Transfer custody explicitly. With the GM’s agreement and a credible handover, one existing Minor or Standard personal asset may carry over. Preserve its condition and upkeep; this transfers the same asset, not a duplicate or an XP refund. Major assets and disputed offices require recognition or securing control through play. Shared faction assets remain with their existing faction rather than becoming free personal purchases.
  5. Resolve personal commitments. A successor does not automatically inherit talents, rites, patron access, personal Boons, or another person’s magical bargain. Establish any new access under its normal rules. A debt changes debtor only when the relevant agreement permits it; otherwise record who still owes it and why the successor might choose to help.

Archive the predecessor’s sheet and mark transferred entries. Do not erase the history. A contact or inherited title is fictional access, not automatically a rated Asset or Follower. Use the acquisition rules when that mechanical benefit is desired.

3. Invoke the Legacy Bond

Once per player per session, across all Legacy Bonds: when your successor commits to a meaningful action upholding a recorded value of their predecessor, describe a specific memory and the risk or obligation you accept. Before resolving that action, gain 1 Boon and mark the invocation used. It can be spent using the normal Boon rules, including on that action where timing permits. The normal cap and scene-end trim apply; there is no overflow store.

The same action cannot also claim a separate Bond-driven Boon award for the same remembered relationship. Ordinary outcome awards still apply when earned. Repeating a safe gesture, invoking an additional predecessor, or switching characters does not refresh the session limit. Memories can always inform portrayal, but do not automatically reveal secrets, improve Effect, change DV, or guarantee a result.

When the successor rejects or transforms the inherited value, discuss the new wording at downtime. Keep any resulting debts and relationships in the fiction. Rewriting the value grants no extra invocation and is not a refund of prior consequences.

4. Build something that can outlast you (optional)

For a Legacy project, write a concrete public benefit, a steward, beneficiaries, necessary permissions or materials, an upkeep plan, and a completion condition. Choose a Growth [6] timer for a focused local institution or Growth [8] for a project with several substantial dependencies. A realm-wide undertaking should contain distinct projects with distinct benefits, not an arbitrarily huge timer.

Agree on these lengths before work begins. One meaningful project action advances one Growth timer by 2 on Clean Success or Success & SB, 1 on Partial, and 0 on Miss. Apply the core outcome, awards, costs, and Story Beats as usual. These progress amounts are the optional Legacy project default, not a change to all timers or crafting. If a specific subsystem supplies its own project procedure, use that procedure instead.

At most one action per project per campaign downtime can earn these default ticks. Extra PCs and followers can assist or resolve dependencies under their usual rules; they do not multiply the same action into several progress awards. A separately played milestone scene may also advance the project if its stake was agreed beforehand. Do not demand a roll for routine work with no meaningful uncertainty; an agreed completed milestone can receive its stated progress without manufacturing danger.

A filled timer delivers its written benefit once. Establish prerequisites before attempting the finishing action; do not invent a missing permission after success. If work creates an opportunity to acquire a rated asset, write that explicitly as the benefit and use normal acquisition costs. If the project is being conducted under an acquisition procedure already paid for, do not charge twice. Record its steward and ordinary upkeep when it opens. Growth never creates free recurring XP, Boons, or additional actions.

5. Give institutions vulnerabilities (optional)

Use a Crisis [6] timer for a named threat to the institution. Write observable warning signs, the event at completion, and concrete triggers: an unpaid upkeep deadline, loss of a supplier, or a rival’s successful move. It is not a second health bar and does not replace an asset’s existing condition rules.

At the campaign review, mark 1 tick at most for a declared scheduled trigger that occurred during that review period. No trigger means no scheduled tick. An on-screen consequence or a legal Story Beat spend can also advance it when justified by the normal rules and the stated stakes. Log the cause: do not count one event twice as both a scheduled trigger and an extra consequence unless two distinct effects were declared.

A successful action that removes the trigger prevents its future scheduled ticks. A meaningful crisis-reduction action removes 2 ticks on Clean Success or Success & SB, 1 on Partial, and 0 on Miss, to a minimum of zero; resolve other costs and consequences normally. These are Legacy defaults and apply only where the declared action can actually relieve that pressure. One such action per institution per campaign downtime earns these default reductions.

At six ticks, play the announced crisis. Do not silently destroy every inherited resource. Record the actual outcome, use normal rules for any affected asset or follower, and close the timer when the crisis is resolved. A new threat needs a new trigger and warning; do not refill the same timer merely because the institution survived.

6. Review, then move the story forward

At downtime: resolve declared projects and recovery, pay or adjudicate upkeep, check each scheduled pressure trigger once, and record changed custody, debts, and relationships. Show the record to the players. On a generation jump, agree on the interval and resolve a small number of consequential turning points; do not mechanically charge decades of session-by-session upkeep or grant decades of free project actions.

At campaign end, each player names one lasting mark supported by play and the people who maintain it. An epilogue is not a guaranteed permanent immunity from history. A later campaign can explore its fate without treating that possibility as a reason to deny the earlier characters their achieved ending.

Worked campaign: the river that feeds the capital

Situation: flood damage threatens the capital’s food supply. The canal guild wants recognition of its workers’ charter; the provincial council wants independent inspection; a merchant house profits from delay. The party wants a functioning canal under a durable agreement.

Visible setup: Restoration Growth [6] starts at 0; Hunger Crisis [6] starts at 2; Charter Hearing [4] starts at 0. Review once per mission plus downtime. Hunger’s scheduled trigger is “another review interval passes without sufficient deliveries.” The hearing advances one tick per review until it convenes. Its completion begins a decision scene; it does not dictate the vote.

  1. Obtain permission. The party negotiates access to the canal. A Clean Success secures the declared agreement. Do not secretly attach a new condition afterward. This milestone was declared to advance Restoration by 2, bringing it to 2/6.
  2. Organize repairs. A project action during downtime yields Success & SB. Restoration gains 2 and reaches 4/6. The GM spends available SB under the ordinary costs on a distinct, foreshadowed complication: a rival learns which supplier supports the repairs. The repair progress stands.
  3. Review consequences. Deliveries are still insufficient, so Hunger advances once, to 3/6. The hearing advances to 1/4. The GM records the causes; the supplier’s exposure does not automatically add another Hunger tick.
  4. Make a meaningful choice. Next interval, temporary barges could restore deliveries and stop Hunger’s trigger, while permanent repairs still need work. Delegation is possible if a suitable follower has the authority and resources. The party chooses who handles which task under the normal limits.
  5. Complete the legacy. A later successful finishing action takes Restoration to 6/6 and opens the canal under the written agreement. Deliveries stop Hunger’s trigger. Its accumulated social damage does not vanish automatically; a declared relief action can reduce that Crisis. The guild becomes the named steward, with ordinary upkeep and accountability.

A generation later: the founder retires. Her successor inherits the recorded standing and a burden to keep the inspection agreement, not her XP or magical access. A contested council seat is resolved in play. The canal remains a shared institution and is not duplicated onto the successor’s personal asset list.
