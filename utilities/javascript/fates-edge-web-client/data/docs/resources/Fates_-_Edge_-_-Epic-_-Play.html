Introduction

At Tier IV and beyond, the game changes. Not because the rules are different—they aren't—but because what the game asks of you shifts fundamentally.

The core loop remains: Declare → Set Position/DV → Roll → Outcome. But the problems are no longer locked doors or guard patrols. They are debts, votes, charters, and successions. The resources are no longer Boons alone—they are Assets, Followers, and Obligation. And the consequences of every roll ripple across the campaign, not just the scene.

The key insight: A Clean Success at Epic Tier can be the most dangerous outcome, because it gives the players no Boons and the GM no Story Beats—they arrive at the climax with empty hands.

Systemic Problems

A systemic problem is one that cannot be solved with a single roll—it must be architected. The GM designs a web of stakeholders, timers, and consequences, and the players navigate it.

The Anatomy of a Systemic Problem

  • What is the problem? A debt, a vote, a charter, a succession, a border dispute, a faction shift.
  • Who are the stakeholders? Factions, Followers, Patrons, named NPCs.
  • What are the possible outcomes? Map each rung of the Outcome Matrix to a campaign‑level change.
  • What timers are ticking? What ticks them? What happens when they fill?
  • What resources are at play? Assets, Followers, Obligation, Boons.
Example — The Cut Vote (from the Epic session):
Problem: The party's charter is under review by the guild.
Stakeholders: The Cut Garrison (Follower), The Rust Thread (Follower), The Iron Gate (Asset), The Dyed Web (terrestrial patron).
Outcomes: Clean → charter unconditional; SB → condition attached; Partial → a timer opens; Miss → charter reverts.
Timer: The vote itself is the timer—every scene advances it.

Resource Shift: Boons → Infrastructure

At Epic Tier, success is cheap. Players have 6–10 dice in their core pools. They will succeed on DV 3 obstacles. The challenge is making that success meaningful.

  • Assume competence. Don't roll for things the character is guaranteed to do. Lyra doesn't roll to open a lockbox—she rolls to put it back without anyone knowing it was read.
  • Delegate the mundane. Followers roll off‑screen for wage rolls, research, and grunt work.
  • Use Fatigue as currency. When Boons are scarce, pay Fatigue for extra effort (like Khor's Web‑Sense).
  • Build cushions. Assets, Followers, and Bonds replace Boons as the primary resource pool.
  • "I know a guy who knows a guy." The real power at high tier is the network, not the dice.
Boons do not come from winning. They come from struggling.
A party that rolls cleanly all evening arrives at the hard scene with an empty pocket. That's the design.

Obligation at High Tier

Obligation is built into the architecture of Fate's Edge—but at Epic Tier, its implications deserve special attention.

  • The larder is empty. The party that has cleared all its Obligation has no resources left. Taking on new debt is a statement: "I am willing to be vulnerable to win this."
  • Terrestrial vs. supernatural. Fighters take on mortal debts (guilds, lords, syndicates); casters carry cosmic debts (Patrons, Rites). Both use the same mechanics; the flavour is what differentiates them.
  • The clean party runs out of fuel. At Tier IV, a party that refuses to owe anything will find itself without Boons, without Favours, and without leverage by the climax.
  • Obligation is plot, not punishment. When a Patron calls in a debt, it's not a penalty—it's a story hook.
Warning: Don't let players hoard Obligation capacity. The game is designed to demand they spend it. If they're never called upon, they never engage with the Hollow Economy.

Escalation Without Inflation

The GM's trap at Epic Tier is raising Difficulty Values on the same old obstacles. That's inflation, and it punishes players for investing in their sheets.

Instead, escalate—change what the game is about.

Tier What is the obstacle? What is the roll about?
I A locked door Can you open it?
II A locked door Who has the key, and what do they want?
III A locked door What noble owns the door, and why are they locking it?
IV A locked door What guild vote decides who gets to open doors in the city?

The DV for a Tier I lock might be 3. The DV for a Tier IV guild vote might also be 3. The difference is the consequences of success and failure.

Player Evolution

Players don't just get better at the rules—they get better at what the game asks of them.

  • From asking for DV to asking for consequence ladders. Priya's Tier I habit of "what's the DV?" evolved into "what does a Partial cost me?"
  • From improvisation to improvisation with infrastructure. Devon still plays fast and loose—but now he has a network of Followers and a Cache Asset.
  • From quiet to carrying the table's patronage. Naomi went from needing a GM invitation to holding the party's terrestrial debt and playing it with confidence.
  • From optimisation to serving the thing that collects. Ollie min‑maxed his Obligation capacity—and now he's bound to a cosmic Patron that will eventually call it in.
The GM's job at Epic Tier is not to teach the rules. It's to give the players the floor and get out of the way.

GM Advice for Epic Sessions

  • State the full Outcome Matrix upfront. Players need to see all four rungs (Clean, SB, Partial, Miss) before they commit. It makes the choice meaningful.
  • Let Followers handle the boring rolls. If it's a wage roll or a research check, it happens off‑screen. The spotlight belongs on the interesting scenes.
  • Use Fatigue as a currency. When Boons are scarce, let players pay Fatigue for extra effort or narrative control.
  • Don't hoard Story Beats. Spend them early to build tension. The climax is made of the SB you spent along the way.
  • Change what the game is about. At Tier IV, the lock is not the problem—the person who owns the lock is the problem.
  • Assume competence. Don't ask for a roll unless failure is interesting. Let the players succeed and let that success mean something.

Final Word

The Epic Play Expansion is not about new rules—it's about a new relationship to the rules. At Tier I, the GM teaches the players how to roll dice and spend Boons. At Tier IV, the GM teaches the players how to build infrastructure, take on debt, and win by architecture, not luck.

The annotated Epic session is the proof of concept. This expansion is the toolkit to let you run that session yourself.
