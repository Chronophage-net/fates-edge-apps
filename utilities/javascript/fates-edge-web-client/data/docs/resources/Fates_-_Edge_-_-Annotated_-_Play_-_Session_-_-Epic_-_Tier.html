📖 How to Use This Document

This is the sequel to An Annotated Play Session, and it assumes you have read it — or at least that you know how the 90-second loop, Position, DV and the Outcome Matrix work. Everything demonstrated there still applies here without restatement. What this document is for is the thing that surprises tables when they arrive: the rules do not change at Tier IV, and the game changes completely anyway.

Same three annotation colours: GM Guide for a GM-facing procedure, Player Guide for a player-facing one, Rule for a reminder of the underlying mechanic. Where this session leans on GM Guide Chapter 11 — Tier IV & V, the box says so.

The one-sentence version of Epic Tier: the party's dice pools got bigger, so they stopped generating Boons, so every scene is now decided by what they have built rather than what they can roll.

If you take nothing else from this document, take that. Chapter 11 puts it plainly: "Boons do not come from winning. They come from struggling." A Tier IV party struggles less on any single roll and therefore has almost nothing in reserve when a roll finally matters. Assets, Followers, Bonds and patrons are not decoration at this tier. They are the resource economy.

🎭 The Table: Six Years Later

Six years after the party took the Symbol out of Castellan Voss's reliquary, Silkstrand is theirs in every way that does not appear on a map. They hold charters, a garrison, a name. The Symbol they recovered made them creditors of a terrestrial patron — and creditors, in Silkstrand, eventually become debtors. Tonight the ledger is opened in public.

Same GM (Jess), same four players, same four characters. Everyone is Tier IV (151–220 XP). Nobody has more than one Boon.

🛡️ Mateo — Warden of the Cut

Body 5, Melee 4, Endurance 3. Heavy armour. Player: Priya
Priya still plans, but what she plans is now other people. She commands the Cut Garrison (Cap 4 Follower) and holds the Iron Gate (Major Asset, Maintained). Her note card no longer tracks her Fatigue — it tracks two Battle Timers and whose turn it is to be relieved. Her Tier I habit of asking for the DV before committing has become asking what happens on a Partial, which is the Tier IV version of the same question.

🗡️ Lyra — Thread-Holder

Wits 4, Stealth 4, Insight 3, Body 2. Player: Devon
Devon's improvising has not calmed down; it has gained infrastructure. He runs the Rust Thread (Cap 3 Follower network) and keeps a Cache (Standard Asset). He is also the player who adapted fastest to Boon scarcity, because he never hoarded them in the first place — Chapter 11 notes exactly this, that chaos players thrive at high tier. He spends what he has and then plays without a net, cheerfully.

🎻 Sera — Voice of the Dyed Web

Presence 5, Sway 4, Insight 3, Spirit 2. Obligation Capacity 7. Player: Naomi
The quiet one is now the one who speaks. Naomi's character carries the party's terrestrial patronage — the Dyed Web of Silkstrand — which means the table's political scenes route through her by design, not by the GM dragging her in. Six years ago Jess had to ask her leading questions to get her into a scene. Tonight Jess's job is the opposite: give her the floor and get out of the way.

🐦‍⬛ Khor — Pact-Whisperer of the Web

Spirit 4, Presence 4, Arcana 4, Lore 3. Obligation Capacity 8. Player: Ollie
Ollie spent six years treating Obligation as a resource to min-max. He is now bound to Inaea, Angel of the Spider, whose entire domain is webs of obligation — debts, promises, favours and the ties that bind. The character optimised for carrying debt now serves the thing that collects it. Jess did not engineer this to punish him; he built it, and she is simply letting it mean something.

What the party holds, and what holds them. Written on the table where everyone can see it, because at this tier the sheet is not where the interesting numbers live.

GM Guide 11 — Do Not Design Adventures Like Tier IChapter 11 is blunt about it: "Linear dungeons and small-scale fetch quests feel beneath legendary heroes. Instead, give them systemic problems." The Tier I session was a building with a thing inside it. This one is a debt with a city around it. Notice that Jess did not make the rolls harder to earn that difference — she changed what a roll is about.

Scene 1 — The Ledger Room

Demonstrates: Boon scarcity as the real difficulty curve, abstracting the mundane, Asset Free Use, and asking for the Partial instead of the DV
GM: The Archivolt's ledger room, an hour before the guild sits. Four clerks, one door, and a strongbox that would have been the whole of a session six years ago. Lyra — you want the ledger.
Lyra (Devon): I want the ledger. Do I need to roll for the box?
GM: No. You've been opening boxes like that for six years and you have a Cache full of tools you made yourself. The box opens. What I want a roll for is the part that's actually uncertain: whether you can put it back in a state where nobody knows it was read.
GM Guide 11 — Abstract the MundaneChapter 11: "At high tiers, do not roll for climbing walls or picking common locks. Assume competence. Save rolls for choices that matter." This is the single fastest way to make a table feel Tier IV, and it costs nothing. Jess did not give Lyra a bonus for being a legend — she stopped asking her to prove it, and moved the roll to the interesting half of the sentence.
Lyra (Devon): Love that. Wits 4 + Stealth 4, eight dice. What am I rolling against?
GM: Controlled, DV 3. And before you roll — a Partial here means you get the numbers but you leave a thread. Someone will know the box was read, they just won't know by whom, tonight.
Mateo (Priya): That's the question I want answered every time now, honestly. Not "what's the DV," but "what does a Partial cost me." At this tier the DV barely moves.
Player Guide — What to Ask For at Tier IVPriya's habit has evolved correctly. At Tier I, knowing the DV told you whether to commit. At Tier IV your pool clears DV 3 most of the time, so the DV tells you almost nothing — the consequence ladder is where the information is. Chapter 11's scene-design questions are written for the GM, but they work just as well read aloud: what changes on a success, what compromises emerge on a Partial, what new danger arrives on a Miss.
Lyra (Devon): Eight dice. 🎲 9 7 6 5 4 3 2 8 Four Successes, no ones. Clean.
GM: Clean Success. You read it, you close it, and there is no thread. Here's what the ledger says: the Web has been paying the garrison's wages for eleven months. Not the city. The Web.
Lyra (Devon): ...and no Boon for that, right?
GM: No Boon. Four successes, no ones, no cost — the system gave you exactly what you asked for and nothing to spend later.
Rule — Boons Come From StrugglingMiss: 2 Boons. Partial: 1 Boon. Clean Success or Success with SB: none. That is the whole curve, and it is why a Tier IV party is poor. Eight dice against DV 3 produces a Clean Success far more often than five dice did, so the party that rolls well all evening arrives at the hard scene with an empty pocket. Chapter 11 calls this brittleness and says to embrace it: "your power comes from skill, not luck."
Mateo (Priya): Wait. If the Web is paying my garrison, then when the Web calls its contract in, my ninety spears are — whose, exactly?
GM: That's the session. Do you want to check the wage rolls yourself, or do you want to be at the vote?
Mateo (Priya): Ardo checks the wage rolls. That's a Cap 4 follower doing exactly what a lieutenant is for — he rolls it while I'm in the room upstairs.
GM: Good. Ardo rolls 4d10 against DV 3, off-screen, and I'll tell you what he finds when you next see him. That's the Iron Gate earning its keep, too — you don't roll to get into your own gatehouse's paymaster records.
GM Guide 07 — Cap and Free UseTwo different tools in one beat. Cap governs what a Follower does unaided: a Cap 4 Follower rolls 4d10 at a DV you set, and is competent enough to be sent away with a job. Free Use is an Asset's standing benefit while it is Maintained — the Iron Gate's paymaster records are simply available, no roll, because the party holds the gatehouse. Delegating the boring roll to Ardo let Priya be in the scene that mattered without the party losing the information.

Scene 2 — The Vote of the Nine Cuffs

Demonstrates: scaling with stakes instead of DV, the Royal Vote pattern, faction timers, and a terrestrial patron with its own agenda
GM: Nine guild-cuffs around a table the colour of dried blood. Master Valeria has not worn gloves in thirty years and does not start tonight. The motion on the floor is whether the Cut's toll charter stays with your garrison or reverts to the guild. Sera — they're looking at you.
Sera (Naomi): Right. I'm not asking them to be generous, I'm reminding them what the Cut was like before we held it. Presence 5 + Sway 4 — nine dice.
GM: Before you roll, here's the whole ladder, out loud, because you should be choosing whether to roll at all. Clean Success: the charter stays, unconditionally. Success with SB: it stays and I spend the beat — Valeria attaches a condition you will not like. Partial: the vote is deferred, and a new timer opens, Charter Review [6], which the guild advances whether or not you're in the room. Miss: it reverts, and the Web is your landlord by morning.
GM Guide 11 — The Royal VoteThis is Chapter 11's worked example, run at the table almost line for line: "No DV was inflated. The stakes did the work." The DV here is 4 — hard, hostile conditions, active resistance — and it would have been 4 for a Tier I party too. What makes it an epic scene is that all four rungs of the Outcome Matrix change the shape of the campaign, and the players can see that before they commit.
Sera (Naomi): Nine dice, DV 4. 🎲 10 8 7 5 4 4 3 2 1 The 10 is two, plus the 8 and the 7 — four Successes, one Story Beat.
GM: Four meets DV 4, and there's a 1 on the table. Success with SB. You keep the charter — and I'm spending that Beat now. Valeria signs, and as she signs she says: "The Cut keeps its gate. The Web keeps its wages. We will want a name from you before the season turns." She doesn't say whose name.
Sera (Naomi): Do I get a Boon for that?
GM: No — a Success with SB pays nothing. You won. Winning is the thing that doesn't pay.
Player Guide — Winning Is Not IncomeNaomi asking is the right instinct and the answer is the lesson. A Tier I party that fought its way through a scene came out the far side richer, because it missed and partialed along the way. A Tier IV party that executes cleanly comes out with the win and nothing else. If you want a cushion for the end of the night, you cannot earn it by succeeding — you have to build it beforehand out of Assets, Followers and Bonds, or take on debt to buy it.
Khor (Ollie): I want to know who they're going to ask for. Web-Sense — I look at Valeria and the cuff to her left and I want the thread between them.
GM: That's a Corruption-tier benefit of Inaea's, once per scene, and it costs you Fatigue rather than a roll. You take 1 Fatigue and you see it: the debt runs from Valeria to the man on her left, not the other way around. She owes him. Whatever name they want, he chose it.
Khor (Ollie): Fatigue for information with no roll is a great trade and I'd like the record to show I said that before it goes wrong.
Rule — Substituting FatigueA player may pay 1 Fatigue in place of 1 Boon, and Chapter 11 calls this a lifeline at high tiers precisely because Boons have dried up. Jess is applying it to a patron gift rather than a re-roll, which is the same principle: at Tier IV, Fatigue is the currency you still generate, so it becomes the one you spend. The guidance asks for a narrative cost attached, and this one arrives in Scene 4 rather than immediately.
Mateo (Priya): Charter Review didn't open, though. We avoided the timer.
GM: You avoided that one. The Web's Contract [4] has been running since before tonight and it's at three. It advances on the Web's schedule, not yours.
GM Guide 11 — Faction Timers Advance Regardless"Advance long-term threats (War [8]) regardless of player success in individual scenes." This is how a GM keeps pressure on a party that is genuinely good at its job. The party can win every roll in the building and the faction timer still ticks, because it is not a measure of how the party is doing — it is a measure of what someone else is doing while they are busy. Jess showed the number rather than sprung it.

Scene 3 — Holding the Cut

Demonstrates: armies as high-Cap Followers, Battle Timers, leadership rolls instead of soldier-by-soldier resolution, and Follower Sacrifice as a Boon substitute
GM: Two hours later, someone is testing the Cut. Not an army — forty men with a battering frame and no banner, which is worse, because a banner would tell you who to bill. Mateo, you're on the gate with Ardo and ninety spears.
Mateo (Priya): Do I roll Melee forty times?
GM: You do not. Two timers. Hold the Gate [6] — theirs, it fills as they get the frame into position. Break Their Nerve [4] — yours, it fills as the garrison makes the cost of this obvious. You give orders; the garrison rolls. First order?
GM Guide 11 — Let Players Command Armies"Treat armies as high-Cap followers. Use Battle Timers (4–6 segments) for skirmishes. Do not resolve each soldier's attack — abstract with leadership rolls." Two opposed timers turn a battle into a race the whole table can read at a glance, and it puts the interesting decision — what do I spend the garrison on — where the player actually is. Note that Mateo is not rolling Melee at all in this scene. He is Body 5, Melee 4, and none of that is the point any more.
Mateo (Priya): Ardo takes thirty spears to the flank stair and makes noise like there are ninety. Presence 3 + Sway... except that's not my strong pair. Can Ardo just do it?
GM: He can. Cap 4, rolling 4d10 unaided against DV 3 — that's what a lieutenant is for. Roll it.
Mateo (Priya): Ardo, four dice. 🎲 7 5 3 6 Two Successes. Clears DV 3? No — two is under three.
GM: Two against DV 3 is a Partial. He gets the noise made — Break Their Nerve ticks to 1 — but he's committed thirty spears to a stair he now can't pull them off quickly. And a Partial pays a Boon. To you, since it was your order.
Mateo (Priya): First Boon of the night. I'm writing it down like it's a birthday.
Rule — Where the Boons Actually Come From at Tier IVLook at where that Boon came from: not from a nine-dice hero roll, but from a Cap 4 Follower rolling four dice and coming up short. Followers have small pools, which means they Miss and Partial the way Tier I characters did — so a party that delegates generates income a party that solos does not. This is not a trick; it is the Boon curve doing exactly what it says. Delegation is the high-tier economy.
Lyra (Devon): I'm on the frame. Not fighting it — cutting the ropes on the near side so it slews when they push.
GM: Wits + Stealth to get there unseen, then it's automatic; the ropes are ropes. Desperate, though — you're crossing open ground under a wall with archers on it.
Lyra (Devon): Desperate means I re-roll a success, right, not a failure. Eight dice. 🎲 9 6 5 4 3 2 2 1 Two Successes and a 1 — and now I have to re-roll one of the successes.
GM: The lowest one that isn't a 10. Re-roll the 6.
Lyra (Devon): 🎲 → 3 Down to one Success. DV was 3?
GM: DV 3, one Success — Partial. You reach the frame and you cut two ropes of three. It slews, it doesn't fall. Hold the Gate stops at 4 instead of filling. You take a Boon, and you're under the wall in the open with a knife and no plan.
GM Guide 11 — Worsen Position, Don't Raise DVJess wanted this to be dangerous and reached for Position, not DV. Chapter 11: "A Desperate roll already re-rolls successes — that is plenty." The DV stayed at 3 all scene. Desperate turned a comfortable eight-dice roll into a Partial, which is both the danger she wanted and a Boon in Devon's pocket. Raising the DV to 5 instead would have produced the same Partial with none of the fiction and, over a whole session, would have starved the table.
GM: Archers on the wall have you. Body + Athletics to get to cover, DV 3, still Desperate.
Lyra (Devon): Body 2 + Athletics 2, four dice, and I'm Desperate. This is where I die, isn't it.
Mateo (Priya): No. Ardo's thirty are on that stair and they can put shields on the wall lip. He takes the arrows.
GM: That's a Follower Sacrifice, and I'll take it. Ardo's section eats the volley in place of the roll — Ardo takes Harm 1 and the Cut Garrison's Loyalty drops from Steady to Strained. Lyra gets to cover without rolling. Once tonight, Priya. Not twice.
GM Guide 11 — Follower Sacrifice as Boon Substitute"A loyal follower (Cap 3+) can take the consequence of a failed roll in place of a Boon. The follower suffers Harm 1 (or a Condition) and the player avoids spending a Boon. This should be used sparingly (once per session maximum) and only when the fiction supports it." Jess applied all three limits out loud — Cap 3+, once per session, fiction supports it — and then charged for it in a currency Priya cares about more than Boons: her garrison's Loyalty, which is now Strained and will assist at −1 die until she fixes it.
Mateo (Priya): Strained. So they assist at minus one, and I did that to them by using them as a shield.
GM: You did. They also held the gate. Both are true, and the second one is why they'll still be there in the morning.

Scene 4 — Inaea's Price

Demonstrates: Obligation over Capacity, a cosmic patron collecting, and Bonds as the primary Boon engine at Tier IV
GM: The frame is burning and the forty are gone. Khor — you're at seven Obligation of eight, and the thing you serve is the Angel of the Spider, whose whole business is what people owe each other. She has been very patient. Tonight she is done being patient.
Khor (Ollie): I knew this was coming the second I took her. What does she want?
GM: A name. The same name the Web wants. Inaea does not want it told — she wants it owed. She wants you to put someone in her web, and she is content to let you choose who.
GM Guide 03 — Obligation Is Plot, Not Punishment"When a Patron calls in a debt, a story hook arrives. Embrace it." Ollie spent six years optimising his Obligation ledger, and the temptation for a GM is to treat the moment it comes due as a comeuppance. Jess does the opposite — she hands him a choice with his name on it. The debt is not a tax on his cleverness; it is the adventure his cleverness bought.
Khor (Ollie): And if I refuse, I go over Capacity.
GM: If you refuse you mark the eighth, and the ninth after it, and past Capacity every mark is Fatigue and the ledger starts making choices you don't. But refusing is real. It's on the table.
Sera (Naomi): Give her mine.
GM: Say more.
Sera (Naomi): The Web wants a name and Inaea wants someone owing her. Those are the same favour twice. So I go into the web instead of whoever they picked — I take the debt personally, in front of Valeria, and it's mine, and the party's ledger with the Dyed Web goes back to nothing.
GM: ...Naomi. Six years ago you asked me whether it was allowed to talk to a guard.
Sera (Naomi): I've been paying attention.
Player Guide — Accepting Obligation for AdvantageChapter 11 lists this among the ways a high-tier party generates Boons: "Accept new obligations or narrative fallout in exchange for Boons." Sera is not being noble at the cost of her character sheet — she is trading the one resource a Tier IV character still produces in quantity, which is debt, for the resource she cannot otherwise get. That is the intended shape of the economy, and a new player arriving at it on her own is the clearest sign a table has learned the system.
GM: Then here's the mechanics of it. You take 2 Obligation to Inaea personally — you're Presence 5, Spirit 2, Capacity 7, so you can carry it. The party's Web Obligation clears to zero. And because you have put yourself into a web on Khor's behalf, the two of you have a Bond, and it is now the most valuable thing either of you owns.
Khor (Ollie): Which I want to use immediately, because Lyra is about to try something stupid and she has nothing left.
GM: She does have nothing left. Go on.
Khor (Ollie): I spend my action to push both my Boons to Lyra through the Bond. "You owe the Web a night's work — I'm buying it forward."
GM: The Bond flares. Lyra has 2 Boons.
GM Guide 11 — Bonds as the Primary Boon EngineChapter 11 gives this exact manoeuvre as its worked example, down to the shouted line about a debt owed. It matters because it converts backstory into a tactical resource: a Bond that exists only in a character's history does nothing, but a Bond that can move Boons across the table is the difference between a party that has spent everything and a party that has spent everything except where it counts. At Tier IV, expect this to be how most Boons reach the person who needs them.
Lyra (Devon): I would like to state for the record that I did not ask for help and I am taking it anyway.

Scene 5 — Let the Roll Stand

Demonstrates: DV 5 as a telegraphed ceiling, zero Boons, letting a failure stand, and failure that changes the situation rather than delaying it
GM: Under the Archivolt there is a door that predates the guild, and the ward on it was not made by anyone whose name survives. I want to be very clear before anyone rolls: this is DV 5. It is the ceiling. Even with your pools, it may fail.
Khor (Ollie): Understood. Spirit 4 + Arcana 4, eight dice. And I have zero Boons, because I gave them away four minutes ago.
GM Guide 11 — When a Higher DV Is Appropriate"Only when the fiction itself demands it: a god's impossible ward, a lock designed by a legendary artisan, a ritual that twists reality. Even then, DV 5 is the ceiling — the ladder ends there — so telegraph clearly." One DV 5 in a session, announced before the roll, reads as a mythic obstacle. A session where every roll crept to DV 5 would read as arbitrary and would have left this table with nothing to spend all night. Jess spent her one.
Khor (Ollie): Eight dice, DV 5. 🎲 10 8 6 5 4 3 2 1 The 10 is two, plus the 8 and the 6 — four Successes. One Story Beat. Four against five.
GM: Four against DV 5 is a Partial. Do you want to re-roll?
Khor (Ollie): I can't. I have nothing.
GM: Then the die is cast. Tell me what failure looks like.
GM Guide 11 — Let the Roll StandChapter 11 gives Jess this line almost verbatim: "You have 0 Boons left. The die is cast. What does failure look like?" — and notes that it "forces players to engage with consequences rather than erase them." Handing the description of the failure to the player who failed is the move that turns a bad number into a scene. Ollie, who has spent six years steering toward the spotlight, gets it here by losing.
Khor (Ollie): The ward doesn't break, it reads me. It opens a hand's width, enough to see there's a stair, and it takes a thread out of me to do it — Inaea's thread. She's in the door now. She can see what's on the other side and I can't.
GM: Yes. And I'm spending your Story Beat to say what that costs: the ward is open a hand's width and it will not close. Whatever the guild kept down there is now something anyone can reach, including the man on Valeria's left. The Web's Contract ticks to 4 and fills.
Mateo (Priya): So we didn't fail to open the door. We opened it for the wrong person.
GM: That's the Partial. You made progress and it cost you.
GM Guide 11 — Failure Must Matter"At high tiers, a failed roll should never mean 'nothing happens.' … If failure merely delays action without changing the situation, you are not using the system to its full potential." Compare the two available rulings. "The ward holds, try again next session" delays. "The ward is open and you no longer control who walks through it" hands the party a worse world to play in — a rival gained leverage, a secret became reachable, and a faction timer filled. Same Partial, three of Chapter 11's four failure patterns at once.
Khor (Ollie): And I take the Boon for the Partial, which means I end the session with exactly one, which is somehow perfect.
GM: One Boon and a patron inside a door. That's where we're stopping.

Session Wrap-Up

Demonstrates: Boon carryover, XP at Tier IV, Legacy Projects, and repairing what the session broke
GM: Scene ends, so trim to 2 Boons — nobody is anywhere near that. Khor has 1, Lyra has 1 left of the 2 she was given, everyone else has 0. That's a normal Tier IV night.
Mateo (Priya): Downtime. I'm paying the garrison out of our own charter income, publicly, so the wages stop coming from the Web. That should take Loyalty back off Strained.
GM: It should, and it's a downtime scene rather than a roll — you're spending time and money on a thing that is entirely within your power. Loyalty goes back to Steady. The Iron Gate stays Maintained.
GM Guide 11 — Embrace Longer TimelinesDowntime is a phase at this tier, not an epilogue. Chapter 11 uses it to advance faction timers, let rumours spread, and let players build legacies. Priya's repair costs no roll because nothing about it is uncertain — the uncertainty was whether she would notice she had to, and she did.
Sera (Naomi): I want to start something. A house on the Cut that pays passage for people who can't — so the next time the Web wants a name, there are people who owe us instead.
GM: That's a Legacy Project. Give it a timer — I'll say [8], advanced by downtime scenes and by fiction, and when it fills you have a Major Asset that generates its own obligations in your favour. It will also make you a target, because a rival web is still a web.
GM Guide 11 — Legacy ProjectsLong-term timers that span sessions are how a high-tier party converts a night's work into permanent change. Note what Sera is really building: an engine for the resource the party keeps running out of. She is not asking for a fortress or a title. She is asking to be owed things. A player who designs their legacy around the scarce resource has read the tier correctly.
Lyra (Devon): XP?
GM: Session award as usual, and you can convert 2 Boons to 1 XP in downtime, max 2 XP that way. Which none of you can do, because none of you have 2 Boons.
Lyra (Devon): Every single session. It's honestly funny.
Rule — Boon Conversion at Tier IV2 Boons → 1 XP during downtime, capped at 2 XP per session by conversion, and it is functionally dead at high tier. That is not an oversight. A Tier I party can convert because it is failing enough to be rich; a Tier IV party earns its XP from play and legacy rather than from leftover currency. If your Tier IV table is regularly converting Boons to XP, they are struggling more than the tier expects — check whether DVs have crept up.

🎬 GM Takeaways — What Changes at Tier IV

What Jess did on purpose, and what she deliberately did not do

She never raised a DV to make something hard. Across five scenes the DVs were 3, 4, 3, 3 and 5 — the same ladder a Tier I party walks. Everything that made the night dangerous came from somewhere else: Position (Lyra crossing open ground Desperate), timers (four of them, all visible), stakes (a vote where every rung of the Outcome Matrix reshaped the campaign), and scarcity (a table that ended the night with two Boons between four legends).

Stop rolling for competence.

The strongbox opened without a roll. The paymaster records were simply available. Priya's garrison repair was a downtime scene, not a test. Every roll Jess did call for sat on a genuine uncertainty. This is the cheapest possible way to make a table feel legendary, and it also buys back the table-time that the bigger scenes need.

Show the timers.

Hold the Gate [6], Break Their Nerve [4], The Web's Contract [4], Charter Review [6] as a threat that never opened. Written where players can see them. A timer the party cannot see is a GM's private note; a timer on the table is a clock the party plays against, and at this tier it is the main way you apply pressure without touching a single number on anyone's sheet.

Let delegation pay.

The only Boons generated by the party all night came from Ardo's four-dice Partial and Lyra's Desperate one. Followers roll small pools; small pools Miss and Partial; Misses and Partials pay. A GM who quietly resolves Follower actions off-screen without rolling them removes the party's last reliable income. Roll them at the table.

Charge in the right currency.

Follower Sacrifice cost Priya her garrison's Loyalty, not a Boon. Web-Sense cost Khor a Fatigue. Sera's play cost her personal Obligation. At Tier IV the interesting prices are not on the character sheet's left column — they are attached to the things the party built, which is exactly why the party built them.

The one thing to watch for. A Tier IV party that is succeeding cleanly all night is a party with no Boons, and a GM who then springs an unwinnable scene has produced frustration rather than tension. The difference is telegraphing. Jess said "this is DV 5, it may fail" before the roll, laid out all four rungs of the vote before Sera committed, and put every timer where it could be read. The party lost the ward anyway — and it landed as drama rather than ambush, because they chose it with their eyes open.

🎲 Player Takeaways — Four Players, Six Years Later

If you plan, like Priya

Your planning has to move up a level with your character. Tracking your own Fatigue was Tier I; tracking your organisation's state is Tier IV — Loyalty, Asset condition, who is where, which timer moves if you spend an hour. Ask what a Partial costs rather than what the DV is. And remember that everything you built is spendable: a garrison is a resource, and spending it has a price that is paid in Loyalty rather than dice.

If you improvise, like Devon

Good news: the tier was built for you. Chapter 11 notes that chaos players thrive at high tier because they never hoarded Boons and adapt to scarcity by relying on skill and position instead. Keep spending. Keep taking Desperate for the better story — a Desperate Partial is a Boon in your pocket and a scene worth watching. Just notice when you have hit zero, and let someone hand you a Bond.

If you were the quiet one, like Naomi

There is a moment in every long campaign where the person who needed to be invited into scenes becomes the person the scene belongs to. It arrives faster if your character holds something the table needs — a patronage, a name, a debt. Sera's biggest play of the night was not a roll. It was noticing that two factions wanted the same favour and offering to be the price. You are allowed to solve a scene by spending your own sheet.

If you chase the spotlight, like Ollie

The thing you optimised will eventually be the thing that has you. That is not the GM punishing you — a resource with no teeth was never a resource. Ollie's Obligation ledger bought six years of advantage and then bought the best scene of the night. When your patron finally calls, take the hook rather than arguing the number, and notice the move that actually mattered: giving both his Boons away, right before he needed them.
