Rules compatibility

The core rules comprise the SRD plus the extensions in the Player’s Guide and GM Guide. This expansion applies only its stated additions and exceptions. Keep the core loop: declare intent and approach, set Position and DV, roll Attribute + Skill, count successes and Story Beats, resolve the outcome, and apply consequences.

Develops the five daughters as aspects of Inaea, with optional devotional blessings, relationship tracks, and faction pressure clocks. They are not separate Patrons and do not grant free Rites. Anything it introduces is an addition to the core loop, not a replacement for it: a named track or timer here is its own measure and never stands in for Fatigue, Harm or the Outcome Matrix, and no option waives the roll, the Story Beats it generates, or the stated cost unless it says so explicitly.

Core Terms Reference

Before reading further, familiarize yourself with these key terms used throughout this expansion:

  • Connection Weight: The accumulated strain of caring for others. When you carry another’s burden—whether emotional, physical, or spiritual—you mark Connection weight. At the end of a scene, meaningful rest or wanted support can clear 1 Connection Weight total; see Threads at the Table for limits and recovery.

  • Dread: The weight of fear, shame, isolation, or the accumulated pressure of unmet needs. Dread is a voluntary consequence of a named fictional pressure, not a penalty for privacy or refusing intimacy. See Threads at the Table for its cap, costs, and recovery.

  • Connection Timer: A 4-segment tracker representing the development of a relationship. It advances when characters share meaningful moments, make promises, or take risks for one another. When it fills, the participants resolve their agreed relationship question together; deepening, changing, and ending a relationship are all valid. See Threads at the Table.

Prologue: The Iron Rose

They called me the Iron Rose. In the pit I was a weapon—something to be wagered on. I fought for coin, for survival, for the hollow thrill of hearing a crowd roar my name. I never fought for love, and I never believed it could be anything but a weakness: a blade at your back, a hand that releases when the weight grows too heavy.

Then I heard him sing.

He was a goat-herder. Simple, soft-handed. Every dawn he delivered milk to the fighters’ barracks, and I never gave him a second glance. He moved through the world with a gentleness that the pit punished, a kindness that cruelty rewarded. I assumed he was foolish.

I was mistaken.

One morning the rains arrived early. He sought shelter in the stable where I was mending a strap, pulled out his grandfather’s lyre—cracked, warped, held together with twine—and sang a ballad about the fall of the Utaran Empire: a soldier who crossed a river and never returned, and the woman who waited on the far bank, clutching a red thread she could not tie.

I wept.

I had not wept since I was eight years old. I had forgotten how.

He did not stop singing. He did not ask why I cried. He simply kept playing. When he finished, he looked at me not as a weapon, but as a person—someone worth seeing for who I was, not for what I could do.

He looked at me. And for the first time in my life I felt truly seen.

The Decision

Breaking my contract was not a single flash of revelation. It was a slow burn, a thread tightening over days and weeks.

He sang again the next morning, and the morning after that. Each time something shifted inside me; each time the walls I had built around myself cracked a little more. I watched him leave the barracks each day with his empty milk pails and thought, That is a life—a real life.

I had never known how to live a real life; I only knew how to fight. Yet I wanted to learn.

The process of freeing myself unfolded as a series of choices:

  • First, I stopped viewing the pit as my home and began to look toward the farm on the hillside.

  • Second, I stopped thinking of myself as a weapon and started seeing myself as a woman who could choose.

  • Third, I began to plan: I counted the days until my contract expired, saved what little coin I could, and listened for the Syndicate’s patrols.

  • Fourth, I told Tullius.

  • Fifth—and the hardest—I told myself I deserved to be free.

Three months passed. Three months of waking each dawn and choosing, again and again, to believe that love was not a weakness. Three months of teaching myself that I was not a thing to be wagered on. Three months of learning that the man with the cracked lyre was worth fighting for.

When I finally left, I did not run. I walked. I walked out of the pit and into the rain, and I did not look back.

The Mentor’s Last Lesson

I went to Tullius. He was my trainer—the man who had taught me to survive the pit. Old, scarred, and wise in the way of those who have lost everything, he had loved once: a mountain healer who died of fever while he fought in a far-off war. He carried her name on a scrap of parchment tucked into his armor. He never showed it to anyone; he never spoke of it.

But I knew.

“I cannot stay,” I told him. “I will die in the pit and become nothing but a cautionary tale for the next generation of fighters.”

He did not argue. He did not try to convince me to stay.

“The Red Silk Syndicate owns you,” he said. “They paid for your training; they will come for you.”

“I know.”

“Do you know what they do to runaways? They nail your feet to the floor of the pit so you cannot run again. They let the new fighters practice on you.”

“I know.”

“And you are still going.”

I met his gaze. He was not angry, not sad—he was… proud.

“I have trained two hundred and seven fighters,” he said. “I have watched a hundred and nine of them die in the pit. I have buried sixty-three. I have seen only twelve walk away free. And only one of those—only one—was truly happy.”

“Who?”

He smiled. “A fool who fell in love with a goat-herder.”

He pressed a pouch of silver into my hand. “Take the north road. Do not stop until you reach the Aelerian foothills. There is a farm there with a red door. Knock three times and say my name.”

“What about you?”

“I am an old man. I have outlived my loves. It is my turn to pay for yours.”

I did not understand then what he meant. I do now.

He gave me three days. He told the Syndicate I was injured, that I could not fight. He bribed the physician to write a false report. He paid the guards to look the other way. He fed them a story about a fever, a cough, a slow decline.

When the Syndicate came looking for me, he pointed them toward the south road—away from me, away from the farm, away from the goat-herder who still sang ballads about empires that fall.

They did not find him.

The Sacrifice

They nailed him to the training post. They left him there for three days—no water, no shade. They wanted him to tell them where I had gone. He did not. He did not even speak. He simply stared at the sun and whispered her name.

The name on the parchment.

He died with her name on his lips.

He died so I could live. So I could love. So I could stand on a hillside at dawn and listen to a man with a cracked lyre sing about empires that fall and lovers who wait on the far bank. So I could know that I was no longer the weapon. I was the one who was waiting.

I do not know if there is justice in the world. I do not know if the gods notice us. But I know this: Tullius was right. Love is not weakness. It is the only thing worth fighting for.

And the five sisters—the daughters of Inaea—taught me that.

The Scholar’s Question

The scholar paused his quill. The fire had burned low, and the Iron Rose had not spoken for a long moment.

“And his name?” the scholar asked. “The goat-herder’s name?”

The Iron Rose was quiet. The fire cracked.

“I never asked,” she said. “He never told. It didn’t matter.”

She smiled—a small, private thing.

“He was not a name. He was a door. And I walked through.”

The scholar wrote something, then crossed it out. He had never heard a love story that was also a story of survival. He was not sure what to do with that—except to write it down.

The Five Daughters of Inaea: An Introduction

Inaea, Angel of the Spider, is the patron of webs, mercy, and the ties that bind. Yet she has five daughters—not literal beings, but aspects—who embody the different ways love can hold us, change us, and set us free.

In Ecktoria the clergy tell a simpler story. They say Inaea bore five daughters to five mortal lovers, each one a lesson in a different kind of love. The temples have statues, hymns, and feast days for each sister. The faithful pray to Solara for courage in confession, to Venara for strength in marriage, to Zephyria for guidance in courtship, to Gaila for joy in friendship, and to Livaea for protection from dangerous desire.

But the truth is more subtle. The daughters are not beings. They are threads—ways of being, ways of loving. You do not pray to them. You learn from them.

The Five Daughters of Inaea
Daughter Focus The Lesson
Solara Witness Love is not what you take. It is what you see.
Venara Staying Love is not the fire. It is the hearth you return to.
Zephyria Anticipation Love is not the kiss. It is the breath before it.
Gaila Joy Love is not the weight. It is the lightness you find together.
Livaea Hunger Love is not peace. It is the fire that burns away what you no longer need.

This expansion explores each daughter in turn, weaving the gladiator’s story through the lessons they offer. It is not a fully mechanical system; it is a framing—a way of understanding romance in a world of consequence.

How to Use This Expansion

  • For Players: Ask yourself which daughter your character is learning from. What lesson are they struggling to accept? What thread are they trying to tie?

  • For GMs: Use the daughters to shape romantic arcs. A character following Solara needs to be seen. A character following Venara needs to stay. A character following Livaea needs to want. Structure scenes to give them that opportunity.

  • For the Table: This book presents Livaea, Zephyria, Venara, Solara, and Gaila as devotional aspects of Inaea. Their practices organize different kinds of love; they do not add five simultaneous Patron contracts.

Threads at the Table: Optional Rules

These procedures complete the blessings described in the five daughter chapters. They take precedence over those chapters’ shorthand whenever timing, cost, or consent is unclear. The daughters are aspects of Inaea, not five additional Patrons: these observances do not grant a familiar, a magic path, free Rites, or relief from an existing Patron’s prices.

Choose a Practice, Not a Captive Audience

At the start of a session, a willing player may choose one daughter as their character’s active practice. Only that daughter’s listed blessings apply until the next session. No XP purchase is required for this optional table-wide module. The practice’s +1 die applies to one relevant roll per scene, declared before rolling; bonuses from multiple daughters never stack. The GM still sets Position and DV from the fiction and resolves the normal Outcome Matrix. Use only the twelve core skills.

A blessing requires a concrete act that changes the situation: keeping a vigil despite losing an opportunity, providing wanted comfort, or accepting a meaningful delay. Repeating a confession, joke, or declaration cannot manufacture benefits. Keep every printed once-per-session limit. Where a benefit lacks a limit, it is once per scene; across all characters, a recipient can benefit from only one Daughter-granted recovery per scene. A transfer must actually reduce an existing track. Boons remain subject to normal carry limits and scene-end trimming.

A boundary is not a failed roll. No blessing, timer, oath, or faction power determines attraction, consent, forgiveness, or whether a PC stays in a relationship. A player can decline a romantic scene, use a safety tool, or leave an unwanted relationship without losing Boons or gaining Dread. These rules also support friendship, chosen family, and nonromantic devotion. Roll for practical obstacles and uncertain actions, not for permission to override another person’s choice.

Connection Weight: Four Places at the Hearth

Connection Weight runs from 0 to 4. Mark it only when a rule explicitly offers a voluntary burden transfer. At 4, you cannot accept another Daughter-granted transfer until you clear weight; ordinary Aid, care, and core recovery remain available. Weight is not Fatigue, Harm, Obligation, or a second health track.

At the end of a scene, clear 1 Weight if someone provided you meaningful, wanted support or you took a protected rest. This is one recovery total for that scene, regardless of how many people helped. A safe downtime interval with genuine relief from the responsibility clears all Weight. Passing the same burden back and forth does not qualify as support.

Dread: Name the Pressure

Dread runs from 0 to 4 and belongs to this optional module only. Before the first mark, the player and GM name its fictional source: a threatened home, a secret being weaponized, a promised rescue whose time is running out. A player may accept +1 Dread as a declared consequence of that pressure, at most once per scene. Do not assign it for declining intimacy, keeping a reasonable confidence, or refusing a demand for disclosure.

At 4 Dread, the next Daughter blessing the character chooses to use costs 1 Boon in addition to its other costs. Pay before the benefit; if the character declines or cannot pay, they simply do not use that blessing. Core actions remain available. Dread never exceeds 4 and does not cause overflow damage. Solara’s recovery works as printed, including this cost if used at 4. Once per scene, wanted support that directly addresses the named pressure clears 1 Dread; removing the pressure during downtime clears the remaining track. The recipient recovery limit above still applies.

Connection Timers: Questions, Not Ownership

Write a four-segment question both participants want to explore: “Can we trust one another with the sanctuary?” is useful; “Will she become mine?” is not. Mark 1 after a scene in which both participants agree the relationship changed through a risk, a kept promise, a repaired injury, or an honest disagreement. A relationship can gain at most 1 segment per scene from all sources, including Zephyria and Livaea. Their blessings supply the mark only when both participants agree; they do not force progress.

On filling the timer, resolve the question together. A deepened bond, an agreed separation, or a changed commitment are all valid outcomes. Each participating PC gains 1 Boon, subject to normal limits, and the timer closes. A new question must concern a genuinely new situation. Neither a full timer nor its reward establishes access to another person’s body, secrets, property, or obedience.

Five Observances with a Price

These are practical uses of the active practice, not additional magic Rites. Each is once per session and costs the action or scene described. Their +1-die benefits use the practice’s once-per-scene bonus rather than adding a second bonus.

  • Solara: The Covered Mirror. Spend a quiet scene witnessing a willing person’s account without demanding proof or disclosure beyond what they choose. Mark 1 Weight to clear up to 2 of their Dread. Write down one permission they give you, such as carrying a message or speaking as their witness. The permission is specific and revocable; it is not leverage over them.

  • Venara: The Divided Vigil. Spend a scene taking a willing ally’s watch, care duty, or other named responsibility. Mark 1 Weight to clear 1 of their Fatigue. You must actually take over the duty: you cannot also travel, study, or perform another downtime task during that interval. This is the chapter’s Cost of Presence, not an additional recovery.

  • Zephyria: The Unsent Letter. Relinquish a real opportunity to press your case and let the other participant choose when to answer. Gain the chapter’s once-per-session Boon; record what opportunity you gave up. You may request a Connection mark under the shared limit. Silence, refusal, and changing one’s mind remain valid answers.

  • Gaila: The Empty Chair. Organize a scene of welcome with room for someone who has been excluded. The guest chooses how to participate, including quietly. Spend 1 Boon to clear 1 Fatigue from one willing guest; this uses the same recovery allowance as any other Daughter blessing. If there is an uncertain practical obstacle, such as persuading a venue to admit the guest, resolve it normally first.

  • Livaea: The Named Fire. Before a consequential action, name what you want and the tangible risk you accept: losing an invitation, exposing your involvement, or spending the night on the road. Take the action and apply that risk when its stated trigger occurs. Gain the chapter’s once-per-session Boon. This does not improve the Outcome Matrix, cancel SB, or grant a second Connection mark.

Faction Pressure: Make the Web Move

Each heresy below has a six-segment Pressure clock, starting at 0. Tell the players what fills it and what its completion will change. Advance it by 1 after a scene in which the sect achieves a concrete objective. Alternatively, spend 1 available SB after a related roll to advance it by 1 as the announced complication. These are alternatives: never charge SB and add a second automatic tick for the same event. A given clock advances at most once per scene.

On filling, enact the listed public consequence and retire that clock. The consequence changes access, institutions, or the physical situation; it never dictates a PC’s feelings or compels a confession or relationship. Subsequent pressure needs a new, openly stated objective. An ordinary successful intervention can prevent a tick. A decisive intervention that removes an established source of leverage erases 1 segment, at most once per scene; the examples below explain what would qualify. A Partial gives its normal progress and Boon, with the remaining obstacle stated plainly. A Miss gives its normal Boons and lets the announced danger arrive; do not secretly turn it into consent.

The First Daughter: Solara, The Witness

“Solara taught me that to be seen is to be free.”

Lore

Solara is the daughter of the unflinching gaze. She dwells in the space between masks, in the moment when pretense falls away and two souls meet as they truly are. She is the patron of confession, of therapy, of the terrifying gift of being known.

The Ecktorian temples tell a simple story: Solara was born when Inaea looked into a mirror and saw, for the first time, the face of someone who owed her nothing. She is the daughter of witness—the one who teaches that love begins not with taking, but with seeing.

Her followers are therapists, truth-tellers, and those who hold space for the broken. They learn that the deepest intimacy is not in what you give or take, but in the courage to let another witness your unedited truth—and to witness theirs in return.

The Gladiator’s Story: The Moment of Seeing

When the goat-herder looked at me like I was not a weapon, that was Solara.

I had been looked at my whole life. The crowd looked at me with hunger—they wanted blood. The Syndicate looked at me with calculation—they wanted profit. My opponents looked at me with fear or contempt. But no one had ever looked at me like I was a person. Like I was someone worth seeing, not for what I could do, but for who I was.

He looked at me as if I were the only person in the world. And in that moment I felt something I had never felt before: I felt seen.

Mechanics: Solara’s Blessing

Characters who follow Solara gain:

  • The Witness’s Gaze: +1 die to Insight or Craft when holding space for another’s vulnerability.

  • The Courage to Be Seen: Once per session, when you allow yourself to be truly witnessed (a confession, a moment of vulnerability), you may clear 1 Dread.

  • The Cost of Knowing: When you witness another’s truth, you may choose to carry their weight. Mark 1 Connection weight to reduce their Dread by 2.

The Lesson

Solara teaches that love begins with seeing. Not with taking, not with wanting, not with staying—with seeing. To be truly seen is to be known. To be known is to be loved.

The Gladiator’s Reflection

I did not know I was being seen until after I had left. I thought he was just being kind. I thought kindness was a mask, like everything else. But when I looked back, I realized he had never worn a mask. He had simply been there. Seeing me. And that seeing was the beginning of everything.

GM Note: The Witness in Play

Solara’s scenes should feel like release. A character who has been holding something—fear, shame, a secret—finally lets it go in front of someone who will not flinch. The roll (if any) should be low-stakes; the drama is in the choice to speak, not in the risk of failure. Reward vulnerability generously.

The Second Daughter: Venara, The Staying

“Venara taught me that to stay is to be brave.”

Lore

Venara is the daughter of the steady hearth. She dwells in the warmth of presence, in the choice to remain when leaving would be easier. She is the patron of enduring love, of the vows that survive grief, of the quiet insistence of showing up.

The temples tell a different story: Venara was born when Inaea wove a thread so strong it could not be cut. She is the daughter of constancy—the one who teaches that love is not a fire, but a hearth. A fire burns out. A hearth holds.

Her followers are caregivers, spouses, and those who stay when staying is hard. They learn that the deepest intimacy is not in grand gestures, but in the quiet decision to remain.

The Gladiator’s Story: The Choice to Stay

When I left the pit, I did not run. I walked. I walked toward the farm on the hillside. I walked toward the man with the cracked lyre. And I walked away from everything I had ever known.

That was Venara.

It was not a single decision—it was a thousand tiny choices, each one a thread pulled tight. I chose to stay when I could have fled. I chose to stay when the Syndicate came looking. I chose to stay when I was afraid.

Staying was the bravest thing I have ever done.

Mechanics: Venara’s Blessing

Characters who follow Venara gain:

  • The Hearth’s Warmth: +1 die to Sway or Craft when offering comfort or presence.

  • The Courage to Stay: Once per scene, when you choose to stay instead of leaving (a promise kept, a vigil held), you may mark 1 Boon.

  • The Cost of Presence: When you stay for another, you may carry their weight. Mark 1 Connection weight to reduce their Fatigue by 1.

The Lesson

Venara teaches that love is not a fire—it is a hearth. A fire burns bright and fast, but it consumes itself. A hearth holds steady, warming even in the darkest hours. To love is to choose to stay. And staying is the hardest thing.

The Gladiator’s Reflection

Tullius stayed. He stayed at the post, even when they nailed him to it. He stayed because he had loved once, and love had made him strong. He did not run. He did not break. He stayed.

I think of him now, when I want to run. I think of how he taught me that staying is not weakness. It is strength. It is the only strength that matters.

GM Note: The Staying in Play

Venara’s scenes are about endurance. A character who could leave—who has every reason to leave—chooses not to. The drama is in the small, unglamorous decision to remain. Reward patience, not heroics.

The Third Daughter: Zephyria, The Anticipation

“Zephyria taught me that to wait is to be willing.”

Lore

Zephyria is the daughter of the almost-touch. She dwells in the space between breaths, in the charged silence before a first kiss. She is the patron of courtship, of anticipation, of the shimmering threshold of mutual discovery.

The temples say: Zephyria was born when Inaea wove a thread that never quite tightened. She is the daughter of possibility—the one who teaches that love is not the kiss, but the breath before it.

Her followers are flirtatious, playful, and those who understand that the greatest gift is not possession but the thrill of what might be. They learn that anticipation is not a flaw—it is a choice.

The Gladiator’s Story: The Waiting

I watched him for weeks before he sang. I did not speak to him. I just watched. And that watching was its own kind of courtship.

I learned his habits—when he arrived, when he left, how he carried his pails. I learned the shape of his hands, the way he hummed while he worked, the way he smiled at the children who followed him through the barracks.

I did not know I was falling in love. I thought I was just… watching.

That was Zephyria.

Mechanics: Zephyria’s Blessing

Characters who follow Zephyria gain:

  • The Gift of Anticipation: +1 die to Sway or Performance when engaging in flirtation or courtship.

  • The Courage to Wait: Once per session, when you choose to wait instead of rush (a confession withheld, a touch not taken), you may mark 1 Boon.

  • The Cost of Possibility: When you linger in the almost, you may advance a Connection Timer by 1. You may do this once per scene.

The Lesson

Zephyria teaches that love is not a destination—it is a threshold. The moment before the kiss is as important as the kiss itself. The breath before the vow is as sacred as the vow. To love is to wait. And waiting is a gift.

The Gladiator’s Reflection

I did not tell him I loved him until the night I left. I waited. I let the anticipation build, let it fill the space between us. When I finally spoke, the words were not a confession. They were a release. The waiting had made them true.

GM Note: The Anticipation in Play

Zephyria’s scenes are about tension. The character wants something and chooses not to take it—not yet. The drama is in the restraint, the shared glance, the almost-kiss. Reward patience and deliberate pacing.

The Fourth Daughter: Gaila, The Joy

“Gaila taught me that to laugh is to be alive.”

Lore

Gaila is the daughter of shared mirth. She dwells in the laughter that dissolves tension, in the silliness that makes the weight bearable, in the quiet thrill of finding someone whose presence makes your heart feel light.

The temples say: Gaila was born when Inaea wove a thread and laughed at the shape it made. She is the daughter of delight—the one who teaches that love is not a weight, but the lightness you find together.

Her followers are jesters, pranksters, and those who understand that the deepest bond is forged not in sorrow, but in laughter. They learn that a smile given freely is a gift that asks for nothing in return.

The Gladiator’s Story: The Laughter

He sang about empires falling. I wept. He did not stop. He just kept playing.

And then, absurdly, I laughed.

I did not mean to. It just came out—a snort, a sob, a strange sound I had not made in years. He looked up, startled, and then he smiled.

And we laughed together.

That was Gaila.

It was not a grand moment. It was not a confession or a vow. It was just… laughter. But it was the first time I had felt joy in years. And it was the moment I knew I could not stay in the pit.

Mechanics: Gaila’s Blessing

Characters who follow Gaila gain:

  • The Gift of Mirth: +1 die to Sway or Performance when engaging in playfulness or shared delight.

  • The Courage to Laugh: Once per session, when you choose to find joy in a difficult moment, you may mark 1 Boon.

  • The Cost of Lightness: When you share joy with another, you may reduce their Fatigue by 1. You may do this once per scene.

The Lesson

Gaila teaches that love is not a weight—it is the lightness you find together. The laughter that dissolves tension, the silliness that makes the weight bearable, the joy that reminds you why you are fighting. To love is to laugh. And laughter is a gift.

The Gladiator’s Reflection

I still laugh with him, every morning. I laugh at his jokes, his songs, his silly dances in the kitchen. I laugh because I can. Because I am alive. Because love is not a weight—it is the lightness I carry with me.

GM Note: The Joy in Play

Gaila’s scenes are about release from weight. The character finds a moment of levity in the midst of struggle. The drama is in the contrast—joy is meaningful because it is hard-won. Reward moments of genuine, uncomplicated delight.

The Fifth Daughter: Livaea, The Hunger

“Livaea taught me that to want is to be human.”

Lore

Livaea is the daughter of desire. She dwells in the fire that consumes, in the obsession that drives change, in the desperate need to be wanted. She is the patron of passion, of hunger, of the dangerous gift of wanting.

The temples say: Livaea was born when Inaea wove a thread so hot it burned. She is the daughter of fire—the one who teaches that love is not peace, but the blaze that burns away what you no longer need.

Her followers are passionate, possessive, and those who understand that desire is not shameful—it is the engine of change. They learn that wanting is not a weakness. It is a force.

The Gladiator’s Story: The Hunger

I wanted freedom. I wanted to be more than a weapon. I wanted to stand on a hillside and listen to a man with a cracked lyre sing about empires falling. And I wanted it so badly I could taste it.

That was Livaea.

It was not a gentle wanting. It was hunger. It was the fire that burned away everything I thought I was, until only the wanting remained. And when I finally walked out of the pit, I was not running away. I was running toward. Toward the farm, the hillside, the man with the cracked lyre.

Toward the life I wanted.

Mechanics: Livaea’s Blessing

Characters who follow Livaea gain:

  • The Fire of Wanting: +1 die to Sway or Performance when pursuing desire.

  • The Courage to Want: Once per session, when you choose to pursue what you want despite the cost, you may mark 1 Boon.

  • The Cost of Hunger: When you pursue desire, you may advance a Connection Timer by 1. You may do this once per scene.

The Lesson

Livaea teaches that desire is not shameful. It is the engine of change. To love is to want, and to want is to be human. The fire that consumes you can also set you free.

The Gladiator’s Reflection

I still hunger. I hunger for freedom, for joy, for the man who taught me to laugh. But the hunger is not a weight. It is a fire. It is the thing that keeps me moving forward.

GM Note: The Hunger in Play

Livaea’s scenes are about risk. The character wants something badly enough to pursue it even when it costs them. The drama is in the consequences—the Syndicate, the debt, the danger. Reward boldness, but let the cost be real.

The Thread That Holds

The Mentor’s Gift

I did not know Tullius had loved until after he died. The Syndicate—they found the parchment. They showed it to me before they let me go. They thought it would hurt me. They thought it would break me to know that he had kept her name so close.

They were wrong.

I took the parchment. I still carry it. I will not give her name to the scholar—it belongs to the life they shared—but I know she existed. I know he loved her. I know that love was real, and that it did not die when she did. It just became a thread.

A thread he tied between himself and the world. A thread he held until the very end.

And when I stood at his grave—I wept again.

The Gladiator’s Choice

I walked up that hillside six times before I knocked on the red door. The first five times, I turned back. I was afraid he wouldn’t remember me. I was afraid I had imagined it all. I was afraid that the love I had felt was only hunger in disguise.

The sixth time, I knocked.

He opened the door and said, “I was starting to wonder when you’d come.”

And I wept again.

He did not ask why. He just took my hand and led me inside. That was not Solara, or Venara, or Zephyria, or Gaila, or Livaea. It was all of them. It was the thread that holds.

The Vow

I have not seen him in six months. He is still a goat-herder. I am still a fighter. But I do not fight in the pit anymore. I fight for the farm. I fight for the red door. I fight for the man who sang about empires falling while I wept in a stable.

I do not know if Tullius is proud of me. I hope he is. I hope he knows that his lesson was not wasted—that I learned to see, to stay, to wait, to laugh, and to want. That I learned to love.

The daughters of Inaea taught me that love is not a weakness. It is the reason you get up in the morning. It is the thread you tie when everything else falls apart. It is the choice to hold on—even when holding on is the hardest thing you have ever done.

I am going to marry him. My goat-herder with the cracked lyre. I am going to stand on that hillside and make a vow—not to the Syndicate, not to the pit, not to anyone who owns me. I am going to make a vow to him. To stay. To witness. To wait. To laugh. To want.

And I am going to keep it.

GM Note: The Complexity of Romantic Partners

The goat-herder in this story is intentionally a catalyst rather than a fully realized character—the narrative is about the Iron Rose’s transformation, not his. At the table, romantic partners should be as complex as the PCs. Give them desires that conflict with the PC’s, fears that complicate the relationship, and choices that might hurt.

Ask yourself: What does your goat-herder want that the PC doesn’t know about? What would make them leave? What secret would change everything?

GM Notes: Running the Five Daughters

Using the Daughters in Play

The Five Daughters are not a separate mechanical system. They are a narrative framework—a way for players to think about romance in Fate’s Edge.

  • For Players: Ask your character: Which daughter are you learning from? What lesson are you struggling to accept? What thread are you trying to tie?

  • For GMs: Use the daughters to shape romantic arcs. A character following Solara needs to be seen. A character following Venara needs to stay. A character following Livaea needs to want. Structure scenes to give them that opportunity.

Mechanical Integration

Each daughter offers a mechanical blessing, but these are not Talents or Feats. They are permissions—ways of engaging with the romance system.

The Gladiator’s Arc as a Campaign Template

The framing narrative is a complete campaign arc in miniature:

  1. The Prison: The gladiator’s contract (a weight, a constraint)

  2. The Awakening: The goat-herder’s song (witness, joy, anticipation)

  3. The Choice: Breaking the contract (hunger, wanting)

  4. The Sacrifice: The mentor’s death (staying, the cost of love)

  5. The Resolution: The farm, the red door, the vow (staying, witnessing, joy)

This structure can be adapted to any romantic arc in Fate’s Edge.

The Daughters and Inaea

The Five Daughters are aspects of Inaea—different ways her web can hold. They are not separate patrons. They are threads in the web. A player can follow one without following the others, but to understand Inaea fully is to understand all five.

Colophon

This account was transcribed from the words of the Iron Rose—former gladiator, runaway, lover of a goat-herder. She told her story to a scholar who was passing through the Aelerian foothills, looking for ballads about the fall of empires. She did not give her name. She did not give his. She only said:

“I am not a weapon anymore. I am a witness.”

The scholar wrote it all down. He could not help it. He had never heard a love story that was also a story of survival.

He left the farm with a new song in his head—not a ballad about empires, but a ballad about a woman who learned to love. He will not forget her.

The daughters of Inaea are not gods. They are not beings to be worshipped. They are threads in a web—threads that hold, threads that bind, threads that set you free.

And sometimes—just sometimes—a thread is enough.

“The soup is free. The price never is. But the thread holds. And sometimes, it laughs.”

Appendix: The Daughters in Ecktorian Lore

The Ecktorian temples tell a different story. It is simpler, more literal, and entirely wrong in its literalism—but it is also true in its own way.

The clergy say that Inaea bore five daughters to five mortal lovers. Each daughter was born from a different kind of love, and each became the patron of that love.

  • Solara was born from a confession—a moment of perfect honesty between two souls.

  • Venara was born from a vigil—a promise kept through the longest night.

  • Zephyria was born from a glance—a moment of mutual recognition before any word was spoken.

  • Gaila was born from a laugh—a moment of shared joy that needed no reason.

  • Livaea was born from a fire—a hunger that could not be contained.

The temples have statues, hymns, and feast days for each sister. The faithful pray to Solara for courage in confession, to Venara for strength in marriage, to Zephyria for guidance in courtship, to Gaila for joy in friendship, and to Livaea for protection from dangerous desire.

But the truth is more subtle. The daughters are not beings. They are threads—ways of being, ways of loving. You do not pray to them. You learn from them.

The gladiator learned. And in learning, she found her way home.

Feast Days of the Daughters

For tables that want to ground the daughters in the calendar of the Amaranthine:

  • Solara’s Feast (1st of Renewal): A day of confession. Faithful speak one truth they have never spoken aloud. Priests offer absolution without judgment.

  • Venara’s Feast (Midwinter Vigil): The longest night. Couples renew their vows; families gather around hearths that must not be allowed to go out until dawn.

  • Zephyria’s Feast (Spring Eve): A day of courtship. Unmarried youths exchange tokens; the first glance of the season is considered an omen.

  • Gaila’s Feast (Harvest Home): A day of laughter. Festivals, plays, and foolishness are not merely permitted—they are sacred.

  • Livaea’s Feast (The Burning): A dangerous day. Desire is acknowledged, not condemned. The temples light bonfires; the faithful write down what they want and cast it into the flame.

Rival Cults and Heresies

“Heresy” is an accusation made by people with shrines, archives, and interests to defend. These movements disagree about what love permits someone to demand. Some have exposed real temple abuses; some repeat those abuses with a different sacred vocabulary. Their rank and file include frightened people, sincere reformers, opportunists, and neighbors who need a meal. Breaking a sect’s power should leave room to rescue its members.

The Solaran Schism: The House of Open Windows

Doctrine: A truth spoken privately can still protect the powerful. Only public witness can cleanse a community. The Schism began when a temple quietly absolved a magistrate while refusing to hear the people he had harmed. Its first open hearings recovered stolen homes. Its present confessors increasingly treat the refusal to testify as evidence of guilt.

Life inside: Window shutters are removed at initiation; members wear polished tin mirrors at their throats. The dawn meal begins with testimony, but senior confessors choose whose testimony is read. Poor members receive free legal help and food. The hidden price is the confessor’s growing archive of names, debts, and family disputes.

Face: Sister Avedra keeps the banned Thepyrgos congregation moving between laundries. She wants the magistrate’s sealed restitution ledger. She will protect an anonymous witness if the PCs can show that public exposure would endanger them; she cannot admit this exception before her rivals without risking her office.

Pressure [6]: The Unmasking. Tick when a useful secret is obtained, a witness is isolated, or an official licenses an open hearing. On filling, the sect publishes a dossier and bars the accused household from its kitchens and services. Public exposure has real social consequences, but it does not prove every allegation true. Counterplay: Secure corroborating evidence without exposing the witness, win a public right of reply, or remove the sect’s sole copy of a coerced statement; a decisive success can erase 1 Pressure.

Mechanical edge: Borrowed Witness. Once per scene, the speaker gains +1 die on a Sway roll when presenting a relevant, verifiable document to an audience. A stolen diary is not automatically relevant evidence. Showing the audience that the document is false or coerced removes this advantage for the scene; resolve the PCs’ approach with Insight, Lore, or Sway as appropriate, usually DV 3.

The Venaran Inquisition: Keepers of the Last Ember

Doctrine: A vow protects the weakest only if no one can abandon it. The order once forced wealthy households to honor promised support for widows and children. Its judges now confuse continued care with continued possession, and enforce marriage as if it were a property boundary.

Life inside: Every chapterhouse keeps an ember from a hearth its members saved. Petitioners may eat before explaining their case. Judges carry iron fire-tongs rather than scepters. The order’s respectable patrons praise its reliable relief kitchens while ignoring the locked guest rooms behind them.

Face: Judge Meros Tal has never missed a relief payment. His late spouse’s final letter asks him to forgive someone he still prosecutes. He seeks a judgment he can enforce, but will accept a binding support agreement that lets both parties live apart if the PCs make it enforceable and publicly defensible.

Pressure [6]: The Sealed Hearth. Tick when the order gains a warrant, seizes a disputed resource, or recruits an ally to deny sanctuary. On filling, its officers seal the contested dwelling and post guards; inhabitants must find shelter or contest the order. Nobody is magically returned to a partner. Counterplay: Arrange independent maintenance, expose a forged oath, or persuade the warrant’s issuer to withdraw it. Freeing a captive is a concrete objective of its own, not a prerequisite to winning a theological argument.

Mechanical edge: Officers at the Door. Two trained officers act as one TL 2 obstacle: relevant pool 6d10, Resilience 3, medium armor using the core conversion. Their posted warrant gives +1 die to Sway when seeking official cooperation, never to attacks. Destroying the paper alone does not erase its legal backing; discrediting or revoking it does. Violence brings witnesses and lawful scrutiny as announced complications, not an automatic increase in DV.

The Zephyrian Suspension: The Order of the Unclosed Gate

Doctrine: Every completed choice murders a possible future. Its earliest houses sheltered people pressured into unwanted betrothals. Wealthy patrons discovered that a sacred delay also postpones debt settlement, inheritance, and wages. The order now sells the language of freedom to people who can afford never to decide.

Life inside: Letters are copied but not sealed. Thresholds are marked with unfinished spirals. A petitioner receives a bed and one unquestioned night before deciding whether to speak. Beyond that mercy lies a bureaucracy in which someone else’s life can remain “under consideration” for years.

Face: Doorwarden Ilyra keeps two hundred undelivered letters, including her own resignation. She needs proof that a patron is buying delays to starve a rival family. She will open the archive to a petitioner with an independently witnessed deadline.

Pressure [6]: Winter Without an Answer. Tick when a hearing is postponed, a messenger is bought, or a dependent household loses another source of support. On filling, the disputed supply contract lapses and a named refuge loses its next delivery. Counterplay: Obtain a dated decision, establish another supply route, or bring the withheld correspondence to its intended recipients. A relationship’s Connection Timer never advances merely because officials have obstructed its participants.

Mechanical edge: One More Condition. Once per scene, spend 1 available SB after a relevant negotiation roll to introduce a missing signature, absent witness, or delayed courier. State a reachable remedy. This either advances Pressure under the shared limit or creates the new obstacle; it does not do both. Success remains success at the intent agreed before the roll.

The Gailan Chorus: The Fellowship of the Unbroken Smile

Doctrine: Joy keeps the world from unraveling; therefore grief is a failure of communal duty. The Chorus began with kitchen plays during a bitter siege. Those performances mattered. Its current masters use the memory of that survival to silence complaints about hunger, injury, and exploitation.

Life inside: Members sew bells into work clothes and leave a painted bowl outside every meeting place. Newcomers eat for free. Performers are told to smile through exhaustion because the next neighborhood needs hope. Their private songs are much sadder than their public repertoire.

Face: Master Bell Nessa can quiet a riot with a joke but cannot bear a silent room. Her apprentice has written a funeral song she refuses to stage. She fears that admitting grief will destroy the institution that fed her as a child.

Pressure [6]: No Mourning Allowed. Tick when the Chorus cancels a memorial, suppresses a petition, or diverts relief into spectacle. On filling, it secures control of the district’s festival fund and excludes a named bereaved household from aid for “spreading despair.” Counterplay: Stage the forbidden funeral song, open the accounts, or build a kitchen whose meals carry no emotional test. The PCs need not make anyone laugh to succeed.

Mechanical edge: The Crowd’s Refrain. Once per scene, an established Chorus performer gains +1 die to Performance before a friendly audience. A meaningful counter-performance or public testimony can win that audience’s attention with a normal opposed obstacle, usually DV 3. This changes who gets heard; it does not suppress a PC’s grief, prevent speech, or cancel a safety tool.

The Livaean Conclave: Those Who Burn the Measure

Doctrine: A desire deferred belongs to whoever taught you restraint. The Conclave smuggles people out of debt bondage and destroys predatory contracts. Its inner circle then demands that liberation mean obedience to every appetite, including the appetites of its leaders. The Chain-Lanterns hunt it, but individual hunters differ over whether to protect its escapees or treat them as accomplices.

Life inside: Initiates burn a written prohibition over a copper bowl. Good cells provide escape routes, changed clothes, and an honest choice to leave. Abusive cells record favors as debts and call refusal cowardice. Both use the same broken-ring symbol, making testimony more useful than iconography.

Face: Cinder Varo has freed seventeen bonded workers and plans to burn an archive containing both their contracts and the evidence that could convict their owners. His courier Sen wants the contracts copied before the fire. She will bargain with the PCs if they promise the escapees a route that does not end in another cage.

Pressure [6]: The Archive Burns. Tick when fuel is placed, a guard is suborned, or a safe exit is lost. On filling, the fire begins; start a visible Rescue [4] clock for the people still inside. Advance Rescue once per successful, consequential rescue action; after each exchange of dangerous action the GM announces how the fire spreads. At four segments, everyone identified as reachable has a route out. This clock does not erase injuries already taken or make inaccessible people teleport to safety. Counterplay: Remove the fuel, duplicate the contracts, or give the escapees another workable means of protection before ignition.

Mechanical edge: Prepared Fire. If the PCs enter a visibly burning or fuel-soaked area, announce Desperate Position before a risky action there. Suitable protection or a secured route can improve Position normally. Do not also inflate DV merely because the danger is frightening. Varo is a TL 2 organizer (6d10 for planning or persuasion, Resilience 3); he has no supernatural ability to make others desire what he wants.

The Five-Knot Concord and the Cost of Shelter

The small shrines seldom agree on cosmology, but most recognize the Five-Knot Concord: food before testimony, witnesses chosen by the petitioner, vows that name a way to leave, a place for silence at every feast, and no debt created by an unwanted gift. The Concord has no central army. Its authority comes from bakers, midwives, porters, and neighbors who can stop honoring a shrine’s promises.

At a Concord hearth, the PCs can ordinarily obtain one night’s simple shelter and a meal for themselves and the people they escort. This is fictional access, not free Fatigue recovery or a purchased Asset. If supplies are scarce, the keeper states the shortage and asks for a specific contribution; refusing a task does not retroactively create a debt. A household that keeps returning without replenishing supplies faces a practical shortage, not divine punishment.

Ecktoria’s covered mirrors belong to Solara’s listeners: only the speaker decides when to uncover one. Aelaerem’s divided hearthstones let separated households carry one half each, keeping obligations of care while acknowledging the separation. Silkstrand’s unknotted ribbons are offers, not claims; either person may return one without explanation. These practices trouble institutions that need love to resemble ownership.

A useful relic: Tullius’s Unfinished Stitch. A patched strip of training leather is said to come from the Iron Rose’s mentor. Its provenance is disputed. Once per session, the bearer can spend a scene helping someone leave a coercive obligation and gain +1 die on one practical roll toward that departure. This replaces, rather than stacks with, a Daughter’s practice bonus. The relic creates no obedience, erases no warrant, and clears no debt by itself. A forged strip can still inspire courage; deciding whether its magic is real is less urgent than deciding whom the PCs will help.

A Playable Opening: Five Seats, One Empty

On the eve of the Burning, five shrines meet over a relief kitchen’s future. Its keeper has vanished. A bound worker carries her signed instruction to give the kitchen to its cooks; an inquisitorial officer carries a contradictory marriage settlement; a smiling festival committee arrives with an eviction order hidden in its accounts.

The truth: The keeper left willingly to escort two escapees. The marriage settlement is genuine but does not mention the kitchen. The committee’s order rests on a debt the Solaran archive can prove was already paid. Varo plans to burn that archive before dawn. None of these facts requires anyone’s romantic history to be made public.

Start The Archive Burns [6] at 2 and The Sealed Hearth [6] at 1. These are independent pressures; only tick a clock when its own trigger occurs. Give each faction one visible move after a scene, applying the shared once-per-scene limit. The PCs can win through evidence, negotiation, rescue, or an alternative kitchen; they need not defeat every opponent.

  1. At the kitchen: Establish who needs shelter tonight. A successful Insight or Lore approach, DV 2, identifies that the settlement does not include the property. On a Partial, the relevant clause is found but its seal needs authentication; the PC still gains the normal Boon.

  2. At the archive: A willing listener can identify the repayment ledger without revealing a confession. Obtaining it under pressure is typically Wits + Stealth or Presence + Sway, DV 3, with Position announced from the actual route and opposition. A Miss may expose the visit or cost the next safe opening; it does not destroy the only clue offscreen.

  3. Before the fire: Offer Varo copied contracts and a real escape route, remove his means of ignition, or prepare a rescue. Winning the debate alone is insufficient if no one has dealt with the fuel. On success, describe the specific leverage removed and erase 1 relevant Pressure where warranted.

  4. At dawn: Let the cooks and residents decide what they accept. If the PCs secured the kitchen, it becomes a recurring contact and safe meeting place. Turning it into an Asset still requires the core purchase and upkeep. Resolve any Connection question the participants actually advanced; do not award a bond merely for finishing the adventure.

Threads Left Uncut: Adventure Hooks

For GMs seeking complications:

  • Who else loves them? A rival, an old flame, someone who has been waiting and will not step aside.

  • What would make them leave? A duty, a fear, a betrayal they cannot forgive.

  • What do they not know about you? A secret that could change everything—and that you have not yet found the courage to speak.

  • Who disapproves? And what will they do about it?

  • What is the thread that holds? And what happens when it frays?
