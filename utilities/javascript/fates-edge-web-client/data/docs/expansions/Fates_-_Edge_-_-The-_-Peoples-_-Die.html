Before the bit starts: what this actually is

This is a complete, standalone d6 conversion of Fate's Edge. You can run the whole game with five ordinary six-siders out of a board-game box. It is played straight at the table and written as a joke, because it began as a concession in an argument about dice and accessibility — one in which every fact the author had was true and none of them were the point. d10s are cheap and common; the barrier was never money. It was friction: an open game ought to be playable with what is already in the cupboard, tonight, without an order arriving first. Right answer, wrong question.

It is published here for two reasons. First, it works — the numbers are in The Commissar's Appendix at the end, and they are real numbers, not jokes. Second, it is the reference example of a community contribution: a variant that departs from the core rules on purpose, states plainly what it changed and why, and shows its arithmetic so a table can judge it. If you want to contribute a variant, this is the shape. The comedy is optional. The appendix is not.

What This Is

A complete, liberated, standalone variant of Fate's Edge that uses d6s exclusively. No bourgeois d10s. No counter-revolutionary probabilities. Just the pure, proletarian dice of the workers.

What survives, by grace of the People's Union
  • Position & Effect (Dominant / Controlled / Desperate, Limited / Standard / Great)
  • The Outcome Matrix (Clean Success, Success with SB, Partial, Miss)
  • Boons and Story Beats (redistributed for the people)
  • Fatigue and Harm (the suffering of the capitalist class)
  • Character creation framework (now accessible to all)
What has been corrected
  • Counter-revolutionary success thresholds
  • The elitist SB economy (pairs. Pairs are for the people.)
  • Bourgeois starting power (redistributed to the workers)
  • Talent costs (deconstructed and rebuilt for the proletariat)

Part 1: The Core Mechanic (As Mandated by the Party)

The Resolution Loop (United We Roll)

  1. Declare intent and approach. For the people.
  2. GM sets Position and Difficulty (DV). Position: Dominant, Controlled, Desperate. DV: 1 (trivial) to 4+ (extreme). Default DV = 2. The people's DV.
  3. Roll dice. Pool = Attribute + Skill, in d6s. The people's dice.
  4. Count Successes. A 5 is 1 success. A 6 is a critical, worth 2 successes, and grants a bonus (below).
  5. Count Story Beats. Any pair of matching dice is 1 SB. Each additional matching die beyond that pair is +1 SB. So 5,5,5 = 2 SB; 4,4,4,4 = 3 SB; 2,2,5,5 = 2 SB. The math of the people.
  6. Apply the Outcome Matrix. Unchanged. The Party has determined it serves the people.
A clarification the Party has graciously permitted.

An earlier printing said both "5 or 6 = 1 success" and "6 = 2 successes", which is two rules wearing one coat and is exactly the sort of bourgeois ambiguity the revolution was fought to abolish. The ruling: a 6 is worth 2 successes. It is a crit; it counts double; it does not also count once. This is the reading the appendix's numbers assume, and it is the more generous one, which is ideologically correct.

Critical Success on a 6

A 6 counts as 2 successes. Additionally, choose one:

  • Gain a free Boon (redistribution of luck)
  • Improve Position by one step (rise up)
  • Add a narrative flourish (a celebration of the people)

Two 6s: choose two benefits, or one powerful effect. Three or more 6s: legendary success — the people triumph permanently.

Part 2: The Economy (The People's Economy)

Story Beats (GM Resource — Now Collectivized)

Earning SB: every pair generates 1 SB; each additional matching die adds +1 SB.

Why pairs? Because the people demanded solidarity over punishment. The 1 was a tool of oppression. Pairs are cooperation. Pairs are community. Pairs are fun.

SB CostEffect
1Minor pressure: noise, trace, tick a 4–6 segment timer +1
2Moderate setback: alarm raised, lose Position, a lesser foe appears
3Serious trouble: reinforcements, key gear breaks, advance a campaign timer
4+Major turn: trap springs, authority arrives, environment shifts dramatically

Cap: banked SB max = Tier + 2 (Tier I: 3 SB; Tier II: 4 SB). To prevent hoarding. Hoarding is counter-revolutionary.

The cap is load-bearing, comrade.

In the d10 game the SB cap is a tidiness rule. Here it is the only thing standing between your GM and an unmanageable pile of Story Beats, because pairs scale with pool size far faster than 1s do. Do not house-rule it away. See the appendix; the appendix is not joking.

Boons (Player Resource — The People's Wealth)

Earning: Partial gains 1 Boon; Miss gains 2 Boons; a bond-driven action gains 1 Boon (once per bond per session); the GM may award for creative solutions, sacrifices and outstanding play.

Spending: re-roll a single die; activate an Asset; improve Position by one step before a roll; power certain Rites or abilities; convert 2 Boons to 1 XP in downtime (max 2 XP per session).

Cap: max 5 Boons held; reduce to 2 at scene end. No bourgeois hoarding.

Part 3: Position & Effect (Re-educated for the People)

Modifiers. A player may spend 1 Boon before rolling to improve Position by one step. The GM may spend SB to worsen Position or reduce Effect.

The ladder ends, comrade.

An earlier printing said that a bonus past Dominant became +1 die and a penalty past Desperate became −1 die. That rule was retired from the core game — it quietly reinstated the zero-dice death spiral the heritage rules were changed to remove — and the variant follows the core game here. Position never rises above Dominant or falls below Desperate. Extra steps do nothing; they do not become dice. A fourth advantage on an already-Dominant roll is the GM's cue to lower the DV, widen the Effect, or simply say yes.

Part 5: Character Creation (For the People, By the People)

Characters begin with 34 XP. A redistribution of starting power. The bourgeoisie had 32. Now the people have 34.

Step 1: Attributes (The People's Strength)

Distribute 2, 2, 3, 3 among the four attributes (minimum 1, maximum 3 at start). Body the strength of the collective; Wits the perception of the people; Spirit the will of the proletariat; Presence the voice of the revolution. Cost to raise: new rating × 3 XP. A fair tax for improvement.

Step 2: Skills (The People's Talents)

Distribute 8 points among skills (maximum 2 in any skill at start). Cost to raise: new level × 2 XP. Accessible to all.

Sixteen skills, comrade, not eighteen.

An earlier printing listed Brawl, Ritual and Survival. The core game has sixteen skills and none of those three are among them: unarmed fighting is Unarmed, ritual work is Lore or Arcana depending on the path, and living rough is Endurance or Insight depending on what you are actually doing. Inventing skills is how a variant stops being playable alongside published material, which is the one thing a variant must not do. The list below is the canonical sixteen.

Steps 4–7: Equipment, Bonds, Complications, Finalise

Equipment as the core game. The people's tools.

Bonds (solidarity): define one or two mutual bonds. Once per session, acting meaningfully on a bond gains 1 Boon; bonded allies may gift 2 Boons per scene to each other.

Complications (the struggle): choose zero to two. Each gives +2 XP at start, and at scene start the GM gains +1 SB until it is resolved.

Finalise: record starting Fatigue 0, Harm 0, Boons 0. Note Assets and Followers.

Part 10: Design Notes (From My Re-educated Soul)

Why pairs for SB?

I was told to say: "Pairs are more celebratory than 1s. They create a more positive emotional response. The math is sound. The feel is better."

What I actually think: "The Party has determined that 1s are counter-revolutionary. Pairs represent solidarity. Pairs represent collective action. I have been re-educated to understand this truth. Long live pairs. Long live the people's dice."

What the arithmetic actually says is in the appendix, and it is the most interesting thing in this document. Pairs are not a reskin of 1s. They behave completely differently as the pool grows, and that difference turns out to be the load-bearing property of the core game's dice economy.

Why default DV 2?

I was told to say: "A five-die pool averages 1.67 successes, so DV 2 keeps roughly the same success probability as the original game."

What I actually think: "The Party determined that DV 3 was too high. The workers demanded easier success. The workers were right. The workers are always right."

What is true: the 1.67 figure assumed a 6 was worth one success. Ruling the 6 as a crit worth two puts a five-die pool at 2.5 expected successes and a 70% hit rate against DV 2, versus the core game's 61% against DV 3. The variant is a little kinder than the original. The Party considers this a feature.

Why 34 XP?

I was told to say: "Starting characters need more points to feel competent with d6s."

What I actually think: "The bourgeoisie hoarded XP. The proletariat demanded redistribution. 34 XP for all. This is the people's wealth."

Why keep Position and Effect unchanged?

I was told to say: "Position and Effect are narrative tools, not math tools. They work the same regardless of dice type."

What I actually think: "I begged the Party to let me keep something. Anything. They granted me this one concession. I am grateful. I am humble. I am broken."

The People's Die abolishes the Clean Success, and it does so by mathematical law.

Look at the last column. At a pool of seven d6, the probability of rolling with no pair at all is exactly zero — six faces, seven dice, so by the pigeonhole principle two of them must match. A character competent enough to roll seven dice can therefore never achieve a Clean Success. Every single roll they ever make hands the GM at least one Story Beat, for the rest of their career, by arithmetic rather than by fiction.

The Party has considered this and declared it correct: under the People's Die, no victory is ever truly without cost, and the most capable comrades bear the heaviest burden of consequence. This is, admittedly, a very good line. It is also an accident.

Why this happens

P(no pair)100%83.3%55.6%27.8%9.3%1.5%0%

The core game's SB comes from 1s, and the expected number of 1s is simply n/10 — linear, adding a tenth of a Story Beat per die, forever. The variant's SB comes from matches, and matches are combinatorial: they scale with the number of pairs of dice, which grows as n(n−1)/2. At two dice the two systems are nearly identical (0.20 against 0.17). At eight they are not remotely comparable (0.80 against 3.40).

This is the whole lesson, and it is worth more than the joke: the load-bearing property of a dice economy is not its average, it is how the average scales. Two systems can be tuned to agree exactly at a five-die pool and still diverge into completely different games at eight, because one of them is adding and the other is multiplying. If you are converting any system to different dice, check the curve, not the midpoint.

What the Party recommends, should you wish to play this straight

  • Keep the SB cap. Tier + 2 is doing enormous work. Without it a Tier III character hands their GM three or four Story Beats on every roll and the game becomes unplayable in about forty minutes.
  • Or count only the largest match. Score 1 SB for the biggest set on the table and ignore the rest. This flattens the curve substantially and keeps the Clean Success alive into larger pools. It is the single best one-line fix if you want the variant to behave like the original.
  • Or cap SB per roll at 2, regardless of how many pairs appear. Cruder, but it works and it is easy to remember at the table.
  • Do not raise the DV to compensate. The problem is not that success is too easy; the problem is that clean success is too rare. Raising the DV makes both worse.
The Party notes that the third option was proposed by the designer during re-education, and that the designer wept while proposing it, and that this weeping was interpreted as gratitude.

If you want to contribute a variant of your own

This document is the reference example, and the shape it demonstrates is the part worth copying: say what you changed, say why, and show the arithmetic. A variant that states its departures plainly can be evaluated, argued with, adopted, or forked. A variant that hides them can only be trusted or distrusted. The comedy here is entirely optional — the appendix is what makes this a contribution rather than a bit.

And then let someone else read it. The framing at the top of this document used to say the author had been wrong about dice accessibility. A reviewer pointed out that this was imprecise and slightly self-flattering in the wrong direction: no factual error had been made, so calling it one quietly avoids naming the real mistake. The facts were all true. They were answers to a different question — affordability, where the live question was friction. That is a category error, not a factual one, and it is a far more useful thing to be able to spot in your own work. It took an outside reader about a paragraph to see it, and the author several weeks not to. Show your arithmetic; then show the whole thing to someone who will argue with the framing.
