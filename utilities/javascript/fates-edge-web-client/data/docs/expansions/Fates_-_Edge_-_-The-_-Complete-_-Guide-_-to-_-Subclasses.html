The Monk: The Unstruck Bell

The Philosophy of the Still Point

On Stillness

The first time you face a drawn blade, you will be afraid. Good. Fear keeps your breath short and your eyes wide. The moment you feel safe, you have already been struck.

A monk is not a soldier. We do not meet force with force. We redirect. Through a guard's reach, through a gap in the formation, through the space between the blow and its target. The world is full of openings—the fist that overextends, the breath that comes too fast, the weight that shifts one inch too far.

The Three Principles of the Unstruck Bell

The Coin That Does Not Move

Never resist what cannot be stopped. A blade from a trained swordsman? He will strike where he expects resistance. When he finds none, he will stumble. A shove from a larger foe? He expects you to brace. When you yield, his own force becomes his downfall.

Redirect what you cannot stop. There is no shame in moving with the storm.

The Witness Who Breathes

Every fight is witnessed. The trick is not to be invisible—it is to be unremarkable. A monk standing still in a courtyard. A beggar sleeping in a doorway. A servant carrying water. The guards see you. They simply do not register you as a threat.

Stillness is the greatest disguise.

The Debt That Is Never Incurred

When you do not strike, you create no debt. Not yours, not theirs. They owe you nothing but the breath they wasted trying to hit you. A true monk does not seek victory. Victory is a chain. The open hand offers nothing, and so nothing can be taken from it.

On Breath

Before the fist comes the breath. Before the breath comes the stillness. A drowning man does not need a stronger kick; he needs to remember that the surface is only a few inches above his head.

The Four Breaths

  • The Entering Breath The inhale that takes in the world—its noise, its threat, its opportunity. Without this breath, you cannot see what is coming.
  • The Holding Breath The pause between. This is where decisions are made. A long holding breath is patience; a short one is a trap.
  • The Releasing Breath The exhale that becomes action. A punch is a breath released. A word is a breath released. A step is a breath released.
  • The Empty Breath The space after the exhale, before the next inhale. This is where monks live. The enemy expects you to inhale, to prepare, to telegraph. When you learn to act from the empty breath, you become invisible to their predictions.

The Meditation Mini-Game

To find the still point, you need three things:

  1. Stillness — A quiet place where you will not be disturbed. (A crowded tavern can work, but the DV increases.)
  2. Time — At least ten minutes of uninterrupted meditation.
  3. Intent — A clear purpose for your meditation (healing, focus, clarity).
The Four Breaths of Meditation

Step 1: Settle the Body (Wits + Craft, DV 2) \ Find a comfortable position and release physical tension. On a hit, gain +1 die to the Meditation roll. On a miss, you are distracted by an ache or itch—start with -1 die.

Step 2: Settle the Breath (Spirit + Meditation skill, DV 3) \ Count your breaths. Each exhalation is a number. When you reach ten, begin again. On a hit, you enter a light meditative state. On a partial, your mind wanders—you may continue but the GM gains 1 Story Beat.

Step 3: The Still Point (Spirit + Endurance, DV 4 for healing, DV 3 for clarity, DV 5 for transcendence) \ Roll to reach the deepest state. On a hit, you achieve your intent. On a partial, you achieve it but emerge drained (mark 1 Fatigue). On a miss, you fall asleep or become lost in thought; the GM gains 2 Story Beats.

Step 4: Return (No roll, narrative) \ You open your eyes. The world is the same. You are not.

Meditation Benefits
  • Healing Meditation: Clear 1 Fatigue and, if you have a minor Condition (Fear, Shaken, Guilty), test Spirit+Endurance (DV 3) to remove it.
  • Clarity Meditation: Gain +1 die to your next Wits-based roll.
  • Transcendent Meditation: Once per session, reroll any single failed roll. The GM gains 1 Story Beat.

Monastic Traditions

The Ways of the Unstruck Path

A monk is defined not only by talents but by the tradition they follow—the philosophical and martial lineage that shapes their understanding of stillness. Each tradition offers a set of techniques, a patron affinity, and a corruption-like path of deepening mastery.

Using Monastic Traditions

Each tradition is presented as a table of techniques, similar to the Patron's Gift and Signature Talent structures found elsewhere. A monk may learn techniques from their tradition by spending XP as follows:

  • Basic Technique (4-6 XP): A foundational ability that defines the tradition's core approach.
  • Advanced Technique (8-10 XP): A more powerful or versatile ability, often requiring a minimum Tier or attribute.
  • Master Technique (12+ XP): A campaign-defining ability that represents the pinnacle of that tradition's path.

Unlike Patron obligations, tradition techniques do not generate Obligation or Corruption unless the tradition is tied to a specific Patron (as noted in the tradition's description). Instead, they cost Fatigue, Boons, or have narrative costs determined by the GM.

The Way of the Unstruck Bell (Ikasha — Shadow)

Patron Affinity: Ikasha, She Who Sleeps

The Unstruck Bell tradition teaches that the greatest defense is to not be where the strike lands. Followers of this path learn to read the gaps in perception, to become forgettable, and to move through shadows as if the shadows themselves had parted for them.

Basic Technique — Shadow Meld (6 XP)

Prerequisite: Stealth 2+

Once per session as an action, step into any shadow larger than yourself and emerge from another shadow within Near range. You cannot be targeted while moving between shadows. Mark 1 Fatigue after using this technique.

Advanced Technique — The Unobserved (8 XP)

Prerequisite: Tier II, Stealth 3+

When you are Hidden, social Story Beats cannot be used to frame you for a crime you did not commit (unless you act first). Additionally, witnesses suffer -2 dice to recall your face or describe you to authorities.

Master Technique — Threshold of Sleep (12 XP)

Prerequisite: Tier III, Spirit 3+

Once per session, walk through a locked door, a guarded threshold, or a magical ward as if it were mist. This does not work on barriers specifically enchanted against shadow-walking (GM discretion). Mark 2 Fatigue and gain +1 Obligation to Ikasha after using this technique.

Creating Your Own Tradition

When designing a new monastic tradition, consider the following:

  1. Choose a Core Philosophy. What does this tradition value? Stillness? Motion? Endurance? Deception?
  2. Select a Patron Affinity (Optional). Is the tradition tied to a specific Patron, or is it a purely mortal discipline?
  3. Design Three Techniques. Basic (4-6 XP), Advanced (8-10 XP), Master (12+ XP). Each should feel distinct and build on the tradition's core.
  4. Craft a Corruption Table (If Patron-Tied). Six tiers of deepening benefits and costs. The costs should be thematic and roleplay-heavy.
  5. Name It. The best tradition names suggest both a technique and a philosophy: “The Way of the Open Hand,” “The Path of the Whispering Stone,” “The School of the Falling Leaf.”

The Psionic Monk

The Silent Mind

“The mind that moves the mountain first learns to be still. The thought that bends the spoon first learns to be empty.”

The monk and the psion share a secret: both have looked inward and found a door. The monk opens it with breath; the psion opens it with will. At the intersection of these paths lies the psionic monk—a practitioner who has learned to weaponise the silence between thoughts.

Integrating Psionics and Monk Talents

A psionic monk treats their Mental Strain (see the Psionics chapter of the Gray Wanderer's Grimoire) as a secondary resource pool alongside Fatigue. The two are not interchangeable, but certain talents allow conversion:

  • Ki as Mental Strain: A monk with the Psionics skill may spend 1 Mental Strain instead of 1 Fatigue to power any monk talent with the [Flow] tag.
  • Mental Stillness: Once per scene, a psionic monk may reduce their Mental Strain by 1 by spending one minute in meditation (no roll required). This cannot be done in combat.

The Silent Mind's Burden

A psionic monk walks two paths simultaneously, and each path demands its price. The monk pays in Fatigue and obligation; the psion pays in Mental Strain and the slow erosion of the self. When both debts come due at once—when the Fatigue track fills and the Mental Strain overflows—the monk risks becoming a hollow vessel: still breathing, still standing, but no longer present.

The Gray Wanderer once described meeting such a monk in the catacombs beneath Thepyrgos: “He sat in perfect lotus, eyes open, pulse steady. But when I spoke, he did not answer. When I touched his shoulder, he did not blink. The body was a temple, but the god had moved out. I left a coin on his palm and walked away. The coin was still there when I returned a year later.”

Walk both paths if you must. But keep one foot on stone at all times.

The Three-Temples Monastery

The Court That Does Not Move

The Three-Temples Monastery is not a fortress. It is a collection of stones, oak trees, and breath. It has no walls, no guards, no treasury. Its only defense is the reputation of its monks: those who enter with violence in their hearts find that violence returned to them tenfold, and those who enter with empty hands find tea and a place to sleep.

I am Brother Tarn, and I hold the rank of Keeper of the Unstruck Bell. Below me are the Five Elders, each responsible for a tradition. Below them, the monks who have taken vows. And at the bottom, the novices who scrub the floors and learn to breathe.

The Hierarchy of Breath

The monastery does not track rank by years served or enemies defeated. It tracks by breath.

  • Novice (First Breath) The novice learns to sit still for one hour. Most fail. Those who succeed begin to learn the Entering Breath.
  • Initiate (Holding Breath) The initiate learns to hold their breath for three minutes while meditating. This is not a physical test; it is a test of the mind's ability to override the body's panic.
  • Brother/Sister (Releasing Breath) The monk takes their first vow: “I will not strike first.” They learn to redirect force, to parry, to disarm. They may leave the monastery but must return each season to renew the vow.
  • Elder (Empty Breath) The monk has learned to act from the space between thoughts. They are unpredictable, uncanny, and feared even by the other Elders. There are rarely more than five Elders at any time.
  • Keeper (The Unstruck Bell) There is only one Keeper. I hold the position now. When I die—or when I vanish into the hills to die alone—the Elders will choose another. Or they will not. The monastery has survived without a Keeper before.

The Monastery's Assets (What the GM Can Offer)

When players align themselves with the Three-Temples Monastery (or a similar monastic order), they can earn access to:

  • The Breath Registry: A record of every trained monk in the region, their vows, and their current location. Useful for finding allies or tracking fugitives.
  • The Silent Passages: A network of hidden paths beneath the city that only monks know. These passages allow stealthy movement between any two locations in the city that have a shrine or meditation alcove.
  • The Novice Network: A group of young monks who act as messengers, lookouts, and distraction. Each is Cap 1, good for reconnaissance but not combat.
  • The Healing House: A clinic run by the monastery where the sick and injured are treated without question. Monks may use the Healing House to recover Fatigue twice as fast (1 Fatigue per hour instead of per rest).
  • The Unstruck Bell: A legendary artifact—a bell that rings only in the mind. Once per session, a monk may ring the Unstruck Bell (as an action) to impose -2 dice on all fear-based effects against the party for one scene. The bell's location is a secret known only to the Keeper.

Adventure Hooks and Rumors

Jobs from the Monastery (d12)

When the players need work (or the GM needs a hook), roll or choose:

  1. The Broken Vow. A former monk has broken their oath of non-violence and is terrorising the docks. Bring them back alive. The Elders will decide their penance.
  2. The Poisoned Breath. Someone is poisoning the monastery's well. The water tastes of iron, and novices are falling into comas. Find the source.
  3. The Silent Passage Thief. Someone has learned the secret of the Silent Passages and is using them to rob merchant caravans. The thieves' guild is not responsible—someone else is wearing their mask.
  4. The Wandering Keeper. Brother Tarn has disappeared. He left a note: “The bell tolls for me. Choose the next Keeper, or don't.” The Elders cannot agree. They need the party to find him—or confirm his death.
  5. The Rival School. A new martial arts school has opened in the city. They teach the “Way of the Closed Fist”—aggression, dominance, and the exploitation of weakness. They are recruiting from the monastery's novice pool.
  6. The Haunted Meditation Cell. One of the monastery's meditation rooms is now haunted. Monks who enter hear whispers that sound like their own thoughts. Something is feeding on their stillness.
  7. The Debt of the Sleeping Master. An old monk lies in a coma, kept alive by the monastery's healers. His debt to a Patron has come due, and the Patron is sending a collector. The party must protect the sleeping man—or negotiate with the collector.
  8. The Hollow Tithe. The Hollow has noticed the monastery's thresholds. Once a year, the monks leave a basket of bread at the crossroads. This year, the basket came back empty—but something was inside the bread.
  9. The Stolen Breath. A thief has stolen the Breath Registry. Without it, the monastery cannot track its monks' vows. The thief is demanding ransom—but the registry cannot be copied. The party must retrieve the original.
  10. The Novice's Vision. A novice has had a prophetic vision: the monastery will burn within the month. The Elders dismiss it as fever dreams. The novice is terrified. The party must investigate—or prepare for fire.
  11. The Inquisition's Visit. The Chain-Lanterns of Ecktoria have arrived. They have heard rumors of “unsanctioned psionic activity” within the monastery. They demand to search the cells. The Elders refuse. The party is caught between.
  12. The Unstruck Bell's Secret. The Unstruck Bell has been stolen—or has it? The bell cannot be moved; it is a concept, not an object. But the Keeper swears it is gone. The party must discover what “missing” means for a bell that only rings in the mind.

Rumors Heard in the Monastery Courtyard (d12)

  1. Sister Mira once healed a dying warlord. He repaid her by burning a village. She does not heal anyone who carries a weapon anymore.
  2. Brother Stone took his vow of silence after accidentally killing a student during a sparring match. The student was his son.
  3. The Abbot of the Empty Breath is actually the ghost of the monastery's founder. He has been dead for two hundred years. The Keeper is the only one who can see him.
  4. The Silent Passages lead not only under the city, but into the Ways Between. Monks who walk too far sometimes come back with empty eyes.
  5. The monastery's well was dug on the site of an ancient execution ground. That is why the water tastes of iron—and why the Hollowed sometimes gather at midnight to drink.
  6. The Unstruck Bell was not forged. It was found. It lay in a field, half-buried, next to a skeleton in gray robes. The skeleton wore the Abbot's seal.
  7. There is a meditation cell that no one uses. The door is locked. The key was thrown into the well. The novice who asked why was expelled.
  8. Brother Tarn is not the first Keeper to disappear. The previous Keeper walked into the hills thirty years ago. Some say he is still walking.
  9. The Drunken Master tradition was not invented by the monastery. It was borrowed from a wandering beggar who beat three Elders with a broken bottle and then asked for tea.
  10. The Hollow does not collect debts from monks. It is afraid of them. This is not a rumor. This is a warning.

The Debt of the Sleeping Master (Sample Adventure)

The Setup

The party is summoned to the monastery's infirmary. An old monk lies on a pallet of gray wool, eyes open but unseeing, lips moving in a language none of the novices recognize. His name is Master Kai, and he was once a teacher of the Way of the Unstruck Bell. Thirty years ago, he walked into the hills and did not return. Last night, a shepherd found him sitting cross-legged in a field, still breathing, still warm, but utterly absent.

Brother Tarn stands at the bedside. “He is not asleep,” the Keeper says. “He is hiding. Something from his past has caught up with him, and he has run into the only place it cannot follow: his own memory. You must go in after him.”

The Threshold

The party must enter Master Kai's mind. This requires a psionic anchor (a character with the Psionics skill and the Telepathy Art) who can pull the others into the dream. The anchor pays 1 Mental Strain per passenger (minimum 1). Non-psions cannot use psionic Arts inside the dream, but they can see, speak, fight, and interact with the environment normally.

The threshold is the pallet. The witnesses are each other. The price is one memory of comfort associated with the Master, which will be lost forever.

The Dream Heist

Once inside, the party appears in a memory version of the monastery's meditation hall. They must descend through nine chambers, each representing a hidden debt or unspoken regret of Master Kai's past. The final chamber contains the secret he has been hiding: the name of a woman he swore to protect during the Ashaani purges, and then abandoned.

Until the name is spoken aloud in the heart of the dream, the Master cannot wake—and the Spector (a gray figure representing his un-faced death) will stalk the party through the chambers.

Mechanics
  • Master's Breath Timer [8]: Ticks every scene. When full, the Master's body stops breathing. The dream collapses; all PCs mark 2 Harm and gain the Haunted condition.
  • Debt's Approach Timer [6]: Ticks when the party speaks a lie, fails a Endurance test, or damages a dream-construct. When full, the woman's spirit manifests and attacks.
  • Name Unspoken Timer [4]: Fills as the party discovers clues about the woman's identity. When full, the name is known and can be spoken in the final chamber.

Resolution

If the party speaks the name, the Master's eyes close, then open. He looks at Brother Tarn and says, “I am ready now.” He dies within the hour, at peace. The party gains +1 Favor with the monastery and may each clear 1 Obligation or Corruption segment.

If the party fails—if the Master's Breath timer fills before the name is spoken—they wake to find the Master dead, his eyes still open, his lips still moving. The Spector has claimed him. The monastery will remember the party's failure for a generation.

The Summoner's Craft

This chapter presents the mechanics of summoning as practiced by those who follow the Violet Steppe or walk the halls of Varnek Karn. All terms used here are defined in the letters above—this section provides the precise rules for play.

Foundational Concepts

Before mechanics, understand these truths:

  • The Leash is a Promise: Binding a spirit creates a mutual obligation. The spirit serves; the summoner honors the spirit's nature and pays its price.
  • Spirits Have Agency: A spirit may refuse service, act on its nature, or leave if the summoner violates the bargain.
  • Power Requires Balance: Strength in summoning comes not from domination, but from understanding and reciprocity.

Essential Talents

Summoning requires two core talents, representing the minimum investment to engage with spirits meaningfully.

  • Pact-Whisperer (2 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: None. \ Grants the ability to Call spirits (1 action). Without this, no summoning is possible. The spirit appears at Near range but may depart immediately if not bound.

  • Lesser Pactwright (2 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Pact-Whisperer. \ Allows binding of Cap 1 spirits. To bind, spend 1 Boon or mark 1 Fatigue. If the spirit accepts, it is bound; if it refuses, it vanishes.

The Leash: Core Mechanic

Every bound spirit has a Leash Timer tracking the strain on the summoner-spirit bargain. Its length reflects the spirit's power and the summoner's resilience.

  • Length = Spirit's Cap + Summoner's Spirit stat
    • Example: A Cap 2 spirit bound by a summoner with Spirit 3 has a Leash of 5 segments.
  • The Leash ticks (+1 segment) when: \\
    • The spirit takes Harm
    • You command it against its nature (as defined in its entry)
    • It crosses a [WARD] threshold
    • You split focus between multiple spirits (+1 per extra spirit beyond the first)
    • You push it beyond its agreed service (e.g., demanding combat from a healer spirit)
  • When the Leash fills: \\

    • The spirit acts once according to its nature (see Spirit Whims Table), then:

    • Departs peacefully (if the bargain was honoured), or
    • Turns hostile (if the summoner abused the bond; GM discretion)

    The summoner gains 1 Corruption.

  • Boon Finesse: Once per round, spend 1 Boon to clear 1 Leash tick.

Spirit Capacity and Advancement

As summoners grow, they gain access to more powerful spirits and deeper bonds.

  • Greater Pactwright (4 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Lesser Pactwright. \ Required to bind Cap 2 or 3 spirits. Without this, you cannot bind spirits stronger than Cap 1.

  • Spirit Synergy (4 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Lesser Pactwright. \ When commanding two spirits, reduce each Leash tick by 1 (minimum 0). Essential for dual summoners—prevents the Leash from filling too quickly when managing multiple bonds.

  • Spirit Link (10 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Tier II, Spirit 3+. \ Spirits act on your turn; commands are free actions. The bond is so deep that direction requires no effort—though the Leash still ticks for violations.

  • True Name Keeper (15 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Tier III, Lore 3+. \ Call any previously encountered spirit by its true name; reduce its effective Cap by 2 for Leash calculation (to a minimum of Cap 1). The spirit cannot refuse the call—but it remembers every slight, and its nature may turn hostile faster if abused.

  • Soul Anchor (12 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Tier III, Rite of Binding. \ Bind a spirit permanently to a location or object. The Leash Timer does not tick unless the spirit is forced to leave its anchor (e.g., by powerful magic or physical destruction of the anchor).

  • Leash-Breaker (8 XP) \\

    • Prerequisite: Spirit Synergy. \ Once per session, voluntarily release a spirit without triggering its independent action. The spirit departs peacefully, but the summoner gains 1 Fatigue.

Spirit Interaction: Calling, Binding, and Service

This is the flow of a typical summoning encounter.

  1. Call (1 action): Roll Wits + Presence (or relevant skill) vs. the spirit's Resist (typically 3 for minor spirits, 5 for powerful ones). On success, the spirit appears at Near range. On failure, it does not come. On a partial, it appears but is wary—you must succeed on a Bind attempt to keep it.
  2. Bind (1 action): To secure the spirit's service, spend 1 Boon or mark 1 Fatigue. The spirit then decides:
    • Accepts: It is bound. Service begins.
    • Refuses: It vanishes. You may try again later, but the spirit may demand a higher price or refuse outright.
    • Acts on Nature: It immediately performs one action from its Spirit Whims table (see below), then vanishes.
  3. Service: While bound, the spirit performs tasks as agreed. You may:
    • Command it to act within its nature (no Leash tick)
    • Command it against its nature (Leash ticks +1)
    • Ask it to use a special ability (may tick Leash if taxing)

    The spirit may also act independently if provoked (e.g., by disrespect to its nature)—roll on Spirit Whims table if the GM deems it appropriate.

  4. Release: To end service peacefully, succeed on a Wits + Presence roll vs. the spirit's Resist. On success, it departs with no Leash tick. On failure, it may act on its nature before leaving.

When a bound spirit acts independently (due to Leash filling, provocation, or GM discretion), consult this table. The GM may choose or roll d8.

The Hollow: The Unseen Witness

The Hollow is not a spirit—it is the attention that gathers when promises are broken. It is the reason the Leash has weight.

  • Not a Target: You cannot summon, bind, or command the Hollow. It is the context of all bargains.
  • Debt Collector: When the Leash fills and the spirit acts on its nature, the Hollow observes. If the summoner abused the bond (e.g., commanded against nature repeatedly without offering fair price), the Hollow may:
    • Inflict 1 Corruption on the summoner (beyond the Leash-fill cost)
    • Cause the spirit to turn hostile instead of departing
    • Mark the summoner with a Hollow's Attention tag (--1 to all Spirit checks until they make amends)
  • The Ninth Knot: The Leash has nine conceptual segments. The ninth is never ticked—it is the space where the Hollow observes. When a spirit acts according to its nature, the Hollow assesses whether the bargain was kept.

Playing the Summoner

This chapter offers advice for embodying the summoner's role—not just mechanics, but the mindset that makes the system sing.

The Summoner's Mindset

Remember these principles at the table:

  • Listen First: Before calling a spirit, research its nature. A wolf-ancestor won't serve a coward; a Dust-Djanni won't appear for an off-key song.
  • Honor the Price: The spirit's price is non-negotiable. If you cannot pay it, do not call the spirit.
  • Respect Agency: A spirit is not a tool. If it acts on its nature, ask why—it may be telling you something important.
  • Balance Bonds: Binding multiple spirits spreads your attention. Use Spirit Synergy early, and consider whether you truly need that third bond.
  • The Hollow Watches: Every time you strain the Leash, consider if you're honouring the bargain. Short-term gain may bring long-term scrutiny.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

  • “I just need more power!”: Seeking stronger spirits without understanding their nature leads to broken Leashes and hostile entities. Solution: Master Cap 1 spirits first—learn the rhythm of bargaining before moving up.
  • “I'll spend Boons later!”: Hoarding Boons leaves you vulnerable when the Leash fills. Solution: Treat Boons like breath—spend them freely to maintain balance, knowing more will come.
  • “It's just a spirit—I can replace it.”: Spirits remember. Burning a bond with a Dust-Djanni may make all desert spirits wary of you. Solution: Treat each spirit as a unique relationship, not a disposable asset.
  • “The GM is being unfair!”: When a spirit acts on its nature, ask: “Did I violate the bargain?” Often, the answer is yes. Solution: Use the moment to deepen the story—not to argue mechanics.

The Witch

Witchcraft

What a Witch Is (and Isn't)

A witch is a threshold worker—one who listens to Echo (what has been), steadies the Veil (what is), and leans into Flow (what wishes to be). Witchcraft is not brute force; it is fit: finding where the world already wishes to move and giving it the precise nudge that sets it irrevocably in motion.

Hedge-witches there are in plenty: the Breath-Wardens of Aeler, the Hedge-Wrights of Viterra, the Bone-Singers of Ykrul. These are good folk, mostly. Their craft is folk-craft, learned by rote and grandmother's whisper. The true witch—the threshold worker—learns by the Cord, by the Thorn, and by the Silent Ninth. They are the ones who know that a doorway is not an absence but a presence, that a promise is a physical weight, and that to erase a name from memory is to rewrite the stone beneath it.

There are places where the world forgets its laws. Doorways that no one admits to knocking upon. Names that rewrite the one who dares to speak them. Shadows are not evil—they are consequences given shape. They are the costs we refuse to tally, the truths we bury, the debts that return to collect themselves.

Witchcraft is reciprocal and intimate. A working binds the witch to something that can answer back: a threshold, a spirit, a vow, a Name, a patron, a memory, a debt.

Core Principles

The Law of Named Existence

To name is to anchor. True Names fasten an Echo; forgetting frays it. Words spoken under witness reshape how a place remembers. Every curse, blessing, or bargain is a small act of creation through recognition. Un-Naming (suppression, forgetting) can weaken or banish.

Shadows, Thresholds, and Name-Truth

Shadows arise wherever truth is denied, debt is unacknowledged, or identity is fractured by unmet need. They are narrative pressure given teeth.

A threshold is any place where a state changes: waking to dreaming, innocence to agency, belonging to exile, life to echo. Crossing invites witness—and witnesses demand accounting.

A Name is not a label. It is a claim with weight. Names can be protected, traded, buried, sharpened, dulled, or stolen. When a working touches a Name, it touches the self behind it.

The Concordant Layers

People, places, and vows exist as chords across Echo / Veil / Flow.

LayerDomainRisk
EchoAccumulated memory and intentionRecursion (past actions repeat)
VeilPresent state, boundary between potential and actualHairline cracks (subtle reality breaks)
FlowDirection of change, will of elementsOvershoot (change goes too far)

Witches feel dissonance within this chord and decide whether to harmonize (benediction), redirect (hex), or lock in place (binding). Wise law: do not sever, do not overrule, do not forget. Work with the existing harmony.

The Silent Ninth

There is a missing step in the scale—acknowledged, never filled. The Ninth is absence with teeth: erasure, gaps, omissions, and the power of what is left unsaid. Manipulating the Ninth is potent and dangerous. Players never declare the Ninth; GMs may apply it only as consequence.

Push It

Before the roll, or after a partial success, you may Push It to increase scope or intensity. Choose one:

  • Mark +1 Shame and gain +1 Effect.
  • Mark +1 Identity Strain and reduce the DV by 1 (minimum 2).

Prices

Every working demands a price. Choose one unless a rite specifies otherwise.

  • Shadow: You accrue spiritual residue and narrative pressure. The GM may introduce shadow-tells: cold glass, wrong reflections, trailing whispers, predatory coincidences. Clears when the tell manifests or at session end.
  • Shame: You accept an internal consequence—a Condition, a vow-compulsion, a visible hesitation, a ritual aversion. Remove by acting against the compulsion or through confession.
  • Identity: You relinquish a piece of self: a cherished memory dulled, a role surrendered, a name-right compromised, a bond weakened. Mark 1 Identity Strain.

Identity Strain

Identity Strain tracks cumulative erosion from rites that touch the self directly.

Identity Strain is a 4-step track. Mark it when a working severs, rewrites, harvests, or binds a Name; when you Push It with Identity; or when a rite demands it.

At 4, you must resolve strain at the end of the scene: roll Spirit + Endurance vs DV 4. On success, clear 1 Identity Strain. On failure, the GM chooses or offers a hard bargain: a memory blurs, a bond becomes conditional, a Name becomes buried, or an echo-self becomes active.

Identity Strain cannot be healed by ordinary rest. It requires a Full Ritual of restoration (DV 5, requires a witness and a personal offering), a significant downtime scene (confession, remembrance, or reclaiming a lost name), or accepting a permanent change (converting Strain into a Scar or Condition).

Promise Timers

Many workings create obligations, represented by a Promise [4] or Promise [6] timer. Mark the timer when the promise is ignored, deferred, or disrespected. When filled, the price comes due—socially, spiritually, or narratively. Examples: sharing grief, tending a grave, protecting a hearth, reconciling rivals.

Curses as Architecture (GM Tool)

A witch does not curse with a word and a gesture. She edits the memory of the world, inserting a rule into the operating system of reality.

“Until a bell is rung in your true name, lamps forget to light for you.”

Anatomy of a Curse

  • The Rule: What changes (Veil-edit, bound to a Named anchor)
  • The Timer: Recursion [6] — ticks when the victim reinforces the story (hiding, refusing witness, denying the curse)
  • The Counter: What undoes it (the bell, the name, the threshold)
  • The SB Menu: Complications the GM can spend SB on (minor echoes, warped thresholds, layer-slips, Ninth propagation)

Universal Workings

These are patterns of craft shared across Orders; the difference is in doctrine, taboos, and cost.

Veil Working

Hide, blur, silence, soften, misdirect. On success, gain +1d on the next stealth, deception, or evasion roll in the scene. On partial, the veil works but leaves a tell: a reflection moves late, a shadow points wrong, a witness feels watched.

Binding Working

Hold, anchor, compel, constrain, mark. On success, impose a constraint that must be narrated and testable (“cannot speak the lie,” “cannot cross the doorway,” “must answer the question,” etc.). On partial, the constraint holds but spawns a counter-pressure (a timer, a spirit notice, a rival claim).

Unmaking Working

Break oaths, dissolve lesser magic, strip labels, undo a threshold knot. On success, remove one active magical effect, one binding tag, or one scene-level occult constraint. On miss, something else unravels instead (GM choice): an unrelated oath frays, a Name loosens, a memory bleeds into the present.

Crisis Working

Forbidden intensity. The working changes the entire scene. Mark +2 Identity Strain. On success, the scene changes as declared. On partial, it changes—and a second consequence arrives with it. On miss, the working becomes a hungry thing: it demands resolution through a timer, a bargain, or a witness.

On Familiars

A familiar is not a pet, a tool, or a summoned thing. A familiar is a shared consequence.

It is formed when a witch externalizes part of their shadow, memory, instinct, or unspoken need into a living conduit that can act, witness, and answer back. Once bound, neither witch nor familiar remains whole in the way they were before.

A Familiar is a persistent bond that allows a witch to relocate risk, perception, and cost. It amplifies magic by distributing consequence—but never removes it.

Creating a Familiar

To create a familiar, a witch performs a binding at a meaningful threshold and commits to an ongoing obligation. This requires a willing or capturable vessel (animal, spirit, echo, object-entity), a threshold significant to the witch, and a personal offering that cannot be reclaimed.

State: what the Familiar Knows (instinct, secret, fear, hunger); what the Familiar Wants (safety, sensation, purpose, attention); what the Witch Gave Up (memory, role, bond, certainty). Roll Spirit + Witchcraft vs DV 4. Success: the bond forms cleanly. Partial: the bond forms with a flaw (GM defines a behavioral complication). Miss: the bond forms—but the familiar defines the terms of attention, need, or return. Mark +1 Identity Strain; this mark cannot be avoided.

Each familiar has a Bond Track [4] shared with its witch. A familiar may be invoked once per scene without a roll for minor assistance consistent with its nature. For greater effect, choose one option and roll Attribute + Witchcraft:

  • Absorb Consequence: Redirect fallout from a working. Convert 1 Shadow or Shame into 1 Bond. On a miss, the familiar manifests the consequence physically or socially.
Harm to Familiars

Familiars cannot be safely replaced. When a familiar is harmed, the witch immediately marks +1 Identity Strain and rolls Spirit + Endurance vs DV 3. On failure, gain a lingering Condition tied to the harm.

If a familiar is destroyed: the witch permanently loses something bound during creation, all Bond clears, and the witch gains a recurring omen, scar, or echo.

Witchcraft Followers

In the world of Witchcraft, followers are not merely assistants—they are the living conduits of power, the silent witnesses to hidden truths, and the keepers of sacred obligations. Witchcraft followers are not easily acquired. A Debate Between Brothers on the Nature of Making

“The Monad teaches that nothing is ever finished—only briefly sufficient. A blade that cannot be improved is a blade that has already begun to die.” — Korrin Iron-Stroke, Runekeeper of the Clockwork Monad

The Crafting Process

The Five Steps of Creation
  1. Conceive / Inspire — Design the item, or let the idea come to you. What does it do? What does it demand? What is its cost?
  2. Gather — Acquire the components. Often a quest in itself.
  3. Shape — Work the materials. This is the crafting roll, using a timer.
  4. Imbue / Animate — Pour power into the item. This is the final test.
  5. Bind / Release — Seal the work, pay the XP, incur the Obligation, and witness the item's birth.
The Timer

Each crafting project has a timer representing the iterative process—the failures, the refinements, the moments where you step back and see the flaw you missed.

Item TierTimer Size
Cantrip-Infused4 segments

Ticking the Timer: Success on a Craft roll advances 1 segment. Partial Success advances 1 segment, but the GM gains 1 SB (complications). A Miss does not advance; the GM may tick a Complication Timer instead.

When the Timer Fills: The item is ready for the Imbuement roll. Rare components on hand may reduce the DV of the Imbuement roll.

The Imbuement Roll

Roll Wits + Arcana (or appropriate skill) against the item's DV.

  • Clean Success: The item is complete. No flaws.
  • Success with SB: The item is complete, but the GM gains 2 SB to spend on a later complication—a rival who senses the power, a flaw that emerges in a year, a debt the item will demand.
  • Partial: The item is complete, but with a flaw (see below). Accept the flaw or start over.
  • Miss: The item is not complete. Components are consumed; you may try again with new components.

  • Storm Lantern: Once per session, as an action, create a gust of wind that extinguishes all mundane flames in Near and gives +1 Position to disengage.
  • Pendant of the Witness/Trust: Once per session, when you detect a lie, the liar suffers 1 Fatigue (no save); or when you are uncertain, the pendant offers a word of comfort.
  • Raven's/Friend's Key: Once per day, unlock any mundane lock or open any door that is not magically sealed.

The Ninth Rule

The Ninth Rule is simple: never make the ninth item. A craftsman makes eight swords, each better than the last. The ninth would be perfect—and it is also always hungry. It remembers what you sacrificed to make it, and it will use that against you.

Korrin's View: Count your breaths. Walk eight steps. Do not make the ninth. The Monad is patient; it can wait for the tenth.

Durin's View: The ninth item is not cursed—it is lonely, made without love. Make it with joy instead, and it will never turn against you.

Use this as a GM tool: an NPC or PC artificer who breaks the Ninth Rule has made something extraordinary and dangerous, at a cost only revealed later.
