Safety first, and not as a formality.

This expansion deals in psychological deterioration, body horror, cosmic insignificance and the loss of self. Run the Session Zero conversation in Safety and Consent below before the first scene, and keep GM Guide Appendix E to hand. Lines and Veils, the X-card and a check-in at the end of each session are all assumed here, not optional. A horror campaign that nobody wants to come back to has failed at the only thing it was for.

What this expansion adds

Horror in Fate's Edge uses parts the game already has: Boons, timers, and Position. There is no sanity score and no second resolution engine. A Dread clock follows the strain fear places on a character, while Boons let players decide when a character finds enough nerve, companionship, or practiced denial to endure. Around that choice sit campaign escalation, breaking points, sensory investigation, and templates for folk horror, cosmic horror, haunting, isolation, and body horror.

All clocks here are ordinary clocks.

Every timer in this expansion is a standard named clock of 4, 6, 8 or 10 segments and advances the way any other clock does. The source draft carried some at 12 and 15 segments; those are now [10], which is the longest clock the game has and quite long enough for a campaign to end inside.

A Letter to the Reader

You have found a thing that should not exist. I am Quentin Asselin, the first to drink from Malachai's wound. I was a paladin once. Now I am a monster—but a monster who keeps records.

The Chained Angel speaks to me still. Not in words. In interest. Every century I return to Zahk'Kozahn to pay tribute. Every century I find something new in the ruins: a confession, a bargaining chip, a thread that leads to a deeper debt.

This expansion is not my work. It is a collection of fragments I have stolen, copied, or been given by those who owe me. The Gray Wanderer transcribed them. I only provide the shelf.

The first fragment is a confession. It was found in a lead-lined box in a Daughter's safe house, hidden beneath a floorboard that had been nailed shut from below. The handwriting is Sister Aris's. The story is about a bargain made at the Well of Ichor, a hollowed child, and a plague that will come due in three generations.

You will read it and think you understand Malachai. You do not. You will think you understand the Cord. You do not.

But you will be closer to the edge. And the edge is where I live.

— Baron Quentin Asselin Belcourt, under a sky that has forgotten its stars

An account written by Lerris Fenwood, fifth son of Duke Braedon Fenwood

I did not want to look into the mirror. But the ritual required it, and a fifth son learns early that wanting has nothing to do with duty. The incense smoke coiled like a serpent before the glass. I stared, awaiting the name of my father's secret to manifest.

The glass fogged. The fog cleared. My own bewildered face stared back—and below it, a name etched not in the glass, but in the very air, written in a scribe's fine hand. A name that should not have been there. A name that chilled me. Asselin.

A Baron of neither kingdom and both. A man whose title Viterra and Vhasia each claimed was the other's problem. A legend, almost. His barony nestled in the shadow of the Aelerian mountains, north of memory. No crown sent for him, nor did any tax collector dare call him to account. The court preferred to forget he existed.

The mirror, it seemed, had a longer memory than any king.

The Road North

The roads north of the Belworth were not for scholars. I needed a guide who knew the ways between maps. I found Dusana of the Raven Road camped outside Synod Square, a living legend. I asked her to lead me to the Baron's holdfast.

“No,” was her reply.

It seemeed oddly short.

I pressed. She offered their traditional refusal: she would listen to a story and weigh its worth. In desperation, I told a story I should not have known—one my brother Markus had rambled in his broken sleep after his mind shattered at the University.

Dusana's charming smile vanished into a flash of horror, then a frown of resignation.

We will take you,“ she said sadly. And no more stories.”

North we went. Past the Black River, through holdings I knew only from ledgers, to a land where the sun seemed to wane and the pallor of the people deepened with every mile. Dusana spoke little. Most of her words were “princeling.”

At a scarred and ancient tollbooth, she stopped. “We're here. We go no further.”

No sooner had her wagons vanished back into the mist than two riders emerged from it. They were dressed in sharp uniforms of dark red and blue. Their cheekbones were as sharp as their sabers.

“We take you,” the left one said, his Vhasian accent inflected by something cold and ancient.

The right rider sniffed. “To the village, and then three nights' ride.”

They knew why I had come. I had not said a word. The Baron's Threshold

We walked through the courtyard, into the keep, and through a gallery of portraits—each of a woman or a man, young, beautiful. Each bore a brass nameplate with a name scratched beyond legibility. The hand of the artwork was the same. And yet the paint faded and the frames aged as we proceeded.

The Baron awaited us in his parlor. He was a tall man, dark of hair and light of eye. His build was that of a warrior, refined by noble blood. His only mark was a small dueling scar on his left cheek. He dressed in fine white silk, emblazoned with a symbol I could not quite place.

“The Order of the Lost Angel,” the Baron stated flatly, his baritone voice echoing through the parlor as if he read my thoughts. “Welcome, young Lerris. My apologies—I am preparing for a long voyage, and my time is limited.”

He gestured idly to two servants carrying a large chest. It sang of silver within as they jostled it.

“You reek of garlic and hedge-witchery,” he said. “So let us dispense with pretense. I am the Baron Asselin. I am ageless. Do you come as a hunter or a guest?”

Quite the greeting.

“I am Lerris,” I said hesitantly, before shaking my head. He knew that. He knew my name. “I come as a guest, my lord. Nothing more.”

“No need for such pleasantries. Your birth and station are far above mine, and you are welcome.” He gestured to his wiry servant—a man of middling years with steel eyes. On his neck was the tattoo of the Chain-Lanterns, marking him as an inquisitor.

“However, I can smell the lust in you. The hunger.” The Baron continued, his voice soft. “I have smelled it a dozen dozen times.” He made a small gesture. Two servants bowed and receded behind the gallery.

“First, a bath. I don't know what smells worse—the garlic, or the hypocrisy of that iron emblem of the Everflame you wear over your heart.”

The water was hot. The steam rose like fog. I did not look for names in it.

The First Night: The Consort on the Ledger of Fear

She came at midnight—a woman clad in riding leathers worn soft as old linen, her hair a shock of white that spoke of terror, not age. Her eyes were the color of week-old bruises, and she carried no weapon. She did not need one. The way she moved was her weapon; a predator's grace barely contained by a veneer of courtly softness. Her smile, when it came, was a scalpel: precise, intimate, and cruel.

She leaned against my doorframe, the candlelight guttering in her wake. Her shadow stayed pinned to the wall behind her, refusing to follow.

“The Baron sends his regards,” she said. Her voice was the sound of velvet dragged over stone. “He says you are afraid. Good. Fear is the first honest thing you have brought into this house.”

She glided into the room. The air grew cold, yet heat prickled at the nape of my neck. I am a scholar, a duke's son, taught to dissect emotion with reason. But this was a primal calculus I could not solve. Her presence violated my quiet study, and a part of me, a part I despised, was fascinated by it.

“You wish to hunt the things that hunt us?” She placed a hand on my arm. Her touch was like polished marble—smooth and impossibly cold. I shuddered, but did not pull away. A gentleman does not. Yet my heart thudded a frantic, shameful rhythm against my ribs. “Then learn the first lesson: fear is not your enemy. It is your coal. You stoke it. You burn it. You let it forge you.”

Her fingers pressed lightly against my wrist, where my pulse betrayed me. She felt it, and a flicker of amusement passed through her ancient eyes. This was as natural to her as breathing. “I know its heat. I hunted him for seven years across the bones of three empires. I burned through every comrade, every coin, every prayer. And when I finally stood before him, I had nothing left. No steel. No silver. No hope. Only the fear.” Her gaze turned inward, staring at a memory I was grateful I couldn't see. “It was the last thing I owned.”

“What did you do?” The question left my lips in a whisper, breathless in a way that had nothing to do with dread.

Her smirk was a silent laugh. She saw my impatience, my need for a hero's ending, and the other, baser curiosity simmering beneath. Her hand, which had stilled, began to move again—a slow, rhythmic circle on my arm. Up. Down. A courtesan's expertise in a hunter's gesture. My skin burned where she touched it.

“The world is cold, princeling. Cold and lonely as a forgotten grave. You must eat your fear. Swallow it whole. Let it arm you. Let it armor you. Let it be the blanket that smothers your weakness.” She took a breath, a perfect mimicry of life. “It will make you hard. It will make you swift. A perfect hunter.” She said the words, but she had been remade into something else entirely, and the pride in her voice was the most terrifying thing of all.

“I thought I was conquering it. With every stake driven, every heart stilled, I felt a little of it leave me. I was so proud of my emptiness. Until I stood before him, truly empty, and realized I had spent my last coin. I looked into his eyes—first in defiance, then in weariness. A mortal life is so long. So short. Fear stretches every second and culls the years. I was tired of both.”

Her lips curled, revealing the tips of sharp canines. The smirk traveled through me, a visceral thrill of danger that coiled low in my belly. I was disgusted with myself. I was enthralled.

“So I knelt,” she said, as if describing a choice of wine. “My fear was gone, and in its place was acceptance. A new purpose.” Her eyes turned wistful; she believed it. She saw the altar and called it a throne.

She leaned close, her breath a winter chill against my ear. I flinched, but did not retreat. “The lesson, scholar: you cannot destroy what you fear. You can only spend it. The wages of fear are a short, miserable life. But they are a hunter's currency. Spend it all, and you will have nothing left to pay for your life. You will kneel.” Her voice dropped to a whisper, a sound meant for bedrooms. “And you will find it a relief.”

At the table: The Dread Timer [10] follows how fear narrows a character's choices. Spending a Boon does not buy sanity; it shows what lets them remain themselves for one moment longer.

She left as silently as she came. Pausing at the threshold, she glanced back, her form already blending with the shadows. “After all,” she murmured, the words hanging in the air like a perfume long after she was gone, “I am his most beloved consort.”

The door closed. The room was colder than the grave. I stood there, my arm still burning from her touch, my mind a riot of terror, philosophy, and a shameful, aching want. I understood nothing.

The Second Night: The Child on the Geometry of Madness

He did not come through the window so much as the window became him. One moment it was a rectangle of night sky, the next it was a frame around a perched silhouette that hurt to look at. He was a boy of sixteen, perhaps, but shaped by a hand that understood anatomy as a suggestion. His hands were too large, his smile a rictus of needle-pointed teeth that did not fit his face. He did not move. He rearranged.

A sound came from him, not a voice but the memory of one, filtered through broken glass and static. “The Baron says you are a question. Questions have shapes. Yours is sharp.”

I clutched my journal like a shield. “What are you?”

A tremor passed through him. “I was a hunter. I was very good. I hunted the numbers. The angles of fear.” His head tilted past the point a spine should allow. “I learned the first rule: the monster is not the point. The point is the hole it makes in the world when you push it through.”

He flowed from the sill, a motion more liquid than human, and began to trace a circle around me. The air hummed. The walls behind him wavered, the mortar between stones bleeding a slow, black sap.

“I unmade three of his get,” he whispered, and the whisper came from the corner of the room. “With each un-making, the world relaxed. The numbers got polite. They showed me their manners. The walls sang to me. They were not good songs. Not songs to be sung.”

He stopped directly in front of me, his eyes two voids where the candlelight drowned. “The other hunters they said ”madness“. They did not understand. I was not mad. I was invited. I saw the guest list for the feast. My name was written in the geometry of a scream.” He began to laugh, a sound like dry leaves skittering down a street at midnight. “I was a good guest. I brought a gift. I brought myself.”

My mind strained to find a thread of logic in the chaos. “How how did you survive?” I begged, desperate for an anchor.

“Survive?” He seemed genuinely puzzled by the word. “The music does not stop. You just learn to dance to it. Or you break. I chose to dance. I knelt. The Baron he likes the dance. He gave me a new shape. A quieter shape.” For a fleeting second, there was a horrifying clarity in his eyes—the ghost of the powerful man he had been, now utterly enslaved. Then it was gone, swallowed by the void.

He leaned in. I smelled ozone and old blood. “The lesson, little question: hunting breaks the world. Every truth you pull loose is a thread in the tapestry. Pull too many, and the picture becomes something else. Something hungrier.”

He flowed back onto the sill, a rewind of impossible physics. The madness in his eyes receded, leaving behind a terrifying, perfect lucidity. He looked at me, and for the first time, spoke with utter clarity.

“After all,” he said, his voice now chillingly normal, “I am his most beloved child.”

Then he was simply not there. The window was closed, latched from the inside. Carved into the stone sill where he had perched was a perfect, smoking fractal that made my eyes water to see.

The Third Night: Michel on the Weight of Witness

Michel did not arrive. He manifested. One moment the chamber was empty, the next he stood at its heart, as still as a gargoyle. The Inquisitor. The Valet. The man who had slain three of the Baron's get and been rewarded with eternity.

He wore servant's livery, but it fit him like a shroud. His eyes, however, were not a servant's eyes. They were the cold, assessing eyes of a master hunter. He looked at me and saw a tool, a potential asset.

“You have seen the carrot and the stick,” he said, his voice low, a conspirator's whisper that did not carry beyond the two of us. “The seduction and the madness. Now you see the truth. He believes he cannot abide a hunter. It is his one vanity. But he is wrong. He needs us. He needs the challenge. The game.”

He took a step closer, and the air grew tight.

“I was the best of the Chain-Lanterns. Thirty years, I hunted his kind. I thought I was closing in on him. I thought I was winning.” A bitter smile touched his lips, the ghost of the man he was. “He let me think it. He let me believe I could be the hero of the story, the slayer in the dark. I cornered his third child in a forgotten crypt. No witnesses. Just me, my silver, and the thing's screams.”

His hand went to his forearm, though it was covered by his sleeve. “I struck the blow. And in the silence that followed I felt it. The victory did not settle in me. The horror of it did. It poured into the empty space. Without another soul to hold the weight, to see it the deed became mine alone to carry. Forever.”

The conspiratorial tone was gone, replaced by a hollow dread. “He found me three days later. I wasn't hiding. I was counting. Counting stones, counting breaths, counting the ways the world had gone wrong. He did not kill me. He offered me a position. ”A guest must be polite,“ he said. ”You were never a guest, Michel. You were always a hunter. Now you can be neither.“”

Michel's steel eyes locked onto mine, and I saw the prison within them. “The lesson is not about hunting. It is about being hunted. The Baron is the land, the sky, the law here. To hunt him is to break the world you stand on. The only way to survive is to cease to be a hunter. To become a guest. And a guest is polite. A guest makes requests. He cannot refuse a polite request. It is the only law he still honors.”

He leaned forward, his voice dropping back to that urgent whisper. “You want his secret? The one he keeps in the lead-lined box? The confession of Sister Aris?” He glanced at the door, a gesture of pure instinct. “I have not told him you seek it. That is my last victory. Ask him for it. Politely. As a guest.”

He straightened up, becoming the valet once more. “The lesson, scholar: hunting is not solitary. You need witnesses. Not for glory. To remember what you did, and why, when terror begins telling the story in your voice.” He rolled up his sleeve. Scars, brands, and old seals crossed his forearm. Beneath them a gray thread, thin as spider silk, coiled around his wrist and pulsed softly.

“Without a witness, the horror does not die. It passes into whoever survives and calls itself necessary. Here,” he said, touching the thread, “he makes certain it passes to him.”

He turned to leave.

“Michel,” I called out, my mind reeling. “The price for asking. What is it?”

He paused at the threshold, his back to me. “The price is always the same. A piece of yourself. For him, it is a trophy. For you” He half-turned, and his profile was etched in the gray dawn light. “It is just the cost of doing business with the edge of the world.”

He faded into the dim light, his final words hanging in the air like a sentence.

“I should know. After all, I am his most beloved servant.”

The Confrontation

The Confrontation

I found the Baron in his study, seated not at his desk, but at a small table set for two, positioned directly before the portrait of the woman with the scratched nameplate. The silver chest sat at his feet, its low, metallic hum a counterpoint to the silence.

He did not look up from the loaf of dark bread he was carefully slicing. “Sit, Lerris. We have matters to discuss, and a guest should not be kept standing.” His tone was not a request. It was a statement of fact, as immutable as the stone of his keep.

A strange lassitude came over me. My indignation, my carefully rehearsed demands, they all seemed to drain away, leaving only a hollow compulsion. I found myself pulling out the chair and sitting before I had even consciously decided to do so. The action felt both entirely my own and completely outside my control.

He placed a piece of bread on a fine porcelain plate and pushed it toward me. Then he took a pinch of salt from a small silver dish and sprinkled it over it. The gesture was ancient, ritualistic. “Bread and salt. The oldest laws are the strongest. They remind us of our places. You are a guest in my house. I am your host. This makes things simple. It makes things safe. Eat.”

I ate. The bread was heavy, nutty. The salt was sharp on my tongue. He watched me swallow, his expression unreadable, and only then did he take a piece for himself.

“You wish to hunt,” he stated, his voice flat. It wasn't a question. “I can smell the ambition on you, the hunger for truths that should remain buried. It is a familiar scent. You will be a hunter, Lerris Fenwood. Of that, I have little doubt.”

He paused, his gaze drifting to the silver chest. “But you seek to hunt me. And things like me. This is not a matter of courage or skill. It is a matter of geometry. You are a fleeting variable attempting to solve an eternal equation. You will fail. It is my duty as your host—and as a fellow noble who understands the weight of ancient obligations—to warn you against this path. There are powers in this world so old that to oppose them is not bravery, but a form of profound discourtesy. One must learn to navigate them, not quell them.”

He finally looked at me, and for the first time, I saw something akin to pity in his eyes. “This is, I suppose, why the Witness chose you. The angel of revelation seeks a vessel, and it often chooses the indignant and the clever. You are the perfect patsy. You arrived on my doorstep armed for a kill, yet you said the word ”guest“. The laws of hospitality are the only chains I willingly wear. So I must abide you. And I must warn you.”

He gestured to Michel, who stood like a shadow in the corner. The Inquisitor stepped forward and placed the lead-lined box in my hands. It was cold, far colder than it should have been.

“You ask for a secret,” the Baron said. “I give it to you. The price is not yours to bear, but you will carry it nonetheless. That is the nature of secrets. They have weight. They accrue interest. And they are never, ever free.”

I clutched the box, the Baron's words chilling me more than its temperature. My earlier vow felt childish now. “I will find a way.”

He sighed, a long, weary exhalation. “Yes. I am sure you will try. Escort for the scholar, Michel. See that he reaches the Black River by nightfall.” He turned his gaze back to the portrait, his hand resting on the singing silver chest. As Michel led me away, I heard the Baron's final, quiet addition.

“And Michel?” “My lord?” “Do not kill him. I have, despite myself, grown fond of his indignation.”

The Baron did not watch us go. He sat beneath the ruined portrait, a king in his castle of bones, having done his duty by one more doomed soul and never asking whether duty was the name he gave his appetite.

The Ways Between

The Ways Between

We did not ride south through Viterra. Michel led me not to the road, but to a section of the mountain's bare rock face. He placed his palm against the stone, and where there had been nothing, a door manifested. It was old, dark wood, banded with iron that seemed to drink the thin morning light.

“The Ways Between,” Michel said, his voice devoid of inflection. “The Baron's gift. Or his insult. You will be across the Belworth by nightfall. The journey will take you one night. It will take something else from you as well. That is the toll.”

He opened the door. Beyond was not a tunnel, nor was it darkness. It was a shifting, impossible geometry of light and shadow that hurt to perceive. I felt a pull, a terrible, vertiginous suction.

I stepped through.

I will not describe what I saw. The description would be a lie, and the lie would become a crack, and the crack would widen. It was not a journey of distance, but of essence. I felt myself unraveling, my memories, my senses, my very self, stretched across a non-space that had no regard for the mortal mind.

When I emerged, I collapsed onto cold, wet earth. The sound of rushing water filled my ears. I was on the far bank of the Black River, the sun bleeding into the horizon behind me. A single night had passed. A thousand years had passed.

I was clutching the lead-lined box so tightly my fingers had cramped into claws. I was whole. I was intact.

But I was hollowed.

A cold panic seized me. I tried to picture my mother's face, to hear her voice chiding me for my recklessness. Her features were clear, her smile kind, her voice a familiar melody.

But her eyes her eyes were voids. I could not remember their color. The memory was simply gone, scraped clean from my mind, a precise and terrible theft. A sob wrenched itself from my chest, a raw, helpless sound. I had to get across the river. I had to get home. I had to see her, to look into her face and find the color there again, before I forgot the rest.

I did not open the box until I was safely on the southern shore, the lights of a familiar crossroads village twinkling in the distance. The confession inside was not my burden.

But I would carry it anyway.

The Bargain at the Broken Well

A confession recovered from the ruins of a Daughter's safe house, written on the back of a grain manifest. The ink is lampblack and tears. The paper is scorched.

Under the lamp, bread and salt. The soup was free. The price was her.

I am Sister Aris, Keeper of the Door, and I am writing this because I will not remember it in the morning. The Cord takes memories as interest. But I have hidden this page in a lead-lined box, and I have paid a Trow to forget where. When the Cord finds it—and it will—I will already be ash.

My Hollowed has no name. I call him little one. He was weak. His mother was dead. A dark, uncertain life lay ahead. I held the cord tight. I did not unwrap it. The Cord demanded a breath or a death; I gave them a death.

He has been my witness for forty years. He does not eat. He does not sleep. He does not age. His eyes are sewn open with silver wire, and he watches every soul I tend, every death I harvest, every thread I tie. I have told myself he is not my son. I have told myself he is a tool.

I have lied.

The Cord does not forbid love. It forbids attachment. Love is a debt; attachment is a loan with no interest. The High Mother weaves attachment into the pattern, but She does not permit us to keep the thread for ourselves.

I have kept the thread.

The Road to Zahk'Kozahn

I fled the Cord at the dark of the moon. I took only my Hollowed, a skin of water, and a silver coin that had been pressed into a dead man's eye.

The cursed city is not on any map. You find it by walking toward the place where the stars forget their names. I walked for nine nights. Through desert. Through dreams. On the ninth, the walls rose from the dust—black basalt, slick with condensation that smelled of honey and copper.

The gates were open. The gates are always open.

Inside, the Worn shuffled past me—Shamblers, Screamers, Stalkers, and one Crackler who asked me for a name. I gave her the name of a Sister who had wronged me. She took it and laughed. Her joints snapped like dry twigs. “She will dream of drowning tonight,” the Crackler said. “That is the interest.”

I did not ask for the principal.

The Chained Angel

The ziggurat rose at the city's heart. I climbed. The steps were worn smooth by feet that had stopped bleeding centuries ago. At the top, a vaulted chamber lit by a single guttering candle.

And there She was.

Malachai hangs in chains of Aetherium—black iron that drinks light. Her wings are bound to the stone. Her mouth is sewn shut with silver wire. Her eyes are open. They are not angry. They are patient.

She spoke through a wound in Her throat, her voice the rasp of rust and honey.

“You have brought a Hollowed,” she sighed. “And you wish to restore his heart.”

I knelt. “I wish to raise him as my son. Not as a witness. Not as a tool. As a child.”

Malachai's eyes flickered. The chains groaned.

“He was dead. He was killed with kindness long ago, letting the breath fade. The Cord accepted that death as payment for another. He is not yours. He is the Cord's.”

“Then I will pay the Cord's price. Whatever it is.”

“You do not understand, little sister. The Cord does not sell. It transfers. You cannot buy a Hollowed's heart from the High Mother. She does not own it. She merely holds it.”

I did not understand. I still do not.

Malachai smiled, ash falling from her lips. Her chains rattled, the sound of bells and damnation.

“I can restore their heart. I can make their lungs fill with air, their blood move with warmth, their eyes close at night. They will grow. They will laugh. They will weep. They will be everything you lost when you held that cord.”

“And the price?”

“A plague upon the Cord in three generations from this night. Not death—death is mercy. A plague. That's all you need know. The High Mother will forget a thread. A knot will loosen. A debt will be lost. The Cord will fray, just a little, just enough to let one soul slip free.”

I thought of my Hollowed. I thought of his silver-wire eyes, his hands that never warmed, his heart that did not beat.

“One soul,” I said. “You will let one soul slip free from the Cord?”

“One soul,” Malachai agreed. “Not yours. Not theirs. But one.”

I agreed.

The Restoration

Malachai reached through Her chains. Her fingers were long, cold, and smelled of the Well of Ichor. She touched my Hollowed's chest.

The gray thread that bound the child's heart unraveled. It did not fall—it dissolved, turning to ash that smelled of milk and iron. The ash swirled, then settled into the shape of a heart—small, pink, pulsing.

The Hollowed gasped.

Her eyes closed. When they opened, the silver wire was gone. Her eyes were brown—warm, bewildered, alive. And yet – the way she tilted her head, the small furrow of her brow – that was his. That was always his. And I loved her for it,

She looked at me. “Mama?” she said.

I wept. I had not wept in forty years. The Cord does not permit tears—tears are a debt unpaid. But I wept.

Malachai watched. Her smile was the smile of a creditor who has just written a new clause in fine print.

“The plague will come in three generations. You will not see it. But you will have raised a daughter. Is that not enough?”

I held my child. She was warm. She was real. I did not ask what the fine print said.

The Cord

We fled Zahk'Kozahn at dawn. My daughter—I named her Bryn, after my own mother, who had no name I ever spoke aloud—walked beside me. Her hand was small and warm.

We reached the Black River by nightfall. I built a fire. I poured two cups of tea.

As she drank, I saw it.

A gray thread, thin as a spider's silk, wrapped around her neck. It was not tight. It was loose—the way a Daughter ties her witness cord before a binding. The thread trailed behind her, disappearing into the darkness.

I reached for it. My fingers passed through.

“Mama?” she said. “What's wrong?”

I looked at her. Her eyes were warm. Her heart beat. She was alive.

But she was not free.

The Cord had not released her. Malachai had restored her heart, yes—but the Cord had never owned her heart. The Cord owned the debt that her death had paid. And that debt had not been erased. It had been transferred—from the dead twin to the living child.

Bryn is a Daughter of the Cord. She always has been. She always will be.

I did not flee the Cord. I delivered my own Daughter into its keeping. My own little sister.

The Aftermath

I have raised her. She does not remember Zahk'Kozahn. She does not remember the Well of Ichor. She does not remember the day her heart began to beat.

Safety and Consent

Horror is as much about trust as fear. Use tools that suit your table; this book assumes some combination of lines-and-veils, X-card, open door, and debrief.

Session Zero Safety Pass

Before characters exist, the table should:

  • List lines (no-go content), veils (fade-to-black), and preferences (desired or avoided motifs).
  • Choose a quick signal for pause and rewind (e.g., “cut,” “hold,” or a hand sign).
  • Decide how to check in mid-session (traffic light, stars and wishes, or one-word mood check).
  • Name anchors for each PC (a person, a place, and an object that stabilize them).

On-Table Procedures

During play:

  • The GM front-loads content warnings before potentially graphic scenes.
  • Anyone may call for a tone shift or content veil without explanation.
  • After heavy scenes, run a breather: water, deep breaths, small talk—then confirm consent to continue.
  • Close with a short debrief: what worked, what to avoid next time, what to emphasize.

Hard Stops

If someone says stop, you stop. Take a break, close the session, or pivot scenes. Content is never worth breaking trust.

Core Mechanical Framework

Dread Timer - Sanity as Resource Management

The signature system replaces traditional sanity points with a Boon-based approach where players must spend resources to prevent psychological deterioration.

Purpose and Integration

This system connects to the existing Boon economy while adding horror-specific tension through the multi-timer system. Players choose when to spend Boons to prevent Dread advancement, creating meaningful decisions about risk versus stability.

Sample Uses:

  1. Witnessing a Mist Wraith causes +1 segment advancement - player spends 1 Boon to prevent it
  2. Learning cosmic truth about reality triggers +3 segments - player must spend 3 Boons or face severe psychological effects
  3. Entity observation advances Dread by 2 segments - party must collectively decide how to manage limited Boon resources

Holding Fast: Spending Boons against Dread

Acquisition Methods:

  • Failing rolls (standard Boon economy)
  • Bond-driven actions with intricate descriptions
  • GM discretion for thematic play
  • Session participation and milestone achievements

Spending Options:

  • Preventing Dread Timer advancement
  • Improving your Position by one step before a roll
  • Activating protective assets and items
  • Converting to XP (max 2 per session)

Narrative Weight: Players must choose between immediate tactical advantages and long-term psychological stability.

Risk/Reward Balance

Dominant — playing it safe:

  • Surface-level investigation with minimal horror exposure
  • Conservative approach: fewer Boons earned, because fewer rolls are missed
  • Avoiding direct confrontation with unknown threats

Desperate — pressing anyway:

  • Deep investigation with significant horror exposure
  • Direct engagement with incomprehensible entities
  • Pursuing forbidden knowledge at personal cost
  • +1-3 Dread segments, potential breaking points

Failure States:

  • Dread Timer fills completely
  • Character becomes Broken - out of control, hostile to allies
  • Permanent psychological damage requiring intervention
  • Transformation into agent of horror

The Worn: Debtors in Flesh

Nature of the Worn

When a bearer of Malachai s curse succumbs completely—when the hunger devours the last scrap of will, when the addiction burns out the soul—they do not simply die. Death would be mercy. Instead, they are drawn to the cursed city of Zahk Kozahn, where they join the ranks of the Worn.

The Worn are not undead in the traditional sense. Their hearts still beat, though slowly. Their lungs still draw breath, though shallowly. They are the hollowed shells of the cursed, worn thin by their own appetites. Peeling skin, eyes like cracked glass, limbs that move too fast or too slow. They are desperate, always desperate, because the hunger never ends. It only changes shape.

“The Worn are not mindless. They remember fragments of who they were. They can be negotiated with, manipulated, even pitied. But they cannot be saved—not by mortal means. The only release is the Well of Ichor, or final death.”

Running the Worn in Horror Campaigns

Atmosphere and First Encounters

The Worn should not be introduced as mere combat obstacles. Their horror lies in their familiarity. A Shambler might wear the tattered uniform of a soldier the PCs once fought beside. A Screamer might shriek in the voice of a lost loved one. A Stalker might leave small tokens—a child s toy, a wedding ring—outside the PCs camp.

First Encounter Example: The party finds a Crackler sitting by a dead fire, alone. It does not attack. It says, “I remember you. You were at the siege of Blackmoor. You left someone behind.” It then offers to trade a secret for a cup of water. What does it know? What will it cost?

Integrating Worn with Timers

The Worn are natural drivers for the Horror Campaigns timer system:

  • Dread Timer: Each new class of Worn encountered advances Dread by 1–3 (as noted in their entries). Recognizing a Worn as someone you once knew forces an immediate Breaking Point test.
  • Reality Fracture Timer: Worn presence indicates a weak spot between Zahk Kozahn and the real world. Each Worn encounter advances Reality Fracture by 1 segment.
  • Collective Sanity Timer: If the party debates whether a Worn is truly sentient or just a monster, the GM may advance Collective Sanity by 1—shared doubt erodes reality.

Using Worn as Antagonists and Allies

Not every Worn wants to kill. Some want to forget. Some want to be released. Some want to drag others down with them.

  • Shamblers are environmental hazards. Use them to block exits, create obstacles, or wear down resources.
  • Screamers are alarms and ambushers. Their shriek can alert other Worn or collapse unstable architecture.
  • Stalkers are recurring threats. A Stalker can follow the party across an entire campaign arc, never striking until the worst moment.
  • Cracklers are wildcards. They can be allies, informants, or betrayers. Their bargains are always tempting and always costly.

Sample Adventure: The Ninth Bell

Hook: The isolated watchtower of Thornhaven has stopped reporting. The last messenger returned with no memory of the journey, only a gray thread tied around his wrist and a single word: “Crackling.”

The party is sent to investigate. The road is wrong. Landmarks shift. By the time they reach the tower, the sky is the wrong color.

Location: The tower is intact, but the interior has been rearranged. Rooms repeat. Stairs lead to ceilings. The garrison has become Worn—Shamblers on the lower levels, a Screamer in the bell tower, and a Crackler in the captain s quarters who claims to remember the party from a war that hasn t happened yet.

Timers:

  • Reality Fracture [10] – each impossible room advances by 1.
  • Collective Sanity [8] – each time a PC argues about what is real, advance by 1.
  • Dread (10) – individual tracking as normal.

Resolution: The tower is a “thin place” where Zahk Kozahn bleeds through. The party can:

  1. Seal the thin place with a ritual (requires knowledge from a Crackler s bargain).
  2. Destroy the tower (collapses the connection but kills any remaining human survivors).
  3. Walk into the bleeding-through and confront a fragment of Malachai directly (suicidal, but possible at high tiers).

Customizing the Worn for Other Patrons

While the Worn are thematically tied to Malachai, their mechanics can be adapted to other horror patrons:

  • For Morag (Hidden Costs): The Worn become “Unspooled”—debtors who have lost everything. Cracklers offer to trade a forgotten debt for a memory. Stalkers follow those who broke a promise.
  • For Khemesh (Abyssal Maw): The Worn are “Drowned”—salt-crusted, waterlogged creatures that drag victims into deep water. Shriekers become “Pressure Wails” that cause ears to bleed.
  • For the Clockwork Monad (Apocalyptic Efficiency): The Worn are “Iterative Failures”—twisted, half-machine creatures that constantly try to optimize themselves into further deformity.

GM Guidance: The Worn as a Campaign Spine

The Worn are most effective when they are not the main threat, but a symptom of a larger sickness. A single Crackler can foreshadow a reality fracture. A pack of Stalkers can drive the party toward an ancient vault they would otherwise avoid. A Screamer s death cry can announce the arrival of something worse.

Use the Worn to answer the central question of horror in Fate s Edge: What are you willing to lose to survive?

The thread tightens. The bell cracks. The ninth hour approaches. You are not hunting the Worn. They are waiting for you to join them.

Fae &amp; Hag Folk Horror: The Alder King vs. The Hazel Queen

The Old Gods of Hedge and Hollow

Long before the Chained Angel rattled her chains, before the Daughters tied their first gray thread, there were the old ones—the lords and ladies of the wild places. They do not dwell in Zahk'Kozahn or in the geometry of madness. They live in the root cellar, the foggy meadow, the copse of alders where the light never quite reaches.

Two stand above the rest: The Alder King and The Hazel Queen.

They are not enemies. They are not allies. They are the two halves of a single, terrible coin: the dark, predatory wood and the cunning, bargain-making hedge. Their war is not fought with armies but with curses, with stolen children, with paths that lead nowhere and doors that open onto yesterday.

Between them walk two others: The Huntsman, who serves neither but hunts both, and The Pathweaver, who mends what the war breaks—and sometimes breaks it further.

“The Alder King takes. The Hazel Queen bargains. The Huntsman follows. The Pathweaver forgets. In the old woods, you are never the hunter. You are only prey that has not yet been named.”

The Alder King — The Root That Strangles

Lore

The Alder King is the spirit of the dark, wet wood—the alder grove where the ground is always damp, where the roots reach up to trip the unwary, where the trees grow close enough to whisper. He is patient, territorial, and cruel in the way of a snare: he does not hate you, but he will not let you go.

His domain is the Thornwood, a shifting forest that appears where alders grow in circles. Those who enter find that the way out requires a sacrifice: a memory, a name, or the warmth of a living body to feed the roots.

Epithets: The Root-Father, The Knot-Weaver, The Lord of the Sunless Copse. Signs: Alder leaves floating in still water. A footprint filled with dark sap. A child's laughter from underground.

Mechanics: The Thornwood Timer [10]

When the party enters Alder King territory, start the Thornwood Timer. It represents how deeply the forest has claimed them.

9–10The Alder King manifests. He does not attack—he offers a bargain. Refusal requires a Spirit+Endurance test (DV 5) or the party becomes Lost (unable to find any exit for 1d6 hours).

Advancement Triggers:

  • Stepping on an alder root: +1 segment
  • Taking a rest within the Thornwood: +1 segment
  • Harming a creature of the wood (even in self-defense): +2 segments
  • Accepting a gift from the Alder King: +3 segments

The Hazel Queen — The Cunning Hedge

Lore

Where the Alder King is dark and patient, the Hazel Queen is bright and quick. She dwells in hedgerows, at crossroads, in the spaces between gardens and wild. She is the hag who offers exactly what you need—for a price you will not understand until the hazel nuts have fallen thrice.

Her domain is the Hedge, a living labyrinth of thorn and hazel that separates the civilized from the wild. Unlike the Thornwood, the Hedge does not trap you. It offers you a choice: turn back, or pay the toll.

Epithets: The Crossroads Wife, The Kettle-Crone, She Who Counts in Nuts. Signs: A single hazel nut on a windowsill. A kettle that whistles without fire. A path that forks into three, then four, then nine.

Mechanics: The Hedge Debt Timer [8]

The Hazel Queen does not imprison. She lends. Each time the party accepts her aid, they incur debt. The Hedge Debt Timer tracks how much they owe.

The Huntsman — The Leash Made Flesh

Lore

The Huntsman serves neither king nor queen. He serves the hunt itself. He was once a mortal—a ranger, a trapper, a man who loved the forest too much. Now he is a creature of pure purpose: to track, to chase, and to bring down whatever the old powers set him upon.

He appears as a tall figure in weathered leather, his face hidden by a hood of stitched leaves. His hounds are not dogs but the ghosts of wolves, the memories of teeth. He does not speak. He leaves notes carved into bark: a name, a direction, a single word: Run.

Epithets: The Silent Tracker, The Warden of the Chase, The Debt Collector of the Wild. Signs: A single arrow embedded in a doorframe. A howl that comes from every direction. A set of tracks that end at a cliff—and resume on the other side.

The Huntsman's Hunt

The Huntsman can bleed and perhaps be killed, but defeating him in a clearing does not end the chase. The Hunt Timer measures how near his hounds have come and how much of the country has learned to betray the fugitives.

  • The Hunt Timer [10]: Starts when the party refuses a bargain, desecrates a grove, or kills a creature of the Alder King or Hazel Queen without permission.
  • Ticks: The Hunt advances 1 segment per day of travel, plus additional ticks for leaving traces, sleeping in the open, or using magic that disturbs the wild.
  • When Full: The Huntsman catches you. He offers no bargain, only a choice: one member of the party stays behind as a sacrifice, or the entire party faces his hounds in a fight that begins at Desperate Position.

Evading the Hunt:

  • Crossing running water resets the Hunt Timer to 1 (the Huntsman cannot follow, but he will wait on the other side).
  • Entering a Hazel Queen shrine or Alder King grove pauses the Hunt—but you must bargain for safe passage.
  • Finding the Huntsman's true name (lost somewhere in the Thornwood) ends the Hunt permanently. The party gains a permanent +1 die to all rolls against fae creatures as the Huntsman's respect buys them a measure of protection.

The Pathweaver — The Mender of Liminal Wounds

Lore

The Pathweaver walks where the Alder King and Hazel Queen have torn the world. She does not fight them. She does not serve them. She weaves. When a path leads nowhere, she reminds it where it should go. When a door opens onto yesterday, she pulls it back to today.

She appears as an old woman with a spindle of gray thread, her fingers moving in patterns that hurt to watch. Her eyes are mismatched: one sees the present, the other sees what could have been.

Epithets: The Gray Spinner, The Door-Mender, The Third Sister. Signs: A single gray thread tied around a door handle. A path that suddenly makes sense after hours of confusion. A dream of a woman weaving with your memory.

The Pathweaver's Boons

The Pathweaver accepts no worship and belongs to neither court. Lost, cursed, or cornered travelers may ask for her aid. She decides whether the road ought to be mended, and she has little patience for those who mistake need for entitlement.

Invoking the Pathweaver:

  • At any crossroads, a PC may spend 1 Boon and roll Spirit + Lore (DV 3). On a success, the Pathweaver appears and answers one question about the local geography or the location of a threshold.
  • If the roll fails, the Pathweaver appears but is annoyed. She answers the question, but the PC loses a minor memory (GM choice) and marks 1 Fatigue.

Pathweaver's Rites (for Runekeepers or Cantors)

  • Unknot the Way (Low, 5 XP): Untie a single knot—a closed door, a sealed promise, a locked heart. The target must be mundane (not magical). Push: Also silence any sound the opening makes.
  • Weave the Lost Path (Standard, 9 XP): Reveal a hidden route between two locations within one day's travel. The route is safe from mundane pursuit, but the Pathweaver will ask for a small favor in return (GM determines).
  • Mend the Threshold (High, 14 XP): Permanently repair a broken threshold—a door that leads nowhere, a bridge that collapses in spirit if not in stone. The repair costs a memory (the caster forgets why they came to the threshold in the first place).

The War of Root and Hedge

The Alder King and Hazel Queen have been warring for centuries—not openly, but through proxies, through curses, through the slow corruption of each other's domains. The party may be caught in the middle.

Sample Conflict Seeds

  1. The Stolen Child: A village child was taken by the Alder King's roots. The Hazel Queen offers to return the child—if the party brings her a branch from the King's oldest tree. The King, in turn, offers to let the child go willingly—if the party burns a Hazel Queen shrine.
  2. The Rival Huntsmen: Two Huntsmen—one in the King's service, one in the Queen's—are hunting the same prey: the party. The party must pit them against each other or find the Pathweaver to escape both.
  3. The Broken Path: A road that once connected two towns now leads in circles. The Pathweaver says the road was caught in a dispute between King and Queen. To mend it, the party must retrieve a token from each: a thorn from the Thornwood and a nut from the Hedge.
  4. The Ninth Threshold: The Pathweaver reveals that the war has torn a hole in reality—a ninth threshold that leads not to another place, but to a hungry void. The party must negotiate a truce between King and Queen long enough to seal it.

Campaign Timers for Fae/Hag Horror

  • Thornwood Timer (10): How deeply the party is trapped in Alder King territory.
  • Hedge Debt Timer (8): How much the party owes the Hazel Queen.
  • The Hunt Timer (12): How close the Huntsman is to catching the party.
  • Threshold Fracture (14): How close the war is to breaking reality. Advances when the party takes sides, breaks a bargain, or kills a fae creature. When full, the ninth threshold opens and something from outside the old war enters—something even the King and Queen fear.

Sample Adventure: The Hazel Nut and the Alder Thorn

Hook: A village on the edge of the Thornwood has stopped paying taxes. The reason: every night, a single hazel nut appears on the mayor's pillow. The next morning, a child is missing. The Hazel Queen is collecting.

Investigation: The party discovers that the mayor made a bargain with the Queen years ago: prosperity for the village in exchange for “the first harvest of each year.” The Queen has interpreted “harvest” to mean children. The Alder King, sensing weakness, has begun encroaching on the village's edges.

Key NPCs:

  • Mayor Halver: Desperate, guilty, unwilling to admit the bargain.
  • The Village Wise Woman: A hedge witch who serves neither King nor Queen but knows the Pathweaver's signs.
  • The Taken Children: They are not dead, but kept in the Hedge, waiting to be “harvested” on their seventh birthdays.

Timers:

  • Hedge Debt (already at 5): The village owes the Queen deeply.
  • Thornwood (3 and rising): The King's roots are reaching for the village well.
  • Threshold Fracture (2): The war is still cold, but heating up.

Resolutions:

  1. Bargain with the Queen: Pay the debt with something other than children—a memory, a skill, a future. The Queen accepts, but the King is now angry.
  2. Bargain with the King: Offer the King a foothold in the village (a well, a crossroads) in exchange for driving off the Queen. The village is safe, but now the King's roots are under every doorstep.
  3. Find the Pathweaver: Discover a third path—a way to nullify the original bargain without payment. Requires finding the original contract (hidden in the Hedge) and burning it at a crossroads. The Pathweaver agrees to open the way, but demands that the village build a shrine to her—a place where neither King nor Queen may enter.

GM Guidance: Folk Horror Pacing

Folk horror is slow, atmospheric, and deeply tied to place. Unlike cosmic horror (which overwhelms with scale), folk horror isolates with intimacy. Use these principles:

  • Kill the road: The party cannot simply leave. The road turns, the bridge is out, the map is wrong. Isolation is the first horror.
  • Make the land sentient: The forest watches. The well whispers. The hedge has thorns that reach for blood. Do not describe these as magical—describe them as natural in a world that has different rules.
  • Rituals have teeth: A simple hedge witch's blessing should feel like a contract. A prayer at a crossroads should leave a mark. Every interaction with the old powers has a cost, even if it is only a forgotten name.
  • Adults fail the young: Folk horror often turns on children taken, bargained away, or replaced while a community looks aside. Use this sparingly and with safety tools. The horror lies not in treating children as tokens, but in the adults who decide whose child may be sacrificed for custom, peace, or prosperity.
The hazel nuts fall. The alder roots rise. The Huntsman's hounds are at the door. Somewhere, a spindle turns. The Pathweaver is weaving your fate—and she is not sure where the thread leads.
