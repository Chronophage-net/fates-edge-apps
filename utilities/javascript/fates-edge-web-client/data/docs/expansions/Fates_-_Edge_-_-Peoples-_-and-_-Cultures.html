What This Covers

This expansion adds playable peoples beyond the human default: the Aeler (dwarven masters of infrastructure and law), the Lethai (elves split by an ancient curse into body-gifted and mind-gifted lines, with root-law forest cultures and their oathbound and urban kin), and the Threshold Folk — small, liminal cultures such as the hearth-bound Aelaerem, the counting Aelinnel and their Mazereth and Archive Keeper cousins, and the shadow-walking Umbral Kin. A closing chapter covers the rarer, mythic Beastfolk, Dragonfolk, and Others.

This is Volume I of three. Volume II — Horse, Salt, and Stone covers the peoples of the steppe, the northern seas, the mist and the deep roads. Volume III — Coin, Court, and Cargo covers the city-states, the trade courts and the southern kingdoms. Each volume stands alone; the rules for building a character from any of them are identical and are set out here.

Every entry keeps its full crunch — Affinities, starting and key Talents, Strings, sample characters, and GM-facing timers and levers — so you can drop a culture into a game session directly from this page. Use the Table of Contents to jump to any people.

The Aeler

The Aeler are masters of infrastructure, economics, and subtle influence. Their power lies in making the world work — and in making everyone else quietly dependent on that work.

Affinity & Starting Talent

Affinity: Stone & Breath / Deep Accounting
  • Stone & Breath: +1 die to Craft/Lore rolls for infrastructure work; +1 Position underground or in urban systems when navigating them.
  • Deep Accounting: Spend 1 Boon to audit a situation — ask one question about hidden resources, costs, or dependencies.
Vent Prior's Training 3 XP · Minor

Requires Craft 1+, Wits 2+. +1 die to rolls involving air quality, ventilation, or structural integrity. You know the Nine Measures. Once per scene, read a structure like a ledger for +1 die to understand its construction or weaknesses.

The Lethai

The Lethai are divided by an ancient curse that prevents any one of them from fully accessing both physical and mental gifts. The split created distinct cultures, each excelling in a different aspect of existence while yearning for wholeness. They master living law, environmental stewardship, and knowledge that only makes sense in context.

Affinity (All Lethai) — Choose One at Creation
  • Gift of the Body (Lethai-al/-ar): +1 die to Body-based skills; once/scene, spend 1 Boon to push a physical ability beyond normal limits for one action.
  • Gift of the Mind (Lethai-thora): +1 die to Wits/Spirit-based skills; once/scene, spend 1 Boon to recall or deduce information beyond immediate knowledge.

The Curse of Division: you cannot take talents that would enhance both physical and mental capabilities at once — if a talent would grant both, choose one benefit.

Lethai-al — Root, River, and the Roof-Tree

Woodwise lawkeepers, merchants of light and shade, stewards of living borders.

Ground & Memory

Lethai-al dwell where roof-trees braid the sky and rivers think aloud. Their memory is arboreal: rings and seasons, storms and healings, disputes settled in coppice and replanting. An oath among them is root-law — it binds living things (people, paths, waters) and is paid in years. Outsiders see quiet; neighbors know them as relentless auditors of footprint and flow.

Forest Commons (Places That Hold)

  • Valewood: tall-canopy forest laced with rain gardens and stone-steps; trails shift with season. Waystones carry moss-tallies — green for open, gray for heal, black for trespass.
  • Roof-Tree Circles: villages built around elders and one monumental tree per circle, scaffolded with walkways and rain-catchers; sap is consecrated for sealing or dismissing pacts.
  • River Courts: significant streams keep court on a shingle bank with a willow-bench and a book of pebbles; trade, ferry rights, and flood work are judged where the water can overhear.
  • Edge-Wards: where wood meets field or steppe, boundary groves are treated as treaties — thorn, ash, and fruit interplanted so the sharp feeds the soft. This hedge law stops hooves while nourishing neighbors.

Courtesy & Everyday Law

Shade Etiquette is observed to earn hospitality and a hearing; ignoring it turns the forest against your plans.

  • Iron Covered: wrap bare iron in leather or cloth — covered iron grants Position +1 in parley or requests to pass.
  • Name Once: speak your name and intent at the edge; never name the forest as property.
  • Step on Stone: use set stones or boardwalks — every crushed sprout is a Light-Due.
  • Water First: pour the first cup to the river or cistern before drinking.
  • Leave the Light: replace shade taken — plant, heal, or pay a seed-tithe.

Timers for breaches: Under-Root Grudge [4] (tariffs, closed ferries), Stream-Clouding [4] (mill shutdowns, fish wardens).

Courts & Factions

  • Wardens of the Roof-Tree: rangers and surveyors measuring canopy health, poacher pressure, and firebreaks; carry leaf-badges and thorn-writs.
  • Merchant Foresters (Syndics of Light): manage sustainable fellings, resin trade, boat-timber quotas, and lantern-wood auctions; argue that trade funds stewardship.
  • Songkeepers: priests of repair rites and remembrance festivals; arbitrate forgiveness by counting regrowth, not coin.
  • Ferrymen of the Rain: river pilots and dam-tenders whose votes swing courts in flood years.

Tension lines: quota vs. canopy health; export timbers vs. local craft; river straightening for mills vs. flood meadows for salmon.

Borders & Neighbors

  • Silence Furlong (with the Ykrul): a speechless border strip — no grazing, felling, or naming. Cross in silence, speak once. Honored, it keeps the peace warm; broken twice in a season, it brings gray-fletched messengers at dusk. Joint patrols heal hedges and lift stones together; concessions are marked as seasonal Strings.
  • Ridge Courtesy (with the Aeler): root-law and stone-law trade proofs — the Aeler build keystone stairs where the Lethai-al choose the line, and the Lethai-al plant edge-wards that keep the roads alive. Disputes end with bread in oven courts or sap at the roof-trees.
  • Moots at the Mouth (with the Linns): ship-timber quotas bind with replanting moots and harbor dues; storm seasons bite, and both sides sing the river clear.

Economy of Light (Strings & Ledgers)

Light and shade are currencies — every fell, ferry crossing, or fire carries a light-due: a balance repaid in replanting, canal-clearing, or trade at kinder rates.

At the Table: Light-Due System

Cash a light-due to gain DV −1 on operations framed as repair, replanting, or flood work. Abuse it, and start Under-Root Grudge [4].

Quotas as Timers: Boat-Timber Quota [6], Lanternwood Allotment [4] — fill the timer to unlock export; overfilling triggers a Canopy Censure.

Threats & Remedies

  • Bark Blight: fungal rot spread by careless carts and damp blades — remedy: ash-wash stations, tool quarantine, fire-lines timed by the moon.
  • Poacher Pressure: syndicates take ripe resin and seedwood without paying dues — remedy: undercover ferries, tariff traps at market, public shaming rites.
  • River Clouding: silt from mills and bad embankments chokes fish beds — remedy: settle-ponds, scheduled sluices, mill-weirs redesigned with Aeler math.
  • Old Fire: lightning scars waking in drought years — remedy: fuel mosaics, controlled burns, ember patrols.

Example front chain: Bark Blight [6] → Canopy Hunger [4] → Famine Tariff [4].

Tools & Tokens (at the Table)

  • Leaf-Badge (Warden): once/scene, upgrade Position by +1 when enforcing forest law or conducting a rescue.
  • Seed-Tithe Seal: convert a sanction into a Repair Project [4] instead of a fine or exile.
  • Ferry-Knot: skip the first River Hazard consequence on a leg or chase; using it costs a light-due.
  • Moss-Tally: reveal one hidden quota timer in a market or lumberyard scene.

Aesthetics & Speech

  • Materials: bark-laminate armor, willow-bone frames, resin-glass panes, river-iron sheathed in leather.
  • Colors: rain green, bark gray, amber, sky-through-leaf.
  • Proverbs: "Leave the forest no thinner than your shadow." "The river remembers who spoke over it, and what they promised." "Cut where it wants to fall, or do not cut at all." "If the hedge is hungry, the treaty is thin."
  • Names: given name plus grove and season or deed — e.g., Talan of Reedfall, Who Unknotted the Flood.

Integration Hooks

  • Political Intrigue: forest syndics lobby city councils for lanternwood allotments; bribes are dressed as "donations" to flood work.
  • Caravans: resin and seed routes require shade-credit; escorts must mind iron covers and the Silence Furlong.
  • Wilderness: use standard Hinterlands procedures with fuel mosaics and blight quarantine as special orders.
  • Urban Districts: dock courts host River Days; Lethai-al ferries grant canoe-lane priority to crews who heal ladders and steps.

Portraying the Lethai-al

Let policy speak before mystique: quotas, ferries, hedges, the cost of light. Make courtesy concrete — stones, coverings, cups — and consequences seasonal, not theatrical. Treat the forest as a party to treaty, not scenery. When in doubt, ask: what did this footstep cost, and who will pay it?

Language & Time (Context Is King)

Lethai speech is context-saturated — syntax legible to grandparents can baffle grandchildren unless framed by the right place, season, and kinship. Meaning travels with context keys: gesture, setting, shared story. A sentence lifted from its grove can die on the road.

At the Table: Context Keys

Place-name, season-mark, kinship-hand, roof-tree sign. Absent any two, assume misreadings. Reading or pleading in older registers increases DV by +1 unless you hold a Context String (gloss-tree, witness, song). Cashing a Context String reduces DV by −1 and creates a Shared Frame (Position +1 on the first exchange).

Peoples & Lineages

Lethai-al — The People of the Body

Woodwise and numerous in most great forests — the elves most humans meet, and most willing to integrate in mixed cities. Embodied and present: heightened senses, quick strength, an ease with weather and work. Most half-elves trace their lineage here.

Everyday: ferry crews, hedge-wardens, resin syndics, ward dancers.
Gifts (examples): Canopy Spring (Position +1 on leaps/climbs once/scene); Scent of Rain (clue on approaching weather or rot); Root-Balance (resist a shove or knockdown).
Strings: ferry knot; resin share; roof-tree blessing.

Lethai-ar — The Oathbound

Wood elves who took a vow to one of the three sisters of the Triad of Transformation — Inaea the Weaver, Isoka the Serpent Queen, or Ikasha She Who Sleeps. Rare in the upper Amaranthine and common along shaded trade arteries and cavernous underways. The oath is not a religion they practise; it is a change they accepted, and it shows. Their three branches are set out in full below, under The Three Oaths.

Everyday: understory judges, silk engineers, antidote keepers, night pilots.
Gifts (examples): Weaver's Reading (trace networks: DV −1 on uncovering plots or paths); Stillness (go unnoticed in dim light until you move to act); Venin Lore (convert Harm 1 from toxins to Fatigue once/scene).
Strings: spider-vow token; serpent oath; silk-tithe.
Courtesy: oath-signs are worn openly; breaking web-law costs Mask Rights for a season.

Lethai-thora — The People of the Mind

Urban circles found chiefly in Thepyrgos — scholars, civil engineers, translators of other peoples' law. Long memories, sharp minds, and (not without irony) many of the leading experts on human and Aeler culture. Their courts weigh arguments like bridges.

Everyday: archivists, canal designers, mint auditors, diplomatic tutors.
Gifts (examples): Memory Canticle (recall a text with line-accurate detail); Number Music (DV −1 on design, repair, or logistics projects framed aloud); Cold Reading (Position +1 on a first parley when you had time to observe).
Strings: gloss-tree charter; canal seal; ledger witness.

Sundered Elves — Itinerant Lethai-thora

Lethai-thora who reject the bench for the road, carrying portable gloss-trees and teaching context literacy in market towns. Neither fully grove nor fully court, they translate between the two.

Gifts (examples): Bridge-Tongue (treat cross-cultural etiquette DV as if in a shared frame, once/scene); Pocket Gloss (create a one-use Context String on the fly).

The Lethai-ar — The Three Oaths

Isoka, Ikasha and Inaea are sisters, and between them they hold the Triad of Transformation: the shed skin, the threshold, and the thread. The Lethai-ar are the wood elves who swore to one of them and were changed by it. Every other Lethai lineage is something you are born as. This is the one you are made into, and every Lethai-ar you meet chose it or had it chosen for them by a parent who did.

How to play any Lethai-ar. The oath is an Affinity and a Limit, like any heritage: +1 Position on the listed Attribute or Skill, −1 Position on the listed Attribute. It is never dice. Each branch also brings a characteristic String and a standing obligation that the GM may call in. A Lethai-ar who breaks the oath does not lose the Affinity — they keep it, and everyone can see they kept it, which is the whole problem.

The Web-Sworn — Inaea's Lethai-ar

The oldest and most numerous branch, and the one most peoples have actually dealt with: the silk syndics, the understory judges, the ones who hold the bridge-tolls along the shaded arteries. Inaea's teaching is that every bond is a thread and every thread can be pulled, so the Web-Sworn do not accumulate wealth. They accumulate obligations owed to them, and they are patient about calling them in.

  • Affinity: +1 Position on Insight, or on any roll to trace, map or exploit a network of people.
  • Limit: −1 Position on Body when acting without preparation.
  • Gifts: Weaver's Reading (DV −1 on uncovering plots or paths); Night Lanes (silk trip-lines and warning strings; the first ambush in dim light begins Dominant for defenders); Patient Tithe (name a favour owed; it becomes a String).
  • Strings: spider-vow token; silk-tithe; web-law charter.
  • Obligation: a debt owed to you may never simply be forgiven — it must be transferred, traded or discharged. Forgiving one costs a String.

The Coil-Sworn — Isoka's Lethai-ar

What other peoples call serpentfolk, and say so in a lowered voice. Isoka teaches that the self is a skin: temporary, and shed when it stops fitting. Her Lethai-ar take that literally enough that it shows on them — a slow change over decades, scale at the wrist and the throat, eyes that stop having a visible white, a jaw that unhinges further than it should. They are not cursed and they are not undead. They are people partway through a change they consented to, and the change does not finish.

Their seat is the Coil Court, which is not a place so much as a standing session: it convenes wherever three Coil-Sworn and a fire are, it hears cases about identity, and its rulings are about who someone is now rather than what they did then. A Coil Court will cheerfully acquit a murderer on the grounds that the murderer no longer exists, and will as cheerfully convict a man for something his shed self did, if it judges he is still wearing it.

  • Affinity: +1 Position on Endurance, or on any roll to resist, process or administer a toxin.
  • Limit: −1 Position on Presence with anyone who knew you before your last shedding.
  • Gifts: Venin Lore (convert Harm 1 from toxins to Fatigue, once per scene); Shed (once per session, discard one Complication tied to your past — and one String with it); Unhinged (swallow, or pass through, something that should not admit you).
  • Strings: serpent oath; the shed skin of a witness; Coil Court standing.
  • Obligation: you owe the Court a shedding. Refuse one for too long and the change stalls halfway, which is visible, painful, and the single thing every Coil-Sworn fears.

Playing the Coil-Sworn without playing a monster. The temptation is to run them as sinister, and it is the wrong read. A Coil-Sworn's whole ethic is that people are allowed to become someone else — they are the only people in the Amaranthine who will take a runaway serf, a broken oathkeeper or a disgraced noble entirely at their word about who they are now. That is genuinely merciful and genuinely dangerous, and both for the same reason. The cults of the Coiled Heart, who drink venom to force the change rather than earn it, are what happens when the ethic is taken by people in a hurry; treat them as a faction, not as the norm.

The Shade-Sworn — Ikasha's Lethai-ar

The shadow elves: the branch that swore to She Who Sleeps, and took on her habit of not acting. Ikasha is the hush between footfalls and the black-feathered watcher at the threshold, and her Lethai-ar keep her discipline — they gather what might happen and decline, at length, to make it happen. Generations of this have marked them. Shade-Sworn are grey-skinned to near-black, uncomfortable in full sun, and hold their voices at a pitch that is genuinely hard to recall afterwards.

They keep the deep underways and the raven-lofts, and they are the Amaranthine's best-informed people about everything, which is exactly why they are so little use to anyone. A Shade-Sworn will tell you what is coming. They will not tell you what to do about it, and they consider being asked a small rudeness.

  • Affinity: +1 Position on Stealth, or on any roll to go unremarked in a place you are entitled to be.
  • Limit: −1 Position on Presence in daylight, or in any room that has been told you are coming.
  • Gifts: Stillness (go unnoticed in dim light until you move to act); Unremembered (once per scene, someone recalls the meeting but not who was in it); Raven Post (send a short message anywhere a raven can reach, and it will be understood).
  • Strings: raven-token; threshold right; a secret held for someone.
  • Obligation: you may not be the proximate cause. Act directly on a matter you foresaw and Ikasha's attention lifts — you lose the Affinity until you have waited out something you could have prevented.

The three do not get along, and it is not a feud. Web, Coil and Shade disagree about the same question — what a person owes their own past — and each answer makes the other two look like a moral failure. Inaea's people say a debt outlives the debtor. Isoka's say the debtor can stop being the debtor. Ikasha's say wait and see which of you is still here. Put two branches in a room and you have a scene without needing a villain.

The Two Gifts & Mixed Blood

Lethai tradition holds that no one may bear both gifts at once. A person chooses, or is chosen by, Body (Lethai-al, Lethai-ar) or Mind (Lethai-thora). Training and rite reinforce the choice, and law and courtesy expect it.

  • Rule of the Two Gifts: a Lethai PC selects Body or Mind at creation and picks Gifts only from that list. Swapping lists requires a season-long project with social cost — losing one String tied to the former gift.
  • Half-Elf Exception: a quarter-heritage half-elf (one quarter Lethai-al or Lethai-ar; one quarter Lethai-thora; the remainder human or other) may take one Body Gift and one Mind Gift under the rare and controversial Bridge-Born Clause.
  • Most Half-Elves: descend from Lethai-al lines and default to Body Gifts unless the Bridge-Born Clause applies.

In play: the Two Gifts are narrative permissions. Body Gifts grant Position shifts and physical resistances; Mind Gifts lean toward DV reduction in planning, repair, or rhetoric. They add no new math — use the core ladders.

Hooks & Complications
  • The Gloss That Wouldn't Read: a treaty text from three generations back refuses to make sense — find the missing context keys, or watch the border go hot.
  • Web-Law, River-Law: Lethai-ar silk syndics claim a bridge-toll by Inae's charter; Lethai-thora auditors counter with canal clauses. Bowl, then Board, then Shade Etiquette.
  • Bridge-Born in Question: a half-elf prodigy manifests both gifts, and the circle moves to forbid the rite — protect or persuade before masks come down.
  • Mask Rights Forfeit: a serpent oath-bearer allegedly broke web-law in human courts — can a seed-tithe seal commute the sanction to a repair project?
  • The Sundered's Lesson: a Sundered gloss-carrier teaches a market to read Lethai context; someone powerful profits from confusion and wants them gone.

Rivals & Distance

With the Aeler — Stone-Law, Root-Law

Stone-law tallies weight and span; root-law tallies shade and years; each doubts the other's ledgers. Aeler keystone stairs bite deep; Lethai-al prefer lines that bend with slope and sap. Trade proofs favor bridges that don't drown salmon and hedges that don't choke roads. Keep councils separate when tempers rise — the Aeler call elves "Aelaef."

Treaties in Practice: Keystone-in-Bark accords — where a road enters a grove, the Aeler choose the tread and the Lethai choose the line. Breaking either creates fines paid as repair years.
Isolation Habits: pass-keeps ring bells to one side; roof-tree circles refuse iron even when wrapped. Delegations meet at split courts — half oven, half river bank — and adjourn before dusk.
Strings: keystone courtesy; shade-credit; river-weir writ; seed-tithe seal.
Timers: Ridge-Root Stalemate [4] (a project stalls until a mixed design is signed); Sluice vs. Salmon [4] (silt, tariffs, tempers).
Levers at the Table: if a scene honors both "iron-covered" and "step-on-stone," begin in a Shared Frame (Position +1 on the first exchange). If either side arrives with bare iron or cuts outside the plan, advance Ridge-Root Stalemate by +1.

With the Ykrul — Grass-Law, Shade-Law

Respect is edged into distance — the Silence Furlong keeps hooves from roots and axes from borders: cross wordless, name yourself once, keep to the stones. Honored, councils run warm; broken twice in a season, gray-fletched messengers arrive at dusk and rings draw tight.

Customs: Bowl, Board, and Shade — fairness, then geometry, then courtesy. A concession on the Ykrul board becomes a seasonal light-due at the forest edge.
Isolation Habits: markets meet on the margin (hedge fairs) rather than deep exchange; joint patrols heal hedges together but camp apart; songs are traded by echo, not chorus.
Strings: light-due receipt; ferry knot; windbreak right; border-song.
Timers: Furlong Breach [4] (speech, grazing, or felling in the strip); Banner at the Hedge [4] (meadow banners crowd the edge and tighten tempers).
Levers at the Table: when both sides keep the Furlong and the iron-covered rule, reduce DV by −1 on any parley for passage or pasture. On breach, increase DV by +1 and tick Furlong Breach.

War by Shade — Deterrence & Asymmetry

The Lethai-al and Lethai-ar avoid open-field banners. They make routes expensive, fragment columns, and end campaigns by ledger long before the first arrow. This war by shade is why most peoples leave the forests alone.

War by Shade Principles
  • Fight the route, not the regiment: break ferries, confuse lanes, erase way-marks, make supply walk twice.
  • One strike, five repairs: every cut obligates replanting or flood-work; the war ends when the repair ledger is signed.
  • Night, rain, understory: engage when wind and water cover sound; withdraw where canopy eats pursuit.
  • Courtesy as weapon: uncovered iron or a crushed sprout is grounds to raise tariffs, close ferries, or call wardens without drawing a blade.
Plays of the Shade
  • Hedge War: edge-wards are cut and reknit to channel intruders into dead-ground pockets watched by wardens (invader Move/Scout DV +1 while Hedge Mosaic stands).
  • River Denial: ferries vanish upstream, weirs open at dusk, mills idle to strip silt from footing pursuers need (start Supply Strangle [4] on any hostile force dependent on crossings).
  • Night Lanes: Lethai-ar web-law lays silk trip-lines and warning strings; Inae's oath-bearers strike once and vanish; Isoka's keepers use venin as medicine with teeth (the first ambush in dim light begins at Dominant for defenders; on a hit, intruders mark Fatigue instead of direct Harm unless they stand and escalate).
  • Canopy Runners: Lethai-al move above sight-lines and arrows fall where footfalls never were (once/scene, defenders convert pursuit into parley or retreat with Position +1).
Deterrence, in Practice
  • Fearsome Reputation: before a faction chooses a forest campaign, start "Leave Them Alone" [4]. On fill, leaders choose tariff, treaty, or route-around over invasion.
  • Intruder Exhaustion [6]: ticks for lost hours, wet powder, spoiled grain, wrong turns. On fill, morale folds without a decisive battle.
  • Arrow Ethics [4]: overseen by Songkeepers. If intruders withdraw and sign repairs, arrows go quiet; if they burn, mask rights come down and ambushes escalate.
Terrain Tags (at the Table)
  • Hedge Mosaic: invaders suffer DV +1 on Navigate/Scout; the first defender action gains Position +1.
  • Resin Smoke: on ignition, obscures vision and stings eyes; converts the first Ranged Harm 1 against defenders to Fatigue.
  • Stone Steps: if invaders step on stone and keep iron covered, begin in a Shared Frame (DV −1 to request safe passage). Breaches tick Under-Root Grudge.

Why most leave them alone: campaigns end in tariffs and repair years, not trophies to sell at court. Columns starve by inches while ledgers fatten with fines and ferry dues. Bad nights have names — Two Bridges Gone, The River That Walked — and generals remember.

Marks — Burden, Blessing, and Lost Context

Many Lethai artifacts carry inherited knowing. Leafblades etched with moss-tallies, resin-glass panels holding songs, riverstones scored by ferrymen — these do not merely remember; they obligate. Among the Lethai, especially the woodwise Lethai-al, past knowledge is a burden on the present. What you inherit, you must carry carefully or set down with witness.

Passing-Knowledge Artifacts
  • Leafblade (moss-tallied): a knife whose edge carries hedge-law. Invoke: gain a Context String for border disputes. Cost: tick Burden Ledger [4] (owe repair work this season).
  • Resin-Glass Pane: sun-cured panels holding a chorus. Invoke: reduce DV by −1 on rites requiring old wording. Cost: Under-Root Grudge +1 if used to excuse harm without replanting.
  • Riverstone Book: pebbles in corded order recording ferry rights. Invoke: create a temporary Ferry Knot String. Cost: pay a light-due before drinking.
Marks of the Body (Animist Rites)

Lethai Marks — ink, scar, resin-inlay — amplify embodied gifts by binding a person to a pattern. Every Mark has Context Keys: place, season, witness. Out of context, a Mark drifts toward curse.

  • Barkskin Mark: skin takes the grain of oak. Gift: once/scene, downgrade Physical Harm by one step (Position +1 when braced). Keys: shaded work, sap rite, roof-tree witness. Curse (lost context): brittleness — cold converts the first Fatigue to Harm.
  • River-Ears: cartilage braided with silver reed. Gift: sense current and voices across water (Clue +1 on rivers). Keys: pour water first, ferryman's knot. Curse: flood-whispers — compelled toward the lowest ground (Downhill Pull).
  • Spider's Patience (Inae oath): Gift: hold still beyond breath; ambush begins at Dominant in dim light. Keys: web-tithe, oath-sign shown. Curse: fixation — DV +1 to abandon a plan once set.
  • Serpent's Breath (Isoka oath): Gift: once/scene, convert Toxin Harm 1 to Fatigue; read heat like color. Keys: antivenin rite, shed-skin offering. Curse: cold-blooded — action slows in chill (Position −1 unless warmed).
  • Storm-Shadow: ink of ash and rain. Gift: vanish in a downpour; ranged attacks against you suffer −1 Effect in rain. Keys: storm cup, gutter song. Curse: thunder-call — loud noises pull attention (start Noticed [4] in loud scenes).
  • Lanternbone: resin set in a healed break. Gift: glow faintly to mark safe steps; once/scene, grant an ally Position +1 on footing checks. Keys: night watch, seed-tithe. Curse: beacon — predators test the light (the GM may spend a Boon to start Hungry Eyes [4]).
Curses as Lost Context

A Mark becomes a Curse when used without its Keys or against its ethic.

  • Context Drift [4]: ticks when a Mark is invoked off-season, off-place, or without witness. On fill, the Mark expresses its Curse until Context is Restored.
  • Context Restored (project [4]): recover the Keys — return to the place, invite a songkeeper, repay the light-due. An Aelinnel Sumwright's gloss-tree can substitute for a witness once.
  • Burden Ledger [6]: tracks obligation from inherited knowledge. On fill, pay with repair years, seed-tithes, or forfeit a String tied to the artifact.
At the Table (Simple Levers)
  • Strings: mark-right, context key, songkeeper witness, gloss-tree charter — spend to reduce DV by −1 on rites or negotiations tied to that Mark or artifact.
  • Position/Effect: most Marks grant Position +1 or Effect +1 once/scene when used with Keys; without Keys, tick Context Drift instead.
  • Mitigation: Aeler null-bells can mute a Mark's Curse for a scene at the cost of lamp-time, or Public Outrage [4] if used in a Lethai court.

The Threshold Folk are cultures of the in-between — hearths, hollows, hedgerows, stacks, and shadows tucked at the margins of bigfolk life. They are not monsters to be found but neighbors to be reckoned with, complete with law, trade, and grudges of their own.

  • Liminal Focus: cultures living in thresholds, borders, and in-between spaces.

Aelaerem — The People of the Hearth

Homeland: Amedell, south of the Aelinnel halls, neighboring the Valewood and the Dales.

The Aelaerem keep a country of gentle slopes and hedged lanes, cider barns and red-doored cottages. They are small in stature and large in memory — a people who bind promises with bread and lantern-light, who measure seasons by harvest masks and market bells. Hospitality is their public law; the hidden law beneath it is older still: hedge-counts, cup-marks, and the quiet attention of the Neighbors.

Hearth-Law & Guest-Right

A red door means bread, salt, and one safe night if you come honest; many Aelaerem homes keep a guest-loaf token for travelers. Those who enter "under bread and lantern" find their next parley gentled — hospitality is a shield as much as a courtesy.

Lantern-Law & the Wardens

Roads in Amedell are trimmed with little laws: stiles counted "eight-and-one," village stones turned inward at dusk, door-nails blessed against trespass. Lantern-wardens keep the lanes bright and note which shadows are wrong, while Wold-Wardens speak hedge-law where crowns have no purchase. Offer oil or heal a lamp, and a lonely mile may find you under warded light.

The Quiet Powers (Neighbors)

Aelaerem speak of Neighbors who walk under hedges and barrows. Good manners keep them content: leave butter at the cup-marks, keep to the festival calendar, count the stiles aloud. Observance smooths the night; neglect draws the Hollow's attention, and small things begin to go wrong — bells toll soft for no reason, red thread appears where you did not tie it, a door leads briefly somewhere it should not.

Seasons of Mask and Harvest

Mummers keep stricter rules than any priest, and the Thresher-King's guard walks in red hoods when the fields demand order. Festivals turn the world sideways for a night: masks legitimate certain crossings after dark, private moots under the Oak settle quarrels, and an elder's blessing can open doors that would not budge for coin.

Yet omens travel with the revels: scarecrows watch the lane, lanterns burn blue at the ford, chalk mazes fill with mist, and sometimes the Moot Oak bleeds sap the color of wine. Wise folk heed the bells, trade a secret for safe passage, and bury a mask at the crossroads when the dance goes wrong.

Trade, Craft, and Tokens

Cider, perry, beeswax, and wool spin Amedell's economy. Orchard grafts from Mother's Orchard serve as living writs in rural parley; a mill-token buys rumor ground fine as flour; a shepherd's whistle can make dogs and door-bolts heed for a scene. Movers of unblessed pressings risk spoilage — favors salvage what coin cannot.

Aelaerem "hearth magic" is housekeeping writ large: red thread to bind promises, lantern-writ to hold the dark at bay, careful count and courtesy to keep the threshold sweet. It is less spell than system — precise attention to seasons, doors, and debts.

Borders & Tensions

North, the Valewood's moods drift over the downs — moon-sap weather and dream-pollen make first-night omens ring true. East and south, the Rivers and Dales host tight-lipped dalesfolk, stubborn parishers and millers, as often allies as rivals when tolls, water-rights, and mumming calendars misalign. When lantern-law frays or taboos break, figures step out of story: a Lantern Bailiff calling a midnight moot, a Hollow Bride feeding on invitations, a scarecrow crowned with wrong knots.

Faces of the Hearth
  • Apple-Matron: power sits where she pours — feast and precedent are one art.
  • Thresher-King: a title that moves but never leaves; his guard opens doors none other can.
  • Lantern-Warden: trims lamps, reads shadows; escorts earned with oil and respect.
  • Mummers' Captain: keeper of lawful masks and after-dark crossings on feast-days — anger them and gates close at dusk.
  • Miller & Watch-Geese: local alarm and local court in one flock and wheel.
  • The Pale Shepherd: once, pass uncounted; always, pay the lane its due.
Play Hooks in Amedell
  • The Guest-Loaf Forgers: counterfeit tokens sour trust from Amedell to the Silk Way; track the mummers' license that legitimized the forgeries, or every night road turns hostile.
  • The Moot Oak Bleeds: a festival omen turns ugly — mediate before the Hollow answers the knives.
  • Lanterns at the Ford: blue flames demand a toll "more than coin" — learn what the Neighbors want this season.
  • Apple-Matron's Summons: refuse her table and prices double; accept, and you inherit a feud with the dalesmen downstream.
Using the Aelaerem at the Table

Treat bread, lamps, and counting as levers: produce a guest-loaf and a lit lantern to soften a risky social exchange; observe courtesies to cancel the first strange complication in a scene; pause at a stile and listen to ask what the Hollow wants right now. Even in danger, a shepherd's whistle or mill-token can turn fiction your way — Amedell rewards those who mind the little laws. In Amedell, the hearth is a treaty, the lane a ledger, and the night a neighbor. Keep the count, pour the cup, and the country will keep you.

Aelinnel — The People of Sums

Homeland: the gnomeholds in the hawthorn hills south of the Valewood; stone, bough, and bright things.

The Aelinnel keep to counted roads and measured courtesies. Their halls run like veins through granite and hawthorn; their bridges hum when tuned; their bargains arrive on two ledgers — what was said, and what was meant. To walk their country is to feel math underfoot: steps that are safer when even, doors that open to the right sequence, moonlight that prefers tidy logic.

The Hawthorn Halls

South of the Valewood, the hills break into stone spurs and thorn-sheltered gullies. Paths are counted by antler-posts; tide-cut stairs descend to black sea-rifts; causeys of pale flags show themselves at dawn, at dusk, and whenever someone is counting aloud. Basalt organ cliffs breathe in primes; a moonwell stains coins green faster than names; dolmen stairs ring true if you tap the right interval.

Winged gnomes — fae-kin cousins with leaf-thin pinions — roost in hawthorn crowns and act as go-betweens to the courts. Gnomes as a whole resemble smaller, bright-eyed elves; the winged are simply closer to the other side of the hedge.

Law of Sums (Courtesy, Copper, Count)

Aelinnel civility is exact: count or be counted. Speak your steps, breaths, or stitches, and the land steadies — Position shifts safer for acts that exploit pattern or timing. Favor copper over iron before the courts; copper is polite, iron is an insult unless named or gilded. Recite a simple sequence when tension frays to cancel the first misstep in navigation or negotiation.

Courts of hawthorn require three clean courtesies: do not bring naked iron; speak in two ledgers; return what points the way (chalk, cord, antler). Keep them, and even thorns hold back; slight them, and arches close, time drifts, and conversations arrive folded and misaddressed.

Table Levers
  • Counting Etiquette: once/scene, careful counting shifts Position +1 for patterned action.
  • Copper over Iron: displaying copper or brass tools avoids the first fae-offense penalty in a scene.
  • Two-Ledger Talk: state what was said and what was meant to cancel the first social SB this scene.

People of Stone & Bough

You'll meet charcoal-burners who read omens by smoke hums, stone-singers whose low chords relax walls, forester-wardens who hammer copper nails where iron offends, and goat-herds who measure danger in hoof-widths. Markets under living roofs sell truths wrapped for travel; weights and measures matter — producing a certified rod cancels the first jurisdiction or commerce snag.

Reputation echoes. Earn Hazel Favors by returning way-cords, restoring antler posts, or paying tide-dues; spend them to downgrade a glamour or geas once per leg. Masks and marks — Thorn-Courteous, Market-Square, Forester-Trusted — change which doors open and which clerks squint.

Courts, Hunts, and Gates

Aelinnel bosses are etiquette engines and logic traps more than villains. The Lady of Thorns punishes breaches precisely and rewards perfect sequences; the Bramble Knight duels by paths and proofs; the Moonlit Ride offers one night's clemency to those who name the right horn-count; the Green Gate demands exact change in truths. When an Ace turns up in their stories, echo the motifs — antlers in shadow, petals that cut, a breathing tide, and shortcuts that insist on proper counting.

Tides, Ledgers, and Names

Rivers and sea-caves carry their own arithmetic. Tide-reeves file plans before neap tide to earn a Tide Window; skip the ledger and the first Dolmis crossing suffers Wrong Tide. Unmaintained barge seals invite Wrong Hour. In green markets, you'll sometimes submit a said/meant receipt — try single-ledger haggling and the stalls mark you Exact Change Only, with memory or name demanded to square the sum.

Aelinnel Mood: Dark-Wonder

Think a math-bright traveler down a hawthorn rabbit-path: routes shorten for those who keep count and lengthen for the proud; petals fall like knives and settle into proofs; moonlight reveals hidden routes; antler-posts rearrange themselves when the land takes offense. Time miscounts, and the sun arrives at the wrong hour with excellent logic.

  • Heart: a hedge-witch prices cures in "unlesses"; pay with a clause you can keep.
  • Club: a hawthorn arch closes behind you; your footfalls no longer match your steps.
  • Diamond: a hazel token allows one cut-free hedge crossing; the scratch you didn't get remembers you kindly.

Winged Kin (Faekin)

Some Aelinnel are born to the boughs, pinions veined like leaves. They keep hawthorn courtesies as breath — no naked iron beyond an arch, always return what points the way, speak debts in daylight so leaves can hear. They are the surest messengers between keep and court, and the quickest to warn when counting turns wrong.

Play Hooks

  • Kiln in Thirds: a charcoal clan's draught hums off-pattern; smoke omens say a geas is miscounted. Set it straight before the court tithe arrives in flowers and warrants.
  • Two Ledgers, One Lie: a broker sells meanings at a green market; catch them in a one-ledger statement or produce a chilled oath-bead to void their trick.

Mazereth — Deep Tunnelers (Aelinnel Subrace)

Stone-song, pressure shafts, and the etiquette of bearings and bridges.

South of the Valewood the hills become ribbed stone and root-warrens, and the Aelinnel sub-culture called the Mazereth trace their lives along pressure lines and strata seams. Their halls feel like organs that breathe — pillars tuned to low chords, vents that whisper the time, chalk glyphs that count the load of a wall. But Mazereth are not only cave-keen: they tend weighhouses, keep ledgers for bridge trusts, and carry bearing-cords across market towns. They greet the hill with a palm on stone and a measure of weight; they greet the street with a counted price and a clean tally.

Shadow Step is near-range and demands real, contiguous shade — alleys, awnings, cart undersides — keeping the talent street-grounded rather than supernatural flight.

Archive Keepers — Knowledge-Bound Aelinnel

Index courts, pattern webs, and the peril of perfect memory.

The Archive Keepers are Aelinnel cousins who hoard what others forget. They curate hidden stacks, stitch ledgers to ledgers, and build "pattern webs" that let a fact walk from one book to another. Their manners are footnotes; their quarrels are citations; their pride is a shelf that never lies.

Index Law (Etiquette of Records)

  • Cite or Be Cited: offer a source when making a claim; once/scene, a clean citation grants DV −1 to Plan/Research/Petition.
  • Return the Page: replace a card where it belongs to cancel the first Index Entropy complication this scene.
  • Two Copies, Two Locks: a binding writ needs twin copies kept apart; declaring both voids the first forgery SB this scene.
  • Right of Errata: admit a small error to avoid a larger duel; convert a looming Sting/Inspection into a lesser Public Correction [4].

Cultural Deep-Dives & Portrayal

Aelaerem Society: the Aelaerem organize into hearth-clans centered on craft specialties — coopers, weavers, brewers, and woodcarvers who maintain both trade secrets and seasonal rituals. Each clan keeps a memory-weaver who tracks debts, favors, and omens across generations through story-braids and token-craft. Information flows through guest-right networks and festival moots, where the Apple-Matron mediates disputes through feast-law and proverb-craft. Threshold guardians like the Pale Shepherd are honored with offerings and careful observance; slight them and household luck turns sour. Clan tokens — bread-seals, lantern-writs, red-thread knots — serve as both currency and covenant, in an economy where reputation weighs more than coin.

Mazereth Engineering: Mazereth architecture operates on pressure harmonics, where pillars, arches, and support beams are tuned to resonate at frequencies that strengthen stone rather than strain it. Master builders read stress-lines in rock like maps, placing copper nodes at compression points and iron dampeners at tension zones. Their deep history is preserved in geological strata — glyphs carved into cave walls that tell stories through the ages as erosion reveals new layers. Tunnel-network politics revolve around bearing-rights and seam-access; clans feud over prime ventilation shafts and mineral veins, settling disputes through measured duels where contestants navigate obstacle courses while maintaining perfect step-counts and load-balances.

Umbral Kin Memory: Umbral Kin share memories through dream-reflection, where important experiences are "polished" until they gleam bright enough to be caught in glass surfaces and still water. Reflection communication allows near-instant long-distance contact — messages passed from mirror to mirror across a city's windows can traverse miles in moments. Shadow-realm territorial disputes arise when one group's reflections begin overlapping another's; boundaries are negotiated through careful light-management and shadow-casting protocols. Memory Echo lets Umbral Kin read emotional residue from surfaces, making them excellent investigators but also vulnerable to psychic pollution from traumatic events embedded in walls and floors.

Archive Keeper Knowledge: Archive Keepers store information in pattern-webs — three-dimensional mental constructs connecting related facts across multiple dimensions of meaning. Their scale-shifting libraries exist in probability space, where books grow larger or smaller depending on the reader's need for detail; a general overview might require a text the size of a grain of sand, while deep research could mean entering a tome the size of a cathedral. Knowledge-debt systems track intellectual borrowing — when an Archive Keeper accesses another's memory palace or information bridge, they owe a tithe of new knowledge or a favor in return. The most dangerous debts are memetic — ideas that carry obligations or change the borrower's thinking patterns.

Portrayal Notes
  • Emphasize capability over diminutive stereotypes: these cultures are not weak or helpless despite their size — they are experts in their environments with sophisticated knowledge systems. Show them solving problems through precision, timing, and deep understanding of their realms rather than brute force.
  • Show complex societies with internal diversity: each culture has factions, dissenters, reformers, and outliers. The Aelaerem have those who reject hospitality laws, Mazereth include those who favor iron over copper, Umbral Kin struggle with those who crave bright light, and Archive Keepers debate the ethics of memory manipulation. Not every member adheres strictly to cultural norms.
  • Use hospitality and reciprocity as mechanical hooks: treat guest-right, copper courtesy, mirror-oaths, and citation protocols as more than flavor — they're tactical resources. Characters can spend cultural tokens for mechanical benefits, but violating these codes creates escalating complications that drive scenes forward.
  • Balance hidden advantages with genuine vulnerabilities: Aelaerem are fragile in mass conflict, Mazereth suffer surface sickness, Umbral Kin are unstable in bright light, and Archive Keepers risk overload. Their strengths come with meaningful costs that create interesting choices.
Scene Framing
  • Start in threshold spaces or realm boundaries: begin scenes at doorways, hedges, cave entrances, shadow edges, or between bookshelves. These liminal zones are where threshold cultures are strongest and where their unique abilities shine.
  • Use scale for tactical variety: small PCs can use tight spaces, hide in unexpected places, or exploit environmental hazards that don't affect larger characters; big PCs can provide brute force but may struggle with finesse or environmental navigation.
  • Frame conflicts around access and visibility: tension often comes from who can go where and who knows what. Information control, territorial disputes, and questions of who gets to cross thresholds create natural dramatic friction without resorting to combat.
  • Let cultural differences drive narrative tension: conflicts arise from different value systems — Aelaerem hospitality vs. Mazereth precision, Umbral Kin secrecy vs. Archive Keeper transparency, surface urgency vs. threshold patience.
Integration Hooks
  • Hidden communities beneath existing venues: every major location in a campaign world has threshold spaces — palace walls house Aelaerem guest-halls, foundation stones contain Mazereth tunnels, chandeliers cast Umbral reflection-lanes, and library stacks hide Archive Keeper sanctums.
  • Small folk as information brokers: Aelaerem hearth-clans know everyone's business, Mazereth tunnels connect distant locations, Umbral Kin read emotional residue, and Archive Keepers maintain vast information webs — excellent contacts for investigation scenarios.
  • Realm connections to major plot points: ancient vaults exist partially in threshold realms, important artifacts hide in scale-shifting spaces, political negotiations happen in neutral threshold zones, and supernatural threats emerge from realm boundaries.
  • Scale-based tactical opportunities: small PCs can sabotage large mechanisms, infiltrate secure areas, or deliver messages through impossible routes; big PCs can provide protection, move heavy objects, or serve as mobile platforms for smaller allies.

Example of Play

Setup

An Aelaerem craftsperson named Thistle works with the surface merchant guild to investigate reports of a haunted vault beneath the old marketplace. Thistle brings Object Bond (lockpick expertise), Threshold Walking (navigation of liminal spaces), and a Liminbound bond (connection to threshold spaces). The guild provides resources and legal authority but is dependent on Thistle's unique abilities to access the vault's hidden sections.

Scene

The vault exists partially in threshold space, with some chambers accessible normally and others requiring passage through liminal boundaries. Thistle uses Threshold Walking to navigate sections that shift between dimensions, Object Bond to pick precision locks that respond to careful touch rather than force, and her Liminbound bond to sense stable passages versus areas that might collapse or shift unexpectedly. The guild representatives follow with lanterns and tools, operating well outside their expertise.

Complications

As Thistle delves deeper, her actions increase Threshold Tension, attracting a territorial threshold guardian that begins manifesting as locked doors reappearing after being opened and shadows that move against the light. Meanwhile, the guild masters above notice unusual activity and start asking questions, raising Visibility and creating pressure to finish quickly. The vault's unstable nature threatens Realm Stability — walls occasionally shift, passages close temporarily, and the boundary between threshold and normal space grows indistinct.

Resolution

The "haunting" turns out to be a Mazereth clan using the vault's threshold sections as a shortcut between their deep tunnels and surface access points, inadvertently disrupting the space through their passage. Thistle brokers a compromise — establishing proper guest-right protocols and payment in copper nails that strengthen the vault's structure. The solution opens new connections between surface and threshold communities, but also raises Notice and Visibility, making Thistle a known quantity to both guild authorities and other threshold cultures who may seek her services in the future.

Beastfolk, Dragonfolk, and Others

In Fate's Edge, Beastfolk, Dragonfolk, and Others are not common "races" but rare, mythic outliers. They dwell primarily in the Valewood and the deep Wilds, rarely appearing in human lands except as rumors, omens, or the subject of contradictory stories.

Design Intent

These origins are meant to emphasize rarity and narrative weight; tie directly into core systems — Position & Effect, Story Beats, Boons, Fatigue, Obligation, and Patrons; and root nonhuman characters in specific places and stories rather than generic ancestry.

Using Nonhuman Origins — GM Guidance

Nonhuman PCs should remain uncommon. As a default: limit the party to 0–1 nonhuman PC unless the campaign is primarily set in the Valewood or Wilds. In human lands, nonhumans always shift Position & Effect — social actions often start from worse Position (risky or desperate), but can achieve higher Effect when leaning into fear, myth, or awe. Nonhuman PCs should come with explicit ties: a Patron, a forest court, a dragon enclave, an old debt, or a prophecy.

The "Other in Human Lands" Timer: when a Beastfolk, Dragonfolk, or Other PC moves openly in human lands, the GM creates an Otherness timer for that community (4–6 segments, as appropriate). Each time the PC acts conspicuously, mark 1 segment. When the timer fills, introduce a consequence — hunters, scholars, inquisitors, curious nobles, street rumors, or religious panic. This timer replaces ambient prejudice with focused, story-relevant pressure.

Beastfolk

Where the road ends, the people of fur and claw remember no empire but the forest. Beastfolk are Lethai rewritten by the Wild through old pacts, curses, or bloodlines bound to animal spirits. They exist as reminders that civilization is not the only intelligence on the map.

Beastfolk clans loosely align with Valewood Circles (semi-nomadic bands tied to groves, standing stones, or ancient trails) and gather rarely at Wild Moots, where multiple clans — and sometimes Others — negotiate seasonal rites, borders, and common threats. The fallen Utar Empire is remembered as an infection that burned out; human kingdoms are regarded as a lingering fever, not the natural order.

Common archetypes: Wolf-blooded (pack-minded scouts and guardians), Stag-marked (antlered wanderers tied to sacrifice and omen), Raven-tongued (tricksters and memory-keepers), Bear-hearted (slow to anger, anchors of their communities, unstoppable when roused).

Core Feature: Beast Instinct

When you create a Beastfolk character, choose an Instinct that defines how the Wild speaks through you: Hunt (stalk, pursue, reveal weakness), Guard (protect, endure, stand firm), Wander (roam, explore, cross boundaries), or Trick (misdirect, unsettle, reframe).

Instinct Move: once per scene, when you clearly act in line with your Instinct in a risky moment, gain +1 die to the roll. On a hit, the GM gains +1 SB to spend later on complications tied to the Wild or your nature.

If you deliberately betray your Instinct to secure safety or comfort, the GM may mark +1 Fatigue, or mark +1 segment on your personal Alienation from the Wild timer (4 segments). When that timer fills, you owe the Wild — or a Patron of the Wild — a serious price to reconcile who you have become.

Dragonfolk

Where a dragon died screaming, someone always wakes up different. Dragonfolk are not full dragons, but living fault-lines where draconic power has leaked into blood, bone, and story. Some claim descent from ancient wyrms of the mountains; others were changed by proximity to hoards, old lairs, or dragon-Patrons. In human lands, most people have never seen a Dragonfolk and would not believe in them outside of folktales.

Common archetypes: Ashscale (scarred by volcanic fire, eyes smoldering with embers), Stormblood (hair and scales crackling with static, voice like thunder), Deepcoil (serpentine, cold-eyed, hungry for secrets and leverage), Shardsoul (reflective, glassy scales, obsessed with memory and perfection).

Core Feature: Draconic Echo

Choose one Echo, a thematic way that dragon-nature manifests in you: Fire (fury, destruction, passion), Storm (change, disruption, omen), Hoard (possession, memory, obsession), or Dominion (authority, command, pride).

Echo Move: once per scene, when you invoke your Echo, describe how it shapes your presence (heat in the air, a rumbling voice, an unblinking gaze, the scent of rain) and gain +1 die to actions that rely on force of personality (Command, Sway, or similar). On a hit, the GM gains +1 SB earmarked for dragon-shaped consequences — old debts, dragon cults, relic awakenings, rival Dragonfolk, or attention from a dragon-Patron.

If you refuse to draw on your Echo in a moment where it would clearly help, mark +1 Fatigue — you are fighting your own nature.

Dragonblooded Resilience

Dragonfolk shrug off some threats but attract others. You gain +1 armor against Fire, Lightning, or Fear effects when it is fictionally appropriate. Artifacts, wards, and rites that bind or repel "dragons and their kin" also affect you, even if they were not meant for your specific bloodline.

Others

The world remembers its own dreams, and sometimes they wake up in the shape of people. The Others are those not wholly of the mortal world — fae-touched, gloam-born, children of forgotten rites, or walkers between dreams and waking. They stand at boundaries: between Valewood myth and human history, between one Patron's claim and another's silence.

Common archetypes: Gloam-touched (eyes like dusk, never fully in one light or shadow), Hollow-bound (something else peers out through their eyes, patient and curious), Green-vowed (sworn to a forest, river, stone circle, or ancient tree), Masks of Dawn (can pass as human until the wrong light hits them).

Core Feature: Liminal Nature

Choose one Liminal Axis that defines your between-ness: Dream/Waking, Forest/City, Life/Death, or Memory/Forgetting.

Liminal Move: when you act to bridge or exploit your Axis, gain +1 die to the roll. On a hit, the GM may mark +1 SB to introduce consequences that blur reality — time slippage, lost memories, omens, strange visitors, and similar effects.

Bound Obligation

Others rarely exist without a binding. Choose one: bound to a Place (a sacred grove, crossroads, riverbend), bound to a Story (prophecy, curse, legend, song), or bound to a Being (Patron, ancient spirit, sleeping dragon, forest court). Write one sentence: "I am bound to ____ and must ____."

Any time you defy this Bond in a major way, the GM may mark +2 SB, or immediately introduce a supernatural consequence tied to your Bond. Any time you honor it at serious personal cost, clear 2 Fatigue, or mark +1 Boon for later use.

Adventure Hooks

Use these peoples to complicate the Valewood and Wilds rather than simply populate them.

  • The Beastfolk Hunt: a Beastfolk circle believes a human village has broken an ancient trail-compact and hunts a "trespasser" who is actually a human PC or ally. Can the party negotiate new terms before blood is spilled?
  • Dragon's Debt: a Dragonfolk PC's Echo stirs an ancient dragon from dormancy. The dragon demands repayment for a boon given to their bloodline long ago — or offers patronage that could reshape the region.
  • The Vanishing Grove: an Other bound to a sacred grove feels it thinning. Human loggers, Utar relic-hunters, or a rising Patron's followers are siphoning its power. Saving it may mean undermining a human ally or opposing a Patron the party relies on.
  • The "Monster" in the City: rumors spread of a beast-headed killer in a human city. The culprit is a terrified Beastfolk youth, trapped far from the Valewood and hunted by scholars and zealots. Do the PCs smuggle them home, conceal them, or weaponize the panic?
