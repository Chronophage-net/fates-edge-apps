What This Book Covers

This sourcebook is the working magic-system companion for Fate's Edge: how each people and culture of the setting understands and regulates magic, the Six Roads a magic-user may walk (Runekeeper, Invoker, Cantor, Witch, Psion, Summoner), a full catalog of fifteen Patrons with lore, Rites, and GM guidance, and the standalone systems for Free Casting (the TAGS grammar) and Summoning (cultural traditions and the Leash). Framed as the collected, opinionated field notes of a single wandering narrator, it is meant to be read as both lore and toolbox — pull the mechanics you need and let the voice set the tone at the table.

Introduction — A Voice from the Crossroads

"You want power? Fine. But don't come crying when the weight finds you." — The Gray Wanderer

This grimoire is not a rulebook. It is a companion — a set of lenses through which to see the magical paths of Fate's Edge. There are no grandmasters to bow before here, no academies whose diplomas protect you from the flame. What follows are approaches: six roads that diverge from the crossroads where power and price shake hands.

In Thepyrgos, a Synod clerk once labeled me with a Vhasian badlands term for those who hear thoughts without asking. I told him I do not read minds; I read the weight of what people carry. He wrote me down anyway, in his register of freaks and witch-marked. He was not entirely wrong, but he missed the point by a mile. The powers I have touched leave fingerprints that look like many things. I have worn the chains of a dozen Patrons and broken most of them — twice. I have been called a Runekeeper in Aeler, a witch in Aelaerem, a Cantor in Silkstrand, a Summoner in Zakov, and a fool everywhere. The road changes the walker, and I have walked them all.

What We Will Explore
  • The Six Roads — not merely the three paths of covenant, but the ways of the Runekeeper, the Invoker, the Cantor, the Witch, the Psion, and the Summoner: who they are, how they think, and why they risk annihilation to keep walking.
  • How to handle Obligation — not as punishment, but as a story engine, with patron-specific ideas, intrusions, and acts of service, plus the price of walking without a patron at all.
  • Rituals without a focus — when your Codex is ash, your Symbol cracked, your voice alone, and your mind turned inward. Components matter again when the ledger is blank.
  • The art of Summoning — binding spirits to will, from the deep stone to the salt foam, and the terrible etiquette required to send them back.

The voice you hear belongs to no one and everyone — a wanderer who has stood at the crossroads of every Patron's attention, who has tasted the Hollow Tithe and climbed the Silent Stair, who has learned that the hedge is what keeps the wolves from the flock. Call me the Gray Wanderer. These notes are my legacy. Use them, lose them, or burn them for warmth. I won't mind.

— The Gray Wanderer

The Weight of Names — How the Peoples of Fate's Edge See Magic

Magic in Fate's Edge is not a single practice. It is a family of arguments with reality — and every culture argues differently. Some embrace the structured pacts of Patrons. Others whisper to spirits at crossroads. A few have learned to bend the world with nothing but a disciplined mind. What follows is not a definitive guide — I have been corrected by village grandmothers and high priests alike — but a wanderer's impressions, gathered over too many years on too many roads.

Aeler — The Deep Watchers

The dwarves of Aeler do not speak of "magic." They speak of "load," "breath," and "covenant." A Rite is not a miracle — it is a contract with stone, sealed by proper measure and witnessed by a bell. A Runekeeper is an engineer of the invisible, and a witch is a mender of cracks in the world's infrastructure.

Respected
  • Runekeeper: the ideal Aeler mage carries a Codex carved in slate and travels beside a Thiasos that chitters in the dark. What recommends the bond is not that it can be counted, but that its duties can be named before witnesses and disputed before harm becomes custom.
  • Witch (Hearth craft): the women who keep the cisterns sweet and the gas vents predictable are salaried, not feared — employed as "Breath Wardens" and "Cistern Mothers."
Mistrusted
  • Cantor: too public, too emotional — the deep stone dislikes loud noises it cannot measure.
  • Free Caster: an abomination. Power without a covenant is a cave-in waiting to happen.
  • Psion: rare, but when one appears the Aeler assign them to the Deep Vaults, where the mind-reading is useful and the instability is contained.

The Breath-Bond: every Aeler mage must learn a meditation that converts psychic strain into measured breaths. A Psion in Aeler carries a bell on a leather cord — each Mental Strain point is one ring. Ring it three times too fast, and a Corrector appears.

Famous Aeler magic: the Spine Underway, a tunnel that remembers every traveler who has passed through it. Runekeepers carve new Rites into its walls each century; witches keep the air sweet; the stone itself is said to be the oldest Summoner, awake and patient.

Aelaerem — The Hearth-and-Hollow

The halflings of Aelaerem do not build temples to Patrons. They leave butter on cup-mark stones and hang red thread on hawthorn. Their magic is domestic — and therefore invisible to those who look only for grand gestures.

Respected
  • Witch: Aelaerem is the heartland of witchcraft. Every village has a Hedge-Mother who knows which knot ties a safe birth and which bell keeps the Hollow from swallowing a child.
  • Cantor: folk songs are the oldest grimoire. A Cantor who sings the old tunes and respects the "no ninth" rule is welcome at any hearth.
  • Summoner: only of brownies, field-wights, and the small dead — Aelaerem summoners do not call demons, they ask the ancestors to move the firewood.
Mistrusted
  • Invoker: carrying Symbols from multiple Patrons reads as greed. The Hollow does not forgive divided loyalty.
  • Psion: reading thoughts without asking violates hospitality. A known psion finds doors closing and cup-mark stones left dry.

The Hollow Tithe: once a year the Aelaerem leave a basket of the year's best bread at the edge of a barrow. Witches conduct the rite; no one speaks on the walk back. Something is always following. It always stops at the village gate.

Aelinnel — The Bright Weird

The gnomes of Aelinnel live in a world where numbers have manners and doors sometimes lead to last Tuesday. Their magic is not chaotic — it is algebraic. They have names for equations other cultures do not know exist.

Respected
  • Free Caster: the gnomes invented the TAGS system, though they call it "Contextual Recursion." A free caster who can improvise on the spot is a philosopher, not a danger.
  • Psion: the mind is the ultimate context. Aelinnel psions train from childhood to "count the thoughts" and "measure the echo."
  • Summoner: spirits are simply data from a previous iteration of reality — treat them politely and document everything.
Mistrusted
  • Runekeeper: too rigid. A Rite carved in stone cannot adapt to a changing context.
  • Cantor: a song that cannot be written in two ledgers (Said and Meant) is dangerous. The gnomes have banned three hymns in the last decade.

The Green Gate: a sea-arch that opens at certain tides onto a forest that appears on no map. Witches and Free Casters travel it; Psions test their resilience against it. The toll is one truth never told and one lie never caught.

Linn — The Storm-Tested

The northmen of Linn do not ask permission from Patrons. They make oaths — and the sea witnesses. Their magic is carved into keels, braided into ropes, whispered into the ears of drowning men.

Respected
  • Summoner (Selkie and Drowned): a tide-witch who can call a selkie for navigation or a drowned captain for warning is worth her weight in whalebone.
  • Cantor: sea shanties are weather magic — the right note at the right moment can calm a storm, or call one.
  • Witch (Wake-Judge): the Linn have formalized witchcraft into a legal system; Wake-Judges arbitrate disputes at the water's edge using promise timers.
Mistrusted
  • Invoker: borrowing power from a Patron reads as weakness — a real northman makes his own luck.
  • Psion: too quiet. The Linn prefer their magic loud enough to hear over the wind.

The Thing-Bell: every Linn longship carries a bell forged from the melted-down sword of its first captain. To seal an oath, a Runekeeper or Witch rings it. The sound carries across water. The dead listen.

Ykrul — The Weather Readers

The orcs of the Violet Steppe do not separate magic from survival. A bone-singer who calls a storm is no different from a scout who reads the grass. Both are necessary. Both can get you killed.

Respected
  • Summoner (Ancestral): the drum is the oldest Codex — one who can call a wolf-ancestor or wind-horse is honored at any campfire.
  • Psion (Precognition): called "Weather Readers," not because they predict rain but because they see the shape of the future as a rider sees the shape of the steppe.
  • Witch (Road craft): one who can bind a promise with a knotted cord or curse a rival with grave-dust is a valued clan member.
Mistrusted
  • Runekeeper: too slow — the steppe does not wait for a carved Rite.
  • Invoker: carrying Symbols from multiple Patrons reads as divided loyalty. A Ykrul warrior serves one banner.
  • Cantor: a song that binds a crowd is a leash, and the Ykrul value freedom too highly.

The Red Weather: when a Ykrul witch curses a horse-thief, she ties a red cord around a splinter of the thief's saddle and burns it in a storm. The localized squall that follows is said to hunt the cursed until they return what they stole.

Vhasia — The Fractured Lens

The Vhasians inherited the ruins of the old Utaran Empire and learned to argue over the pieces. Their magic is legalistic, factional, and surprisingly bureaucratic.

Respected
  • Runekeeper: a notary with teeth — their Codex holds Rites, contracts, property deeds, and the terms of three unresolved feuds.
  • Invoker: multiple Patrons is called "diversification." A noble keeping Symbols for escape, contracts, and truth is merely prudent.
  • Witch (Cunning craft): every village has a hedge-witch who speaks for the old saints; the state church pretends not to notice.
Mistrusted
  • Free Caster: too unpredictable — the legal system cannot bill for damages without a contract.
  • Psion: reading minds without permission is a crime; enforcement of the three laws against it is spotty.

The Parlement of Dust: in catacombs beneath the Sun Palace, a secret court of Runekeepers adjudicates disputes between magical factions by candlelight, sealing judgments in wax mixed with grave-dust. No one admits the court exists. Everyone with real power obeys its rulings.

Viterra — The Hedge-Law Kingdom

The Viterrans are practical people. Their magic grows in hedgerows and hides in parish ledgers. A witch in Viterra is not a mystic — she is a "map adjuster" who knows where the boundary stones should really be.

Respected
  • Witch (Threshold): the "hedge-wright" is a legal as well as magical profession — drawing property lines, witnessing oaths, keeping the new mill off the ancient right-of-way.
  • Runekeeper: only those specializing in "precedent Rites," spells that make a past judgment binding on the present.
  • Cantor: a village Cantor who sings the old ballads at harvest is beloved; one who sings politics is exiled.
Mistrusted
  • Summoner: spirits cannot own property — the courts have ruled a ghost's testimony inadmissible.
  • Psion: the Queen's Justiciars keep a standing bounty on unlicensed telepaths. Privacy is the foundation of property law.

The Tilt-Yard Audits: before a Viterran Runekeeper can invoke a land-affecting Rite, the Codex is struck three times by a Dawn-Knight's sword before a panel. Survive three blows and the Rite is lawful. I have seen a Codex shatter on the second; the Runekeeper was fined for "magical noise pollution."

Ecktoria — The Marble Ledger

Ecktoria sits on the bones of the old Utaran Empire and pretends they are a throne. Their magic is ceremonial, political, and obsessed with appearances.

Respected
  • Runekeeper (Everflame): the state religion fields its own "Censer-Knights," who march in procession and purify heretics with blessed fire.
  • Invoker: nobles collect Symbols the way they collect statues of forgotten emperors. Display outweighs use.
  • Cantor: one who can make a crowd weep with a hymn is a political asset — the Grand Magistrate employs three.
Mistrusted
  • Witch: dismissed as "peasant magic," though every noble's wife knows a witch who can brew a love potion.
  • Psion: hunted with particular zeal — a mind that cannot be seen might be planning rebellion.
  • Summoner: the Ecktorian view is simple: if it's dead, it should stay dead. The Inquisition has burned three summoners in my lifetime.

The Censor's Mask: at the annual Triumph, the Censor wears a mask said to be a psionic artifact of the Great Silence, letting him see a petitioner's true intentions. I knelt before him once. He saw that I wanted his wine. He laughed and gave me a cup. The mask is real.

Thepyrgos — The City of Stairs

Thepyrgos is a nation of clerks and philosophers who believe a properly argued case can bend reality. Their magic is academic, verbal, and exhausting.

Respected
  • Free Caster: the University teaches TAGS as "practical metaphysics." A free caster with a degree charges triple.
  • Psion: the Synod's "mental hygiene" committee licenses psions as legal investigators. Unlicensed ones are hunted.
  • Witch (Bell craft): the bell-towers are thresholds, and Bell-wardens are respected civic employees.
Mistrusted
  • Runekeeper: too reliant on a single Patron — Thepyrgos prefers negotiators, not worshippers.
  • Cantor: a crowd that sings together is a crowd that might riot; the Archon limits public performances to three per week.

The Silent Stair: behind the Synod Hall, a staircase that leads nowhere — psions claim it leads to the space between thoughts. I climbed it for an hour once. The stairs did not end. I turned around and walked down, and was back in the same courtyard. I do not climb it anymore.

Lethai — The Fair Folk's Echo

The elves of the Valewood do not practice magic. They are magic, or have forgotten where they end and the Weave begins. A Lethai Runekeeper is a contradiction. A Lethai Cantor is a redundancy. A Lethai Witch is a priestess of a religion with no name.

Respected
  • Witch (Silk and Shed): two traditions — the Silk Vigil (mercy, continuity) and the Shed Vigil (transformation, hard choices) — both woven into Lethai law.
  • Summoner: spirits are simply neighbors who live in a different room, treated with courtesy and a healthy dose of suspicion.
  • Cantor: a shira (song-weaver) can wrap a promise in a melody so beautiful you forget you are bound. Their betrayal is legendary.
Mistrusted
  • Invoker: why borrow power from a Patron when you could simply be the power? The Lethai see Invokers as beggars in silk robes.
  • Psion: reading a Lethai mind without context is impossible — their thoughts are saturated with memory, season, and relationship. Psions who try often leave mad.
  • Free Caster: too crude. The Lethai prefer their magic to have rules, even secret ones.

The Ninth Walk: once a generation, a Lethai witch walks a pilgrimage through thresholds on no map, returning with a new law for the Lethai courts. The last Ninth Walk produced the rule "no bell may ring nine times." No one remembers why. Everyone obeys.

The Gray Wanderer's Conclusion

I have been called a Runekeeper in Aeler, a witch in Aelaerem, a Cantor in Silkstrand, a Summoner in Zakov, a Psion in Thepyrgos, and a fool everywhere. The truth is simpler: every culture sees magic as it sees itself. The Aeler see a covenant. The Linn see an oath. The Thepyrgos see a debate. The Lethai see a dream. You will find your own path. You will be misunderstood, feared, honored, hunted, or hired — sometimes all in the same day. Remember: the power is not in the Rite or the Song or the silent thought. The power is in the choice to use them.

The Six Roads — Lore and Roleplay

"I have worn the face of a knight, a warlock, a hedge-priest, and a hunted freak. The path chooses you as much as you choose it, and the price is always the same weight, though the coin differs." — The Gray Wanderer

There are not three paths through the thornwood, but six, diverging from the crossroads where power and price shake hands. Do not mistake these for guilds or orders — there are no charters signed in ink, no grandmasters to bow before. These are approaches, lenses through which the world's hidden gears become visible. A Runekeeper sworn to the Inquisitor Prime shares more with that burning zealot than with a Runekeeper of Grimmir, though both carry Codices. The road shapes the walker.

The Runekeeper — Keeper of the Inked Covenant

"I wrote the promise where both of us could read it. That does not make it just. It makes denial harder."

Runekeepers covenant with the great powers and act in their names, sometimes as priests, judges, gardeners, healers, or executioners. The covenant pressures a Runekeeper's identity but does not erase it. A Patron who wanted a tool could have made one; choosing a mortal leaves room for conscience, disobedience, and blame.

But this does not mean uniformity. A Mykkielan Templar is paladin and notary both, adjudicating wounds as lawful or heretical. A Grimmiric Druid maintains the treaty between planted field and wild wood. An Artificer of Aelinnel treats her Patron's Rites as formulae, arguing she performs no miracles, merely applies context. An Inquisitor of the Everflame is less mage than mobile courthouse, sentencing corruption to annihilation.

What unites them is structure. The Codex makes the covenant readable. The Thiasos is a follower with a life and judgment of their own: often an animal or spirit, sometimes a person changed by the Patron's country. Lose the Codex and part of the promise becomes difficult to prove. Lose the Thiasos and you have lost a companion—perhaps through the very service you claimed to share.

Roleplay Hooks
  • Your Codex is incomplete — the last page was torn out by your predecessor. What was on it, and why did they fear it enough to destroy it?
  • Your Thiasos grows more demanding. Last week it required a drop of blood. This week, it whispers for a finger.
  • You have begun to dream in your Patron's voice, and on waking are never certain which of you made yesterday's decisions.

The Invoker — The Gambler at the Crossroads

"I do not serve. I rent. And when the landlord comes to collect, I had best have the coin ready, or the interest will be paid in teeth."

If the Runekeeper is the sworn knight, the Invoker is the cardsharp who has learned the house rules well enough to cheat — briefly, dangerously, at terrible interest. Invokers carry Symbols, physical anchors to Patrons they have never fully sworn to. They bet not on luck but on knowledge: precisely what each Patron requires, what each Symbol will bear, and how far they can push before the weight becomes unbearable.

Their rites are slow, respectful, almost contractual — polishing Symbols, leaving offerings, learning the true names of a Patron's servants. But when the knife is at the throat, an Invoker performs the Crack the Seal: invoking the Rite as a creditor calling in an emergency loan. The Symbol may crack. The Patron may arrive personally to discuss terms. But for that moment, she holds power she has not earned, and the world bends.

Roleplay Hooks
  • You carry four Symbols — the limit before friction sets in. Every morning, roll them like bones. Which two whisper to each other today?
  • You found a Symbol in a dead man's hand and use it anyway, not knowing which Patron it belongs to.
  • You have never met your Patron and aren't certain they know you exist — but the weight grows anyway, and their servants have begun watching from across crowded rooms.

The Cantor — The Touched Voice

"You think you need a lute? My larynx is older than any tree. Hum, and the world will listen. Scream, and it might answer back."

Cantors are the wild singers, the mad pipers, the hymn-leaders who do not worship at the altar but become the altar. They do not swear to Patrons as Runekeepers do, nor bargain as Invokers do — they echo a Patron's will through their own flesh, becoming resonant chambers for forces never meant to fit inside a mortal throat.

Because they do not swear, they cannot break an oath. But they can be touched. Corruption for a Cantor is not a debt but a transformation — a voice that develops harmonics that should not exist, eyes that change color when they sing, shadows that lag half a step behind. They are corrupted not by obligation but by proximity: standing too close to the fire for too long.

They require no focus but their own bodies. A Cantor without a voice can tap rhythm on their ribs. One without hands can whistle through their teeth.

Roleplay Hooks
  • You sang a forbidden note once, and now a fragment of a dead Cantor lives in your head, humming tunes that predict the weather three days hence.
  • Your voice is unmistakable — enemies recognize you by timbre; allies weep at certain words for reasons they cannot name.
  • You are trying to write a new Song, but every attempt leaves you feverish for a week. The Song is writing you.

The Witch — The Quiet Architect

"They call it hedge-magic. They call it petty. They do not see that the hedge is what keeps the wolves from the flock, the blight from the crop, the forgotten god from waking hungry."

Witches practice the systemic magic that maintains the world — quiet, overlooked, and essential. While Runekeepers swear and Cantors sing, Witches tend. They know that a threshold must be swept three times counter-timerwise to keep the Hollow from noticing it; that milk sours not from bacteria but from a promise broken three houses down. They are the plumbers of the metaphysical.

This makes them feared more than loved. A Runekeeper's power is legible — a Codex, a Patron, a clear chain of command. A Witch's power is organic, grown from relationships with places and spirits that have no names in any grimoire. Every culture has Witches under different names — Hedge-Mothers, Breath-Wardens, Map-Adjusters, Cistern-Keepers.

Roleplay Hooks
  • You owe bonds to three different spirits — the river bordering your village, the oak at the crossroads, the crow that follows you. None have called them in yet.
  • You cannot cross running water without an offering. You don't remember why, but the one time you tried, you nearly drowned in three inches of rain.
  • A village that once shunned you now begs for help against a returned blight. You could save them, but the price paid last time — your grandmother's name, erased from memory — still stings.

The Psion — The Isolated Mind

"I carry no Symbol. I keep no Codex. My power has no scent, no sound, no outward sign. And that is why they fear me most of all."

Psions are the isolated ones, the untrusted, the inward-turned. Where other paths borrow from outside powers, the Psion looks only to the self. There is no glowing staff, no familiar, no song to warn you — which, in a world built on bonds and covenants, makes them suspect. In Aeler they are sealed in the Deep Vaults; in Thepyrgos, licensed and monitored; in Aelaerem, shunned for violating hospitality. Even among the free-minded Aelinnel they are treated with caution, for a mind that needs no Patron answers to no law.

Their power is attrition. They pay not in Obligation but in themselves — every thought bent, every future glimpsed, every object moved by will alone leaves a hairline crack in the vessel. They are slow to burn and slow to recover, patient as glaciers, and just as capable of crushing what stands in their path.

Roleplay Hooks
  • You have seen your own death in a precognitive vision — banal, a fall from a horse three decades hence. You cannot stop checking your saddle straps.
  • You once read the mind of a lover and found exactly what you feared. You have never trusted anyone since.
  • The Synod has a file on you that you were never meant to see. They do not yet know you are a psion. The game of cat and mouse sustains you.

The Summoner — The Door-Opener

"I do not command the dead. I do not command the fey, the demons, the angels, or the things that wear the shapes of forgotten gods. I ask. I pay the price. And sometimes, when the contract is fair, they answer."

Summoners are diplomats of the damned and the blessed alike, opening doors and hoping to close them before something follows through. Understand: all things beyond the veil are spirits — the dead who cling to memory, the fey who trade in stolen time, the animistic forces that sleep in stone and storm, the angels of judgment, the demons hungry at the edge of sin. All speak the language of contract.

A Summoner of Aeler awakens the indigenous memories of stone. A Summoner of Ykrul drums the ancestors from the earth. A Summoner of Zakov swallows cursed coins and calls the drowned. The church-burned heretic who calls an angel is still a Summoner; so is the desert madman who whispers to the storm.

The difference is the Leash — the spiritual strain of keeping an Outsider in the world. The dead want to return to death; the fey want to return to the wild; the demons want to stay, and make room by pushing you out. When the Leash fills, the spirit acts once according to its nature — often destructively — then departs, turns hostile, or simply takes what it was promised and leaves you holding the empty air.

Roleplay Hooks
  • You once called a spirit that still follows you — not hostile, just curious, watching from reflections and shadows.
  • Your mentor disappeared during a summoning. You found their Symbol but not their body, and suspect they still walk the Ways Between.
  • A spirit you bound years ago now demands renegotiation, claiming the contract has a loophole. It is right. It is always right.

These are the Six Roads. They cross, they diverge, and sometimes they run parallel until the walker looks down and realizes they have been walking the same path all along. Choose yours. But choose knowing that the road changes the walker, and the price is never fully paid.

Patrons of the Gray Wanderer

"You wish to walk the road of power? Then listen well. The world is old, and older still are the voices beneath it. We call them Patrons, though they were never sworn to us. They are the great constants, the landlords of reality, and we are merely tenants who have learned to pick the locks." — The Gray Wanderer

Patrons are vast intelligences — not gods in the way the Everflame preaches, though some worship them as such. They are embodiments of concepts and forces older than the first stone. Each offers power, but always with cost: fatigue, scars upon fate, or a slow unweaving of one's own story.

A Patron does not care how you approach them. The Six Roads all lead to the same altars. A Runekeeper of Grimmir is his agent, his voice in the wild places. An Invoker of Grimmir rents his power for a season, a gambler betting on green growth. A Witch of Grimmir tends the threshold between field and forest, owing nothing but the breath she takes while crossing. All three draw from the same source. All three pay different prices.

This chapter presents fifteen Patrons chosen for utility and distinct themes, organized into five categories. Each entry includes lore, cultural variants, playing notes, suggested acts of service, a Patron's Gift (an at-will Imbuement), a Signature Talent, sample Rites, and GM guidance for intrusions.

"The Patron's Gift is what you get for showing up. The Signature Talent is what you earn for staying. One is a loan; the other is an investment. Both come due." — The Gray Wanderer

The Wild — Patrons of Nature and Season

Grimmir — The Wild Speaker Nature Seasons Wilderness

"In the space between planted row and forest edge, where the first grain met the first acorn, Grimmir waits. He speaks in the rustle of leaves, the migration of birds, and the patient growth of ancient oaks."

Grimmir walks the threshold between cultivated lands and untamed wilderness. Neither wholly beast nor entirely human, he speaks in root and stone, seasons and sap.

"I planted an acorn on the grave of my enemy. Now I water it with my own tears. Grimmir does not ask for forgiveness. He asks for growth." — Brynhild, Hermit of the Thornwood

Cultural Variants: Aeler call him the Root-Father, patron of deep fungus and stone-lichen; Ykrul know him as a sky-wolf who herds the seasons; Aelaerem call him the Thorn-Father, keeper of hedgerow magic.

Playing a Follower: The Agent (Runekeeper) maintains the treaty between field and forest. The Gambler (Invoker) carries a sprig of rowan or a wolf's tooth; when she Cracks the Seal, the wild comes fast and hungry. The Threshold Keeper (Witch) is the hedge-mother, working in cup-mark stones and counter-timerwise knots.

Suggested Acts of Service: plant a tree or restore a damaged grove; kill a predator grown unnaturally bold; teach a villager to read a seasonal sign.

Patron's Gift (Imbuement): your Thiasos or hands glow with verdant energy — for one scene, +1 die to Lore and Nature, and melee strikes deal +1 Harm against creatures of corruption or blight.

Signature Talent — Wildshape Major, 8 XP (Prereq: Follower of Grimmir, Body 2+) — spend an action and mark 1 Fatigue to assume the form of a small beast for up to one scene: +2 dice to Stealth and Perception in natural terrain, but you cannot speak or use tools. Revert as a free action. Once per session.

Rites:

  • The Speaking Seed Low, 4 XP — commune with local plant life.

Free Casting — The Grammar of the Raw Weave

"The Six Roads are paved stone. Free casting is the mud between the cobbles, the river beneath the bridge, the air in the lung. It has no patron to catch you when you fall. It has no leash to pull you back. The risk is not in the casting — it is in the caster." — The Gray Wanderer

The Six Roads are mapped, signed, and toll-paid. But beneath them runs a current older than the first carved Rite, older than the first sworn Symbol, older perhaps than the Patrons themselves. The Aelinnel call it Contextual Recursion. The Thepyrgosi Synod calls it practical metaphysics. In the hedgerows and burned-out tenements, they call it simply the knack, the gift, or the curse.

It is Free Casting: the direct manipulation of the Weave through will, word, and gesture, unmediated by the covenants that govern the Six Roads. A Runekeeper channels her Patron's obligation; a Free Caster reaches into the raw stuff of reality and persuades it to change. The difference between a hearth-witch burning her fingers lighting a candle with a thought and a Thepyrgosi magnus collapsing a wall with a gesture is not the kind of power, but the discipline of the mind that wields it.

The Thepyrgosi Lament — On Unsanctioned Magi

"They call it 'free casting.' I call it a lit match held to a powder keg. No Codex. No Symbol. No Patron to rein them in. Just raw will and a prayer that the Backlash doesn't eat them — or the village." — Inquisitor Voss, Thepyrgos Witch-Hunter Commissariat

Among the tower-clad streets of Thepyrgos, where the Synod's bells measure law in chimes, the free caster is the most feared and hunted of all magi — not because they are evil, though some are, but because they are unaccountable. A Runekeeper's power is chained to a Codex and a Thiasos; if she burns a district, the Patron's name is written in the ash, and the weight can be called. A free caster leaves no receipt. The Backlash is theirs alone to bear, and when they fail, the Weave lashes out without theme or mercy. Even so, the risk varies: the Inquisition does not hunt the University don practicing in a sealed, warded laboratory — they hunt the gutter-mage who learned three tags from a dying grandmother and now improvises storms to pay his rent. Both are free casters. The Weave does not distinguish, but the Synod must.

The TAGS Philosophy

Free casting is built from TAGS: the verbs, nouns, and adjectives of the Weave's hidden grammar. Intent (what you wish to do) + Target (what you wish to affect) + Tags (the qualities you impose). Base Difficulty equals 1 plus the number of TAGS employed. Dangerous TAGS — those manipulating space, creation, or fate itself — add +2 to the DV instead of +1.

Casting roll: Attribute + Arcana versus the DV. Each 1 rolled generates a Story Beat (SB), representing the Weave's resistance to being forced into shape.

Backlash — The Weave's Revenge

Without a Patron to absorb the strain, without a Thiasos to bleed off the excess, the Free Caster's failures strike the caster directly. Severity depends not on the spell but on the cleanliness of the casting — a scholar's precise ritual may suffer only a nosebleed, while a desperate improvisation may shatter bones.

Minor (1–2 SB)

  • -1 die on next roll

  • GM gains +1 SB for a thematic twist

The Three Faces of the Free Caster

Not all unsanctioned magi are madmen or desperate gutter-mages. Risk varies with the practitioner. The Thepyrgosi Scholar practices "practical metaphysics" in a salt-ringed laboratory, calculating probabilities and accepting Backlash as a cost of research — predictable, expensive, and boring, but safe relative to the alternatives. The Hedge-Witch knows six reliable combinations passed down through her family, has never cast with more than three TAGS, and sleeps soundly; her risk is low because her ambition is low. The Prodigy was born knowing the grammar, improvising tags he was never taught, pulling effects from the air like a musician humming a new tune. He is the most dangerous of all — not from malice, but because he does not know which combinations are forbidden until he tries them, and the Weave remembers his mistakes longer than his successes.

Summoning — The Art of the Opened Door

"I have shaken hands with the drowned, signed contracts in cobweb ink with things that wear my grandfather's face, and once — only once — borrowed a cup of sugar from a demon. The door is the easy part. It is the conversation that follows that will unmake you." — The Gray Wanderer

Of the Six Roads, Summoning is the one most often mistaken for simple thaumaturgy. The uninitiated see only the salt circle, the smoking thurible, the muttered name, and assume the matter is one of command — of a master issuing orders to a slave. This is the delusion that gets novices killed. Summoning is not command; it is diplomacy with teeth. It is the art of opening a door into a crowded room and convincing something older than stone to step through on terms that do not end with your skin being hung out to dry.

Summoners do not carry Obligation as the Runekeepers do, nor do they suffer the Cantor's creeping Corruption. Their currency is simpler and more terrible: negotiation. Every spirit has a price — blood, memory, silence, a name, a promise — and the Summoner must weigh whether the service rendered is worth the cost exacted. Some cultures approach this as a respectful parley between equals; others as domination, the spirit bound by chains of contract or force of will. Both paths are valid. Both are dangerous. The spirit remembers which hand held the leash, and spirits have long memories.

What Passes Through the Door

To speak of spirits is to speak of everything that is not quite matter. The dead who cling to their final moments; the Neighbors who live in the gaps between maps; the animistic intelligences that wake in deep stone and old trees; the beings that theologians call angels and demons; even the conceptual entities, personifications of plague or fortune or the space between thoughts — all are spirits.

  • The Indigenous (Aeler): not summoned but awakened — native to stone, root, deep places. They rise from here rather than come from elsewhere.
  • The Ancestral (Ykrul): the honored dead, blood-bound to clan and drum, returning with counsel or wrath.
  • The Drowned (Zakov, Linn): those who died in water, vengeance, or betrayal, now liquid and hungry.
  • The Neighbors (Lethai, Aelaerem): the fair folk and hedgerow-dwellers who were here before the current age.
  • The Outsiders: things from the spaces between stars, thoughts, and seconds, that strain the world's architecture merely by existing.

The Two Philosophies

The Path of Negotiation — prevalent among the Linn, the Aelaerem, and the free ports of Zakov — treats the spirit as a guest. The Summoner offers hospitality (salt, bread, a name spoken in trust); the spirit offers service in return. The relationship is ongoing: a Linn tide-witch knows her selkie by name, with history and inside jokes. When the Leash fills, the spirit might simply depart in a huff, or deliver a warning wrapped in riddles. The danger is intimacy — treat with a spirit long enough and you may find yourself defending its interests against your own kind.

The Path of Domination — favored in Ecktoria, the Aeler Deep Vaults, and the martial traditions of Viterra — views spirits as resources, dangerous but manageable through strength of will, precise geometry, and ironclad contract. Here, the Leash is a choke-chain, and when it fills, the spirit lashes out with hatred. The danger is rebellion: a bound spirit waits for the circle to waver, the name to be misspoken, the master's attention to lapse, then collects years of deferred rage in a single bloody instant.

Cultural Traditions

Aeler — The Stone-Whisperers

Summoning is employment, not eviction. The spirits of stone are indigenous — they sleep, not travel. The Rite of the Carved Contract inscribes terms onto slate with diamond dust, then taps the stone with a bell hammer; if the stone warms, the spirit wakes to negotiate. Most Aeler Summoners are also Runekeepers, treating spirits as mineral resources with legal rights. Ask a vein-spirit to "find gold" and it may collapse the mountain to bring you a single nugget. Convention: domination through legal precedent, bound by meticulously worded contract.

Ykrul — The Bone-Singers

The steppe orcs summon the dead and animal-totems through rhythm. The drum is the focus, made of the specific creature or ancestor being called — a Wolf-Ancestor summons on a drum of wolf-skin stretched over bone. The spirit arrives proud and expects respect; the Summoner must match its heartbeat in the drumming to prove kinship. Failure results in a spirit that hunts the caller. Convention: negotiation through kinship and respect — you cannot dominate an ancestor, only remind them of their obligation to blood.

Linn — The Tide-Riders

The northmen bind sea-spirits — selkies, drowned captains, storm-wardens — with the Rite of the Nine Knots, a hemp rope tied to represent a storm surge's waves. The spirit is asked, not commanded, and payment is usually a service to the sea. The relationship is covenantal; a Linn Summoner often keeps the same spirit for decades. Convention: pure negotiation — to dominate a sea-spirit is to invite the storm, and the Linn consider such practice barbaric.

Zakov — The Salt-Binders

The pirate-fleets specialize in the vengeful dead — mutineers, betrayed lovers, drowned captains. Fathom-Callers swallow blood coins (silver that paid for murder), marking the body as a vessel; the spirit is lured up through the salt water in the Summoner's own lungs. Dangerous, intimate work; the spirits are angry, confused, and often malicious. Convention: domination through temptation and binding, holding the spirit by the weight of its own unfinished business.

Lethai — The Courtesy of Neighbors

The elves invite spirits over rather than summon them, leaving a place at the table and a cup of wine poured and left untouched. The Fair Folk who arrive are capricious, powerful, and easily offended; negotiation runs through etiquette — the angle of a bow, the precise number of times a bell is rung (never nine). Convention: negotiation through absolute adherence to archaic courtesy; the only leash is mutual respect, and that leash is made of glass.

Viterra — The Legal Binding

The hedge-wrights approach Summoning as property law, drafting Crown-enforceable contracts that bind spirits as indentured servants — a ghost may be bound to a threshold for nine generations to resolve a boundary dispute. Spirits are classified by the College of Scriveners: Class I (ancestral, manageable), Class III (animistic, unpredictable), Class VII (demonic, forbidden without license). Convention: bureaucratic domination, enforced by the state's metaphysical weight.

Ecktoria — The Chained and the Burning

The Inquisition practices the most brutal domination: summoning infernal entities for interrogation and execution. The Chain-Lanterns trap spirits in blessed iron and silver mirrors, forcing them to witness their own sins until they agree to serve. The spirits are prisoners, not guests, and each summoning is an act of spiritual warfare — the Leash here is literal chains of inscribed metal that burn the spirit's essence. Convention: violent domination; the Inquisition accepts collateral damage as holy necessity.

The Leash and the Bargain

Whether through negotiation or domination, the Summoner must maintain the Leash — the spiritual strain of keeping an Outsider in the material world. The Leash is not a moral debt but a tension, a rope pulled tight between summoner and summoned. Each time the spirit acts against its nature, takes Harm, or crosses a ward, the Leash ticks tighter.

When the Leash fills, the spirit acts once according to its essential nature — often destructively, always truthfully — then departs. If treated with respect, it may return when called again. If dominated, it may hunt the summoner through dreams until the price is paid in blood.

The Pact-Bound Spirit

Some Summoners establish long-term relationships with specific spirits, negotiating Pacts — standing agreements that reduce or eliminate the need for the Leash during routine service. A Pact might have a spirit guard the summoner's home in exchange for a monthly offering of honey and blood. While the spirit works within the Pact's terms, the Leash does not tick — but Pacts are living contracts, and violating the terms means the spirit is owed triple in return.

Sample Spirits

The Vein-Serpent (Aeler)

Nature: Indigenous, mineral, slow-witted. Appearance: a serpent of polished copper and malachite, slithering through stone as if it were water. Services: locates ore veins, stabilizes tunnels, collapses enemy fortifications. Price: a promise to maintain the stone's stability — an ounce of mortar laid for every ounce of ore taken. If Dominated: twists the stone, opening sinkholes underfoot. If Respected: warns of earthquakes three days in advance, humming through the bedrock.

Services: tracking any terrain, guarding camps, teaching forgotten battle-songs. Price: the summoner must uphold clan honor; cowardice breaks the bond immediately. If Dominated: hunts the summoner's pack-mates first, testing their worth through blood. If Respected: grants dreams of the future in the language of scent and instinct.

The Selkie-Maid (Linn)

Nature: Sea-spirit, mournful, capricious. Appearance: a seal that sheds its skin to become a woman with hair like kelp and eyes like storm clouds. Services: navigation through impossible storms, drowning enemies with summoned currents, retrieving objects lost to the deep. Price: a tear cried into the sea, or a memory of happiness sacrificed to the salt. If Dominated: leads ships onto rocks in clear weather, humming lullabies that deafen the crew. If Respected: brings word of currents and fish schools, and may save a drowning man on a whim.

The Drowned Mutineer (Zakov)

Nature: Vengeful dead, confused, bitter. Appearance: a bloated figure in rotting finery, ringed by salt-crusted coins. Services: sabotage of ships, whispering secrets into sleeping ears, cursing cargo to spoil. Price: a promise of vengeance against the living, carried as the summoner's own grudge. If Dominated: turns on the summoner the moment the circle wavers, drowning them in their own lungs. If Respected: serves with sullen efficiency, though other drowned things follow in its wake.

The Wanderer's Warning

Do not mistake the spirit for a tool. The Aeler think they do, treating their vein-serpents as mining equipment, but I have seen those serpents wait centuries for a tunnel to collapse on a foreman who spoke slightingly of stone. The Ecktorian Inquisition thinks it dominates the infernal, but I have watched a "bound" demon whisper seven words to an Inquisitor that made him question his faith for the rest of his life. The Leash is not a guarantee. It is a courtesy extended by the spirit while it finds your measure. Negotiate honestly. Dominate absolutely, if you must, but never sleep with both eyes closed. When the summoning ends, the salt swept up and the circle broken, the spirit remains. It remembers your face. It remembers your terms. And if you have treated it poorly, it will find you when your own Leash is frayed, and it will collect its due with interest.

The Cantor's Voice — Echoes and Entropy

"I have seen a woman sing a storm into stillness, and a man hum a king into madness. The voice is the oldest door, and the Cantor is the one who knocks without asking who is home." — The Gray Wanderer

If the Runekeeper is the sworn knight and the Witch the quiet architect, the Cantor is the wild card — the burning letter sent without a seal. They are magical bards, storm-singers, hymn-leaders who borrow power not by covenant or ledger, but by resonance. They do not swear to Patrons; they echo them, shaping their throats into instruments of divine will, knowing full well that the throat is flesh, and flesh warps under pressure.

Do not mistake the Cantor for a mere entertainer. In Aelaerem they are the Hearth-Mothers who sing the hollows to sleep; in Linn, the Storm-Callers whose shanties calm or summon the tide; in Zakov, the Siren-Priests of the Salt-Queen, whose songs bind sailors to pacts deeper than any written contract. Like the Runekeeper, the Cantor wears many masks depending on the Patron they echo — a singer of Palinode becomes a prophet of rapture; a devotee of Thrysos becomes a lord of revels; a chanter of the Silent Choir whispers truths that dismantle empires.

The Price of the Song — Corruption as Metamorphosis

Where the Runekeeper pays in Obligation and the Invoker in Condition, the Cantor pays in Corruption — not a moral stain but a physical one, a transformation undergone rather than a debt settled. The voice that sings too often to the storm begins to carry thunder in its timbre; the hands that conduct too many hymns of Isoka begin to shed skin like serpents; the eyes that reflect too deeply on the Ninth begin to see angles that do not exist. This is the inevitable consequence of intimacy — the Cantor opens their mouth and lets the power pass through, and some of it always remains, lodged in the teeth, the lungs, the marrow.

Most Cantors push their songs to immediate effect until the Corruption timer fills, and then comes the bloom: a permanent alteration, neither wholly blessing nor curse, granted by the last Patron whose Rite they performed. And then, often, comes the end.

The Three Fates of the Cantor

Cantors are often outcasts because their power is public, loud, and inherently destabilizing. Their story tends toward one of three endings.

Persecution — The Cantor sings a truth the local theocracy does not wish to hear, or summons a storm that ruins the harvest, or simply draws too large a crowd. The Everflame names them heretic; the Chain-Lanterns hunt them as unlicensed magi. The pyre is the common end for the public singer who cannot, or will not, silence themselves.
Madness — The voice becomes too heavy, the Ninth's harmonics ringing constantly. The Cantor forgets which words are theirs and which are the Patron's, singing until their throat bleeds or walking into the sea following a voice only they can hear. Many a lighthouse keeper on the Kahfagian coast is a Cantor who chose the ocean over the asylum.
The Cult — The rare Cantor who masters their Corruption often finds themselves at the head of a following — high priests not by ordination, but by attraction. They command desire rather than armies, and to follow such a Cantor is to risk your own identity in their song.

The Patrons of Performance

Any Patron might answer a Cantor's song, but certain powers are drawn to the voice like moths to flame — the patrons of ecstasy, revelation, the mask, and the encore.

Palinode — Queen of Encores

Once a road-cantor who died between feasts with a chorus unfinished, Palinode refused to let the refrain end. She is the promise that the song never dies, the encore that becomes eternal — a domain of Performance, Rapture, and the terrible beauty of the unending refrain. She arrives where crowds lean forward and makers hold their breath, tempting with relief from solitude: an audience that always leans closer, a song that never dies, a bond that feels like family even as it devours you.

Playing a Cantor of Palinode: you seek the perfect performance, the moment the boundary between artist and art dissolves. Your rites grant power through music, dance, and the manipulation of crowd emotion. You are never alone, but may lose yourself in the crowd's embrace.

Thrysos — King of Revels

Where torches flare and cups spill, where dancers stamp until dawn, Thrysos strides crowned with ivy and horn — both host and beast, a patron of license who laughs at law. He promises freedom without end: no oaths, no chains, only song and cup and fire. Yet those who drink too deeply wake to find they serve not joy, but appetite.

Playing a Cantor of Thrysos: you are lord of the feast and lord of the revel. Your magic is infectious, turning quiet gatherings into riots of emotion and physical transformation. You offer liberation, but your price is always the self-control of those who accept your cup.

Singing the Rites — Mechanics of the Cantor

Cantors do not invoke Rites with symbols or codices. They sing them, requiring the Cantor's Path talent (Major, 8 XP), which grants access to Low Rites performed as Songs.

The Performance: roll Lore + Performance against the Rite's DV. A Song requires one action to begin, resolving at the start of your next turn unless you Push It — accelerating to immediate effect at the cost of marking 1 Fatigue and advancing your Corruption timer.

The Corruption Timer: you possess a personal Corruption Timer with segments equal to your Spirit rating. Mark Corruption when you Push a Song to resolve immediately, perform a Resonant Rite, or the GM spends a Story Beat involving your occult activities. When the Timer fills, you immediately gain a thematic boon and drawback from the last Patron whose Rite you performed; your followers and familiars also gain a trait of the same corruption. The Timer resets to zero, but cannot go below your current Tier − 1.

The Repertoire: unlike a Runekeeper's fixed Codex, a Cantor's Repertoire is fluid. Track a Repertoire Timer [6]; mark a segment for each unique Song learned. At 2 segments, reduce the base DV of Songs by 1 (minimum 2). At 4, gain +1 die to Performance. At 6, learn one Standard Rite as a Song (temporary; requires ongoing practice to retain).

The High Cantor: Tier II+ characters may take the High Cantor prestige talent (18 XP), weaving Standard Rites into instant, powerful Songs. Each such casting marks Corruption, but the effects are immediate and devastating.

Embracing Corruption — The Art of Blooming

"You fear the feathers in your hair? Child, I have known Cantors who plucked them out and wept. I have known others who let them grow, who preened, who flew. The difference is not in the Patron's touch — it is in the courage to be touched." — The Gray Wanderer

Most Cantors fear the bloom, seeing the Corruption Timer as a countdown to obsolescence. They are wrong. There exists a philosophy among the older voices — embraced corruption — that treats the metamorphosis as ascension rather than accident, trading mundane flesh for a vessel more suited to the song. When the feather grows, you do not pluck it; you oil it.

Mechanically, when you voluntarily fill your Corruption Timer through deliberate, ritual Resonant Rites (not desperate Pushing), you may choose your corruption trait from your Patron's Corruption Table rather than rolling randomly — but you must embody the trait physically and socially.

The Threshold of Ascension: a door opens only when the Corruption Timer has filled seven times. Those who survive that long and return speak of the Second Voice — not the Patron's echo, but their own, externalized, walking beside them. The Embraced call this the Fugal Self, granting +1 die to all Performance rolls, but requiring the Cantor to negotiate with their Corruption for control of their body each dawn; on failure, the Corruption acts through them for a scene.

Talent — Embraced Corruption Major, 12 XP (Prereq: Cantor's Path, Tier II+)

When you voluntarily fill your Corruption Timer through Resonant Rites, choose your corruption trait rather than rolling randomly (and embody it). You may Push Songs without marking Fatigue once per session, accepting the Corruption mark as the only cost. Once you have filled your Corruption Timer seven times cumulatively, you develop the Fugal Self: +1 die to all Performance rolls, but each morning test Spirit + Endurance (DV 3) or your Corruption controls your body for the first scene of the day.

Sing if you must. Bloom if you dare. But remember: flowers open to be seen, and to be seen is to be picked.

The Invoker's Ledger — On Bargaining Without Bondage

"I do not kneel. I sign. I do not pray. I calculate. The Patron receives my offering — a Symbol polished, a contract whispered — and in exchange, I am permitted to borrow the flame. But make no mistake: when the covenant closes, the weight is mine alone." — The Gray Wanderer

There exists a breed of magus who treats with power not as a supplicant, but as a factor: the Invokers, natural philosophers, alienists, and chancellors of the unseen who operate at the margins of the great houses and the edge of the law. Where the Runekeeper is a servant sworn to a single lord, the Invoker is a free agent — a gambler who has bet their soul on their own ability to read the fine print.

In the universities of Thepyrgos and the scriptoria of Ecktoria, they call themselves Natural Philosophers, treating magic as a mechanical science to be catalogued. In the asylums and charnel houses, they are Alienists, bargaining with the voices in the walls not with holy water but with contracts written in iron gall ink. An Invoker does not ask what a Patron wants; they determine what a Patron lacks.

The Symbol as Contract

The Invoker's power flows through the Symbol: a ring, a seal, a blackened coin, or a bound sheaf of papers serving as the physical locus of a bargain. Unlike the Runekeeper's Codex, a record of service, the Symbol is a contract made tangible, representing a specific obligation owed to a specific Patron. An Invoker may carry multiple Symbols, but power is jealous — each additional Symbol creates Cross-Resonance, friction that makes Rites harder to invoke and obligations harder to track. Most Invokers limit themselves to four Symbols; beyond six, the ledger becomes unmanageable.

In their natural state, Invokers are slow and respectful, spending Significant Time on geometric calculation and the careful polishing of the Symbol — each such working increases Obligation by one segment. But when the knife is at the throat, the Invoker performs the Crack the Seal: invoking the Rite instantly, without preparation, as an emergency loan. Obligation increases by two or three segments immediately, and the Symbol may become Compromised — cracked, blackened, or weeping — until ritually cleansed.

Patrons of the Invoker

Invokers gravitate toward Patrons who understand the language of exchange, debt, and hidden clauses.

The Confessor Beneath the Bell — Burden & Exchange

"Speak, and be freed. But understand — what you cast off, I will bear. And what I bear, you will one day inherit."

The Confessor waits in places where guilt gathers, under church spires and in confessionals long abandoned. He does not judge or absolve — he takes. Each confession is a transaction: your burden for his silence.

Strategy: you are the party's emotional anchor and psychological investigator. Your Rites excel at absorbing the harm of others, extracting truth through burden-sharing, and binding agreements under the weight of shared secrets.

Suggested Acts of Service: absorb a secret that would ruin another and carry it a full season without speaking it; offer a formal confession in a place of power, naming a sin that costs you social standing; take on another's physical illness or madness for a day and a night.

Patron's Gift (Imbuement): your presence feels like the hush after a bell toll — for one scene, +1 die to Command and Insight, and you may transfer one Condition or 1 Fatigue from an ally to yourself as a free action.

Alternative Gift — The Silent Ledger: once per session, place a secret obligation on a target by naming a truth they have never spoken aloud; until they confess it, they suffer -1 die to deception or social maneuvering.

Rites:

  • Burden Transference Low, 5 XP — transfer one Condition, Fatigue, or 1 Harm from another target to yourself.

GM Guidance — Intrusions: a secret you confessed years ago appears written in ash on your doorstep; you manifest the physical symptoms of a disease you once absorbed for another; a bell rings in a soundproofed room — the Confessor is listening, and finds your accounts out of balance.

Morag the Hag — Weaver of Hidden Costs

"Yes, yes—the moon trembled, the road wept, and you alone were clever enough to want something. Drink your tea. Then tell me who is expected to suffer for your happy ending."

Morag receives petitioners at crossroads because people become honest when every direction costs them something. She offers what they actually need, often more plainly than they can bear, and asks the question their grand speeches avoid: if this wish is granted, whose work, safety, love, or future will make it possible? She enjoys ceremony, especially when she can puncture it.

Strategy: you are the ultimate pragmatist, the dealer who always takes a cut. Your Rites grant immediate benefits with deferred costs; you excel at negotiation, finding the loophole, and ensuring every favor creates a weight to be collected later.

Suggested Acts of Service: broker a trade giving away something of seemingly lesser value that proves crucial later; accept a gift from a stranger without asking the price, then discover what you surrendered; mend a broken oath by weaving a new, more binding one.

Patron's Gift (Imbuement): your touch leaves a faint scent of hawthorn and old coins — for one scene, +1 die to Sway and Deception, and any bargain you strike creates a Promise Timer [4] toward a favor you may call in later.

Alternative Gift — The Crooked Thread: once per session, declare that a target owes you a weight from a forgotten interaction; they test Spirit + Endurance (DV 4) or accept the truth of the debt and owe you one significant service or truth.

Rites:

  • Borrowed Luck Low, 5 XP — share in another's fresh success for +2 dice on one roll, but the original bearer suffers misfortune later.
  • The Unfinished Promise Low, 4 XP — frame a bargain with an escape clause only you understand.
  • The Speaking Knot Standard, 8 XP — bind a promise with faerie knots; both parties gain +1 die keeping the bargain, breaking it escalates consequences (Harm 1, then 2, then Curse).
  • The Threefold Exchange Standard, 7 XP — broker a trade where each party yields hidden value; you claim the difference as Morag's tithe.
  • The Crossroads Court Advanced, 14 XP, Obligation 8 — convene Morag's court at a true crossroads at twilight; all present must bargain, each pact granting a marked boon and hiding a cost that ripens at the worst time.

GM Guidance — Intrusions: a deal you thought closed resurfaces with a clause in a language you suddenly understand; the price of a past favor comes due — a finger, a memory, a firstborn's laughter; you are compelled to grant a favor to an enemy, bound by a thread tied in your sleep.

Roleplay Hooks for the Invoker
  • You carry four Symbols, but one was stolen by a former student who now uses your patron's power against you — the weight still accrues to your ledger.
  • You have never met your Patron; you found the Symbol in a ruin. Does the Patron even know you exist? The letters you burn suggest they are learning.
  • A rival Invoker's syndicate audits you, claiming your Cross-Resonance interpretations are heretical, intending to collect your Symbols by force.

The Invoker walks the tightrope between power and perdition — proof that magic need not be served with devotion, only with precision. But remember: the Patron is not your friend. They are your creditor. And when the ledger finally balances, the interest is always paid in flesh.

The Triangular Trap & The Tidal War — On Carrying Conflicting Obligations

To carry one Symbol is to hold a candle. To carry five is to juggle torches in a powder magazine. But to carry Symbols of Livaea, Maelstraeus, and Mor'iraath — or to meddle with both Raéyn and Khemesh — is to strike sparks against the flint of your own ribcage.

The Triangular Trap: Livaea binds through desire, making you want the weight. Maelstraeus binds through accounting, making the weight exact. Mor'iraath offers the illusion of cancellation — but he does not erase the record, he erases the signatory. The weight remains, held by a void that cannot be paid, and the Obligation transfers to your bloodline as a curse of erasure. Likewise, using Livaea's seduction to soften Maelstraeus's terms is like sweetening poison with honey; you will find yourself owing both a firstborn's laughter and a firstborn's weight in silver, simultaneously.

The Tidal War: never invoke Raéyn and Khemesh in proximity. Raéyn is the surface — trade wind, fair tide, safe passage. Khemesh is the depth — the trench without light. They are bound by blood and hatred. To invoke Raéyn's blessing on a vessel while carrying a weight to Khemesh invites a rogue wave that crashes with a silence tasting of iron. If you must deal with the Sea Court, choose one: the Tidemark or the Trench. Never both.

The Rule: if your Patrons' domains touch, your soul is the fault line. Keep them separated by fire, salt, and silence.

The Quiet Work — On Witchcraft and the Threshold

"The thread that comforts is the same that binds. The knot that saves is also the snare. In the space between the candle and the shadow, that is where we work." — Inaea, Angel of the Spider

Not every woman with a herb garden is a witch, though the villagers may call her so. Not every man who sings to the storm is a witch, though the sailors fear him. The true witches — the ones discussed here — are rarer and more terrible. They understand that magic is not merely a weight to be carried nor a power to be stolen, but a fitting: the art of finding where the world already wishes to move and giving it the precise nudge that sets it irrevocably in motion.

Hedge-witches there are in plenty — the Breath-Wardens of Aeler, the Hedge-Wrights of Viterra, the Bone-Singers of Ykrul — good folk, mostly, who mend fences that are more than fences and keep milk from souring with a word and a touch. But their craft is folk-craft, learned by rote and grandmother's whisper. The true witch, the threshold worker, learns by the Cord, the Thorn, and the Silent Ninth. They know that a doorway is not an absence but a presence, that a promise is a physical weight, and that to erase a name from memory is to rewrite the stone beneath it. They serve six patrons, though "serve" is too weak a word — they embody them.

The Six Weavers

Where the Runekeeper swears an oath and the Invoker bargains at arm's length, the witch incorporates. Her power is intimate, cellular, corrupting in the old sense — not rotten, but changed. The patron's will flows through her like milk through a jug, leaving the vessel altered.

Morag the Hag — The Price Unspoken

Morag meets people where roads divide and excuses become choices. Her witches broker promises in milk, slate, gossip, hospitality, and whatever else the parties will later pretend was not binding. They know that a gift may be paid for a generation afterward, usually by someone absent when the terms were praised. Morag calls flowery talk about destiny “tablecloth laid over a knife.” Her law of hidden costs asks who holds the knife, who receives the feast, and whether a descendant owes anything merely because an ancestor agreed.

The High Mother — The Cord

A coven claims a patron older than Morag, or perhaps younger — time runs strangely in the House Without Doors. They call her the High Mother, Weaver of the Third Spindle, sustained by sacrifice so subtle it is indistinguishable from care. The Daughters of the Cord are midwives and deathbed nurses who hold your hand when you are born and when you die, and between those moments may take your shadow, your milk-teeth, or your grief, woven into their flesh-codices. They need no grimoire — their skin is the page. They keep the Hollowed: heartless children with milk-white eyes who witness every working. Their magic is Flesh-Weaving: a Daughter can store Fatigue as knots beneath her skin, appearing unharmed while her body weeps blood-thread, or pass disease from host to host by unweaving the knots of obligation. Their blessing is mercy; their curse is that mercy always has a price, paid in flesh or memory.

Inaea — Mercy and the Web

Inaea is the hearth-mother, keeper of continuity, the spider at the center of the web. Where Morag demands payment, Inaea offers connection. Her witches bind families with invisible threads, ensure oaths hold, and heal the cracks in the world's memory. The witch of Inaea knows that the thread that comforts is the same that binds: she can lay a line of sanctuary across a threshold — no violence may cross it while the thread holds, but the thread remembers every blow it deflects, and when it snaps, the weight comes due.

Isoka — The Serpent's Shedding

Isoka is change made manifest, the venom that burns away the old self to make room for the new. Her witches are shapeshifters in the original sense — not beasts, but souls who have learned to molt their identities like snakeskin. A witch of Isoka can walk into a room as a stranger and leave as your sister, the shift so seamless that reality itself accepts the change. She can cure a disease by forcing the patient to become someone who never had it, though the former self is lost forever.

Ikasha — She Who Sleeps in Silence

Ikasha is the patron of the threshold itself, of the space between what is said and what is meant. Her witches move unseen not by bending light but by occupying the absence — the Ninth space, the gap between numbers, the forgotten step on the stair. To call upon Ikasha is to court erasure; her witches can remove a name from a record, a memory, from history itself, leaving only a gap that aches like a missing tooth.

The Thorns of Malachai — The Gift That Bites

Malachai is the Chained Angel, the Cruel Messenger, the one who answers when desperation has made tomorrow feel theoretical. Her witches include the cursed, the addicted, and those who learned to turn their own fractures into leverage. She states the truth that matters, as she told Sister Aris that the Cord transfers rather than sells. Then she dwells on the relief: warm blood, closing eyes, a child who can finally say Mama. The petitioner supplies the softer question. Malachai answers it truthfully and lets the harder one go unasked.

The Thorn's Bargain

A witch of Malachai does not ask "What will this cost?" She asks "Who will pay the interest?"

Signature Rite — The Leashed Gift Standard, 7 XP — grant a target +2 dice on their next roll. They gain a Debt Timer [4]; when it fills, Malachai collects a memory, a name, or a service. The witch cannot refuse the collection, only redirect it to another target.

Identity Strain tracks cumulative erosion from self-touching magic. Promise Timers and Curses as Architecture

Many workings create obligations, represented by a Promise Timer (typically 4 or 6 segments): at 0, a debt is owed; at 1–3, it is acknowledged but unpaid; when full, the price comes due socially, spiritually, or narratively. Mark the timer when a promise is ignored, deferred, or disrespected.

A witch does not curse with a word and a gesture. She edits the memory of the world, inserting a rule into the operating system of reality: "Until a bell is rung in your true name, lamps forget to light for you." A curse has an anatomy: The Rule (what changes, a Veil-edit bound to a Named anchor), The Timer (Recursion [6], ticking when the victim reinforces the story), The Counter (what undoes it), and The SB Menu (complications the GM may spend SB on).

A Secret Chapter Found in the Margins

Content Warning: this chapter contains themes of body horror, coercion, loss of identity, and non-consensual sacrifice. It is presented as an in-world artifact; reader discretion is advised.
"The margins of this grimoire were blank when I received it. The writing appeared overnight — in my own hand, though I never wrote a word. The ink smells of milk and iron." — The Gray Wanderer, marginal note

The following pages were found tucked between the leaves of the chapter on Cantors, pressed flat as a pressed flower. The paper is soft and fibrous; the ink has a reddish-brown tint that flakes when touched. The handwriting shifts — sometimes neat, sometimes a trembling scrawl — as if penned by many hands over many years. Transcribed as faithfully as possible. — G.W.

Dear reader, you who turn these pages by lamplight, who think witchcraft is a matter of herbs and gentle knots and old women muttering over cup-mark stones — put the book down. Walk away. Because what follows is not craft. It is not magic. It is maintenance.

I am called Sister Deru, though that name died forty years ago, and the woman who bore it is woven into the Cord. I write this for no one — the Cord does not permit confession — and for everyone who will ever accept a warm meal from a stranger, a cool cloth on a fevered brow, a lullaby hummed beside a sleeping child. You think the Daughters of the Cord are midwives, nurses, hospice workers — the kindly women who sit with the dying. You are not wrong. We are also bait.

The Hollowed Are Not Familiars

Every Daughter receives a Hollowed — a child or animal, taken from a home that no longer remembers them, hollowed out by the High Mother's needle. The heart is removed, preserved in the Kettle, replaced with a bundle of gray thread and the ash of a forgotten name. The eyes are sewn open with silver wire. They watch, always. They witness every working, every whispered price, every tear shed at a deathbed. The Hollowed do not speak. They do not eat. They do not age. Sometimes, when a price comes due, they weep blood — warm, smelling of milk and iron — and the Daughter must collect it in a cup and pour it onto the Cord. You have seen them, in market squares, in church pews, standing in corners like forgotten coat racks. You did not notice. That is the miracle of the Chain: no one ever notices the help.

The Subtle Sacrifice Is Not a Choice

The elders teach that sacrifice is an offering freely given. They lie. Real power — the kind that stops a child's cough cold, that turns a husband's wandering eye back to the hearth, that makes a rival's crops wither while yours swell — requires taken things. Not given. Taken. A tooth from a sleeping child. A shadow snipped with silver scissors on a moonless night. A name whispered into a bowl of milk and poured down a well. The victim never knows; they simply wake one day a little less present, less remembered, less real. I have hollowed three children. I have stolen seven names. I have cut the shadows of nine men who beat their wives, and watched those men fade over months — first from ledgers, then from mirrors, then from the memories of their own mothers. The world is cleaner without them. And the Cord is richer.

The Dream of the High Mother

Once a year, on the night of the dark moon, the Daughters gather in the House Without Doors and dream. In the dream, we see the High Mother as she truly is — not a woman on a throne, but a vast, pulsing tangle of gray cord, stretching from floor to ceiling. Each thread is a memory, a sacrificed name, a price paid, humming with hunger and whispering the names of everyone we have ever touched. The High Mother does not speak. She weaves. And when she weaves a thread into the pattern, someone, somewhere, forgets something vital. That is the true purpose of the Chain — not to heal, not to bind, but to edit. We are the eraser marks on the world's memory, and the High Mother is the hand that holds the quill.

The Price of Kindness

You have accepted hospitality from a Daughter, perhaps without remembering it — a cup of tea, a bowl of broth, a blanket on a cold night. You think it was kindness. It was a hook. Every Daughter carries a spool of gray thread spun from the hair of the Hollowed; when she offers you food, she ties a thread around your wrist, invisible, weightless. When you accept, she ties the other end to the Cord. Now you are bound — not in a way you would notice, but the next time you face a choice, you will lean slightly toward the option that benefits the Chain. Enough small things, and a kingdom falls.

How to Unbind Yourself (If You Dare)

I write this knowing the High Mother will read it. But perhaps someone will see these words before they are erased.

  1. Find a threshold — a doorway, a riverbank, a crossroads at dawn. The thread is weakest where two things meet.
  2. Light a candle of pure beeswax. The High Mother's thread is spun from tallow; beeswax burns it away.
  3. Speak your own name three times, aloud — not the name your mother gave you, but the one you have kept secret. That is the name the thread has stolen.
  4. Drink a cup of salt water. Salt is memory. The water will taste like tears — that is the taste of the cord loosening.
  5. Do not look behind you. The Hollowed will be there, watching. If you meet its eyes, you will forget why you began.

If you succeed, you will feel the thread snap — a small, sharp pain behind your left ear. The Daughter who tied it will taste blood in her mouth for a day and a night. She will know you have broken free. She will not come after you — the Cord does not chase. It waits. And it remembers.

The Final Confession

I have written too much. My hand is shaking. The Hollowed in the corner of this room has not blinked in the time it has taken me to fill these pages. I know what comes next: tonight, the dream will take me, and in the morning I will remember nothing of what I have written. But maybe — just maybe — someone will find these words before the erasure. If that someone is you, do this: go home. Boil water. Salt it. Drink it. Speak your secret name into the steam. Light a beeswax candle. And never, ever accept soup from a Daughter of the Cord. The soup is always free. The price never is.

— Sister Deru, Weaver of the Third Spindle, Keeper of the Cord, bound by choice, damned by necessity.

The Wanderer's Confession

I have seen a witch of Inaea hold a dying city together with nothing but red thread and a song. I have seen a Daughter of the Cord take a plague into her flesh, storing the Fatigue of a hundred souls beneath her skin until she could unweave it into a goat. The Silent Ledger — Psionics and the Mind's Edge

"I have seen a man stop a drawn sword with his forehead. I have watched a woman read a sealed letter without breaking the wax. I have felt a child's hand on my shoulder when no child was there. And I still cannot tell you how any of it works. Magic? I understand magic. Covenants, Patrons, the weight of a broken vow. But this? This is something else entirely." — The Gray Wanderer

I met a monk at a crossroads inn, the sort where the floorboards are older than the kingdom. He wore the gray robes of a meditant from the Vhasian badlands, a man who had spent forty years learning to sit still and listen to the shape of his own thoughts. "You're a psion," I said. He smiled. "I am a tongue of the silent mind. But yes. You name it psionics. The Aeler call it deep accounting. The Ykrul call it weather-reading in the skull. The Lethai call it hearing without a bell." I asked how something so powerful could have no cost. He laughed. "No cost? Wanderer, you mistake the nature of the price. A Runekeeper owes a Patron. A Cantor owes their voice. A Witch owes the threshold. A psion owes themselves. Every thought you bend is a thought you cannot unthink. The cost is not external. It is attrition — the slow wearing away of the self." He tapped his temple. "This is the only ledger that matters. And it always comes due."

What Psionics Is (and What It Is Not)

Psionics is the Sixth Road — the path requiring no sworn oath, no stolen Symbol, no audience, no spirit's bargain. It is the disciplined mastery of the mind's hidden potentials, power turned so far inward it becomes a weapon, a shield, and a burden. Different cultures view psions with varying suspicion or fear, but rarely trust.

Psionics is not...
  • Magic. It invokes no Patrons, elements, or the Ninth. Anti-magic wards do not stop it — but mental silence does.
  • Innate. Despite requiring no external focus, psionics demands training and decades of meditative practice.
  • Safe. Mental Strain is real. Mental scars are permanent. The mind that bends reality bears the cracks of that bending.
Psionics is...
  • Internal. Power is measured by the Mind's Ledger, a capacity for sustained exertion recovering only with rest and solitude.
  • Subtle. Effects often have no visible component, making psions excellent investigators, infiltrators, and spies.
  • Consequential. Every psionic action generates Story Beats; the GM is encouraged to spend these on social complications.

The Mind's Ledger — Core Mechanics

Mental Strain represents your capacity for sustained psychic exertion, calculated as your Spirit (minimum 2). Mark it when you use psionic Arts, resist psychic intrusions, or push your mind beyond its limits. Mental Strain is separate from physical Fatigue — it does not clear when you take Harm; the mind does not reset with a wound, it only fractures further. When you cannot pay a Mental Strain cost, convert the unpaid point to +2 standard Fatigue or +1 Harm (Stress). If you ever exceed your Mental Strain capacity, immediately suffer +1 Harm (Stress) and set Mental Strain to your maximum; you cannot over-mark further that scene.

The Psionics skill (0–5) gates access to psionic Arts. You cannot use them without investing in this discipline. Each Art has a Key Attribute; using a non-key attribute imposes +1 DV or -1 die (GM's choice).

The Great Silence — A History Written in Scars

Two centuries past — perhaps three, perhaps ten, the records are as fractured as the minds that kept them — the world experienced what scholars now call The Great Silence: not a silence of sound, but of thought. For generations the doors of the mind were barred; what had once been natural gifts became rare talents requiring desperate training, and those who pushed too hard found their minds shattered like dropped porcelain. The Aeler sealed their psychic academies, calling it a "misalignment of the mental ledgers." The Ykrul spoke of the "breaking of the Kon'reh." The Lethai mourned the loss of context, for a mind without the proper keys is simply noise.

Resonance Scars remain: the Whispering Vaults of Aeler amplify Mental Strain but grant +1 Effect to telepathy; the Empty Circle of the Violet Steppe nullifies all psionics, a grave where the last conclave of psions attempted to end the Silence and instead created a permanent void; the Singing Stones of Lethai allow communication across vast distances, but only when tuned through proper ritual and relationship.

Awakening Cycles: since the Silence lifted, abilities return in waves. We are in the First Awakening (the last century and a half), marked by telepathy and telekinesis. The Second Awakening (four centuries past) brought precognition and biofeedback. The Third (six centuries gone) granted astral projection and empathic manipulation. No one knows what the Fourth will bring, nor what it will cost.

Cultural Faces of the Silent Art

Not all peoples fear the psion equally, but none trust them fully.

Aeler — The Deep Watchers

Psionics is treated as infrastructure, a utility to be maintained and contained. Psions are assigned to the Deep Vaults, their "breath-weights" monitored in bell-rings; ring the bell three times too fast and a Corrector arrives to audit the ledger. They practice Breath Accounting and specialize in Structural Sense and Resonance Mapping.

Lethai — The Context Keepers

Telepathy requires context keys — the proper season, relationship, and place. Without these, a Lethai psion reads only static. Their minds are saturated with memory and social webbing, making them hard to read (psions who try often leave mad) but requiring careful ritual for contact. They mark psions with resinous Mind-Gift tattoos; taken from context, they are deafened.

Ykrul — The Weather Readers

The orcs of the steppe read the future in the Kon'reh, the sacred geometry of probability. Their psions, called Riders of the Flow, read the shape of the future the way a cavalryman reads the land — tactical, immediate, bound to the weather. Storm conditions enhance telepathic range but increase Mental Strain costs; clear weather allows precise precognitive readings. They specialize in Herd Sense and Banner Communication.

Vilakari — The Information Brokers

In the free ports, psionics is commerce. The Vilakari run Thought Markets where memory and information trade like grain. Their psions are Surface Readers, adept at detecting deception in negotiations, and Memory Traders who temporarily share recollections for coin — but a Vilakari psion in a crowded market is overwhelmed by the noise of surface thoughts, and many fall to addiction, burning out their Mental Strain in weeks rather than years.

The Silent Orders — Fragments of a Scattered Tradition

No one knows where psionics came from. The Thepyrgos Synod has a standing, unclaimed bounty for evidence dated to the Great Silence. Scholars argue endlessly: some say the ancient psions chose silence as quarantine against something from the spaces between thoughts; others point to a struck harmonic balance, a weapon never found; a third camp whispers of a forgotten Patron whose domain is forgetting itself, and whose true name was spoken once, then never again; the Kuvani say the old psions walked into the Empty Circle and are still walking, and will return when the Silence ends; a skeptical Thepyrgos clerk suggests they simply exhausted themselves, having no patrons, codices, or symbols to lean on. No one knows. The Silence remains silent.

Scattered fragments of that ancient culture survive as small, secretive orders of meditants who bargain with themselves rather than with Patrons.

The Order of the Unstruck Bell (Aelerian Deep Vaults)

These monks dwell in sealed galleries beneath the oldest Aeler holds, maintaining "quiet zones" with null-bells that ring only in the mind. Null-Bell Bond: once per session, ring your bell to gain +1 Position on a single Mind Shield or Telepathy roll. Resonant Exhale: once per scene, reduce Mental Strain by 1, but the GM gains 1 Story Beat as the vibration escapes into the environment. Gallery-Bound: ignore the first level of environmental penalty from underground or confined areas when using psionic Arts.

The Order of the Empty Circle (Violet Steppe)

These monks live in tents around the Empty Circle, entering only to meditate in absolute silence, believing the Silence was a lesson, not a wound. Circle-Trained: once per session, cancel an SB generated from Mental Strain on a non-psionic roll by marking 1 Fatigue. Still Mind: for a scene, reduce SB from Mental Strain on non-psionic actions by 1 (minimum 0), but you cannot use any psionic Art above Tier I. Herd Sense: passive +1 die to detect emotional states within Near range.

The Order of the Shared Breath (Eastern Isles)

These monks practice paired meditation, believing obligation is communal rather than individual. Circle Bond: link with up to three willing allies within Near for one scene. Shared Burden: transfer generated Mental Strain (up to your Spirit) to a willing Circle member; the GM gains 1 SB each use. Circle's Breath: while linked, +1 die to all psionic rolls, but if one member fails a Endurance test against fear or intrusion, all test at +1 DV.

The Thought Trade — The Economics of the Mind

In the shadowed exchanges of Silkstrand and the damp cellars of Zakov, a new currency circulates: the thought itself. Memory Merchants store sensitive information in living minds for a skilled craftsman's daily wage per hour — dangerous memories cost tenfold. Calculation Circles of mathematical psions serve as living abacuses for engineering projects, charging in Mental Strain as much as coin. In underground markets, psions trade raw Experiences — the memory of a dragon-flight, the sensation of true love, the fear of a battlefield — as equivalents to silver and gold. Skill Debt arrangements provide mental services over time for immediate payment, enforced not by law but by the Silent Orders' guilds — and by the fact that a psion who reneges finds their own thoughts strangely leaky.

Psions as the Sixth Road

The Runekeeper keeps a covenant, the Invoker wagers, the Cantor gives voice, the Summoner negotiates, the Witch tends. The Psion begins with no Patron's permission and receives no Patron's protection. That does not mean they walk alone unless frightened societies make it so. Chain-Lanterns hunt them, the Synod licenses them, Aeler holds confine some for study, and Aelaerem hearth-keepers argue over whether thought heard without consent is trespass. Mental Strain is not a bill presented by the skull. It is the fatigue of touching thought with thought and discovering that no mind, including your own, is private by nature.

Last Words from the Gray Wanderer

"A book is a door, not a room. You stand at the threshold now, and something on the other side has heard you breathing." — The Gray Wanderer

You have walked the Six Roads with me — seen how the Aeler count their breaths, how the Linn bind their oaths to bells, how the Thepyrgosi stamp their imprimatur on thoughts themselves. You have read the confession pressed between the leaves, the one written in milk and iron by a hand that no longer remembers its own name. You think this knowledge protects you. It does not. The Runekeeper's Error

You are an agent, not a servant. The Codex is not your shield — it is your chain, and the Thiasos is the weight at the end of it. Keep one Rite unwritten. Keep one page blank. If your Patron ever asks why that page is empty, lie. If they ask twice, run.

The Invoker's Math

You carry four coins, but the fifth pocket is always growing heavier. The Patrons do not care about your etiquette; they care about the asymmetry of the weight. The moment you Crack the Seal in desperation, you have stopped being a gambler and become the collateral. Polish your Symbols weekly. Polish them daily if you have stared into the dark.

The Cantor's Touch

Your voice is not yours anymore. It belongs to the song, and the song belongs to the silence between the notes. The corruption is not a punishment — it is a translation. When you find feathers in your hair, or shadows that lag behind your heels, do not sing for three days. If the music continues without you, break your instrument. If it continues still, break your fingers. It is cheaper than what comes next.

The Witch's Thread

You have read about the Chain. You know the soup is free. But understand: the Daughters are not heretics. They are maintenance. If you find a red thread around your wrist and do not remember tying it, do not cut it. Find a threshold. Pour salt. Speak your true name backward until the thread goes slack. If it tightens instead, you have already been collected.

The Psion's Silence

I am not one of you, but I have walked beside your kind when the bell rings three times too fast. Your mind is a fortress with no gates — safe until it isn't, and when it cracks, it crumbles inward, not outward. When you feel the edges of your self growing thin, when you begin to see the geometry of the air, stop. The Silence is not emptiness. It is appetite.

The Summoner's Door

The Leash has two ends, and the spirit holds the other. You do not command the dead, the fey, the demons — you rent them, and your landlords are hungrier and less patient than any Invoker's. When the Leash ticks, do not look at your bound spirit. Look at your own shadow. If it moves independently, the door you opened was never yours to close.

What Waits at the Crossroads

The Green Gate, the Ninth Walk, the Silent Stair — these are not metaphors. They are infections in the skin of the world, places where the Six Roads knot together and the map stops being honest. If you find yourself at such a place, do not make a wish. Do not ask a question. Offer a truth you have never spoken and a lie you have never told, and walk away without looking back. The Wanderer you see at the crossroads is not always me. Sometimes it is the thing that ate the last traveler who looked too long.

Final Counsel: keep your components dry. Keep your voice warm if you have one, and cold if you must. Keep your mind your own — guard it as the Aeler guard their Deep Vaults, with bells and silence and the willingness to seal the door from the outside. The Patrons can forgive a missed payment. They can forgive a broken covenant. They cannot forgive being outthought by a mortal who remembers that power is, at its root, a conversation — and conversations can be walked away from. The book is closed. The door is open. I will be at the inn, or I will not. But the roads remain, and they are hungry.

— The Gray Wanderer

Appendix: The Debt of the Sleeping Master

"You think you know someone after thirty years. You share bread, you spill blood, you count each other's breaths. Then one night the body stops — and the mind, that clever thief's mind, runs ahead to hide the one thing you never asked. The door is open. The threshold is warm. What will you find on the other side?" — The Gray Wanderer, at a wake that turned into a heist

This one-shot uses the Obishaal dream-realm mechanics from the Psionics chapter. Master Kai, a psion-thief and teacher, lies dying, his mind holding a secret he could not speak aloud in life. His students must enter his dreaming mind — literally the realm of Obishaal, the sweet water that drowns, the door between the last breath and the next — and descend through nine memory-chambers to recover the name he could never say, before his breath stops for good.

For the Heist

Only one character needs the Psionics skill and the Telepathy Art, acting as the anchor who pulls the rest of the party into the dream (1 Mental Strain per passenger). Non-psions cannot use psionic Arts inside Obishaal, but can see, speak, fight, sneak, and interact normally. The anchor pays the cost; the threshold holds. Treat all scenes as Echo (memory) or Veil (current dream) layers. Each PC carries a Memory Debt Timer [4] that ticks with every intrusive psychic Art used; when full, a personal regret manifests as a hostile dream-creature.

The Threshold: the room smells of old paper and dried herbs. Master Kai lies on a pallet, eyes half-open, lips moving in a language none recognize. A small gray bird with sewn-shut eyelids — his Hollowed — perches unblinking on the headboard. A letter, delivered by a mute courier, reads: "The lock is my skull. The key is my name. Take what I could not speak." The party must decide to enter (Threshold: the pallet; Witness: each other; Price: one memory of comfort with the Master, lost forever). The secret is a name — a woman the Master swore to protect and then abandoned during a historical purge, whose unspoken vow has grown into a Spirit Anchor keeping him from passing.

Timers

  • The Master's Breath [8] — ticks every scene or when a PC marks Mental Strain. When full, the Master's body stops breathing, the dream collapses, and all PCs mark 2 Harm and gain the Haunted condition.
  • Weight's Approach [6] — ticks when the party lies, fails a Endurance test, or damages a dream-construct. When full, the final Burden appears early and the Spector manifests immediately.
  • Name Unspoken [4] — the party must discover the forgotten name; each successful Investigation or Telepathy roll in a memory chamber fills one segment. When full, the name is known and can be spoken at the heart.

The Spector

The Spector is a looming presence tied to the Master's Breath timer — not a creature to fight, but a tall, featureless figure in gray with long fingers trailing threads of fading light. It cannot be harmed or reasoned with. On any tick of the Breath timer, it may manifest at a chamber's entrance and advance one zone per round. If it touches a character, they are ejected from the dream, suffer 2 Harm (Stress), and lose 1 permanent Memory. It can be avoided by a group Stealth test (DV 4), by sacrificing a String or Memory to delay it a scene, or by reciting the name of a death-adjacent power to pause it for 1d4 rounds (at the cost of 2 SB, as it grows curious). If the Master's Breath timer fills, the Spector appears in the final chamber and cannot be avoided — the party must speak the forgotten name while evading it.

Traps and Shadows of Life

The Master's mind bristles with defense techniques. Recognized Techniques (exercises he actually taught) grant the party +1 die to disarm, but require confessing a minor weakness. Alien Techniques (utterly unfamiliar) impose +1 DV to disarm, and on a miss reveal a hidden shame of the Master. Shadows of Life are echoes of people the Master knew — invisible and harmless until perceived (Wits + Notice, DV 3), at which point they become hostile; they cannot be fought directly, only outrun or appeased with an unwelcome truth, and killing one advances Weight's Approach by 1.

Rowan, the Grifter — Psionics 3 (Telepathy, Empathic Manipulation). Burden: sold a client's memory of their child's face to a rival.
Sable, the Scoundrel — Psionics 2 (Telekinesis, Psychic Assault). Burden: crushed a guard's windpipe with her mind; he had a daughter.
Lyra, the Archivist — Psionics 3 (Clairvoyance, Precognition). Burden: saw a death coming and said nothing. It came.
Jax, the Brute — Psionics 2 (Telekinesis, Mind Shield). Burden: pulled an unarmed man into a press with telekinesis.
Mira, the Whisper — Psionics 4 (Telepathy, Empathic Manipulation, Mind Shield). Burden: made a beggar believe he was king for a week; he woke and jumped from a bridge.
Thorn, the Needle — Psionics 3 (Psychic Assault, Precognition). Burden: erased a mother of three's memory of how to breathe.
Dove, the Veil — Psionics 3 (Empathic Manipulation, Mind Shield, Telepathy). Burden: broke a man's will to love so he'd leave his wife. Both died.
Rook, the Turncoat — Psionics 3 (Clairvoyance, Astral Projection, Telekinesis). Burden: betrayed a fellow student to save himself; they never learned it was him.

The Nine Chambers (Threshold Journey)

  1. The Meditation Hall (Echo) — relive a training session; a Burden appears as a younger student who failed.
  2. The Vault of Stolen Secrets (Veil) — floating memory-orbs; one holds the first clue: "Not treasure. A woman. The Ash-Fenn market."
  3. The Anxious Stair (Flow) — steps that grow teeth; each PC confesses a regret aloud, drawing a Doubt Wraith attack.
  4. The Court of the Witness (Echo) — a mirror-hall where Burdens manifest; speaking a Burden's name shifts the mirrors into a labyrinth.
  5. The Iron Well (Veil) — a deep shaft with a locket at the bottom, guarded by a Burden Guardian.
  6. The Harrow (Flow) — a corridor of gray-thread thorns; each PC sacrifices one String.
  7. The Forgotten Oath (Echo) — a courtroom where the Master is judged by his own conscience; the party must defend him with acts of kindness.
  8. The Hollowed Garden (Veil) — a grove where a faceless woman asks "Why did he leave?"; an Empathic success reveals the answer, failure summons Thorn-Kin.
  9. The Heart (Obishaal pure) — a bare stone altar; speaking the name Finley fills the Name Unspoken timer and the Master's spirit rises. If the Master's Breath is at 1 or less, the Spector appears immediately and the party must speak the name while evading it.

Resolution

The party wakes. The Master's body is still. The Hollowed bird opens its sewn eyes, chirps once, and flies out the window. In his hand is a slip of paper: "You found it. The treasure was my shame. Now I can rest. The Academy is yours — if you can keep it." Each PC clears one Obligation or Corruption segment of their choice, and gains a permanent Dream-Echo: once per session, they may reroll a failed Endurance test in Obishaal or against fear.

That is the closest our kind comes to prayer." — The Gray Wanderer, closing a bottle of Sidhi port
