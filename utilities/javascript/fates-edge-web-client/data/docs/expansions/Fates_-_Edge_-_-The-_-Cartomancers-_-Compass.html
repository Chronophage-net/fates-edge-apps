🃏 The Cartomancer's Compass

Rules compatibility. The core rules comprise the SRD plus the extensions in the Player's Guide and GM Guide. This expansion applies only its stated additions and exceptions. Keep the core loop: declare intent and approach, set Position and DV, roll Attribute + Skill, count successes and Story Beats, resolve the outcome, and apply consequences.

Cartomancy adds no new resolution mechanic. Suits name the Place, Actor, Pressure and Leverage of a scene; rank sets a DV on the standard 2–5 ladder or converts to a Timer. Where a draw would push past DV 5 it becomes a Timer instead — the ladder ends at 5. Readings are Quick Workings or Full Rituals as written in the Player's Guide, and every price they exact (SB to the GM, a Condition, Identity Strain, Obligation) is an existing currency, not a new one.

What This Is.

The card-draw method that every regional generator in Fate's Edge is written against, gathered in one place: the four suits and what each one answers, how rank sets weight and Timers, how face cards and Aces behave, the step-by-step procedure for running any generator at the table, and the Tulkani tradition that treats a reading as witchcraft with a price. It ships inside both worldbooks and stands alone here.

“The world does not speak in words. It speaks in suits, ranks, and the spaces between. Every bridge, every bribe, every broken oath can be laid out on a table of felt and dealt from a worn deck. This is not superstition. It is method.”

1. A Tulkani Perspective on the Art

I was seven when my grandmother first placed a deck in my hands. Not a new deck — a worn one, its edges soft as silk, its faces faded by a hundred thousand shuffles. “This is your first threshold,” she said. “Learn to read it, and you will never be lost.”

The Tulkani do not build temples. We build fires. We do not carve scriptures into stone; we let the cards tell us what the road has seen. Cartomancy is our witch-work, our way of reading the Echo (what has been), steadying the Veil (what is), and leaning into Flow (what wishes to be). It is not fortune-telling. It is witnessing — a conversation with the world's memory, conducted through the language of suits and ranks.

The other peoples of Fate's Edge have their own names for what we do. The Aeler call us “thread-readers” and hire us to audit their ledgers. The Ykrul call us “bone-speakers” and consult us before every migration. The Ecktorian Inquisition calls us heretics and burns our decks when they find them. We keep shuffling.

This chapter is my gift to the road. It explains how to read the cards as we do — not as a tool for controlling fate, but as a way of listening to what the world has already decided to tell you.

2. The Four Suits: A Universal Grammar

Every card in every generator carries the same four suit-meanings. They are the alphabet of the world's witness. In the Tulkani tradition, we say the suits are the four breaths of the world.

The four suits at a glance
SuitReadsAnswers
♠ SpadesPlace — the StageWhere are we?
♥ HeartsActor — the FaceWho is here?
♣ ClubsPressure — the ComplicationWhat goes wrong?
♦ DiamondsLeverage — the RewardWhat do you gain?

♠ Spades: Place — The Stage

Where does the scene happen? Spades are the threshold, the landmark, the ground beneath the action.

  • High rank (10–A): A major landmark — a fortress, a capital, a haunted ruin. The place has a name that echoes in local songs.
  • Mid rank (6–9): A functional location — a ford, a market, a shrine. The place has a purpose and a keeper.
  • Low rank (2–5): A liminal or neglected place — a crossroads, a collapsed bridge, a smuggler's cove. The place is defined by what it lacks.

In the regional chapters, spade entries are always places. See the bridges and canals of Silkstrand, or the cairns and passes of Ubral.

♥ Hearts: Actor — The Face

Who drives the scene? Hearts are the person, the faction, the voice that speaks.

  • High rank (J–A): An elite, a ruler, a power-broker. The actor has authority and reach beyond the immediate scene.
  • Mid rank (6–10): A professional, a functionary, a skilled operator. The actor has a trade and a price.
  • Low rank (2–5): A commoner, an outcast, a victim. The actor needs something, or has been failed by someone.

In the regional chapters, heart entries are always people or factions. See the merchants and spies of the Brass Coast, or the spirit-wardens of the Mistlands.

♣ Clubs: Pressure — The Complication

What pushes back? Clubs are the storm, the burden, the knife in the dark.

  • High rank (10–A): A catastrophe, a betrayal, an invasion. The complication changes the shape of the journey.
  • Mid rank (6–9): A serious obstacle — a feud, a quarantine, a broken bridge. The complication costs time, resources, or reputation.
  • Low rank (2–5): A minor hazard — a sudden squall, a lost token, a raised toll. The complication is a nuisance, not a disaster.

In the regional chapters, club entries are always complications or threats. See the river floods and witness-cord cuts of Ayokha, or the market riots of Silkstrand.

♦ Diamonds: Leverage — The Reward

What opens the door? Diamonds are the token, the writ, the secret that changes Position.

  • High rank (J–A): A major asset — a noble title, a keystone charter, a general pardon. The reward reshapes the party's standing.
  • Mid rank (6–10): A significant tool — a pass-stone, a witness cord, a lighthouse charter. The reward solves a specific class of problem.
  • Low rank (2–5): A minor advantage — a bridge token, a guest-cup, a rumor. The reward grants a single use of improved Position.

In the regional chapters, diamond entries are always rewards or leverage. See the keystone charters of Ngomebe, or the water-share tablets of Fhara.

3. Cartomancy as Witchcraft

In the Tulkani tradition, cartomancy is not separate from the craft — it is the craft. Every reading is a Quick Working, performed at a threshold: the table, the crossroads, the moment between decisions. The cards are the tools; the reader is the witness.

The Three Layers of Reading

Like all witchcraft, cartomancy operates across the three Concordant Layers.

  • Echo (what has been): The cards reveal the accumulated weight of past decisions. A spade from Echo shows the history of a place; a heart from Echo shows a debt that has not yet been paid.
  • Veil (what is): The cards show the present moment, the boundary between potential and actual. A club from Veil is a current threat; a diamond from Veil is an opportunity the party is ignoring.
  • Flow (what wishes to be): The cards reveal the direction of change. A high card from Flow is a promise; a low card is a warning.

The Reader's Price

“The cards do not give; they lend. And they always collect.”

Every reading exacts a price. When you read the cards, choose one of the Three Prices.

  • Shadow: You carry the weight of what you have seen. The GM gains 1 SB, which they may spend on a complication tied to the reading.
  • Shame: The reading reveals something you did not wish to know. You gain a Condition (Guilty, Haunted, or Doubtful) until you act on what you have learned.
  • Identity: The reading changes you. Mark 1 Identity Strain. You are not quite the same person who sat down at the table.

The Cartomancer's Gift

Once per session, a cartomancer may perform a full reading using the procedure in §7. This is a Full Ritual: it requires Significant Time, a witness, and the willingness to pay the price. On a success, the GM must answer one question about the scene, the journey, or the campaign truthfully. On a partial, the answer is cryptic. On a miss, the cards show a false path — the GM gains 2 SB and may introduce a complication.

4. Rank and Severity: The Weight of the Card

The rank of a card — from Ace (high) to 2 (low) — sets the scale of its suit's meaning. The Tulkani say: “The rank is the breath; the suit is the voice. A high breath speaks louder; a low breath is a whisper.”

Rank as narrative weight
RankNarrative weight
AceLegendary, campaign-altering, mythic
King / Queen / JackMajor, faction-level, lasting consequences
10–7Significant, scene-defining, costly
6–4Moderate, manageable, a real cost
3–2Minor — a complication, but not a crisis

Timer Conversion

When a generator instructs you to set a Timer by the highest rank, use this conversion.

Highest rank drawn → Timer size
Highest rankTimerScale
2–54 segmentsShort, local problem
6–106 segmentsStandard, journey-length
J, Q, K8 segmentsExtended, regional problem
A10 segmentsEpic, campaign-length
Weight is not DV.

A heavy card makes an obstacle bigger, not harder to touch. Scale it with segments, stakes and fiction — the DV ladder still ends at 5. An Ace is a 10-segment Timer the party has to work at from several angles, not a single DV 7 roll.

5. Card Faces: The Court as Character

Face cards are not merely high ranks. They are persons, with agency, personality, and often a name. The Tulkani say: “The court does not sit in judgment of you. You sit in judgment of them.”

  • Jack: An agent, a functionary, a messenger. The Jack acts on someone else's authority. Competent, but replaceable.
  • Queen: A power in their own right, with influence, resources, and a network. Not easily replaced.
  • King: The ultimate authority in their domain — the source of law, the peak of the hierarchy. To challenge a King is to challenge the order itself.

When a face card appears in a heart (actor) draw, give the NPC a name, a motive, and a quirk. When it appears in a spade (place) or club (pressure) draw, treat it as a personified force: a King of Clubs might be a feud that has lasted generations; a Queen of Spades might be a bridge that remembers every toll.

6. Aces and the Ninth: The Hollow's Attention

An Ace is not merely the highest rank. It is a threshold. “The Ace is the door that opens when you are not looking. Do not step through it lightly.”

When an Ace appears in any draw, the Hollow takes notice.

The GM gains 1 extra Story Beat — beyond any generated by rolled 1s — to spend on a supernatural complication: a ghostly procession, a bell that rings without a clapper, a shadow that walks independently.

In regions where the Ninth Taboo is strong (Theona, Aelinnel, the Ways Between), an Ace may also trigger a specific regional omen.

  • In Theona, an Ace of ♠ might add a “missing step” to a bridge.
  • In the Ways Between, an Ace of ♥ might cause a reflection to speak.
  • In the Mistlands, an Ace of ♣ might cause a bell-line to fail.

Read an Ace as a signal to escalate the uncanny.

7. By the Numbers: Procedure for Any Generator

When you sit down to generate a scene, a journey leg, or a settlement, follow this procedure. “The cards are not a script. They are a conversation. Listen to what they tell you, but do not let them speak for you.”

  1. Name the destination. Which region? Which route? Which authority controls the gate?
  2. Shuffle and draw. Draw cards face-up, one for each suit you need. Most generators require all four; some, like travel legs, use only three.
  3. Read the ♠. This is your place. Describe it first. Set the stage.
  4. Read the ♥. This is your actor. Introduce them. What do they want? Why are they here?
  5. Read the ♣. This is your pressure. Complicate the scene. What just went wrong, or what is about to?
  6. Read the ♦. This is your leverage. What can the players gain if they succeed? Be specific.
  7. Set the Timer. Use the highest rank among the four cards to size the scene's primary Timer (see §4).
  8. Interpret faces and Aces. Give names to face cards. Apply regional Ace effects.
  9. Play to find out. Use the generated elements as prompts, not scripts. The cards are seeds, not fences.

8. Regional Flavors: How Suits Shift Meaning

The core suit meanings hold everywhere, but each region inflects them with its own texture. A spade in Ecktoria is a forum or a coin-house; a spade in the Valewood is a phasing ruin or a star-road. A heart among the Black Banners is a mercenary captain or a condotta broker; a heart in Ykrul is a khatun or a bone-singer.

“The same card speaks differently in every land. The fool who reads it the same way twice is a fool twice over.”

Use the regional chapters as your lexicon. When you draw a card, consult the relevant table — the tables are built to evoke the specific flavor of the region.

Example: A Draw in Acasia

You are running a journey through the Broken Marches. You draw:

7♠
J♥
4♣
Q♦
  1. ♠ 7 — Hill-Motte. Fresh palisade; ditch scattered with caltrops.
  2. ♥ J — Bride with No Dowry but a Claim. Daggers in the wedding chest.
  3. ♣ 4 — Wedding Turns Ambush. You are caught between two bloodlines.
  4. ♦ Q — Marriage Proxy. From the Margravine; bind a hill-king to your cause.
  5. Highest rank: Queen. The scene's primary Timer is 8 segments: The Feud Escalates [8].
  6. Interpretation: The party arrives at a hill-fort on the eve of a wedding. The bride is caught between two feuding bloodlines. The wedding turns to violence. If the party survives the ambush and brokers peace, the Margravine grants them a Marriage Proxy — a powerful tool for binding a hill-king to their cause. The 8-segment Timer tracks how quickly the feud spirals into open war.

That is the generator at work: four cards, one scene, immediate fiction.

9. The Unwritten Suit: When a Draw Is Incomplete

Sometimes a generator does not require all four suits. A travel leg may need only spades, hearts and clubs; a social encounter, only hearts and clubs. When a suit is missing, treat its absence as a question.

  • No ♠: The place is ambiguous, shifting, or hidden. The scene occurs in a liminal space — a crossroads, a fog-bank, a threshold.
  • No ♥: The actor is absent, silent, or dead. Pressure and place act without a face, which can be more frightening than a named NPC.
  • No ♣: The scene is calm, peaceful, or mercifully straightforward. This is rare in Fate's Edge. Treasure it.
  • No ♦: The reward is not a token but an outcome. The players gain Position, information, or a cleared Timer rather than a physical asset.

10. Interpreting Any Draw, Any Region

When you need a prompt in a hurry — a random encounter, a rumor at a tavern, a discovery in a ruin — draw a single card.

Single-card quick reference
CardAsk
♠ SpadesA place or landmark. What is its name? What happened here?
♥ HeartsA person or faction. What do they want? What do they fear?
♣ ClubsA complication or threat. Who or what is the obstacle?
♦ DiamondsA reward, token, or secret. What can be gained?

The rank tells you the scale: low is local and personal, high is regional and political. A face card demands a name; an Ace demands the Hollow's attention.

11. Tulkani Cartomancy Traditions

The Tulkani have developed practices around cartomancy that differ from the regional generators. These are the traditions my grandmother taught me.

The Traveler's Spread 3 cards Quick Working
  • First card — the road behind. What have you left behind, and what follows you?
  • Second card — the road ahead. What waits for you, and what will you face?
  • Third card — the road beneath. What is true, whether you wish it or not?

No roll required, but the reader must accept the Shadow price.

The Hollow's Spread 5 cards Full Ritual
  • First: the weight you carry.
  • Second: the weight you owe.
  • Third: the weight you have forgotten.
  • Fourth: the weight that carries you.
  • Fifth: the weight that will collect.

Requires Significant Time, a witness, and the acceptance of Identity Strain. On a success the GM must answer one question about the nature of the debt; on a partial the answer is cryptic; on a miss the reader becomes the debt's next collector, and a Complication Timer begins.

The Cartomancer's Deck as a Symbol

For Tulkani characters, a cartomancer's deck is a Symbol in the Invoker sense — a Minor Asset that grants:

  • Access to the cartomancy rituals described above.
  • A Patron's Gift (Imbuement) effect: once per scene, the cartomancer may spend an action to draw a card and gain +1 die to a related roll. The GM gains 1 SB.
  • Obligation to the Hollow: each reading marks 1 Obligation, as the cartomancer borrows from the world's memory.

12. Final Guidance: The Cards Are Oracles, Not Scripts

The generators are tools, not tyrants. If a draw gives you an entry that contradicts established fiction, or if you simply prefer a different interpretation, overrule it. The deck is a prompt, not a prison.

But trust it, too. The most memorable sessions often come from the card you did not expect — the Ace that arrives at the wrong moment, the Jack who speaks a name no one wanted to hear, the diamond that glows with a secret.

“The cards do not lie. But they do not tell the whole truth. The rest is yours to live.”

Shuffle. Draw. Interpret. And let the road unfold.

“The road is never closed. It is only passed to the next walker.”
— Mira of the Wandering Circle, on the eve of a journey she did not survive

13. GM Tools: Using Cartomancy at the Table

Reading the Cards as a GM

The cartomancy framework is for GMs as much as players. When you need a scene, a complication, or a reward, draw a card and interpret it through the lens of the region and the current fiction.

Example.

The party is in Vhasia, negotiating with a border patrol. You draw ♣ 9. The entry reads: “Feud Spark — a name spoken wrong reopens an old debt; blood is the only coin that pays.” You interpret: the patrol captain's family has a blood feud with one of the party's allies. He recognizes the name and demands a price — a ransom, a duel, or a favor.

Players as Cartomancers

If a player wants to play a Tulkani cartomancer, use this framework.

  • Affinity — Cartomancer's Eye: +1 die to Insight or Lore rolls involving patterns, hidden connections, or the weight of obligation.
  • Starting Talent — Cartomancy (4 XP): grants access to the Traveler's Spread and the Hollow's Spread.
  • Patron — The Hollow: the cartomancer's Obligation is to the memory of the world itself. The Hollow collects through readings.

The Cost of Cartomancy

Every reading is a transaction. The cards lend, and they collect. A player who reads too often, or who ignores what the cards reveal, will find the Hollow's attention turning their way.
