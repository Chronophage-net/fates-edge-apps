Daughters of the Cord

A Fate's Edge Patron / Faction Expansion

Faction Expansion Cord Mechanics Player Options Author: Nicholas A. Gasper

This expansion details the Daughters of the Cord, a mendicant coven bound to the High Mother — a threshold entity sustained by accumulated debt, subtle sacrifice, and the silent witness of the Hollowed. They appear as midwives, hospice nurses, deathbed attendants, and keepers of alms-houses. They offer the mercy of warm hands and the comfort of final words.

But the mercy has a ledger. The comfort has a price.

In these pages, Game Masters will find the philosophy and hierarchy of the Cord; mechanics for the Cord Timer, a personal Obligation track for player Daughters; the Hollowed as followers and witnesses; rivalries with other Patrons; player options for Runekeeper and Witch Daughters; sample Rites, adventure hooks, and GM tools; and a confession from a midwife who serves the Cord.

The soup is always free. The price never is.

Core Rules Reminder (Fate's Edge SRD)
  • Obligation Capacity: Spirit + Presence. Exceeding capacity = 1 Fatigue per segment.
  • Boons: Max 5. Reduce to 2 at scene end. Convert 2 → 1 XP (once/session).
  • Harm: 1 = -1 die, 2 = -2 dice, 3 = incapacitated.
  • Fatigue: Each point worsens Position (Dominant → Controlled → Desperate). Full track → Harm+1, clear Fatigue.

What the Daughters Are (And What They Are Not)

The Daughters of the Cord have no temples, holy texts, or public summons. They are midwives, mourners, confessors, poisoners, and family advisers whose private practices join households across dozens of cultures. Most people meet a Daughter at birth, illness, or death and never learn what passed between her hands.

What the Cord Carries

The Cord binds generations through what they inherit: shelter and privilege, injury and silence, duties accepted by the dead and imposed upon the unborn. The High Mother sits within that tangle, deciding which child inherits protection, which inherits blame, and which family will be preserved at another's expense.

Two Faces of the Cord

Some Sisters embrace the power. They see themselves as surgeons of society, cutting away dead weight, nurturing worthy bloodlines.

Others feel trapped — bound by oaths sworn in desperation, unable to leave because the Cord knows their truest secrets.

A few have tried to flee; those who succeeded are now hunted. Most remain, hollowed by degrees, tending the dying while the Cord tightens around their own throats.

The Unspoken Ethic

The Daughters do not preach. Their ethics are encoded in practices, not scriptures:

  1. Nothing ends with one life. Shelter, cruelty, privilege, and grief all outlive the people who began them.
  2. Mercy binds the future. A kindness may save a life; it may also become the story used to command that person's children.
  3. A good death protects the living. No one should die alone or unmourned. The cruel Sisters turn that care into permission to decide whose life is worth extending.
  4. Witness creates responsibility. To know a secret is not to own it. The Daughters' gravest abuse is insisting otherwise.
  5. No thread is ever cut. Debts are not forgiven; they are transferred — to a child, to a rival, to a village. The Cord never breaks; it only changes shape.

Hierarchy — The Three Offices

The Coven is organized in triads of three offices. These are functions, not fixed ranks. A Sister may serve in one role for a decade, then shift to another. The High Mother is the only fixed position; she who sits at the Cord's center is effectively immortal.

OfficeFunctionSacrifice
Keeper of the Door (Weaver)Sets terms, knocks, enters. Midwife, hospice intake, first contact.Must never be alone. Always witnessed by another Sister or a Hollowed.
Keeper of the Name (Widow)Remembers debts, records names, holds the coven's collective memory in her flesh.Must keep fresh the memory of one dead thing. If she forgets, she loses a finger (added to the Cord).
Keeper of the Price (Winter)Collects debts, carries Thorn-Pins, decides when obligations come due. The only Office allowed to lie to outsiders.Cannot feel heat. Fire burns unnoticed until she is ash.

A full coven consists of three Sisters, one of each Office, bonded by a shared Hollowed. Larger covens are multiple triads, each reporting to a senior Winter.

The High Mother is not an Office; she is the Cord's avatar. She no longer walks the world. Her whispers guide the Winters; her dreams shape the pattern. To meet her is to enter the House Without Doors — a liminal space at the center of the Cord — and to leave behind a piece of yourself.

The Cord — Mechanics

A Daughter (player character) has a Cord Timer — a track representing her accumulated debt to the Cord.

The Cord Timer [6]

Mark a segment when:

  • She accepts a major debt from a dying person (grief, a secret, a name) → +1
  • She performs a Rite of the High Mother without a Hollowed witness → +1
  • She fails to collect a promised debt within a season → +1
  • She disobeys a direct order from a Winter → +2
  • She helps someone escape the Cord (knowingly) → +3
When the Cord Timer Fills (6/6)

The High Mother demands payment. The Sister must perform one of:

  • Harvest a life (cause a patient to die a "good death" — the debt is paid in soul-stuff)
  • Give up a memory (permanent — choose a significant NPC or event to forget)
  • Cut a thread (sever a relationship — lose a Bond with another PC or a significant ally)
  • Accept a Scar (permanent visible mark — all social rolls are Desperate with those who know the Cord)

After payment, the Cord Timer resets to 0, but the Sister's Obligation to the High Mother increases by 1 permanent segment (cannot be cleared except by leaving the coven — see Breaking the Cord).

The Hollowed

Every Sister receives a Hollowed — a child or animal whose heart was removed and replaced with a bundle of gray thread and the ash of a forgotten name. The eyes are sewn open with silver wire. They do not eat, sleep, or age. They witness every working.

Mechanics
  • The Hollowed is a Cap 2 Follower that never needs upkeep. It cannot fight, speak, or be targeted by mundane attacks (spirits can harm it).
  • While within Near range, you count as having Soft Witness (improve Position by one step for any Rite of the High Mother).
  • If your Hollowed is destroyed, you immediately mark +1 Obligation and your Cord Timer ticks +2. You may acquire a new Hollowed after a significant ritual (4 XP and a major sacrifice — a memory, a scar, or a life).

Flesh-Codex

A Daughter's Rites are not stored in a book; they are tattooed into her flesh. These tattoos writhe, weep, and occasionally bleed.

  • You do not need a Codex asset. Your body is the Codex.
  • Your Rites cannot be stolen or destroyed (short of flaying you alive).
  • Anyone who sees your tattoos can attempt a Wits + Lore test (DV 3) to deduce which Patron and which Rites you carry. Inquisitors can identify you on sight.

Player Options — Daughter of the Cord

Players may join the Cord as a Runekeeper (most common) or a Witch. Invokers cannot become full Daughters (they carry too many conflicting Symbols), but they can be contractors.

Requirements

Spirit 3+, Body 2+. Lore (Thresholds) 1+. Talents: Disciplined Body (3 XP, Monk) and Gray Thread (new, 4 XP).

Patron: The High Mother (treated as a Patron for Obligation). Her Rites appear below and in Witches of Fate's Edge, §IV.

Unique Mechanic — Cord as Obligation: Your Obligation to the High Mother is tracked separately from other Patrons. You cannot swear to another Patron without breaking your vow to the Cord.

The High Mother's Shadow Epic 18 XP

Requires: Tier IV+, Unnamed Strike, Cord of Flesh

You become Cord-blooded. You no longer age. Fatigue converts to Obligation automatically. You may spend Obligation segments as if they were Boons (1 segment = 1 Boon) for rolls involving silence, memory, or binding.

When you would die, the High Mother claims your body; you rise as a Hollowed under her control for a number of days equal to your current Obligation segments. If you have no Obligation, you die permanently.

Requirements

Breaking the Cord — Leaving the Coven

Leaving the Daughters is a major arc, not a simple respec.

Costs
  • You lose all Daughter-specific talents (they become inert)
  • You gain the Unbound condition: the High Mother's attention follows you. Once per session, the GM may impose a Debt Echo — a former obligation resurfaces unpredictably
  • You may never again take a Daughter talent, nor accept a Hollowed
  • You must perform the Rite of Unbinding

Rite of Unbinding (20 XP arc or narrative quest): Find a threshold (doorway, crossroads, ford) at dawn. Light a beeswax candle. Speak your true name three times. Drink salt water. Do not look behind you. The Hollowed will be there, watching. If you meet its eyes, you will forget why you began.

If successful, the Cord Timer shatters. Your Obligation to the High Mother becomes 0 (cannot be raised). You are free — but the Cord remembers.

Join two bloodlines through a debt that will be paid by a child not yet conceived. Write the terms in blood on a strip of linen, then feed the linen to your Hollowed. Cost: 3 Obligation, and the Hollowed must witness the union. Push (Mark +1 Identity): The debt is paid immediately, but the Cord takes a permanent interest in the bloodline (GM may introduce complications whenever that family is involved).

The Conspiracy — Propping and Culling Dynasties

The Daughters are the hidden hand of succession across dozens of noble houses, merchant dynasties, and patron-bound bloodlines.

How It Works

A Sister attends a dying matriarch. She listens to the matriarch's secrets, her fears about her heirs, her desire to see the family continue — or her wish to see it burn. The Sister offers a good death: peace, release, the promise that the matriarch's will is done. In exchange for a thread — a name, a memory, a future favor.

The thread is woven into the Cord. Later, when an heir contests the inheritance, a Daughter appears — not as a witch, but as a neutral nurse who "happens" to recall the dying matriarch's final words. Or a key document is "found" in a Daughter's alms-house. Or a rival family suffers a sudden plague, tended by Sisters who ensure a very efficient death rate.

The Goal

The Cord deals in generational debt: shelter inherited with the silence that paid for it, privilege built from injuries descendants did not commit, and duties laid upon children by promises made before their birth. It favors dynasties willing to call that inheritance stewardship and obedience peace. Inconvenient houses fade through supposedly natural deaths; useful ones endure because they fund the Daughters' hospices, overlook their work, or supply unclaimed bodies for the Hollowing. The Cord's horror is not that it balances the past. It appoints heirs to both benefit and blame, then punishes anyone who asks whether an inherited debt can be refused—or owed to someone other than the ancestor who names it.

Player Hooks

  • A PC learns that their own noble house has been managed by the Cord for generations. Their grandmother's death was not natural; it was a collection.
  • A rival house is collapsing from a "mysterious wasting disease" — actually a targeted curse delivered through a Daughter's soup. The party must decide whether to expose the Cord or exploit the weakness.
  • A Winter approaches the party with an offer: help her cull an upstart magocracy that threatens to destabilize the region, and the Cord will forget a debt the party owes (from a previous adventure).

Rivalries with Other Patrons

The Daughters are not allied with major Patrons; they exist in tension with many, and outright hostility with some.

Malachai — Addiction vs. Generational Debt

Malachai offers immediate relief — a silver coin that buys sea-lungs, a thorn that makes arrows strike true. The Daughters offer deferred relief. A Daughter sees a Malachai-touched addict as a wasted asset — they burn out before their debt can compound.

Conflict: Daughters refuse to tend Malachai's followers; Malachai sends cursed items into Daughter-run hospices.

Ikasha — Latent Debt vs. Hidden Potential

Ikasha is the patron of latent debt — obligations not yet called, secrets not yet spoken. The Daughters deal in active debt — threads already woven. They see Ikasha's followers as hoarders of potential.

Conflict: A Daughter and an Ikasha cultist both seek the same dying person. The Daughter wants the death to be witnessed and recorded; the Ikasha wants it silent. Neither can act openly; a cold war of whispers.

Inaea — Web of Mercy vs. Web of Debt

Inaea weaves mercy — threads that comfort, knots that bind gently. The Daughters weave debt. They are the dark mirror of Inaea's covenant.

Conflict: An Inaean silk-weaver and a Daughter both tend the same community. The weaver establishes a Wedding Line (sanctuary); the Daughter establishes a Hospitality Toll (every meal incurs a debt). The party must mediate — or choose which web to support.

Isoka — Transformation vs. Perpetuation

Isoka teaches shedding — the old self dies, the new self emerges. The Daughters teach perpetuation — the debt continues, the bloodline continues. A Daughter sees Isoka's shedding as waste; Isoka sees the Cord as stagnation.

Conflict: An Isoka cult is trying to help a noble heir "shed" their identity — escape the family, abandon their debts. The Daughters have a vested interest in keeping that heir bound.

Minor Rivalries

  • The Pale Shepherd: Respectful distance. Both tend thresholds between life and death. The Shepherd guides; the Daughters harvest.
  • The Witness: Hostile. The Witness records truths; the Daughters hide, omit, and occasionally destroy records. A Witness-bound Runekeeper is a natural enemy.
  • The Traveler: Neutral. Daughters maintain waystation hospices — useful to travelers. The Traveler's followers respect their hospitality, even if they dislike the cost.

Sample SB Spends for Daughter Encounters

  • 1 SB: A Hollowed's gaze lingers on a PC; they feel watched. Next roll involving secrecy is Desperate.
  • 2 SB: A thread of gray cord appears tied around a PC's wrist. They cannot recall when it happened. Remove it (action, DV 3) or suffer -1 die to social rolls with anyone who knows the Cord.
  • 3 SB: A Winter invokes a Threshold Contract the party unknowingly accepted (they ate soup at a Daughter's hospice). The party must choose: accept a minor debt (lose 1 Boon, gain 1 Obligation to the Cord) or reject it (gain 2 SB banked for the Winter in the next scene).
  • 4 SB: The High Mother's attention falls on a PC. They dream of the Cord. When they wake, they have forgotten a significant memory (GM chooses — a face, a secret, a location). To recover it, they must travel to a Daughter's hospice and bargain.

Adventure Hooks & Campaign Frames

1. The Trapped Sister Introductory

A Daughter contacts the party secretly. She feels the Cord closing around her — she has been ordered to harvest a child's breath to pay a debt she did not incur. She wants help to reroute the debt to a deserving target. The party must decide: assist her (gaining a Daughter ally, but incurring the Cord's attention) or betray her (earning a String with the local Winter, but losing a potential ally).

2. The Winter's Cull Political

A Winter has decided to cull a noble house that has grown too independent. She has infiltrated their staff, and their matriarch is dying. The Cord wants the house to fragment, so its lands become available for a "loyal" family. The party is hired by a loyalist of the house to ensure a peaceful transition — one that passes leadership smoothly. They must outmaneuver the Winter's agents (who are also acting as nurses and healers). No one is obviously evil; both sides want a good death — just different heirs.

3. The Debt That Walks Horror

A Duke's daughter was saved from a plague by a Daughter, but the price was a promise on her firstborn. She now has a child, and the Cord is coming to collect — not the child's life, but a shadow (rendering the child legally invisible, unable to inherit). The Duke hires the party to break the promise. They must find a way to nullify the debt without harming the Daughter (who is just following orders) or inciting the High Mother's wrath. Options: find a loophole (the promise was made under duress), pay an equivalent debt (steal a shadow from a criminal), or confront the Winter directly.

4. The High Mother's Shadow Epic

A powerful necromancer threatens one of the Cord's key hospices. The Daughters cannot fight openly; they ask the party to act as their weapon. In exchange, they will erase a debt the party owes. But as the party investigates, they discover that the necromancer is actually a former Daughter who broke free — and she claims the Cord has been manipulating royal bloodlines for centuries to breed a perfect vessel for the High Mother. Decide: help the Daughters (perpetuate the conspiracy) or help the necromancer (risk unleashing a greater evil).

5. The House Without Doors Dungeon

The party is invited to a Daughter's hospice — a place of healing and peace. They soon realize that every kindness is a thread, every meal a debt. The doors do not lead where they expect. The Hollowed watch from corners. A Winter offers them a deal: help her prune a troublesome ghost, and she will let them leave. But the ghost is the spirit of a woman who died in childbirth — and her baby now toils as a Hollowed. The party must find a way to unbind both mother and child without breaking the Cord's laws.

The Hollowed — A Confession

The following confession was recorded by a silenced scribe of the Witness, found in a lead-lined box. It is told from the lips of Sister Aris, Keeper of the Door, to her Hollowed son.

The first thing you learn, little one, is that a woman in labor does not see you. She sees the pain. She sees the face of her mother, or the face of her god. She does not see the boy who never blinks, who holds the linens with hands that do not warm.

You are good at linens. I am proud of you.

This was a farmhouse, a good one. Stone floor, thick beams, ash on the hearth. The father was the nervous type. He kept touching his belt where a knife should hang. I told him to boil water. Men need tasks. It keeps them from counting their fears.

The mother was young, maybe eighteen, with the broad hips of a woman built for this. Her name was Ellia. I had delivered her own mother twenty-three years ago, a quick birth, a quiet one. That mother paid with a lock of hair and a promise to name her first daughter after my own mother, who had no name I ever spoke aloud.

That debt is still in the Cord. It will be collected eventually. Maybe by you.

Ellia screamed. Good screams — productive, open-throated. I checked her progress. Eight centimeters, then nine. The child was positioned well. I told her to push.

You stood by the washbasin, your hands folded, your eyes open. The father glanced at you once, then again. He could not hold your gaze. No one ever can. They think it's shyness. They think you are simple. They do not see the silver wire, even when it glints in the firelight. That is the miracle of the Cord, little one. No one sees the help.

The first twin came fast. A boy, red-faced, strong, with fists that knew how to hold the air. I tied the cord, passed him to the mother. She laughed and wept at once. The father touched the boy's cheek, and his hand did not shake.

Then the second twin.

He was smaller, paler, and his cord was wrapped around his neck twice, maybe three times. I felt the knot with my fingers. I could have unwrapped him. I have done it before. But I also felt the weight of the thread in my pocket — the thread that would have bound him to a lifetime of coughing, of debts to physicians, of nights when his parents would not be sure if he would wake.

I looked at you. You were watching the father.

He was still smiling at the first boy. He had not yet looked at the second.

So I did not unwrap the cord. I held it tight, just for a moment. I felt his heart slow. It was quick. It was quiet. It was kind.

I wrapped the second boy in a clean cloth and laid him on the table.

"The second one did not make it," I said. My voice was calm. It is always calm. That is why they trust me.

The mother's face did what faces do — the grief tried to arrive. I placed my hand on her forehead. "You have one healthy son. Focus on him. Do not let the shadow of the one you lost touch the one you hold."

She nodded. Her eyelids fluttered. I had not given her anything yet. But the words were enough. The debt of the dead child was already transferring — from her memory to the Cord.

The father looked at the still bundle. "He was so small," he whispered.

"He was weak," I said. "He would have grown sickly, frail. A lifetime of coughing, of debts to physicians, of nights when you are not sure if he will wake. This is easier. Kinder."

He did not argue.

I cut two pieces of the umbilical cord. One for his mother's memory box. One for the Cord.

You took it. You placed it in your pocket, next to your heart that does not beat.

The mother asked for tea. I poured it. Dark, a little milk, a little iron. The father asked if he could have some. I poured a second cup. He drank it without tasting.

As he drank, he looked at you.

He saw you.

Not the shy boy. Not the helper. He saw the thread where your mouth had been sewn. He saw the hollow where your heart should be. He saw the gray cord trailing from your chest, pulsing with the rhythm of his own heartbeat.

His hand went to his throat. His mouth opened.

"Drink," I said. "The tea will help you forget."

He drank.

I told him: "The child was weak. This is better. You have one healthy son. That is enough."

He nodded. He was already forgetting the shape of the dead boy's face.

— · · · —

The testimony continues for several pages, describing the three days of recovery, the forgetting, and the eventual visit months later when the father offers water and calls the Hollowed a "kind face." The confession ends with the midwife's ambiguous penance:

— · · · —

I do not know if I am confessing because I am sorry, little one, or because the ritual demands it. Perhaps there is no difference.

The mother was young. The father was kind. The dead child was weak. I told the truth.

But truth, like tea, is a matter of preparation.

The soup is always free. The price never is.

Colophon

This expansion was written in the margins of hospice ledgers, on paper that smelled of milk and iron. The ink is lamp-black, cut with the ash of a forgotten name. The binding is gray cord, tied with nine knots. The ninth knot is not cut.

The Cord waits. The Hollowed watches.

The ink is fresh. The road is south.
