The Fenwood-Everblood Chronicles

Setting chronology and the disputed succession

The setting’s present is 840 AR, a few months after Veralyn’s coronation and shortly after Braedyn Fenwood’s death. The chronicles’ faction and age tables describe 838 AR, when Brightstride’s death opened the crisis; they are a historical snapshot, not the current court. Adventures can revisit those years without prescribing their outcomes.

Elianor’s marriages and the two succession questions

  1. The betrothal: Baldvyn and Elianor’s betrothal ended the Province Wars. When King Wymund IV’s queen—Baldvyn’s mother—died, Wymund married Elianor instead.
  2. Wymund’s recorded children: Elianor bore two daughters and Valdais during this marriage. They are recorded as Wymund’s children. Rumors name Baldvyn as the father of some or all three. Elianor is unquestionably their mother.
  3. Wymund → Harald → Baldvyn: Wymund died; his elder son Harald, the Tenday King, succeeded him and died after ten days. Baldvyn, Harald’s younger brother, then became king and married Elianor six months later. Their marriage was scandalous, but their children Wyllmot, Aryka, and Arwyn were the recognized Viterran heirs. Valdais is Wyllmot’s maternal half-brother in the record, possibly his full brother by blood; either way, he is Veralyn’s uncle.
  4. The final marriage: Elianor’s marriage to Baldvyn ended before she married the last Blood King of Vhasia. That final marriage predates Lence’s destruction in 832 AR; it did not wait for Baldvyn’s death in 834 AR. No precise date or legal mechanism for the end of the second marriage is established here.
  5. The Vhasian claim: Valdais claims through Elianor’s Blood kin after his royal cousins died. The last Blood King may have left a son. Whether that son survived, and whether the hidden Dauphin is he, remain open. Elianor’s marriage to the king does not establish the boy’s mother. Neither the boy’s identity nor Valdais’s paternity supplies an automatic answer to the other question.

The early dynastic dates are editorial approximations and are now anchored at c. 785 / c. 797, chosen so that three constraints hold at once: Veralyn is fifteen at Lence in 832 and so is born c. 817; her father Wyllmot is the first child of the Baldvyn–Elianor marriage and so cannot be born before 798; and Valdais, the child of the first marriage, is only just older than Wyllmot, which puts his birth and acknowledgment at 796 and Wymund’s death within the year following. The Everbloods carry the Linnic custom of marrying in the middle teens, which is what makes those generations fit; assume fifteen to seventeen at marriage unless a source says otherwise.

On that anchor: Wymund IV b. c. 746 (39 at Belworth, 51 at Ubbin Falls); Oswyn b. c. 760, fourteen years his brother’s junior, 80 in 840 — his longevity is the point of him, since he outlives his brother, both nephews, a great-nephew and a great-great-nephew; Harald b. c. 765 (32 at Brigh); Baldvyn b. c. 767 (67 at his death in 834); Elianor b. c. 769 (16 at Belworth, 17 at her first marriage, 71 in 840); Braedyn Fenwood b. c. 770, a near-contemporary of Baldvyn rather than a later generation — 15 at Belworth, where they were already fellow second sons who had squired together under Oswyn, 37 at the Concordance, 51 at Charau, 62 at Lence, 70 at his death in 840. This supersedes the earlier derivation of Braedyn’s birth from an age of 66 in 838, which made him a child during the Province Wars. Logan Thornhill, spared as an infant in 797, is about 35 at Coldmorrow.

The Belworth, and what lies on either bank

The river. The Belworth rises in the north of Lence and runs north to south the whole length of both kingdoms, into the Amaranthine Sea. Viterra lies east of it, Vhasia west, and the seam has been the whole politics of both for two hundred years. Most canton and county borders on either bank are tributaries, and nearly every one of them eventually runs into the Belworth.

The Scar. Two tributaries enter the Belworth almost opposite one another — one Vhasian, out of the west, one Viterran, out of the east — and together they make a single continuous cleft across both kingdoms and the river between them. This is the Scar. It is the only natural east–west line in the valley, it divides the north of both kingdoms from the south, and it is where border judgements have been given since before either crown existed. (Seint Michel, the Judge of the Scar, is the Vhasian saint of law that does not bend; his shrines are on it.) Whoever holds the Scar holds the middle of the board.

Viterra, east bank.

  • The Aelerian Foothills hold the top of the kingdom — Renard country, and the country of the other Northern Earls. North over the crags is Ubral, and that border has been skirmished for two centuries. The Mound (the Shadow-Court) is seated in these ridges.
  • The Northern and Eastern Earls sit north of the Viterran tributary — that is, north of the Scar. This is why “going north” and “crossing the Scar” mean the same thing in 840, and why an army on the Scar cuts the north off from everything.
  • Valora, the crownlands and the capital, lies south of the Foothills in the eastern heart of the country, reached from the Belworth along the Viterran arm of the Scar. East of Valora is the Dolmis.
  • House Fenwood holds the Belworth valley from Tarlington south — roughly two thirds of the valley and effectively the western half of Viterra. Tarlington is the northernmost Fenwood holding, and north of it the house holds little but the farmland that feeds the city, east and north of the walls. The Fens and Wood, south of Tarlington, were marsh until the Valvano drained and terraced them.
  • Fairport lies far south on the Belworth: the last great river port, the customs chokepoint, and the transfer point for Dolmis cargo (hence its long quarrel with Marcott). Beyond Fairport is the Black River.
  • The Black River comes west out of the Hyro Mountains, runs mostly east to west, bows north, and forms the southern border of Viterra with Thepyrgos — together with the Belmont Marshes, which do the same work where the river does not — before joining the Belworth near Fairport. It is not a tributary of the valley’s politics so much as the floor of them.

Vhasia, west bank, running north to south and then west.

  • The Belworth’s headwaters are in the north of Lence. Further south, across the water from Tarlington, is a sliver of the Lence Crownlands.
  • Directly south of that sliver, north of the Scar: Aberiel (seat Ravier). Aberiel is a Vhasian duchy held by the Everbloods: a foreign-descended house holding a great fief in a kingdom that is not the one it wears a crown in, doing homage for the fief to a king it treats as an equal everywhere else. It is the reason a king of Viterra is a duke of Vhasia, and the reason one river has been the whole politics of both.
  • South of the Scar, facing the Fens and Wood across the river: Istovar (seat Charel), granted to Oswyn Everblood in 797 when the native house failed. Its seizure during the Province Wars is one of the quarrels that caused them.
  • West of Istovar: Ethor (seat Roulen). North of Ethor: Adria (seat Avimont). Both are Longblood, and both march with Acasia, which lies west of the pair.
  • The remaining cantons fill out the rest of Vhasia, their borders mostly tributaries.

Valdais’s campaign, 840, read off the map. He came up out of Adria through Ethor and through Istovar along the west bank, crossed the Vhasian arm of the Scar into Aberiel — which is a Vhasian duchy in Everblood hands, and therefore the Queen’s seam and not the Queen’s country — and took it in eleven days. He then crossed the Belworth and turned east along the Viterran arm, south of Tarlington, which cuts Veralyn in the north from Valora and from every southern ally she has. He never crossed into the Fens and never marched on Tarlington, which is what a man avenging Lence would have done.

Safiya al-Fhara, and the Diwān

Safiya al-Fhara is the author of the Diwān of Roads and Boards, whose short leaves are in the Concordance and whose best-known page is dated the twenty-ninth night of Shahr al-Hirr, 212 SWR (≈ 804 AR), at the Nine Lanterns Way-House on the reshima kah mharga. The Diwān of Roads is her own long account of how she got there, and is thirty years earlier than the Belworth books and independent of them.

  • Born ≈ 192 SWR to a spice merchant of Ashaan, Rashad al-Mizan, who took the third cup from the Black Hand and was taken off the rolls for it. Her mother left four years earlier, having taught her to read. Her birth name is nowhere in the record and should stay there.
  • Sold at fourteen into a training house kept by Sitt Amina al-Hawd, a secret weaver of Morag: fed first, told the terms once and truly, forbidden letters, with the rest of the terms cut into a board in the hall she could not read. Amina never broke one word she said. That is the point of her.
  • Bought at sixteen for four hundred and seven in Ecktorian silver, on the fourth of Shahr al-Rutab, 208 SWR, by Rurik Dornvael of the Aeler, called Breath-Steady — an Edgewalker, rich past use, sixty-eight, with a glass eye and three gold ringlets in his beard: a scoundrel, a con-man, superb company, and desperately lonely. He named her without asking her name and they walked the Way of Silk for four years.
  • Her father’s end is not in the record and must stay out of it. She returns to Ashaan at forty-four with money and a coat, puts her hand through the same brass grille, and cannot say the name that would make the clerk turn the page. There is no story at the end of that office and nothing should supply one.
  • The Gift of Words. In the Palm-Moon of 209, at a dry well at the edge of the Ykrul marches, she poured a cup in as any Fhara does and a drowned well-keeper answered — a column of steam at dusk, canon to Fhara. She weighed, honestly, her mother’s memory, her father’s, and her love of poetry, and kept all three, and offered her name. “You do not own your name.” — “No. I own my name. I just do not have it.” She won the argument on title against possession, out of Aeler hold-law she had read aloud to a man who could not read, said the name once out loud, and has not been able to say it since. She has eleven languages and takes new ones the way a face arrives. She does not believe any of it happened and says so, and the book gives the two columns and no conclusion. Play it exactly that way: the magic is never confirmed and never denied, and she is a woman who keeps registers.
  • She knew he was lying about the eye and the hands within a fortnight, tested him for two years, never caught him once, and gave it up when she understood that being hunted was the best thing that had happened to him in a decade. This is not a twist and should never be played as one. The open question — which she wrote in a margin at seventeen and which he pointedly declined to answer — is what the lies were for.
  • He freed her the same day and never told her. The deed of manumission is in his own hand, dated and sealed within the hour of the bill of sale, and she found it after his death. He then spent four years pretending he could neither read nor write, so that she would be necessary rather than owned. Both lies were visible the whole time: the honorific, and the fact that he always sat with his blind side toward her.
  • He died at the Nine Lanterns in the Heat-Moon of 212 and is buried east of the yard, above the water-road, at his own request and not in the mountain.
  • She became a chronicler of way-houses and a poet, and she is fluent in three of the Five Daughters and cannot do the other two, and she learned the leaving from him.

Note for narrative work. The Diwān counts in threes and sevens where the Belworth books count in nines and elevens; this is deliberate and should be kept if anything is added to it. She never heard the Ecktorian names of the Five Daughters and should not use them; she found the five threads locally, under local names, in every country she passed through. The three coins — the one that does not clink, the one cold in a vault, the one warm and alive — are his and are quotable anywhere on the road.

The tongues

Utaran is the classical language — the speech of the Utaran Empire, and now the language of law, liturgy, the schools and the archive. It is not spoken as a mother tongue anywhere. Every charter, indiction, writ and instrument in Vhasia, Viterra, Lence and the cantons is drawn in it; every churchman has it; a noblewoman is taught it if her house expects her to read what she signs, and is not if it does not. Canré is the Utaran form; Canray is what the Vhasian court made of the same word.

Utaro is its living descendant, spoken in Ecktoria and the old imperial heartland — the daughter of Utaran the way a court is the daughter of a camp. Utaran and Utaro are close enough that an Ecktorian street-porter can follow a sermon and not a statute, which is a distinction the Censor’s Hall finds convenient.

The Vhasian tongues are Utaran’s children too, at a greater distance. Viterran is written in the Vhasian hand and spoken with a Linnic mouth, and the argument about which of those it really is has been going on for two hundred years and is never about grammar. In the north and the Aelerian foothills, Linn is still the first language in the house and Utaran the only one in the court, and that gap is most of what the Northern Earls are angry about.

Note for narrative work on language. The months of the Civil Count are Utaran words and its last four are Utaran numerals. The months of the Old Count are not Utaran at all, in any kingdom — everyone says them in whatever they speak at home, and they translate cleanly everywhere, which is the plainest evidence there is that the calendar is older than the language of law.

Note for narrative work. The Renards are a Vhas house in Viterra — pushed east over generations out of old Vhas into the Viterran north, keeping the Vhas face and names and grievance while paying Viterran tax. Kaerndal is reached off the Valora road, not from Vhasia; the Aelerian Rising was an internal Viterran rebellion; and Alaysha Renard’s marriage to Wyllmot settled a quarrel inside one kingdom, not between two.

Fenwood men have two names

An old custom of the house, dating to the exile: every male Fenwood is given an Ecktorian name and a Viterran one. The Ecktorian name is for the instrument — rolls, writs, musters, grants, deeds, stones, anything drawn in Utaran, which is to say anything that will outlive him. The Viterran name is for the mouth: what his mother calls him and what his men shout across a yard.

Note for narrative work. Which name a person uses is a declaration of how they know the man, and nobody in the fiction ever explains it. A petitioner who says Constano has read a document; a man who says Conlin was at Tarlington before the war. In the rolls the marshal is only ever Seniro, and no soldier has called him that since Lence.

Two consequences worth keeping:

  • The B. in B. V. Valvano III is Burcerio, not Braedyn, and two centuries of anthologists have expanded it wrongly. He never corrected it.
  • Lerris is on the University rolls as Levis Fenwood. The Ninth Proof has Markus searching for a brother whose name in the record is not the name he calls him — and Markus is the one who refuses his own second name, which is why it would not occur to him.

Istovar, and the two Wymunds

There are two Wymunds in the middle of this chronology and they must not be run together.

  • Wymund IV, King of Viterra and Duke of Aberiel, d. 797 at Ubbin Falls. Elianor’s first husband.
  • Wymund Everblood, eldest son of Oswyn of Istovar, d. 832 at the Ford of Ashes. Note that the Fenwood marshal’s Viterran name is also Wymund and that both men were at Lence, four hundred yards apart, in the same cause, and one of them came out of it with a red byname. Neither family has ever remarked on it. They have noticed. He took the Istovar levy across for Braedyn Fenwood’s tenure quarrel and made the second ply of the weld that broke the Sun Court, and he died doing it.

Istovar moved at Lence. The second ply was not Aberiel; it was Istovar’s own duke, who did not march himself but sent his heir, which is a different and more expensive thing than being bought. This is the fact that explains 840: Oswyn Everblood did not move in the winter of 840 because he buried his eldest son in 832 in another man’s quarrel, and has spent the eight years since making his canton safe for a second son who is not built for it — Harald Everblood, careful and courteous and no soldier (not to be confused with Harald “the Bold,” the Tenday King, d. 797). Elyas, the youngest, was Braedyn Fenwood’s ward at Tarlington and is the Summer Knight. Oswyn has never given the reason and never will, and the books should not give it for him.

Seniro Fenwood, Braedyn’s second son and marshal of the Belworth, is entered in every roll under that name and has not been called it by a soldier since Lence. The army calls him Wymund the Red. Neither family has explained the borrowing and neither has objected to it.

The Lethai-al in the north. The Sundered Kin are in the Aelerian hills and Ubral as they are under the Valewood canopy and out on the sea-cliffs — the same people and the same wound, worked differently by each country that holds them: an age that ended, carried. The northern ones walk the drove ways in file with the beasts and the year, landless and lordless, keeping no feud and taking no side in one, carrying their dead as names and hanging a slat for each on the crossroads shrines. Do not present any one population’s origin story as the Lethai story; each has its own and none of them is the whole of it.

In 774 they stopped an attack on a Rocke yarl at the flint-hills shrine by counting aloud until it stopped happening, and took nothing for it. The yarl’s sixteen-year-old daughter, due to be married that spring, walked out of the pass behind the procession and went to Silkstrand. Riarhin was born there the following year, 775, Lethai-al on her mother’s side. She was seven-and-fifty at Lence and every deposition puts her at six-and-twenty; she is of an age with Braedyn Fenwood and does not look within forty years of him; and nothing in the record says she is dead. A Roll of the Fallen tells the story in a wash-house in 809 and names nobody; Count Your Exits names her and gives the arithmetic. Nothing else should add to either.

Riarhin, a scout in the Fenwood army at Lence, reported to the marshal and to nobody else. She found the village at Coldmorrow doing her own work and reported it correctly; then, drunk, at a fire after the sack, she said the name aloud where a fifteen-year-old girl could hear it. She did not know the girl was there. She has never offered that in her own defence, which is in character for her, and for what she carries: she is the bearer of Remorse, the first of the Three Forged, who chose her not for victory, but for sorrow endured. The books name her and do not name the blade.

The brothers and the Ninth

Markus Fenwood enrolled at the University of Thepyrgos in 826. Lerris followed him in 827. Markus went first and Lerris went after him, being of a scholarly disposition and having watched his brother go; the order matters, because everything that happens to Lerris happens on a road his brother opened.

Markus’s 826 enrollment to Alayse’s 840 letter is fourteen years; Lerris’s 827 enrollment to that letter is thirteen. Their enrollments are one year apart. Lexicus’s 648 fragments precede the present by 192 years, Markus’s enrollment by 178, and Lerris’s by 179. These intervals should not be conflated.

They are Fenwoods, and a Fenwood at Thepyrgos is a Fenwood across the Black River — out of the valley, out of the duchy’s law, and in a city where the Synod files what the family’s hedge-law cannot reach. Nobody in the Belworth books remarks on it. The degree of their kinship to the ducal line is not settled here and should not be settled casually.

The 838 letter concerns Lerris’s return for another term, not his first enrollment. Alayse’s 840 letter recalls Markus’s last visit home; he has returned to Thepyrgos. Her belief that Lerris remains enrolled reflects incomplete knowledge, not proof that he is safe.

Run The Ninth Proof in 838–839 to play through the disappearance, or begin an investigation of its aftermath in 840. Preserve the table’s outcome—rescue, loss, sealing, destruction, or another established result. The chronology does not decide an adventure ending in advance.

The three calendars

Years are Amber Reckoning (AR) everywhere the Empire’s paperwork reached. Three calendars sit under that shared number; the full reference is The Three Reckonings.

  • The Old Count (the Amaranthine Reckoning). Twelve thirty-day months of three tendays each, ten named workdays to a tenday (Yoking, Furrow, Water, Stone, Market, Hammer, Tally, Road, Hearth, Still), plus five uncounted holy days: Greenmeet (spring equinox), the Long Day (summer solstice), Goldmeet (autumn equinox), the Long Night (winter solstice, the year’s last day), and Winter’s Crest (the day after it, the year’s first). A sixth, the Borrowed Day, is added after the Crest one year in four, and nothing sworn on it binds. Months: Mending, Ashing, Thawing, Planting, Greening, Shearing, Haying, Firstfruits, Reaping, Leafturn, Salting, Kindling. This is the calendar of work, rent, hiring and festival, and it is what Braedyn Fenwood means when he dates a letter “Ashing Year 813.”
  • The Civil Count (Ecktorian). Twelve months — Portium, Cinaria, Verrus, Floralis, Maiora, Lavius, Solaris, Utaran, Septhis, Octis, Nonis, Decis — of our own lengths, and a seven-day week named for the lamps of the Basilica: Prima, Fanis, Forensis, Censis, Armis, Curis, Vespris. A leap day, the Ash Day, is inserted at the end of Cinaria one year in four and is not numbered by the Censor’s Hall, so nothing registered on it exists in law. This is the calendar of writs, charters, tax and muster, and it is the one the Belworth novellas date by.
  • The Seven Wells (SWR), Fharic. Pure lunar, 354 days, months Shahr al-* begun on a witnessed first crescent, the day beginning at sunset. 840 AR ≈ 249–250 SWR.

Note for narrative work. Old Count and Civil Count share the year and its length and do not drift; Winter’s Crest falls on the twenty-second of Decis. Use the Old Count for anything happening to people who work and the Civil Count for anything with a seal on it. The Linns and the Aelers keep the Old Count entire; the Ykrul keep neither. Harald Everblood’s ten-day reign is exactly one tenday of the Old Count, which was not arranged by anyone.

A Note on Two Faiths

Two gods haunt this chronicle, and neither is named often, because both are dangerous to name.

Myrmis
The older claim on this family — a goddess of bargains kept in blood, who answers prayers by taking as much as she gives, and whose favored instrument is a blade that does not choose its wielder so much as it accepts them.
Canray, called Kon'reh in the old Aeler tongue
Not a god at all but a game, a discipline, a way of thinking that the Fenwoods carried out of their exile: patience, clauses, the slow multiplication of small advantages, the belief that every debt can be resolved if you are willing to wait long enough and write it down correctly.

Myrmis is the fire that answers a scream. Canray is the long patience that answers a silence. Every figure in this chronicle chooses one, or is chosen by one, or spends their life trying to hold both at once and is torn for it. Where the histories below feel haunted — a hound sacrificed, a slate left unfinished, a blade that will not dull — that is the shape of the choice underneath. Watch for it. It does not announce itself.

A Note on Women and the Law

In both Vhasia and Viterra, women may inherit lands and designate their property to whomever they wish. A man cannot wrest land from his wife by right of marriage alone. Titles come with land and vassalage, but a landed woman may own more than her lord husband. Women warriors are rare but not unheard of. Neither Vhasia nor Viterra, however, will knight a woman. This legal paradox — the right to rule without the right to bear arms — shapes every conflict in the chronicles below. Viterra practices male-preference primogeniture for titles (equal inheritance for plain property); Vhasia practices agnatic-cognatic succession for titles, with the same equal rule for property.

The Baldvyn Dynasty — A Clarification

To avoid confusion: there are three Baldvyns in this chronicle, spanning four generations.

BaldvynIdentity
Baldvyn the Good (King Baldvyn II)Braedyn Fenwood's sworn brother and one-time bedmate, second son of King Wymund IV. Married Elianor of House Longblood, his father's widow, six months after his elder brother Harald's death. Father of Wyllmot. Died of a wasting stomach cancer in 834 AR.
Baldvyn "Brightstride" (King Baldvyn III)Son of Wyllmot and Queen Alaysha, grandson of Baldvyn the Good, brother of Veralyn. The beloved young king, poisoned in 838 AR.
Baldvyn MaesonA Fenwood cousin, twelve years old in 838 AR, named for the old king out of love rather than descent. Mentioned here only so no reader mistakes him for a claimant.

The peace ending the Province Wars rested on the betrothal of Baldvyn and Elianor of House Longblood. When Baldvyn’s mother, King Wymund IV’s queen, died, Wymund married Elianor himself. Their marriage produced two daughters and Valdais, all recorded as Wymund’s children. Rumor names Baldvyn as the father of some or all three; it does not dispute Elianor’s motherhood. After Wymund died, his elder son Harald reigned for ten days before dying at Brigh. Baldvyn then became king and married his father’s widow six months later. The marriage was a scandal, but their children Wyllmot, Aryka, and Arwyn became the recognized Viterran heirs. After that marriage ended, Elianor married the last Blood King of Vhasia. When Valdais’s Blood cousins died at Lence, he pressed his Vhasian claim through his mother’s kin. The last Blood King may have left a son, however. Neither that son’s existence and identity nor Valdais’s disputed paternity is settled here; they are separate questions.

A Note on Queen Alaysha

Queen Alaysha is Wyllmot's wife and the mother of Baldvyn Brightstride and Veralyn Demon-Eye. She is a Renard by birth — freakishly tall, even beside her husband, with the pale coloring and colorless eyes of old Vhasia — and her family's secret devotion to Myrmis would prove disastrous for the crown. She is not to be confused with Alayse Everblood, Baldvyn the Good's youngest sister, whose rape by Branton Thornhill began the Thornhill Rebellion decades before Alaysha ever came to court; nor with Veralyn Renard, a third and separate ancestor, three generations removed, who married Harald III and won the Renards their long forbearance of Myrmis worship. Vhasians consider it an ill omen that the names recur so closely. They say Myrmis borrows names the way a house reuses a room — the same walls, a different woman in them, and the same weather coming through.

On the eve of her marriage to Wyllmot, Alaysha prayed to Myrmis for two great warriors and three lovely daughters, sacrificing her favorite hound on a cold altar within sight of the Belworth. She got two children, not five: a son and a daughter, both warriors, no daughters to speak of beyond Veralyn herself. She has spent every year since wondering whether the goddess shorted the bargain out of spite, or fulfilled it exactly, and simply priced a warrior-daughter as worth three ordinary ones. Her prayer set in motion the events that would claim her husband's life at Eryas and poison her son's legacy long before any assassin's hand was needed.

The Chronicle, Year by Year

780 AR Year -58

The Province War

A war between the Viterran-aligned provinces of Istovar and Aberiel and the loyalist provinces of Ethor, Adria, Ylerea, and Lence. A decisive victory for King Wymund IV “the Smiling King,” who crushed the rebels and solidified the Everblood hold on the eastern marches. It set the table for the Thornhill Rebellion fourteen years later; many of the grievances that erupted in the Longblood Defiance and the Sack of Lence were seeded here, including the enduring Everblood-Longblood rivalry over Ethor and Adria.

Key Figures: King Wymund IV (Viterra) — victorious, but exhausted; Duke Oswyn Everblood (Aberiel) — loyalist commander; the Bloods of Lence — fought for the crown; the Ethor Marchers — brought to heel.

794 AR Year -44

The Thornhill Rebellion

The Thornhills, a Linnic house in northwestern Viterra (Brigh), rose against the crown after Lord Branton Thornhill raped his ward, Alayse Everblood — the youngest sister of Wymund IV's sons. King Wymund IV died avenging the rape, falling at the Battle of Ubbin Falls. His heir, Harald “the Bold”, reigned exactly ten days before an arrow took his eye at the Siege of Brigh — the origin of his crueler epithet, the Tenday King. Baldvyn — the second son, the unexpected king — was crowned a fortnight later and rode out at once to storm Brigh, slaying Branton in single combat. House Thornhill was extinguished; its survivors scattered. Braedyn Fenwood, then a young knight and Baldvyn's sworn brother, caught the new king's hand as he drew a dagger to kill the Thornhill heir: “He is a child. Prune him gently, or the root will spread.” The child was spared. Thirty-eight years later, the root would be pulled from Coldmorrow — by the mace of Baldvyn's own granddaughter.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the unexpected king; Branton Thornhill — rapist and rebel, slain in single combat; Duke Oswyn Everblood — defender of Aberiel; Logan Thornhill — the infant spared at his father's body, the debt that would come due twenty-eight years later.

821 AR Year -17

The Longblood Defiance

After the death of Arwyn of Adria, Queen Elianor and King Baldvyn the Good had a falling out. The queen rallied Adria, her cousins in Ethor, and the Bloods of Lence against her husband. Valdais Longblood — Elianor's son by Wymund IV, or, as the court whispered even then, by Baldvyn himself — sided with his mother; Wyllmot, the king's undisputed son, sided with his father. It ended at the Battle of Charau in a decisive victory for Baldvyn, who incarcerated his wife but pardoned Valdais — a mercy later remembered, and resented. Braedyn Fenwood, who commanded the left flank and captured Valdais himself, told the king: “A wrong left standing is a leash.” The leash held for thirteen years. After Baldvyn's death, it would snap.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the victor; Queen Elianor — the rebel, the exile; Valdais Longblood — the pardoned claimant; Wyllmot — the loyal son, the future king.

832 AR Year -6

The Sack of Lence

The Vhasian king rebuked House Fenwood for holding lands in Vhasia while refusing to pay homage. Duke Braedyn Fenwood “rebelled” — a convenient fiction — and invaded Vhasia, burning Lence to the ground; the major houses of Istovar joined him, and the Everbloods of Aberiel followed under Wymund, son of Oswyn. Lence was sacked, pillaged, the last Blood King and known royal Blood cousins killed, with reports of a surviving son unproved. Princess Veralyn Everblood — fifteen, dressed as a page, hidden in a supply wagon — squired for Seniro Fenwood against her father's express wishes. After the sack, she tracked Logan Thornhill — the child spared thirty-eight years before — to a village in the royal domain: Coldmorrow. He wounded her cousin Lyonel; she killed him with a mace, and burned the village in her rage. She was sixteen. Logan's own infant daughter, Ylena, survived; raised by strangers, trained as a courtesan, she would return to Veralyn's own court a generation later, wearing another woman's household as a disguise.

Key Figures: Duke Braedyn Fenwood — architect of the invasion; Seniro Fenwood — Veralyn's mentor and protector; Princess Veralyn Everblood — the young killer; Lyonel Vayle — the wounded cousin; Logan Thornhill — about thirty-eight, the infant of Brigh grown, the debt paid in blood; and his daughter Ylena, twelve years old, who watched it paid and did not look away.

834 AR Year -4

The Death of King Baldvyn the Good

King Baldvyn the Good, feeling himself dying, pardoned Queen Elianor, ending her exile. Before he died he threw a grand tourney — a final celebration of chivalry. Princess Veralyn, though not a knight, was permitted to compete: she unhorsed Sir Arin Tymber, Sir Lerris Fenwood, and Sir Ouen Longblood (the last insulted her; she killed his horse in the tilt), was defeated by her friend and cousin Sir Tylan Vayle, who was then defeated by her brother, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride. The old king watched and wept — not from sadness, but from pride.

836 AR Year -2

The Seven Week Uprising

A fanatical sect of Myrmis worshippers rose against King Wyllmot “the Warrior,” assassinating nobles in retaliation for the destruction of their holy sites. Wyllmot besieged their mountain fortress, Eryas Citadel, and died attempting to storm it. Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn Demoneye crushed the rebellion in seven weeks — Veralyn, fighting the sect's champion, her distant cousin Gelfrey Renard, disarmed and killed him on an altar to Myrmis with his own dagger. A blade was found beside the altar afterward, and every person present agreed that none of them had set it down — agreed readily, and with a confidence that ought to have troubled somebody. Perfectly balanced, unmarked, edge unchipped. She has not put it down since, and has never given a reason for that which satisfies anyone, herself included. The court calls it Shadowsteel. Many Renards were secretly Myrmis worshippers, including Queen Alaysha; some at court presume she engineered the Uprising herself, to appease a goddess she felt had been shorted. There is no proof, only the question of who benefited and who did not sleep for seven weeks.

Key Figures: King Wyllmot — killed at Eryas; Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn — who crushed the rebellion; Gelfrey Renard — the Shadow-Court's champion, slain on the altar; Queen Alaysha — whose secret faith was exposed.

837 AR Year -1

The Incident of Aleric

Arwyn's son, Aleric, was removed from the Viterran line of succession for plotting against his cousin, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride, with his aunt Aryka. Both were exiled to Acasia — not the Petty Kingdoms as some later, garbled retellings claim, a distinction that matters, because Acasia keeps its own account of exiles, and its own opinions about what Viterra owes them. Aleric's claim was broken, but he did not forget.

Key Figures: Prince Baldvyn Brightstride — the intended victim; Aleric Renard — the exiled claimant; Aryka Renard — the conspirator.

838 AR Year 0

The Death of Baldvyn Brightstride

Prince Baldvyn Brightstride fell ill and died suddenly; the kingdom mourned the loss of their beloved young king. He was suspected of being poisoned — likely by someone seeking revenge for Lence or the Seven Week Uprising. The poison was subtle, slow-acting, and untraceable, administered weeks before symptoms appeared. The prime suspect was Jaennis Fenwood, his young queen, Braedyn's granddaughter — small and waifish, married to Baldvyn barely a season before his death, rumored to have taken abortifacients in secret rather than bear his child. The evidence was circumstantial, and the accusation would have meant war with the Fenwoods. No one spoke the suspicion aloud.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn Brightstride — the beloved, the poisoned; Queen Jaennis Fenwood — the suspected poisoner; Princess Veralyn Demoneye — the heir, the warrior.

The Current Crisis (838 AR)

Veralyn is the acknowledged Duchess of Aberiel and the named heir of King Baldvyn Brightstride. Her grandmother, Elianor, and her uncle Valdais seek to expel the Everbloods from Vhasia. House Fenwood is propping her up. Her cousin Aleric, exiled to Acasia, presses his claim as the eldest male of the major line. Adria and Ethor are for Valdais; Aberiel and Lence are for Veralyn; Istovar is split.

Veralyn is unsure of being anything more than a killer. Her challenges: learning to be a leader, not just a soldier; learning diplomacy, the art of the table rather than the blade; learning that a ruler has no friends, only allies and debts; learning who to trust, and knowing everyone has a price; carrying the trauma of her father's rejection and the Temple's misogyny; confronting the self-hatred for what she did at Coldmorrow.

A Note on the Queen's Household

Veralyn — queen in everything but the crowning — keeps fair company about her and has never troubled to explain it. The court settled on preference some years ago and moved along to more profitable gossip. Jaennis, who chooses the ladies, is said to share it, and to choose accordingly: women who will abide, who will not carry it out of the room, and who understand that being chosen is itself a kind of appointment.

Ylena of Coldmorrow was chosen. She has spent two years within arm's reach of the woman who killed her father, and has never once given anyone cause to look at her twice. The household believes it lifted a clever girl out of a burned village. The girl has her own view of who lifted whom.

A Note on What Follows

This chronicle ends at the open wound of 838 AR, with the crown unclaimed and the factions still circling. It does not resolve here. Within the Demon's Eye carries the story through Veralyn's coronation and Duke Braedyn Fenwood's death in 840 AR, recorded in his own words in The Old Duke's Wars. Two older adventures reach further back. The Reckoning Bridge, set in 640 AR, records the Linns' arrival at Tarlington Gap and the first oaths between the Fenwoods and the Everbloods-to-be. The Exile Road, set in 337 AR, follows the Valvano cadet branch fleeing Acasia into the protection of a Tarling dux not yet calling himself king. The names recur, but inheritance changes their meaning. Each generation receives protection purchased by someone else's compromise and must decide whether gratitude requires repeating it.

GM Guidance: The Shadow of the Past

Every event in this timeline is a thread the party may pull. Consider player characters as: survivors of Lence seeking justice or vengeance; Fenwood loyalists who have seen too much; Veralyn's companions, the only people she trusts; agents of the Shadow-Court manipulating the crisis for Myrmis's ends; Temple inquisitors seeking to expose the heresy that brought the crown low; or Acasian exiles, allies of Aleric Renard.

The timeline is not a cage. It is a family argument conducted across graves. The party will meet descendants sheltered by old betrayals, victims asked to accept apologies from people who did not strike the blow, and heirs who confuse gratitude with obedience because their childhood depended on both. Beneath it run two traditions: the goddess who answers fear with strength and asks what that strength will destroy, and the Fenwood discipline of patience, clauses, and survival in another house's country. Let the campaign ask when an inheritance becomes a duty, when it becomes complicity, and whether repair is possible without pretending the past can be settled.
