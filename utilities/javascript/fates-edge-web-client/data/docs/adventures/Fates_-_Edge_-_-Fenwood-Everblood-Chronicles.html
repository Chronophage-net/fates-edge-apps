The Fenwood-Everblood Chronicles

Setting chronology and the disputed succession

The setting’s present is 840 AR, after Veralyn’s coronation. The shared northern and Belworth campaign takes place in spring 841 AR; Braedyn Fenwood’s death is undated, and Riarhin knows he is dead by The Thread. The chronicles’ faction and age tables describe 838 AR, when Brightstride’s death opened the crisis; they are a historical snapshot, not the current court. Adventures can revisit those years without prescribing their outcomes.

Elianor’s marriages and the two succession questions

  1. The betrothal: Baldvyn and Elianor’s betrothal ended the Province Wars. When King Wymund IV’s queen—Baldvyn’s mother—died, Wymund married Elianor instead.
  2. Wymund’s recorded children: Elianor bore two daughters and Valdais during this marriage. They are recorded as Wymund’s children. Rumors name Baldvyn as the father of some or all three. Elianor is unquestionably their mother.
  3. Wymund → Harald → Baldvyn: Wymund died; his elder son Harald, the Tenday King, succeeded him and died after ten days. Baldvyn, Harald’s younger brother, then became king and married Elianor six months later. Their marriage was scandalous, but their children Wyllmot, Aryka, and Arwyn were the recognized Viterran heirs. Valdais is Wyllmot’s maternal half-brother in the record, possibly his full brother by blood; either way, he is Veralyn’s uncle.
  4. The final marriage: Elianor’s marriage to Baldvyn ended before she married the last Blood King of Vhasia. That final marriage predates Lence’s destruction in 832 AR; it did not wait for Baldvyn’s death in 834 AR. No precise date or legal mechanism for the end of the second marriage is established here.
  5. The Vhasian claim: Valdais claims through Elianor’s Blood kin after his royal cousins died. The last Blood King may have left a son. Whether that son survived, and whether the hidden Dauphin is he, remain open. Elianor’s marriage to the king does not establish the boy’s mother. Neither the boy’s identity nor Valdais’s paternity supplies an automatic answer to the other question.

The early dynastic dates are editorial approximations and are now anchored at c. 785 / c. 797, chosen so that three constraints hold at once: Veralyn is fifteen at Lence in 832 and so is born c. 817; her father Wyllmot is the first child of the Baldvyn–Elianor marriage and so cannot be born before 798; and Valdais, the child of the first marriage, is only just older than Wyllmot, which puts his birth and acknowledgment at 796 and Wymund’s death within the year following. The Everbloods carry the Linnic custom of marrying in the middle teens, which is what makes those generations fit; assume fifteen to seventeen at marriage unless a source says otherwise.

On that anchor: Wymund IV b. c. 746 (39 at Belworth, 51 at Ubbin Falls); Oswyn b. c. 760, fourteen years his brother’s junior, 80 in 840 — his longevity is the point of him, since he outlives his brother, both nephews, a great-nephew and a great-great-nephew; Harald b. c. 765 (32 at Brigh); Baldvyn b. c. 767 (67 at his death in 834); Elianor b. c. 769 (16 at Belworth, 17 at her first marriage, 71 in 840); Braedyn Fenwood b. c. 770, a near-contemporary of Baldvyn rather than a later generation — 15 at Belworth, where they were already fellow second sons who had squired together under Oswyn, 37 at the Concordance, 51 at Charau, 62 at Lence, about 70 at Veralyn’s coronation; his death is undated. This supersedes the earlier derivation of Braedyn’s birth from an age of 66 in 838, which made him a child during the Province Wars. Logan Thornhill, spared as an infant in 797, is about 35 at Coldmorrow.

The Belworth, and what lies on either bank

The river. The Belworth rises in the north of Lence and runs north to south the whole length of both kingdoms, into the Amaranthine Sea. Viterra lies east of it, Vhasia west, and the seam has been the whole politics of both for two hundred years. Most canton and county borders on either bank are tributaries, and nearly every one of them eventually runs into the Belworth.

The Scar. Two tributaries enter the Belworth almost opposite one another — one Vhasian, out of the west, one Viterran, out of the east — and together they make a single continuous cleft across both kingdoms and the river between them. This is the Scar. It is the only natural east–west line in the valley, it divides the north of both kingdoms from the south, and it is where border judgements have been given since before either crown existed. (Seint Michel, the Judge of the Scar, is the Vhasian saint of law that does not bend; his shrines are on it.) Whoever holds the Scar holds the middle of the board.

Viterra, east bank.

  • The Aelerian Foothills hold the top of the kingdom — Renard country, and the country of the other Northern Earls. North over the crags is Ubral, and that border has been skirmished for two centuries. The Mound (the Shadow-Court) is seated in these ridges.
  • The Northern and Eastern Earls sit north of the Viterran tributary — that is, north of the Scar. This is why “going north” and “crossing the Scar” mean the same thing in the 841 campaign, and why an army on the Scar cuts the north off from everything.
  • Valora, the crownlands and the capital, lies south of the Foothills in the eastern heart of the country, reached from the Belworth along the Viterran arm of the Scar. East of Valora is the Dolmis.
  • House Fenwood holds the Belworth valley from Tarlington south — roughly two thirds of the valley and effectively the western half of Viterra. Tarlington is the northernmost Fenwood holding, and north of it the house holds little but the farmland that feeds the city, east and north of the walls. The Fens and Wood, south of Tarlington, were marsh until the Valvano drained and terraced them.
  • Fairport lies far south on the Belworth: the last great river port, the customs chokepoint, and the transfer point for Dolmis cargo (hence its long quarrel with Marcott). Beyond Fairport is the Black River.
  • The Black River comes west out of the Hyro Mountains, runs mostly east to west, bows north, and forms the southern border of Viterra with Thepyrgos — together with the Belmont Marshes, which do the same work where the river does not — before joining the Belworth near Fairport. It is not a tributary of the valley’s politics so much as the floor of them.

Vhasia, west bank, running north to south and then west.

  • The Belworth’s headwaters are in the north of Lence. Further south, across the water from Tarlington, is a sliver of the Lence Crownlands.
  • Directly south of that sliver, north of the Scar: Aberiel (seat Ravier). Aberiel is a Vhasian duchy held by the Everbloods: a foreign-descended house holding a great fief in a kingdom that is not the one it wears a crown in, doing homage for the fief to a king it treats as an equal everywhere else. It is the reason a king of Viterra is a duke of Vhasia, and the reason one river has been the whole politics of both.
  • South of the Scar, facing the Fens and Wood across the river: Istovar (seat Charel), granted to Oswyn Everblood in 797 when the native house failed. Its seizure during the Province Wars is one of the quarrels that caused them.
  • West of Istovar: Ethor (seat Roulen). North of Ethor: Adria (seat Avimont). Both are Longblood, and both march with Acasia, which lies west of the pair.
  • The remaining cantons fill out the rest of Vhasia, their borders mostly tributaries.

Valdais’s campaign, 841, read off the map. He came up out of Adria through Ethor and through Istovar along the west bank, crossed the Vhasian arm of the Scar into Aberiel — which is a Vhasian duchy in Everblood hands, and therefore the Queen’s seam and not the Queen’s country — and took it in eleven days. He then crossed the Belworth at Kettling in Lavius, losing thirty-one men and four days, and turned east along the Viterran arm, south of Tarlington, which cuts Veralyn in the north from Valora and from every southern ally she has. He never crossed into the Fens and never marched on Tarlington, which is what a man avenging Lence would have done.

Safiya al-Fhara, and the Diwān

Safiya al-Fhara is the author of the Diwān of Roads and Boards, whose short leaves are in the Concordance and whose best-known page is dated the twenty-ninth night of Shahr al-Hirr, 212 SWR (≈ 804 AR), at the Nine Lanterns Way-House on the reshima kah mharga. The Diwān of Roads is her own long account of how she got there, and is thirty years earlier than the Belworth books and independent of them.

  • Born ≈ 192 SWR to a spice merchant of Ashaan, Rashad al-Mizan, who took the third cup from the Black Hand and was taken off the rolls for it. Her mother left four years earlier, having taught her to read. Her birth name is nowhere in the record and should stay there.
  • Sold at fourteen into a training house kept by Sitt Amina al-Hawd, a secret weaver of Morag: fed first, told the terms once and truly, forbidden letters, with the rest of the terms cut into a board in the hall she could not read. Amina never broke one word she said. That is the point of her.
  • Bought at sixteen for four hundred and seven in Ecktorian silver, on the fourth of Shahr al-Rutab, 208 SWR, by Rurik Dornvael of the Aeler, called Breath-Steady — an Edgewalker, rich past use, sixty-eight, with a glass eye and three gold ringlets in his beard: a scoundrel, a con-man, superb company, and desperately lonely. He named her without asking her name and they walked the Way of Silk for four years.
  • Her father’s end is not in the record and must stay out of it. She returns to Ashaan at forty-four, in 236 SWR with money and a coat, puts her hand through the same brass grille, and cannot say the name that would make the clerk turn the page. There is no story at the end of that office and nothing should supply one.
  • The Gift of Words. In the Palm-Moon of 209, when she is about seventeen, at a dry well at the edge of the Ykrul marches, she poured a cup in as any Fhara does and a drowned well-keeper answered — a column of steam at dusk, canon to Fhara. She weighed, honestly, her mother’s memory, her father’s, and her love of poetry, and kept all three, and offered her name. “You do not own your name.” — “No. I own my name. I just do not have it.” She won the argument on title against possession, out of Aeler hold-law she had read aloud to a man who could not read, said the name once out loud, and has not been able to say it since. She could already read Fharic, including the way-house book and translations of Aeler law. Her bill of sale and the manumission deed are in Utaran, called Ecktorian on this road; she cannot read that language at the sale. Utaran comes after the well. She can read the bill when she finds it again after Rurik’s death. She has eleven languages and takes new ones the way a face arrives. She does not believe any of it happened and says so, and the book gives the two columns and no conclusion. Play it exactly that way: the magic is never confirmed and never denied, and she is a woman who keeps registers.
  • She knew he was lying about the eye and the hands within a tenday, tested him for two years, never caught him once, and gave it up when she understood that being hunted was the best thing that had happened to him in a decade. This is not a twist and should never be played as one. The open question — which she wrote in a margin at seventeen and which he pointedly declined to answer — is what the lies were for.
  • He freed her the same day and never told her. The deed of manumission is in his own hand, dated and sealed within the hour of the bill of sale, and she found it after his death. He then spent four years pretending he could neither read nor write, so that she would be necessary rather than owned. Both lies were visible the whole time: the honorific, and the fact that he always sat with his blind side toward her.
  • He died at the Nine Lanterns in the Heat-Moon of 212 and is buried east of the yard, above the water-road, at his own request and not in the mountain.
  • She became a chronicler of way-houses and a poet, and she is fluent in three of the Five Daughters and cannot do the other two, and she learned the leaving from him.

Note for narrative work. The Diwān counts in threes and sevens where the Belworth books count in nines and elevens; this is deliberate and should be kept if anything is added to it. She never heard the Ecktorian names of the Five Daughters and should not use them; she found the five threads locally, under local names, in every country she passed through. The three coins — the one that does not clink, the one cold in a vault, the one warm and alive — are his and are quotable anywhere on the road.

The tongues

Utaran is the classical language — the speech of the Utaran Empire, and now the language of law, liturgy, the schools and the archive. It is not spoken as a mother tongue anywhere. Every charter, indiction, writ and instrument in Vhasia, Viterra, Lence and the cantons is drawn in it; every churchman has it; a noblewoman is taught it if her house expects her to read what she signs, and is not if it does not. Canré is the Utaran form; Canray is what the Vhasian court made of the same word.

Utaro is its living descendant, spoken in Ecktoria and the old imperial heartland — the daughter of Utaran the way a court is the daughter of a camp. Utaran and Utaro are close enough that an Ecktorian street-porter can follow a sermon and not a statute, which is a distinction the Censor’s Hall finds convenient.

The Vhasian tongues are Utaran’s children too, at a greater distance. Viterran is written in the Vhasian hand and spoken with a Linnic mouth, and the argument about which of those it really is has been going on for two hundred years and is never about grammar. In the north and the Aelerian foothills, Linn is still the first language in the house and Utaran the only one in the court, and that gap is most of what the Northern Earls are angry about.

Note for narrative work on language. The months of the Civil Count are Utaran words and its last four are Utaran numerals. The months of the Old Count are not Utaran at all, in any kingdom — everyone says them in whatever they speak at home, and they translate cleanly everywhere, which is the plainest evidence there is that the calendar is older than the language of law.

Note for narrative work. The Renards are a Vhas house in Viterra — pushed east over generations out of old Vhas into the Viterran north, keeping the Vhas face and names and grievance while paying Viterran tax. Kaerndal is reached off the Valora road, not from Vhasia; the Aelerian Rising was an internal Viterran rebellion; and Alaysha Renard’s marriage to Wyllmot settled a quarrel inside one kingdom, not between two.

Fenwood men have two names

An old custom of the house, dating to the exile: every male Fenwood is given an Ecktorian name and a Viterran one. The Ecktorian name is for the instrument — rolls, writs, musters, grants, deeds, stones, anything drawn in Utaran, which is to say anything that will outlive him. The Viterran name is for the mouth: what his mother calls him and what his men shout across a yard.

Braedyn Fenwood’s five sons

He had five and found a use for every one of them.

  • Conlin / Constano — the banker. The eldest. Born with a foot that turns; not a warrior and never was; has never sat a horse to war and never will. He runs the money and he is the best of them, and no ballad has ever been made about him. Duke from 840.
  • Wymund / Seniro — the warrior. Marshal of the Belworth. The army began calling him Wymund the Red at Charau in 821, at two-and-twenty, for a reason that is in four depositions and not the same reason in any of them; after Lence nobody used anything else.
  • Braedyn the Younger / Burcerio the Younger — the diplomat. The talker, sent where a Fenwood must be seen and not fought. He is the only one with no Ecktorian name of his own: on paper he is his father repeated, which is to say that in every instrument he has ever signed he is a repetition, and he is extremely good at his work. The books do not suggest those two facts are related and do not have to.
  • Markus — the student. At the University of Thepyrgos from 826. Refuses his second name and gives a different reason every time he is asked.
  • Lerris / Levis — the wastrel. Sent after Markus in 827 “being of a scholarly disposition,” which is what that family calls a fifth son who will not settle to anything. Later a cultist of the Witness and a hunter of monsters; he appears in Horror Campaigns, The Witnessed Prey, and The Ninth Proof, in which he is the one who disappears.

Note for narrative work. Which name a person uses is a declaration of how they know the man, and nobody in the fiction ever explains it. A petitioner who says Constano has read a document; a man who says Conlin was at Tarlington before the war. In the rolls the marshal is only ever Seniro, and no soldier has called him that since Lence.

Two consequences worth keeping:

  • The B. in B. V. Valvano III is Burcerio, not Braedyn, and two centuries of anthologists have expanded it wrongly. He never corrected it.
  • Lerris is on the University rolls as Levis Fenwood. The Ninth Proof has Markus searching for a brother whose name in the record is not the name he calls him — and Markus is the one who refuses his own second name, which is exactly why it would not occur to him to ask for the other one.

Istovar, and the two Wymunds

There are two Wymunds in the middle of this chronology and they must not be run together.

  • Wymund IV, King of Viterra and Duke of Aberiel, d. 797 at Ubbin Falls. Elianor’s first husband.
  • Wymund Everblood, eldest son of Oswyn of Istovar, d. 832 at the Ford of Ashes. Note that the Fenwood marshal’s Viterran name is also Wymund and that both men were at Lence, four hundred yards apart, in the same cause, and one of them came out of it with a red byname. Neither family has ever remarked on it. They have noticed. He took the Istovar levy across for Braedyn Fenwood’s tenure quarrel and made the second ply of the weld that broke the Sun Court, and he died doing it.

Istovar moved at Lence. The second ply was not Aberiel; it was Istovar’s own duke, who did not march himself but sent his heir, which is a different and more expensive thing than being bought. This is the fact that explains 840: Oswyn Everblood did not move in the winter of 840 because he buried his eldest son in 832 in another man’s quarrel, and has spent the eight years since making his canton safe for a second son who is not built for it — Harald Everblood, careful and courteous and no soldier (not to be confused with Harald “the Bold,” the Tenday King, d. 797). Elyas, the youngest, was Braedyn Fenwood’s ward at Tarlington and is the Summer Knight. Oswyn has never given the reason and never will, and the books should not give it for him.

Seniro Fenwood, Braedyn’s second son and marshal of the Belworth, is entered in every roll under that name and has not been called it by a soldier since Lence. The army calls him Wymund the Red. Neither family has explained the borrowing and neither has objected to it.

The Lethai-al in the north. The Sundered Kin are in the Aelerian hills and Ubral as they are under the Valewood canopy and out on the sea-cliffs — the same people and the same wound, worked differently by each country that holds them: an age that ended, carried. The northern ones walk the drove ways in file with the beasts and the year, landless and lordless, keeping no feud and taking no side in one, carrying their dead as names and hanging a slat for each on the crossroads shrines. Do not present any one population’s origin story as the Lethai story; each has its own and none of them is the whole of it.

In 774 they stopped an attack on a Rocke yarl at the flint-hills shrine by counting aloud until it stopped happening, and took nothing for it. The yarl’s sixteen-year-old daughter, due to be married that spring, walked out of the pass behind the procession and went to Silkstrand, and one of the file went with her, or was already going, which is not the same thing and the Rocke telling has never been careful about which. Riarhin was born there the following year, 775: her mother a Rocke and a yarl’s daughter, her father Lethai-al — styled a prince in the Rocke telling, which is a Rocke word and not a Lethai-al one, and which nobody has ever been able to make mean anything. She is Lethai-al on her father’s side. She was seven-and-fifty at Lence and every deposition puts her at six-and-twenty; she is of an age with Braedyn Fenwood and does not look within forty years of him; and nothing in the record says she is dead. A Roll of the Fallen tells the story in a wash-house in 809 and names nobody; Count Your Exits names her and gives the arithmetic. Nothing else should add to either.

Riarhin, a scout in the Fenwood army at Lence, reported to the marshal and to nobody else. She found the village at Coldmorrow doing her own work and reported it correctly; then, drunk, at a fire after the sack, she said the name aloud where a fifteen-year-old girl could hear it. She did not know the girl was there. She has never offered that in her own defence, which is in character for her, and for what she carries: she is the bearer of Remorse, the first of the Three Forged, who chose her not for victory, but for sorrow endured. The books name her and do not print the blade’s name.

What Remorse is. A sword, not a dagger — and that is the first strange thing about it, because she carries two knives and neither of them is this. Narrow, straight, double-edged, no belly, no working edge. It is wrong for a Lethai-al blade, and wrong in the one way that matters: a Lethai-al blade is also a tool, and will cut a rope and quarter a deer and shave a stake, and every Lethai-al who has ever seen this one has looked at it slightly too long. It will do none of those things. It is for killing people and it is for nothing else, and nobody has ever explained that, and she has never once asked.

She knows the name and has no idea what it means. She has had it since before Lence. She says it the way you say a word in a language you do not speak.

The two hands. She draws Remorse for work she would call clean — a rescue, a duel taken up on someone else’s behalf, a thing done in the open and in daylight — and she draws Toothpick, which was Tulgoth’s Fate’s Edge, for the other kind. She sorted her whole life into two hands without deciding to, and she did not notice for a very long time, and anyone travelling with her can see it inside a month. Nobody who has noticed has told her. Do not let the books tell her either.

The brothers and the Ninth

Markus Fenwood enrolled at the University of Thepyrgos in 826. Lerris followed him in 827. Markus went first and Lerris went after him, being of a scholarly disposition and having watched his brother go; the order matters, because everything that happens to Lerris happens on a road his brother opened.

Markus’s 826 enrollment to Alayse’s 840 letter is fourteen years; Lerris’s 827 enrollment to that letter is thirteen. Their enrollments are one year apart. Lexicus’s 648 fragments precede the present by 192 years, Markus’s enrollment by 178, and Lerris’s by 179. These intervals should not be conflated.

They are Fenwoods, and a Fenwood at Thepyrgos is a Fenwood across the Black River — out of the valley, out of the duchy’s law, and in a city where the Synod files what the family’s hedge-law cannot reach. Nobody in the Belworth books remarks on it. The degree of their kinship to the ducal line is not settled here and should not be settled casually.

The 838 letter concerns Lerris’s return for another term, not his first enrollment. Alayse’s 840 letter recalls Markus’s last visit home; he has returned to Thepyrgos. Her belief that Lerris remains enrolled reflects incomplete knowledge, not proof that he is safe.

Run The Ninth Proof in 838–839 to play through the disappearance, or begin an investigation of its aftermath in 840. Preserve the table’s outcome—rescue, loss, sealing, destruction, or another established result. The chronology does not decide an adventure ending in advance.

The three calendars

Years are Amber Reckoning (AR) everywhere the Empire’s paperwork reached. Three calendars sit under that shared number; the full reference is The Three Reckonings.

  • The Old Count (the Amaranthine Reckoning). Twelve thirty-day months of three tendays each, ten named workdays to a tenday (Yoking, Furrow, Water, Stone, Market, Hammer, Tally, Road, Hearth, Still), plus five uncounted holy days: Greenmeet (spring equinox), the Long Day (summer solstice), Goldmeet (autumn equinox), the Long Night (winter solstice, the year’s last day), and Winter’s Crest (the day after it, the year’s first). A sixth, the Borrowed Day, is added after the Crest one year in four, and nothing sworn on it binds. Months: Mending, Ashing, Thawing, Planting, Greening, Shearing, Haying, Firstfruits, Reaping, Leafturn, Salting, Kindling. This is the calendar of work, rent, hiring and festival, and it is what Braedyn Fenwood means when he dates a letter “Ashing Year 813.”
  • The Civil Count (Ecktorian). Twelve months — Portium, Cinaria, Verrus, Floralis, Maiora, Lavius, Solaris, Utaran, Septhis, Octis, Nonis, Decis — of our own lengths, and a seven-day week named for the lamps of the Basilica: Prima, Fanis, Forensis, Censis, Armis, Curis, Vespris. A leap day, the Ash Day, is inserted at the end of Cinaria one year in four and is not numbered by the Censor’s Hall, so nothing registered on it exists in law. This is the calendar of writs, charters, tax and muster, and it is the one the Belworth novellas date by.
  • The Seven Wells (SWR), Fharic. Pure lunar, 354 days, months Shahr al-* begun on a witnessed first crescent, the day beginning at sunset. 840 AR ≈ 249–250 SWR.

Note for narrative work. Old Count and Civil Count share the year and its length and do not drift; Winter’s Crest falls on the twenty-second of Decis. Use the Old Count for anything happening to people who work and the Civil Count for anything with a seal on it. The Linns and the Aelers keep the Old Count entire; the Ykrul keep neither. Harald Everblood’s ten-day reign is exactly one tenday of the Old Count, which was not arranged by anyone.

The wind, and the anti-Tally

Every theology the books have built so far is a theology of keeping. Myrmis keeps the account. The Road keeps the memory — the well remembers every cup, four words and no god in them, and no Fhara can tell you what they mean. The Unity keeps the doctrine. The Grain Covenant keeps the Tally, and The Widow’s Walk is four hundred years of a bet that if you write it down and keep it true, people eat in bad years.

Harrada, the Wind of the Sahel is the one principle in the system that says the record is the problem. She is derived from Ashaan and not from weather: in Ashaan the Black Hand lifts a name off the rolls so cleanly that the grammar closes over the gap, and the Scour — the dry wind out of the interior — is the only other power in that country that does the same work and does it to everyone without a petition to sign. Harrada is that instrument turned: erasure chosen by the erased instead of suffered by them. She keeps nothing. She scatters, and her people scatter: cut chains in a sandstorm, scour the ink off a corrupt instrument, open a lock by rattling it rather than forcing it. What the wind scatters, no chain can gather. Her surest way to end an injustice is to make it impossible to prove it ever happened.

That is not a villain and should never be played as one. It is the other answer — the one the Covenant was built to prevent, and the one nothing in the Covenant can give. The book is for the thousand tribes who agree about hunger; it is not for the individual it has already counted and closed. A person in one of the eleven districts in a short year, holding a debt he cannot pay and a record that correctly says he owes it, has nothing else to appeal to. Neither does a child in a debt-broker’s ledger of indentures.

This gives three positions on one axis — what becomes of a debt that is not paid:

  • the Tally writes it,
  • the wind scatters it,
  • the Hollow collects what falls through.

Harrada is not the Hollow and must not be conflated with it. She is upstream of it: what she scatters still has to go somewhere, and the Hollow is what notices. A cult of the Red Dust that has been working well is a cult that is being watched.

The rule for use. Keep her named in the game material, where she is a patron with rites and tiers and the respect is explicit and on the page. In the fiction, write her register and not her name — the Scour, the dust that covers tracks in a morning, the rust that outruns memory, the white cloth tied to a thorn before the dry season, the door opened by rattling — and let the reader decide whose wind it is. This is how the corpus handles every theology it has: Myrmis is carved on an altar nobody visits and never explained, the Road is four words, and Safiya’s well is given in two columns with no conclusion. Do not make Harrada the exception, and do not put her on the gallery: put her in the Covenant’s shadow, where a person who has been correctly counted goes looking.

The Widow’s Walk carries the one seeding of this — the third thing that was not said on the gallery, and a warden at the back of the room who had heard the offer properly and stood in the red anyway. The novella does not name the wind, does not say what the warden thought, and does not settle whether she was right. Nothing should add to that.

Note on the names. The wind-patron and the twin-stone patron were both drafted under borrowed names — Oya and Ibeji, which belong to a living religion and to people who still keep it. They are now Harrada, the Wind of the Sahel and Awenji, the Twin Stones, and the invented names are canon. Nothing else about either was changed: the register was always the point, and the register is still there. Where the setting needs a theology it invents one, so that it can be handled freely, broken, argued with, and put in the wrong.

How she is met. Only ever one way, and never by name. A bad year, a district the Tally has quietly stopped reaching, a broker at the lane’s end with a lawful paper; a handful of red dust blown across a threshold; the broker forgets what he came for and goes away with the paper still correct in his coat and the entry behind it going soft. The person in the house is free and something is one tick closer, and when a neighbour asks who did that, the answer is the wind. The Cult of the Red Dust is not a religion of the plateau — it is a Covenant heresy, and it meets where the wind never stops: dry riverbeds, abandoned quarries, the lee side of tall buildings in a sandstorm.

The cracked keystone is a hook, not decoration. In Ngomebe a keystone that cracked is a building the Tally cannot account for. There is a structure in eastern Oshiira no engineer will certify and none can explain: the stone sound, the load right, the arithmetic closing, the keystone split top to bottom for ninety years and the building unmoved. Three surveys on file, all three reconciling, all three wrong. The locals say Harrada taught the stone to break, they go in at need, and they do not say what is under it. Awenji is carved at the crown of the Ngomebe keystone and Harrada is scratched at the fracture — the two African-register patrons are opposite ends of the same stone, and every mason on the plateau knows which of them is easier to reach at three in the morning.

A dust-weaver’s dust comes from where the Tally has visibly failed — a weigh-house whose figure would not reconcile, a granary that stood empty in a year the book called full, the ground of the short take-out in the second bad harvest — and not from a generic place of oppression. That is what makes the pouch mean something on this board.

Paget, and the Avimont file

Paget is the fifth Belworth book and the only one that is not narrated: it is an archive, forty-five items, kept and numbered by Jaennis Fenwood — Braedyn’s granddaughter, Baldvyn Brightstride’s widow, the standing suspect in his poisoning. It runs from Octis 840 to Solaris 841 and it counts in fives and thirteens, as the trilogy counts in nines and elevens and the Diwān in threes and sevens.

  • Vaelrie Longblood, called Paget, is Valdais’s third child and second daughter by Madilyn of Ethor, who died before she was two. Her siblings: Ouen, the heir, married to Maellery Tasford; Maegwen, married to Jaron Ravyne; and by Taliya of Lilywin, her stepmother since infancy, the half-brothers Braymond and Cullan. Valdais, like his father, kept many lovers and acknowledged his bastards without ever being made to.
  • The name is the mechanism. A paget carries a man’s things and stands where he can be seen from. She was at her father’s elbow for ten years, so she knew where he would be and when — not because anyone told her, but because she was there.
  • Jaennis never lies to her and never breaks a promise, and that is the trap. The honesty is not cover for the operation; it is the operation. She names her own method in the fourth month, refuses to deny the poisoning rather than deny it falsely, and answers the final accusation with a flat yes. She asks not one question about the army. The only operational question in the file is when is there a morning when the house is empty? — and the answer to that question and the date of the crossing are the same fact.
  • Kettling Ford, the low gravel bar below Tarlington, the fifth of Lavius 841: the crossing is betrayed nine days ahead, the works at Marcott are abandoned and the labour moved, Valdais gets across and it costs him thirty-one men and four days. Five of the thirty-one are named in the girl’s own earlier letters.

  • The paternity is never confirmed. The book’s evidence is entirely circumstantial and entirely visible: he is Baldvyn over again — bard, adventurer, dry like Elianor and warm and a little flamboyant like the old king, and a man with a great many lovers. Asked outright whether it is true about his father, he has had one answer since he was nineteen: “Which one?” Elianor says he is his father’s son and means the women, and says the other thing out loud in a room that cannot hear it. Nobody in the book ever settles it. Nothing added to this should.
  • Ylena of Coldmorrow served Jaennis from 837 until her execution in Lavius 840. She cannot be the unnamed woman who carries Vaelrie’s bag upstairs in Lavius 841. The household note does not name that later woman.
  • The last document in the folder is a joiner’s bill, receipted and paid, for one chair re-seated and carried up to the east room over the yard, dated the third of Cinaria 841 — six days before the girl’s letter about the marriage, exactly as Jaennis said in Letter XX. She bought the chair for no reason, on an ordinary Hammer day, before there was anything to buy. Everything she said was true, and the bill is the only document in the folder that is in neither woman’s hand.

Ahakar, the three wisdoms, and the daughter

The Daughter of Ahakar is an Ashaan piece and the third of them, and it counts in threes and nines as everything out of that country does. It is a performance, taken down at Fairport in the winter of 819 AR from a woman who gave the house three names in an evening. She tells it in the third person throughout, and the reason is the last line of the tale and must never be moved earlier.

Ahakar was a prophet out of the poor quarter of the old city between the desert and the sea, nine hundred years back. He took whoever would come into the red country and taught three wisdoms from three animals, because he was talking to people sitting on rock:

  • The Snake — be able to leave it. One coat; when it stops fitting, leave it in the sand and do not go back to look at it.
  • The Spider — use what is in the room. Not a better corner, the corner you have; and never eat more than you need, because a fat spider is a dead spider.
  • The Raven — look at it. Not death is beautiful; that one is dead, and I am not, and I will be — and none of the three is payment for either of the others. The raven counts and does not pretend the count comes out even.

And a fourth thing, which is not a wisdom but the only sentence of his every tribe can still say: each will be forgotten in its turn, and on the day the last is forgotten, my people are taken.

They kept the Snake and the Spider and let the Raven go — out of comfort, in about sixty years, once they were safe in the grass and had children who had never buried anybody. Then the carts came over the rise in a good year with the grass high. The tribes call the takers kûls, which is not what the takers call themselves, and the tale points at that.

The Veiled Sister at the trestle table. The taking of names is done in person, courteously, under an awning outside the wall: she asks, the tongue answers whether the owner wills it or not, and she leans in and says thank you — you are now Ahloh Zada, which is not an insult but a category, the word for a person whose column has been closed. For one child she stopped, sat back, and tilted her head for three breaths, like someone who has tasted something unexpected, and then shook her head and sent her on. What that was is never explained and must stay unexplained. The best answer in the tale is a Breath-less woman’s: she does not spit them out — ask instead what she was going to do with it and did not.

The daughter of Ahakar was that child. Bought by a spice family as a chamber-slave; sold on to the men who murdered the merchant, who taught her music, poetry in three tongues, mimicry and the grammar of a man’s heart, with a rod, for hours; given at fifteen to a court mage, Sarem Esoiti-broken, who was the first happiness she had and who was killed using her. She crossed the Amaranthine at nineteen in the hold of the Half Moon’s Grace and made her first name on a Fairport dock in the rain: Aevana. Then Elysia, Alisaunde Neith, and a great many since; none lasts three years.

  • Her name is nowhere in the record and must stay there, exactly as Safiya al-Fhara’s is. She went to the great green flame nine years after Fairport; the High Keeper had nine hundred names and did not know the last nine, and said all nine aloud, and nothing happened. She will not say whether one was hers. “If I knew that, I would know it.”
  • The Fairport matter, c. 816–817. The Bloodgoods — Jaymes the elder, a clerk of the river watch; Jaymes the Younger; Fauline, murdered in her own kitchen; and a boy of three — against Camus Renard, a cadet Renard pushed east out of old Vhas and sliding downhill in a dignified manner, and his twins Mangus and Jordanus. Mangus by a garrote in a fraudulent medium’s tent, Jordanus by a stage trap, Camus by hemlock and a warrant signed in his own hand. Mangus was told to kill the child and instead carried him into the other room and told him to hide, was not paid extra for it and was not told to, and the tale gives her that fact and does not resolve it.
  • She covets tales more than money, and will not take money for a telling. “A tale is portable.” Press her on why and she changes the subject in a way you do not notice for an hour.
  • Honour before reason is a Sidhi saying and is an insult, and she uses it as one twice.
  • The Fairport Renards are a cadet line and are not to be tangled with Kaerndal, Alaysha, or Gelfrey.

The Last Blade: Tulgoth, Kargath, and the Duke’s sickness

Tulgoth ate the Violet Steppe for nine years; Braedyn Fenwood and Baldvyn Everblood fought him under the Black Banners, and never afterward discussed it. Rekedim Many-Scars’s father opposed Tulgoth, and his people carried for the Black Banners before running south and east off the grass, were moved on through the Ecktorian marches for four years by people who were perfectly polite about it, and came at last a very long way west, where a young Fenwood put them on ground he had drained himself. Rekedim was born in the Fens roughly ten years after Tulgoth’s death and is just over thirty in The Last Blade. He speaks Ykrul; the children’s assumption that he did not was mistaken. His service, teaching and manual must fit that age, without reviving the earlier forty-year-old biography.

Kargath is Tulgoth’s hidden heir, alive during the 841 campaign. He is the antagonist of the Belworth’s one campaign novel and the reason it is called The Last Blade, and he is quoted in the Kon’reh literature as a doctrinal authority — “Count exits, not victims.”

The daggers. Tulgoth carried Fate’s Edge and had a dagger made for each of his sons, one apiece. The last he had made he called the Last Blade, because it was the last, and it went to the son nobody was told about; and Kargath answers to the knife’s name rather than his own, and there are people who do not know he ever had another. Riarhin took Fate’s Edge off Tulgoth and renamed it Toothpick, and has called it nothing else since. At the end of the novel she takes the Last Blade and buries the brothers’ daggers with him.

Braedyn Fenwood does not go north with Veralyn. He is mortally wounded in an attempt on his life after the coronation, before the 841 expedition, and the wound is dressed, in every sense: the house gives out that the duke is sick, and the injury is ultimately fatal, without an established date of death, and the fact that he was struck is kept off every instrument House Fenwood controls. Seniro and Braedyn the Younger go north in his stead — the marshal and the talker, which is the pairing the house reaches for when the head of it cannot be seen to be absent. Elyas is sent away with Rekedim, which reads as removing the ward from danger and is also removing the two people most likely to work out who ordered it. The novel ends on the steppe: Rekedim stays, Elyas takes Lyra, Alric, Pell and the evidence toward Veralyn’s court, and Riarhin follows the southern lead. No return to Tarlington is narrated.

The unfinished board. Braedyn and Riarhin played for years, and one game was never finished, and it has stood set up in his study since she left. He kept it out of longing. She has never once looked at it straight.

  • His illness is public and is a lie; the wound is the secret, and the household maintains the lie without ever quite being told to.
  • The novel’s cast beyond canon — Gyllan, the oathbound old knight; Lyra, his student; Alric, the junior scholar — are the campaign’s own, and only Riarhin, Rekedim, Elyas, Braedyn and the Fenwoods are load-bearing here.

The Renard six

A Roll of the Fallen is paced as horror and its number is six, which is the wrong number for this shelf on purpose: the Belworth books count in nines and elevens, and six is the disease.

It is never joined up, by anybody, in any book. Mourne Renard counts three and three and one on her fingers and has “counted to six four hundred times and never once been able to make myself finish the thought.” The word struck out under ROCKE is six letters and Rocke is five; two women in a hundred and sixty years took a thread to it, both got six, and neither told a man. The word that has appeared in Alaysha’s own price column, in the corrector’s hand, is six letters. And the Lethai-al at the flint-hills shrine in 774 counted aloud until the attack stopped happening — and the Rocke yarl said once, in the first week, that they reached six and stopped there, and his grandson says he never said it.

Four sixes and no line drawn between them. Do not draw one.

Three shadows were added to the same book and all three survive a sceptical reading, which is the standing rule of that novella and is printed in it:

  • The interrupted count. Spoken to mid-sequence, Mourne began again at the first finger, and a household of sixty learned without being told never to address her between the third and the seventh. A footman who did it twice in a week was moved to the stables at the quarter, was not told why, did not ask, and said afterward that it was fair. The accommodation had a roster and the roster had a discipline and she never once had to ask for any of it.
  • The seventh nail. The Mound’s schedule of hearing-places for that circuit lists six and has since 641, amended four times without the number changing. The girl counted seven, walking it in the cold at nine. The seventh is on the Corbie lane on a byre door, and a hearing-place must have a hearth, and that byre has not had one in living memory. The book corrects the Renards about the sign and declines to explain the byre.
  • The lamp on the roster. The cellar under the east range is empty — a floor, four walls, a drain, and a bracket with a lamp in it, full. It is filled on the first of every tenday by whoever has the week, under a heading of two letters and a point that no steward in a hundred and nine years has been able to expand. Four of them wrote notes to their successors about it, each shorter than the last, and the fourth says only as before. Every one of them went on filling it. That is the Renard disease stated plainly: function without purpose, maintained perfectly. It is also why the dog will not go down there, and the book does not say so.

Women in arms, and the open secret, on the two banks

Vhasia puts women in the saddle. For as long as there have been cantons they have been at war with one another, under the Sun Court’s immaculate forms. The men were forever away at some other canton’s war, and the women who held the halls held them with the garrison. After enough generations of that, they demanded what the code already gave anyone who held a wall: a place in the field, a patent, and a banner. They won it canton by canton, not by edict, and the code absorbed it without ever admitting it had changed.

  • Among the nobility, duchesses and dames holding military commands are unremarkable. A dame may be knighted, hold a company, or command a canton’s horse. A duchess who rides to war with her levy is doing her duty, not making a statement. Heiresses are expected in the saddle by about fifteen, and songs get made about them. Aryka says it plainly in The Thread: “Vhasia would have put me in a saddle at fifteen and given me a company and made a song about it.”
  • Among common folk, women in the line are rarer. They go to war as scouts, as nurses (the old word for field medics), and as secretaries in the classical sense: an officer’s confidential writer and keeper of papers.
  • The code is still the code. A dame in the field ransoms a peer at the fair rate and hangs a village by the same rules as her brother. Admitting women did not make it kinder, and the books should not suggest it did.

Viterra does not knight women. Degree and order are held to be the shape God gave the world. A lord’s daughter may be taught arms, as Aryka was, by “a good master-at-arms, in a courtyard, with the door shut.” A queen may command an army by right of blood. But there is no Viterran patent for a woman knight.

  • Foreign patents are entered separately. Elodie Venn was knighted in Vhasia and sits as the Queen’s Justicar (A Justicar’s Judgment). The clerk enters her Vhasian patent apart from her appointment, and her commission asks the court to accept no other title. Men still object to the sword at her hip.
  • Veralyn is the exception the realm has not written down. She is queen and commander, and by Viterran law not a knight. Nobody raises it to her face.
  • Common Viterran women serve as scouts and nurses in the north more often than in the south, and are rarely named in the rolls.

Desire, on both banks. Viterra’s order keeps desire outside the approved shapes private: an open secret everyone knows and nobody records. The official silence is sincere, and so is the tolerance underneath it. Vhasia is more open, above all among its nobility, and a dame may keep a companion without anybody pretending otherwise. Both kingdoms have rooms beyond the official world, such as private suppers, riverside houses and a dowager’s household, where the open secrets live. Two rules hold for narrative work:

  • Nobody’s desire is evidence of their morals.
  • The books never treat desire salaciously or as a token.

A Note on Two Faiths

Two gods haunt this chronicle, and neither is named often, because both are dangerous to name.

Myrmis
The older claim on this family — a goddess of bargains kept in blood, who answers prayers by taking as much as she gives, and whose favored instrument is a blade that does not choose its wielder so much as it accepts them.
Canray, called Kon'reh in the old Aeler tongue
Not a god at all but a game, a discipline, a way of thinking that the Fenwoods carried out of their exile: patience, clauses, the slow multiplication of small advantages, the belief that every debt can be resolved if you are willing to wait long enough and write it down correctly.

Myrmis is the fire that answers a scream. Canray is the long patience that answers a silence. Every figure in this chronicle chooses one, or is chosen by one, or spends their life trying to hold both at once and is torn for it. Where the histories below feel haunted — a hound sacrificed, a slate left unfinished, a blade that will not dull — that is the shape of the choice underneath. Watch for it. It does not announce itself.

A Note on Women and the Law

In both Vhasia and Viterra, women may inherit lands and designate their property to whomever they wish. A man cannot wrest land from his wife by right of marriage alone. Titles come with land and vassalage, but a landed woman may own more than her lord husband. Women warriors are rare but not unheard of. Neither Vhasia nor Viterra, however, will knight a woman. This legal paradox — the right to rule without the right to bear arms — shapes every conflict in the chronicles below. Viterra practices male-preference primogeniture for titles (equal inheritance for plain property); Vhasia practices agnatic-cognatic succession for titles, with the same equal rule for property.

A Note on Queen Alaysha

Queen Alaysha is Wyllmot's wife and the mother of Baldvyn Brightstride and Veralyn Demon-Eye. She is a Renard by birth — freakishly tall, even beside her husband, with the pale coloring and colorless eyes of old Vhasia — and her family's secret devotion to Myrmis would prove disastrous for the crown. She is not to be confused with Alayse Everblood, Baldvyn the Good's youngest sister, whose rape by Branton Thornhill began the Thornhill Rebellion decades before Alaysha ever came to court; nor with Veralyn Renard, a third and separate ancestor, three generations removed, who married Harald III and won the Renards their long forbearance of Myrmis worship. Vhasians consider it an ill omen that the names recur so closely. They say Myrmis borrows names the way a house reuses a room — the same walls, a different woman in them, and the same weather coming through.

On the eve of her marriage to Wyllmot, Alaysha prayed to Myrmis for two great warriors and three lovely daughters, sacrificing her favorite hound on a cold altar within sight of the Belworth. She got two children, not five: a son and a daughter, both warriors, no daughters to speak of beyond Veralyn herself. She has spent every year since wondering whether the goddess shorted the bargain out of spite, or fulfilled it exactly, and simply priced a warrior-daughter as worth three ordinary ones. Her prayer set in motion the events that would claim her husband's life at Eryas and poison her son's legacy long before any assassin's hand was needed.

The Province War

A war between the Viterran-aligned provinces of Istovar and Aberiel and the loyalist provinces of Ethor, Adria, Ylerea, and Lence. A decisive victory for King Wymund IV “the Smiling King,” who crushed the rebels and solidified the Everblood hold on the eastern marches. It set the table for the Thornhill Rebellion fourteen years later; many of the grievances that erupted in the Longblood Defiance and the Sack of Lence were seeded here, including the enduring Everblood-Longblood rivalry over Ethor and Adria.

Key Figures: King Wymund IV (Viterra) — victorious, but exhausted; Duke Oswyn Everblood (Aberiel) — loyalist commander; the Bloods of Lence — fought for the crown; the Ethor Marchers — brought to heel.

794 AR Year -44

The Thornhill Rebellion

The Thornhills, a Linnic house in northwestern Viterra (Brigh), rose against the crown after Lord Branton Thornhill raped his ward, Alayse Everblood — the youngest sister of Wymund IV's sons. King Wymund IV died avenging the rape, falling at the Battle of Ubbin Falls. His heir, Harald “the Bold”, reigned exactly ten days before an arrow took his eye at the Siege of Brigh — the origin of his crueler epithet, the Tenday King. Baldvyn — the second son, the unexpected king — was crowned a tenday later and rode out at once to storm Brigh, slaying Branton in single combat. House Thornhill was extinguished; its survivors scattered. Braedyn Fenwood, then a young knight and Baldvyn's sworn brother, caught the new king's hand as he drew a dagger to kill the Thornhill heir: “He is a child. Prune him gently, or the root will spread.” The child was spared. Thirty-eight years later, the root would be pulled from Coldmorrow — by the mace of Baldvyn's own granddaughter.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the unexpected king; Branton Thornhill — rapist and rebel, slain in single combat; Duke Oswyn Everblood — defender of Aberiel; Logan Thornhill — the infant spared at his father's body, the debt that would come due twenty-eight years later.

821 AR Year -17

The Longblood Defiance

After the death of Arwyn of Adria, Queen Elianor and King Baldvyn the Good had a falling out. The queen rallied Adria, her cousins in Ethor, and the Bloods of Lence against her husband. Valdais Longblood — Elianor's son by Wymund IV, or, as the court whispered even then, by Baldvyn himself — sided with his mother; Wyllmot, the king's undisputed son, sided with his father. It ended at the Battle of Charau in a decisive victory for Baldvyn, who incarcerated his wife but pardoned Valdais — a mercy later remembered, and resented. Braedyn Fenwood, who commanded the left flank and captured Valdais himself, told the king: “A wrong left standing is a leash.” The leash held for thirteen years. After Baldvyn's death, it would snap.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn the Good — the victor; Queen Elianor — the rebel, the exile; Valdais Longblood — the pardoned claimant; Wyllmot — the loyal son, the future king.

832 AR Year -6

The Sack of Lence

The Vhasian king rebuked House Fenwood for holding lands in Vhasia while refusing to pay homage. Duke Braedyn Fenwood “rebelled” — a convenient fiction — and invaded Vhasia, burning Lence to the ground; the major houses of Istovar joined him, and the Everbloods of Aberiel followed under Wymund, son of Oswyn. Lence was sacked, pillaged, the last Blood King and known royal Blood cousins killed, with reports of a surviving son unproved. Princess Veralyn Everblood — fifteen, dressed as a page, hidden in a supply wagon — squired for Seniro Fenwood against her father's express wishes. After the sack, she tracked Logan Thornhill — the child spared thirty-eight years before — to a village in the royal domain: Coldmorrow. He wounded her cousin Lyonel; she killed him with a mace, and burned the village in her rage. She was sixteen. Logan's own infant daughter, Ylena, survived; raised by strangers, trained as a courtesan, she would return to Veralyn's own court a generation later, wearing another woman's household as a disguise.

Key Figures: Duke Braedyn Fenwood — architect of the invasion; Seniro Fenwood — Veralyn's mentor and protector; Princess Veralyn Everblood — the young killer; Lyonel Vayle — the wounded cousin; Logan Thornhill — about thirty-eight, the infant of Brigh grown, the debt paid in blood; and his daughter Ylena, twelve years old, who watched it paid and did not look away.

834 AR Year -4

The Death of King Baldvyn the Good

King Baldvyn the Good, feeling himself dying, pardoned Queen Elianor, ending her exile. Before he died he threw a grand tourney — a final celebration of chivalry. Princess Veralyn, though not a knight, was permitted to compete: she unhorsed Sir Arin Tymber, Sir Lerris Fenwood, and Sir Ouen Longblood (the last insulted her; she killed his horse in the tilt), was defeated by her friend and cousin Sir Tylan Vayle, who was then defeated by her brother, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride. The old king watched and wept — not from sadness, but from pride.

836 AR Year -2

The Seven Week Uprising

A fanatical sect of Myrmis worshippers rose against King Wyllmot “the Warrior,” assassinating nobles in retaliation for the destruction of their holy sites. Wyllmot besieged their mountain fortress, Eryas Citadel, and died attempting to storm it. Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn Demoneye crushed the rebellion in seven weeks — Veralyn, fighting the sect's champion, her distant cousin Gelfrey Renard, disarmed and killed him on an altar to Myrmis with his own dagger. A blade was found beside the altar afterward, and every person present agreed that none of them had set it down — agreed readily, and with a confidence that ought to have troubled somebody. Perfectly balanced, unmarked, edge unchipped. She has not put it down since, and has never given a reason for that which satisfies anyone, herself included. The court calls it Shadowsteel. Many Renards were secretly Myrmis worshippers, including Queen Alaysha; some at court presume she engineered the Uprising herself, to appease a goddess she felt had been shorted. There is no proof, only the question of who benefited and who did not sleep for seven weeks.

Key Figures: King Wyllmot — killed at Eryas; Prince Baldvyn Brightstride and Princess Veralyn — who crushed the rebellion; Gelfrey Renard — the Shadow-Court's champion, slain on the altar; Queen Alaysha — whose secret faith was exposed.

837 AR Year -1

The Incident of Aleric

Arwyn's son, Aleric, was removed from the Viterran line of succession for plotting against his cousin, Prince Baldvyn Brightstride, with his aunt Aryka. Both were exiled to Acasia — not the Petty Kingdoms as some later, garbled retellings claim, a distinction that matters, because Acasia keeps its own account of exiles, and its own opinions about what Viterra owes them. Aleric's claim was broken, but he did not forget.

Key Figures: Prince Baldvyn Brightstride — the intended victim; Aleric Renard — the exiled claimant; Aryka Renard — the conspirator.

838 AR Year 0

The Death of Baldvyn Brightstride

Prince Baldvyn Brightstride fell ill and died suddenly; the kingdom mourned the loss of their beloved young king. He was suspected of being poisoned — likely by someone seeking revenge for Lence or the Seven Week Uprising. The poison was subtle, slow-acting, and untraceable, administered weeks before symptoms appeared. The prime suspect was Jaennis Fenwood, his young queen, Braedyn's granddaughter — small and waifish, married to Baldvyn barely a season before his death, rumored to have taken abortifacients in secret rather than bear his child. The evidence was circumstantial, and the accusation would have meant war with the Fenwoods. No one spoke the suspicion aloud.

Key Figures: King Baldvyn Brightstride — the beloved, the poisoned; Queen Jaennis Fenwood — the suspected poisoner; Princess Veralyn Demoneye — the heir, the warrior.

The Current Crisis (838 AR)

Veralyn is the acknowledged Duchess of Aberiel and the named heir of King Baldvyn Brightstride. Her grandmother, Elianor, and her uncle Valdais seek to expel the Everbloods from Vhasia. House Fenwood is propping her up. Her cousin Aleric, exiled to Acasia, presses his claim as the eldest male of the major line. Adria and Ethor are for Valdais; Aberiel and Lence are for Veralyn; Istovar is split.

Veralyn is unsure of being anything more than a killer. Her challenges: learning to be a leader, not just a soldier; learning diplomacy, the art of the table rather than the blade; learning that a ruler has no friends, only allies and debts; learning who to trust, and knowing everyone has a price; carrying the trauma of her father's rejection and the Temple's misogyny; confronting the self-hatred for what she did at Coldmorrow.

A Note on the Queen's Household

Veralyn — queen in everything but the crowning — keeps fair company about her and has never troubled to explain it. The court settled on preference some years ago and moved along to more profitable gossip. Jaennis, who chooses the ladies, is said to share it, and to choose accordingly: women who will abide, who will not carry it out of the room, and who understand that being chosen is itself a kind of appointment.

Ylena of Coldmorrow was chosen. She has spent two years within arm's reach of the woman who killed her father, and has never once given anyone cause to look at her twice. The household believes it lifted a clever girl out of a burned village. The girl has her own view of who lifted whom.

A Note on What Follows

This chronicle ends at the open wound of 838 AR, with the crown unclaimed and the factions still circling. It does not resolve here. Within the Demon's Eye carries the story through Veralyn's coronation and Duke Braedyn Fenwood's death in 840 AR, recorded in his own words in The Old Duke's Wars. Two older adventures reach further back. The Reckoning Bridge, set in 640 AR, records the Linns' arrival at Tarlington Gap and the first oaths between the Fenwoods and the Everbloods-to-be. The Exile Road, set in 337 AR, follows the Valvano cadet branch fleeing Acasia into the protection of a Tarling dux not yet calling himself king. The names recur, but inheritance changes their meaning. Each generation receives protection purchased by someone else's compromise and must decide whether gratitude requires repeating it.

GM Guidance: The Shadow of the Past

Every event in this timeline is a thread the party may pull. Consider player characters as: survivors of Lence seeking justice or vengeance; Fenwood loyalists who have seen too much; Veralyn's companions, the only people she trusts; agents of the Shadow-Court manipulating the crisis for Myrmis's ends; Temple inquisitors seeking to expose the heresy that brought the crown low; or Acasian exiles, allies of Aleric Renard.

The timeline is not a cage. It is a family argument conducted across graves. The party will meet descendants sheltered by old betrayals, victims asked to accept apologies from people who did not strike the blow, and heirs who confuse gratitude with obedience because their childhood depended on both. Beneath it run two traditions: the goddess who answers fear with strength and asks what that strength will destroy, and the Fenwood discipline of patience, clauses, and survival in another house's country. Let the campaign ask when an inheritance becomes a duty, when it becomes complicity, and whether repair is possible without pretending the past can be settled.
