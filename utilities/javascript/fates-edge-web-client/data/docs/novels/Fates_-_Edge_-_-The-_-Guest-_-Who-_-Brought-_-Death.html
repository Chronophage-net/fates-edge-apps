The Guest Who Brought Death

Part I — The wedding house

Chapter 1 — The green cups

I had put my mother’s cups out for the wedding. There were eleven, though the chest had places for twelve, and Nerla made me count them twice before she would take the key off its string.

“The small one is in the blue cloth,” I said.

“I know where the small one is.”

She held the cloth open in her lap. Its folds had left fine dark impressions on her fingers. The cup inside was greener than the others, a glassy, bottle-bottom green that thickened at the foot, and my mother had used it for sour wine because she said the colour improved the taste. It had a little silver mend on one side. I put my thumb over the mend, as I always did, and Nerla struck my hand lightly with the cloth.

“You’ll loosen it.”

“It’s been there longer than I have.”

“And look at you.”

I sat back on my heels. My knees had begun to hurt, and there was dust on the front of the wool skirt I had intended to keep clean until noon. Upstairs a man was moving a chest across a floor without lifting it. The sound went through the ceiling into my teeth.

“Bellan!” Nerla called.

The scraping stopped.

“Your mother said carry.”

There was a pause, and then a subdued voice asked whether a person could carry something by himself if it took two people to lift it.

“Find another person.”

Above us, footsteps. Nerla folded the blue cloth once, then again, although it was not going back into the chest. She had been folding things while listening to my family for longer than I had been alive. I used to believe she heard better with her hands occupied.

We were in the little room beside the kitchen where my father kept those possessions he called my mother’s when he wanted them admired, and his own when I asked to take them away. It smelt of dried apple peel and the lime Nerla scattered behind the shelves. The window was a slit looking west across the service yard. Through it came the washhouse smoke, flattening beneath the eaves before the wind lifted it, and an intermittent view of the boy carrying split wood to the brewhouse. He carried three pieces at a time. Nerla had told him four; I had told him three would do. He watched the window on every journey.

“Are you taking these?” she asked.

“I haven’t asked.”

“I didn’t ask if you’d asked.”

I looked at the cups on the shelf. In daylight their green was pale enough to show the little islands where the glaze had gathered. One had a crooked lip. Another bore, under the foot, a thumbmark that did not belong to anyone I had known. My mother had run her finger round that mark when she drank from it. I remembered the movement better than anything she had said while making it.

“I’d like to.”

“Then put them back yourself when we’re finished. Don’t let them go into the hall chest with the pewter.”

She gave me the key. For a moment I had the childish feeling that I had stolen something with her help.

Outside the door someone dropped a pan. A woman laughed, and a second voice, younger and less pleased, said it had already been bent. Nerla shut her eyes. She stayed with me for the space of one breath, then got up with a hand pressed to the shelf.

“Don’t carry all eleven at once.”

“I am twenty-nine.”

“Then you’ve had time to learn.”

When she had gone I put six cups on a tray and carried them into the kitchen. It was barely past breakfast and the room had already become impossible. The great pot was off its hook, steaming on a bed of stones while its contents were divided into smaller pots for reheating. Two women had possession of the kneading board and were arguing over whether yesterday’s oatcakes could be served to the drovers. An old cousin of my father’s sat in the warmest corner with a bowl held under his beard, offering opinions on food he would not be asked to make. A child slept beneath the table with her cheek on a dog. Nobody admitted to bringing either of them.

There had been more people than beds the night before. There would be more again tonight. I stepped over a pair of boots set to soften by the hearth and waited while a girl backed past me with an armful of empty dishes. She was carrying them too high to see.

“Eitha.”

She stopped at once. She had a broad, pretty face, very pale eyebrows and one little curl standing out from the damp at her temple. I knew the curl because I had watched her turn her head to arrange it against her cap before Massen came in to breakfast.

“Put half down.”

“Nerla wants them washed.”

“She wants them whole.”

The dishes clattered on the board. Eitha took four back into her arms and went past me, with the expression of someone obeying a foolish order exactly. I could have let that pass. Instead I said her apron was wet through and she must change it before going into the hall.

She looked down. The wet place was where the dishes had rested.

“Yes, mistress.”

I set the cups beside the warmer. It was a shallow iron tray raised on feet above a small brazier, and Nerla had spread a folded cloth across it. My mother had hated being given a cold cup. Nerla had warmed them every winter since, whether there was anybody left who would complain or not.

The service hatch opened at shoulder height. Beyond it the hall was grey with morning smoke and full of shapes that had seemed familiar in the dark. Cloaks hung from the antlers above the west door. Two spears leaned where a wet blanket usually dried. The long tables had been drawn away from the hearth to let the floor be swept, and the rushes were banked against the wall, carrying the mingled smells of beer, crushed herbs and too many people kept warm together.

Massen stood on the other side of the hatch, watching me.

“You’ve something on your nose,” he said.

“You’ve something in your beard.”

He felt for it. I had guessed correctly: a flake of oatcake came away between his fingers.

“Where?” I asked.

He touched his own nose, then, when I wiped the wrong side, reached through the hatch. His thumb was warm. He rubbed the flour away with an attention I found excessive for the task and entirely welcome. I did not move until someone behind me asked for the space.

“There,” he said.

“You’ve made it red.”

“It was red before.”

“I can still send you home.”

“Yes. But you’d have to feed my aunt first, and she’s settled in.”

He withdrew his hand. Behind him my father was coming down from the tower with his belt still loose and one sleeve rolled above the elbow. He was a broad man, though not as broad as he had been, and in the mornings before he fastened everything about himself he looked like a man preparing to do work. He had used to lift me to the upper shelf of this hatch so I could steal pieces of hot cake. Now his eyes travelled past me to the green cups.

“We ought to have silver for the Lorns.”

“The silver was borrowed for my first wedding,” I said. “It hasn’t come back.”

He laughed because Massen was there. I could hear the laugh being chosen. Massen looked at the cups instead.

“These are better.”

“You haven’t drunk out of them yet,” my father said. “She’ll give you the small one.”

“I’ll take it.”

He glanced at me, and I was absurdly pleased. The pleasure made me dislike my father for having made so small a kindness necessary, and then dislike myself for feeling it as an injury. Behind the hatch, Eitha had begun washing the dishes with a vigour that made the water strike the wall.

“We’re going down to look at the horses,” Massen said. “Will you come?”

“There are beds to find.”

“For horses?”

“For your aunt, since you mention her. She’s decided she can’t sleep over a kitchen.”

“She can. She’s done it all her life. Tell her we put the kitchen under her at home and she never noticed.”

My father laughed properly then, belly first, and called for somebody to bring him his boots. Massen asked again if I would come. He asked my father nothing.

“I’ll find a cloak,” I said.

I went out by the women’s stair, passing the cupboard where Nerla kept soap, good candles and the things she said we had no more of. The stair bent tightly above it. On the inner curve the stone had been polished by shoulders and sleeves; on the outside, beneath the narrow windows, it remained rough enough to catch wool. I had torn a wedding veil there when I was seventeen. Not my own. I had put on my cousin’s because she had left it where I could reach it, and when the lace caught I had pulled instead of going back.

Bran had bought new lace and said a mouse had taken it.

My room was above the kitchen passage, below the room I would use tomorrow night and the night after. I had declined to move until the last day. Otherwise someone would put a guest in my old bed and I would have nowhere to sit alone. For four years the room had been both too small and difficult to leave. I knew where the draught came through the shutter. I knew which floorboard could be stepped on without waking Nerla below. The plaster beside my bed had a stain shaped like the southern bay, if the southern bay had possessed a long thin tail.

My everyday cloak hung on the peg. Beneath it was a darker outline where Kennet’s coat had hung for a winter after I returned. His mother had sent it with my clothes. She had kept his good horse, the bed, the copper pans I had ordered, and the serving woman who knew how I liked my hair done; but the coat she had thought I might want.

I had wanted it. That was the most irritating part.

I put my hand into the cloak pocket and found a length of green thread, a broken nut and the little bone comb I was forever losing. For some reason I began to laugh. I was still laughing when Lira appeared in the doorway carrying the sleeve of my new gown on her arm.

“You’re not wearing this,” she said.

“I hope to be wearing more of it.”

“No, look.”

She held the sleeve against herself. The lining had turned under at the wrist. I could see that it was wrong, but not why it had required pursuit up two flights of stairs.

“Can it be altered?”

“It can be taken out and put in again. That’s what altered means when people say it can be altered.”

“I’ll do it when I come back.”

“From where?”

“The ford.”

“With him?”

She did not sound disapproving, only interested, which somehow annoyed me more. Her hair was pinned up badly and escaping in bright straight pieces beside her cheeks. She was twenty-two. At her age I had already learned to wake before the woman who brought the fire, so I could appear to have risen without help. Lira still came down with the mark of her pillow on her face and expected breakfast.

“Do you want to come?”

“No.” She folded the sleeve over her arm. “Bran’s asked me to help him with something.”

“Then ask him to help you with that.”

She looked at me, and I wished I had not said it. But she only stepped aside to let me pass.

At the stairhead she called after me.

“Brin. Do you remember the welcome with the door in it?”

“All welcomes have a door in them.”

“The old one. The bit after the fire.”

I was already thinking about my father making Massen wait in the cold while someone found a better saddle.

“Ask Nerla.”

“I did.”

“What did she say?”

“That I should ask you.”

“Then we both ought to have learned it.”

I went down before she could answer. In the kitchen passage Massen was waiting with my gloves. He had found them under the settle, which was where I always put them when I meant to remember them. He held them out, then kept one until I looked at him.

“You don’t have to come.”

“I know.”

His hand relaxed. I took the glove, and we went out through the hall together.

The main door stood open. Cold air lay low over the threshold, fresh enough to taste after the smoke, and on the hill above the gate the first snow had caught in the heather. My father was telling a groom that a horse could not be expected to respect a man who apologized to it. The groom was apologizing to the horse under his breath.

Massen heard him. I felt the laugh move through the arm beside mine before he let it out.

Chapter 2 — Below the gate

My father rode as far as the outer gate and then discovered a reason to remain there. A tenant had brought two geese in a basket, and the smaller goose was not, in his opinion, a satisfactory goose. He dismounted to demonstrate the difference to a man who had carried both birds three miles uphill.

Massen and I went on foot. There was no need to ride down to the ford, and I had a particular dislike of being lifted into a saddle while people watched. Massen had offered once, when he first came to see me in the summer. I had refused so sharply that he had looked at the horse to discover what it had done. He had not offered again.

The path dropped south from the gate, between a wall built of fieldstone and a bank where the sheep had worried the heather down to its black roots. Under the snow the path was brown and soft. We kept to its edges, where the grass held, and passed the little shrine my grandmother had put up after a horse broke her ankle. Its roof had fallen in. Someone had set the broken pieces upright beneath the remaining arch, making a more complicated ruin than the weather had managed by itself.

“Would you mend that?” Massen asked.

“If I had time.”

“I meant with the money for the passage.”

“You’d spend road money on my grandmother’s shrine?”

“I’d spend a little of it on keeping your father from complaining that I’d left your grandmother in the rain.”

“She wasn’t in it.”

“He’d find a way.”

I smiled, looking down so he would not get too much satisfaction from it. In the summer I had found his readiness to make jokes exhausting. I had thought he would prove one of those men for whom a woman must laugh before she can be answered. Then I had seen him at a horse fair with a boy whose pony had been sold without the boy knowing it would be, and he had stood for a long while without saying anything clever at all.

Below us the ford showed between bare hazels. The burn was swollen from rain higher up, dark in its middle and pale where it pressed over the flat stones. On this bank stood a low house with a turf roof and a drying frame bare for winter. Across the water, farther up the opposing slope, three cottages faced the road. Smoke came from two. The third was shut. I supposed its owner had gone to her daughter’s for the gathering. A cat had been demanding admission for most of yesterday afternoon.

We heard it again as we descended.

“Is that a child?” Massen asked.

“No. A judgment on her daughter.”

The horses were waiting on our side of the water. Four men had brought them up from the lower grazing: two pack animals, a bay mare and the grey Massen had been recommending to everybody for two days. He called the grey sensible. Gedran, his cousin, said it had the sense to make other people do everything it disliked.

The men were drinking from a leather bottle when we reached them. One tried to put it behind his back and discovered that the horse he was holding had opinions about leather. Massen took the bottle from him before the horse could get its teeth in it.

“Is this breakfast?”

“Cold medicine.”

“Whose cold?”

“The hill’s.”

Massen drank, grimaced, and handed it to me. I declined. The smell was sufficient treatment for anything I had.

Gedran stood by the grey, running his hand along its neck. He was younger than Massen, narrow in the shoulder and handsome in a way that required him to know it. I had liked him at supper the night before until he asked whether Dorn women were always so quiet. My aunt had explained that we waited for something worth answering, and he had spent the rest of the meal making more effort than anyone needed.

“This is the bride’s horse,” he said.

“Is it?”

“Massen thought—”

“I thought she might like to try him,” Massen said.

I went to the grey’s head. It smelt cleanly of cold hair and the sweet trace of its last feed. One ear moved towards me, the other remained with the bay mare. The horse was larger than the one I usually rode and had a little old cut on its upper lip.

“How old?”

“Seven,” said Gedran.

“Eight,” said Massen.

“It won’t be eight until spring.”

“Then let it be seven till spring without making that the first lie we tell her.”

I touched the horse’s face. Its lip searched my glove.

“He expects something.”

“He knows people.”

“Has he a name?”

“Dapple,” said Gedran.

There was a silence. The horse was almost entirely grey, with no dappling I could find.

“My sister named him,” Massen said. “He was different then.”

“How old was your sister?”

“Old enough to know better. She didn’t like the name he came with.”

“What was that?”

“Dapple.”

I laughed before I could help myself, and the grey lifted its head at the sound. Massen was watching me. He did not smile quite as broadly as he might have. I became aware of Gedran pretending to inspect a stirrup.

I would have liked to ride the horse. I would have liked even more to ride away from all four men and return when I chose. But the road on the far bank was wet, and somebody would have to accompany me because I was the bride and therefore more breakable than I had been three months before.

“I’ll try him after the wedding.”

“Whenever you like,” Massen said.

Gedran let go of the stirrup. “She could cross him now. He’s as steady as a table.”

“The tables in our hall rock,” I said.

“Not this one.”

He swung into the saddle before anyone could object. The horse took two surprised steps, then stood while he settled himself. The groom holding the bay moved aside. I saw the pack animals draw towards one another as the grey passed, their loads brushing with a little dry creak. One carried sacks of meal. The other had folded blankets under a cover tied too loosely on the left.

Gedran rode down to the water at an angle. It was not a bad approach in summer. In summer the stones at that end were barely wet and a horse could walk across without putting a hoof in the current. But the lower flat stone had shifted during the autumn spate. I had crossed here a week before with Nerla and a basket of hens, and she had made me take the higher line, scolding me as if I still needed to be told which parts of a river were moving.

“Not there,” I said.

Gedran looked back.

“The upper stones.”

He drew the rein, but the grey had already put its forefoot down. The hoof slid. The horse stumbled against the current, lifted its head, and Gedran pulled too hard on the rein. For a moment I saw the white of the grey’s eye. Then its hindquarters swung away and Gedran went into the water over one shoulder.

Massen was past me before I had finished saying his cousin’s name.

He did not go to Gedran first. He caught the grey’s trailing rein near the bit and gave it room to get its feet under it, talking in a low voice that I could not distinguish from the water. The horse scrabbled, found the upper edge and came towards him. He turned his shoulder as it passed so it would not knock him down. One of the grooms caught the loose rein on the bank.

Gedran was on his knees in the burn. He had tried to stand and been pushed sideways against a stone. The water was no higher than his thigh, but it had got inside his coat and was dragging at him. He was laughing. It was the wrong laugh.

Massen took him under one arm. I went to the edge and held out the end of my cloak. Both men ignored it until I told them it was stronger than Gedran’s pride. He caught it then, and with the groom pulling beside me we got him onto the stones.

He stood dripping, one knee of his hose torn open. The laugh had gone out of him. His face was white beneath the flush left by the cold drink.

“Steady as a table,” said the groom.

Gedran opened his mouth.

“Don’t,” Massen said.

It was spoken quietly enough that only those beside him heard it. Gedran shut his mouth. Massen took the loose blanket from the pack horse, stripped off his cousin’s outer coat and wrapped him in it. The groom who had made the joke looked at the water. For the first time I wondered what Massen sounded like when men did not do as he asked.

A woman had come from the house beside the ford carrying another blanket. She was barefoot inside wooden pattens and wore a thick wool dress with the sleeves rolled back. She took one look at Gedran and told him there was a stool by her fire if he could get his wet self that far without making more work.

Massen thanked her. She did not seem to notice the thanks. She looked instead at me.

“You’ve the wedding tomorrow?”

“The morning after.”

“That’ll be why the road’s full.”

She helped Gedran towards the house. I had known her face all my life without knowing her name. I thought it was Thessa. I was sufficiently unsure that I could not ask her anything without finding out first, and while I hesitated she disappeared through the door.

Massen stood beside me, water running from his coat into his boots.

“You’re wet too,” I said.

“I noticed.”

“Come inside.”

“In a moment.”

He watched the grey walk up the bank. The horse put its feet down carefully but showed no sign of favouring one. Massen asked the groom to run a hand down both front legs before putting it away. Then he began to laugh.

“What?”

“I’d spent two days telling your father it never made a mistake.”

“The horse didn’t.”

“No.” He wiped water off his face. “No, it didn’t.”

I took his hand. The fingers were cold and rough, with a fresh scrape beside the nail of his thumb. He looked down at our hands as if he had not expected mine to be there. He was thirty-four, and for a few breaths he looked younger than Gedran.

The woman called from the house. I let go.

Inside, the single room smelt of smoke, warm damp wool and onions hung from a beam. Gedran sat on a stool with his boots off and steam rising from his stockings. The woman’s husband was away with a flock; her daughter, perhaps twelve, watched us from the ladder to the loft with such fierce attention that I wanted to apologize for not being more remarkable.

“It is Thessa, isn’t it?” I asked.

“It is.”

“I knew, but—”

“You’ve a great many people to know.”

She gave Massen a cloth and told him to take his coat off. I could not decide whether she had been kind. The uncertainty made me too eager to help. I took the kettle from its hook when she turned towards it and burned the heel of my hand through the cloth. She took it from me without comment.

Gedran began an account of the accident in which the stone had moved beneath him while the horse remained blameless. Massen listened until the stone had travelled a considerable distance and then asked whether it had followed them indoors. Thessa’s daughter laughed down from the loft. After that Gedran was permitted to be cold and foolish without having to remain grand.

We stayed longer than we needed. Thessa gave us a drink made with hot water and a sharp herb I did not know. Through the open top of the door I could see the grey standing on the bank, its rein held by a man who was feeding it something from his pocket. Massen sat with his shirt sleeves drying in uneven patches. There was a white scar on his forearm I had not seen before, long and slightly sunken at its middle.

“How did you do that?” I asked.

“A gate.”

“A gate attacked you?”

“A mare went through before it was open.”

“And you tried to stop her.”

“I tried to stop the gate.”

I put one finger beside the scar. He was still, but I felt his arm tighten beneath my touch. Thessa had gone into the little lean-to behind the house. Gedran was arguing with the girl about whether a person could teach a hen to come when called.

“Does it hurt?”

“Sometimes in the cold.”

“Now?”

“No.”

He turned his wrist. My hand remained against his arm. I thought he would kiss me and wished he would, and was grateful when he did not do it in front of his cousin.

We left a little money for the fuel. Thessa took it after a pause, then put the better blanket back on Gedran and told him somebody could bring it after it dried. At the door I asked her to come up for the wedding food.

“With the girl,” I added.

“We’ll see how the beasts are.”

“There’ll be enough.”

“I didn’t doubt it.”

The answer troubled me all the way up the hill. I did not mention it to Massen. He was carrying his wet coat over one arm and walking carefully because of the water in his boots. Behind us Gedran rode a pack horse with a blanket over his shoulders, looking sufficiently unhappy that I kept turning away to smile.

My father was still at the gate. He had accepted both geese. He was holding the larger one by its body while a child touched its head with a finger, withdrawing the finger very quickly after each touch. He looked up at Massen, took in the wet clothes, and began to laugh before anyone had told him what happened.

I went past them into the yard. The warmth from the kitchen door reached me before the smell of dinner. Eitha stood in the doorway with a dry apron. She saw the mark on my hand and asked if I wanted grease for it.

“It isn’t anything.”

She looked at it again.

“There’s some beside the salt if you do.”

She went back inside. I followed her as far as the kneading board and put my hand into the little crock before anyone could tell me to.

Chapter 3 — The room above the fire

The sleeve did not fit because Lira had made it fit me.

I discovered this standing in my shift in the bridal chamber, with the new gown hanging open at the back and a pin between her lips. She had let out a seam beneath my arm where it had been too tight at the fitting. In doing so she had disturbed the lining, and the lining had taken revenge at the wrist. I stood while she worked it loose again, feeling cold in all the places the gown had not yet covered.

“You could have told me,” I said.

She took the pin out of her mouth.

“I did tell you.”

“You told me it needed altering.”

“That was what it needed.”

The room was bright for an afternoon in that season. Its west window had been unshuttered to clear the smell of fresh plaster, and the pale sky lay along the floorboards in a shape interrupted by the bed. The bed was my grandparents’ and had been brought upstairs in pieces before my first wedding. It had never gone down again. Its posts were thick, black with handling at the height where a person might steady herself, and above them the tester held an embroidered hunting scene whose hounds had faded to the colour of the sky they chased beneath. One stag retained its red tongue.

I had slept here on the last night before leaving with Kennet. I remembered very little of the night. My mother had been alive. She had come up to ask if I wanted the window closed, and I had said no because I was afraid she would stay and speak seriously to me if I admitted to being cold.

The next morning there had been frost on the inside of the glass.

“Lift your arm.”

I lifted it.

“Not like you’re surrendering.”

“I’ve never surrendered. How would I know?”

“You’ve seen people do it.”

She moved the seam with her thumb and a crease released itself across my shoulder. I felt the difference at once.

“There,” I said.

“Don’t move.”

The room had been used by guests since I came home. One had put a hot pot on the chest and left a dark circle; another had scratched a name on the window ledge and then tried to scrape it off. I knew who both of them were. The scratches remained because my father said nobody looked there. I looked there every time I entered.

My wedding linen lay on the bed in folded heaps. Some was mine, some my mother’s, and some had been taken from other beds because there was not enough good linen in the house to make the display Nerla thought proper. The upper sheet smelt of the lavender she grew in a pot and carried indoors at the first frost. Under it was the warmer, human smell of wool that had been stored through summer.

Lira pinned the seam and stood back.

“Turn.”

I turned. She looked at the gown, not at me, which was a relief until I wanted her to say something admiring.

“Does it look well?”

“It will when I’ve finished.”

“Lira.”

“Yes, it looks well.”

“You sound astonished.”

“I’m hungry.”

That made me laugh, and her mouth moved reluctantly into a smile. She was not like me. People said we resembled one another because we both had brown hair and Dorn eyes, which could mean anything from grey to the colour of dishwater depending on the light. Her face was narrower. When she was tired, a small vertical line appeared between her brows that made her look offended by the whole room. I had disliked that look until I saw it on myself in a mirror.

“There’s bread downstairs.”

“There was bread downstairs. Now there are six men discussing whether it ought to have caraway in it.”

“You’re frightened of six men?”

“I’m frightened they’ll tell me.”

I reached for the fastening at the back of the gown and she caught my hand.

“I said don’t move.”

A knock came at the door. Before either of us answered, Bellan pushed it open and his mother put her head round. Her hair was bound in a dark cloth spotted white with lime. She looked first at my bare feet, then at the open window.

“You’ll stiffen before you’re dressed.”

“I’m not staying dressed,” I said.

“Then do it quickly.”

She came in with her son behind her. Bellan carried a basket of plaster fragments in both hands. He was not yet fully grown, though his shoulders had begun to acquire the breadth that made people give him things too heavy for him. His face was powdered with dust except for the wet marks where he had wiped his nose. He kept his eyes fixed above my shoulder with such determination that I became more conscious of the open gown than I had been before he entered.

“Set that by the door,” Druven told him. “And fetch the small brush. The small one.”

He put down the basket and went out. She waited until his footsteps were on the stair.

“I told him to knock.”

“He did.”

“I meant knock and wait.”

Lira made a sound through the pin in her mouth. Druven looked at her, then at the pin, and Lira removed it before being told. There were very few people who could make her do that without an argument.

Druven went to the wall beside the chimney. A patch had been opened where the plaster bulged. Beneath it the masonry changed from the broad pale blocks of the tower to smaller stones set unevenly round the flue. She put her hand against one of them and kept it there.

“Will it smoke?” I asked.

“It smoked before I came.”

“Will it smoke after?”

“A little when the wind comes down the north face. Less if people stop putting wet peat on it and then opening every door to admire the weather.”

“Can you tell my father that?”

“I have.”

She tapped the stone with a short tool. The sound was dull. She moved to the next and tapped again, listening with her head slightly turned. I could hear a difference, but not what difference I was meant to hear.

“The flue is sound here,” she said. “There’s a bad joint lower down. I’ve asked for the cupboard emptied.”

“What cupboard?”

“The one beneath this.”

“That is a wall.”

“It was a cupboard when I worked here last.”

She rubbed a patch of dust with her thumb. I tried to remember the kitchen passage beneath us. The soap cupboard was farther west, under the stair. Beside the hearth there was a shallow recess with a door that had not been opened in years because a fixed bench stood against it. We kept empty baskets on the bench. I had never considered what might be behind the door.

“Bran can have it moved.”

“He says the lower stones were seen to already.”

“Then perhaps they were.”

Druven looked at me.

“Perhaps.”

I disliked having to stand still in a half-fastened gown while a woman waited for me to understand something. Lira bent over my wrist. I could feel her trying not to look up.

“Do you need it opened today?” I asked.

“I need to know before someone builds a great wedding fire against it.”

“I’ll ask him.”

She nodded. That was all. She took the basket by one handle, tipped it to look inside, and took out a curved piece of plaster. There was colour on its underside: reddish lines, faint beneath the old soot.

“Bellan thought you might want this.”

She handed it to me. It fitted in my palm. A thumb and three fingers remained, painted against a cream ground. The fourth finger had broken away at the edge. Whoever had painted it had made the nails dark.

“What was it?”

“A hand.”

“I can see that.”

“Then you know what I know.”

She left us with it. Lira took it from me and turned it so the thumb pointed down.

“There’s more under the old plaster,” she said.

“How do you know?”

“I looked.”

“When?”

“When I wasn’t putting this sleeve in.”

I took the fragment back. The hand looked different upside down, as though someone above us were reaching towards the floor. I put it on the chest, where it slid into the dark ring left by the pot.

The chest rocked when I leaned on it to take off the gown. Lira swore softly as one of her pins bent. When I had stepped free, I knelt and felt beneath the chest’s front foot. There was an old wedge there, shrunken and split along its grain. I pulled it out and pushed the plaster into its place.

“That’ll break,” Lira said.

“It’s broken.”

“It might have been part of something.”

“So was the wedge.”

She folded the gown. I heard how quietly she did it. I could have taken the fragment out again. Instead I stood on the chest’s lower edge to test it. It held.

Bellan returned with the small brush and was sent to sweep the patch beside the chimney. I put my old dress on behind the bed curtain. There were tiny moth holes near its upper edge. Through one I could see Lira’s face as she bent to gather the loose pins. Her expression had settled into something I could not name. With the curtain between us I found I wanted to speak to her more kindly.

“Did Nerla remember your song?”

“She remembered some.”

“Sing it, then.”

“Now?”

“Unless it needs a different day.”

She did not answer at once. Bellan brushed plaster into his pan. Beyond the window a crow flew past close enough for me to hear the separate strokes of its wings.

Lira sang very softly.

“Fire for your fingers, Water for your feet—”

She stopped.

“I know that,” I said.

“Everybody knows that part.”

“Bread for your hunger.”

“Yes.”

“And something about sleep.”

“Room for your sleep.”

I came out from behind the curtain, fastening the last hook at my throat.

“My mother sang it when the cousins came. She changed it if she didn’t like them.”

“What did she change?”

“Space for your horses, if they smelt of horses. A chair for your stories, if she thought they were going to go on all night.”

Lira smiled. “I remember that.”

“There. You’ve remembered it.”

“There’s more.”

She put the gown on the bed and ran her hand along the shoulder, smoothing a crease that had not been there until she touched it.

“Bran says there’s a verse for going out again.”

“Then make him sing it.”

“He doesn’t know the tune.”

“He never does. It doesn’t stop him.”

Bellan laughed. Lira looked at him, surprised, and he bent so quickly over his brush that he hit his head on the mantel.

I went to close the window. On the sill lay three little chips of stone, darker than the plaster, with a thread of pale material through one. I swept them into my hand, intending to put them in the basket. They were cold enough to make me pause.

“Are these Druven’s?”

Lira turned.

“Yes,” she said, too quickly for a question that required no haste. “Leave them. She might need them.”

I set them down again. Outside, someone was leading the grey towards the stable. It walked steadily beside the groom, who was telling it a story in a low voice. Beyond the yard, the burn showed through the bare trees as a narrow strip of moving light.

The window would not fasten. The wood had swollen and the hook fell short of the eye. I pressed my shoulder against it, trying not to put weight on the hand I had burned, and felt the frame give a little. Something tapped beneath the sill. I thought a chip of stone had fallen, but all three were still there.

“Brin?”

“It’s stuck.”

Lira came beside me. Together we forced it shut. The hook caught at last. I stood with my palms against the wood, feeling the cold through it, while she looked down at the floor.

“What?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

Then she bent and picked up a pin. She held it between us so I could see it.

“You’ll put your foot on one if you keep walking about bare.”

I found my stockings on the bed. Bellan had finished sweeping. The room was almost ready, except for the little open wound in the plaster and the gown which Lira took downstairs to put right.

I stayed after they had gone. The bed was so large that it seemed to take the warmth from the room even when no one lay in it. I sat on its edge, pressed the mattress with my hand, and thought about Massen’s wet shirt drying against his skin.

Below the floor, something scraped slowly along stone.

I waited for it to happen again. When it did not, I went down to ask my uncle about the cupboard.

Chapter 4 — My uncle’s hands

Bran had taken possession of the small room over the gate because from there he could see people coming and pretend he had not seen them. He said the light was better for his work. The window faced east and by afternoon there was no light at all.

I found him cutting a piece of leather into a shape too small to be useful. He had his spectacles on, though one glass was chipped and he preferred to look over them. On the table stood a candle, an open wooden case and a plate on which somebody had left two bones and a crust. His dog slept beneath his chair with its head on his boot.

“You’ve come to tell me to dress,” he said.

“I’ve come about a cupboard.”

“Worse.”

He held the leather up to the light. It was a little disk with a slit through its middle.

“What is it?” I asked.

“Something for your father’s buckle. It catches his shirt. He thinks the shirt ought to be made differently.”

“Perhaps it ought.”

“I’ll tell the weaver.”

He put it down and pushed the plate towards me. I took the crust. The dog opened one eye, discovered that I was not bringing anything additional, and shut it again.

My uncle’s hands were broad, browned where the gloves did not cover them, with a burn scar that climbed from his right wrist into his sleeve. As a girl I had traced that scar while he pretended to sleep. I had believed it was from a battle until he told me he had dropped a pot of hot fat. I was so disappointed that he added a bandit to the account, and for years I could not remember which version he had given first.

“Druven wants the recess below the bridal room opened.”

His knife stopped moving.

“She’s looked at the flue.”

“She says there’s a bad joint lower down.”

“There was. We’ve had it seen to.”

“That’s what you told her.”

“Then she has a good memory.”

He bent over the leather again. I stood by the table and ate the crust slowly enough to make it clear I was not leaving. At last he put the knife down.

“Your father has had half the house turned upside down. If every workwoman who comes through the door is allowed to find another day’s work—”

“She didn’t ask for another day. She asked to see it.”

He looked at me over the spectacles. There was a little red place where their bridge rested on his nose. It made him seem more tired than the complaint had.

“All right,” he said. “After supper. I’ll move the bench.”

“Thank you.”

“And you’ll go and put something warm on. You’re blue at the mouth.”

“I’m not.”

“You always were when you came down from that room.”

I touched my mouth before I could stop myself. He smiled and returned to the buckle.

The wooden case beside him was divided into compartments. I knew its contents from childhood: sealing wax, a little whetstone, needles too large for sewing clothes, two metal stamps, twine wound round a stick and the things he put on hinges to make them stop crying in the night. There were more compartments now, added in a smaller tray over the old ones. A narrow packet, stoppered at one end, lay in a fold of dark wool. I had no reason to look at it longer than at the rest.

“Will you need the cart tomorrow?” he asked.

“For the linen.”

“What time?”

“After breakfast.”

“I’ll have the axle greased.”

“I thought Lorn was sending one.”

“They are. This is ours.”

I understood then. The chest, the linen, the things my father had agreed I could take: Bran meant to have them ready before there could be a reason to delay.

“He said I might have the cups,” I lied.

“Did he?”

I looked at the dog. Bran put his spectacles on the table.

“I’ll find straw for them.”

He did not ask again.

For a while we sat without speaking. The room above the gate had a smell of leather and candle grease that I associated with being allowed to stay up too late. My uncle had slept here during one winter when the tower roof leaked. I had been eight, or perhaps nine, and had spent afternoons beside the table while he mended harness, waiting for him to finish a sentence he had begun before deciding the stitch needed both hands.

He was the person who had come for me when Kennet died.

My father had sent a horse and a man with instructions about what should return. Bran had come himself, two days behind them, with a cart he had hired on the road because he thought there might be things that would not sit well on a horse. There were very few. I had been ashamed when I saw how empty the cart was. He filled the space with feed sacks bought cheaply from a neighbour and complained all the way home about the bargain.

He had also found Kennet’s last letter in the pocket of a coat being put away. The letter was not to me. It concerned a pair of dogs and a debt too small for anyone to collect. I had kept it because the last line said he had to stop writing, I was calling him to eat.

Bran wrapped it in oiled cloth before giving it to me. I still had the cloth.

“Are you pleased?” he asked now.

“With the cart?”

“With him.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He bent to rub the dog’s ear. I watched his hand disappear into the thick fur at its neck.

“I am,” I said.

“I heard you.”

“I don’t want people thinking—”

“What people?”

“All of you.”

He looked up. I could not explain without insulting every kindness he had shown me. I wanted to tell him that I was not marrying merely because my father had found an arrangement that moved me from one house to another without anybody having to call it a favour. I wanted to say that I liked Massen’s body and his foolish songs and the way he stopped talking when I had something to say. I could say none of that to the man who had taught me to sit a pony.

“It’s different this time.”

He nodded. He did not ask me how.

“Your aunt thinks he talks too much,” he said.

“She talks while she’s asleep.”

“That’s when she has the most attentive company.”

I laughed. He let the laugh finish, then reached behind the plate and took up a little cloth parcel.

“This was meant for tomorrow. You’ll only spend tonight wondering what I’ve forgotten.”

Inside was a pair of riding gloves. They were brown, plain, lined with a soft wool whose ends showed at the wrist. The left thumb had a little room built into it for the joint I had injured as a child. Shop gloves always pulled there.

I put them on. They fitted so well that for a moment I could not look at him.

“They may stretch,” he said. “Don’t soak them.”

“I went to the ford today.”

“I heard.”

“Everybody heard.”

“Your father has improved it considerably.”

I flexed my hand. The leather moved without catching.

“Thank you.”

He began to answer and stopped. Instead he picked up the buckle disk and threaded it onto the strap lying beside him. I stayed until he had finished, because leaving at once would have made my thanks seem like the end of a visit made for a purpose.

Below the window somebody called for the gate to be opened wider. A cart wheel grated against stone. Bran rose and looked down. From where I sat I could see only the grey light on his face.

“Another one?” I asked.

“Tor.”

I went to the window beside him.

Three riders waited outside the gate. Their cloaks were dark with weather and one led a fourth horse carrying a covered pack. The woman at their head sat quietly while the porter struggled with the swollen gate leaf. She did not look up at us. Her horse did, rolling one eye towards the window, then lowering its head as if ashamed of the curiosity.

“Should I fetch Father?”

“He’ll be here before you reach him.”

He was right. My father came out of the hall with his good belt on and both sleeves down, speaking loudly enough for the riders to hear the welcome before they entered. The woman inclined her head. When she dismounted she did it stiffly, holding the saddle for a moment before she let go.

Lady Catrane had witnessed my first marriage. She had been sent from Tor then too. I remembered her saying that Kennet had a good seat on a horse, which I had taken for an opinion about his character. Now, looking at her in the yard, I wondered whether she had simply meant he could ride.

“I must go down.”

Bran caught my sleeve lightly as I turned.

“Brin.”

“Yes?”

“Don’t let her make you answer everything tonight. She likes people tired.”

I nodded. At the door I remembered Druven.

“The bench, after supper.”

“I said I would.”

He sounded impatient, and I went before I could make him say it a third time.

Lady Catrane was removing her gloves when I reached the hall. Her hands were narrow and reddened at the knuckles. She had a band of dark material round one wrist where the glove had rubbed. My father called me forward with the same gesture he used when asking for a chair.

“You remember Brin.”

“I do.”

She took my hand. Her grip was firm and cold.

“You look well,” she said.

“So do you.”

“No, I don’t. I’ve been on a horse since before light. But you may say it again after I’ve washed.”

She smiled then, and I liked her more than I had intended to. I took her up the tower stair to the room prepared for her, carrying the smaller of her bags. She asked about my mother while we climbed, not how she had died but whether the apple trees she planted were still bearing.

“Two of them. The third came down in a gale.”

“The one by the wall?”

“Yes.”

“She said it would. Your father wouldn’t have it cut.”

I had never heard that argument. It pleased me to imagine my mother being proved right about something so ordinary.

At the landing Catrane stopped to get her breath. She looked through the narrow opening towards the west roofs and the smoke lying among them.

“There are more buildings.”

“The brewhouse was enlarged.”

“And the lower way?”

“What lower way?”

She looked at me, then back through the opening.

“I may be remembering another house.”

I showed her the room. The best basin stood ready on its table, the water still steaming. She thanked me for carrying the bag and began to unfasten her cloak. I had reached the door when she asked whether Massen had come alone.

“With his household.”

“Of course.”

“Did you mean something else?”

“I meant whether his brother was here.”

“He’s stayed with their mother.”

She nodded. I waited, but she had turned to the basin. On the way down I wished I had known whether she had a brother.

Chapter 5 — A table for everyone

By supper the grey horse had thrown three men into the burn and chased a fourth halfway to the gate. My father told the story from his chair with a cup in each hand, one his own and the other taken from Massen to illustrate the height of the water. He had to drink from both before the illustration could be completed.

Gedran bore it badly until Lady Catrane asked whether the horse was still willing to marry into the family. After that he surrendered enough to be agreeable. He claimed the horse had expressed doubts but would be guided by its elders. My father laughed until he coughed. Nerla took the cups away before he could improve the account again.

I sat at the eastern end of the high table, on the settle with my back to the hearth. It was the place I had used since my mother died. From it I could see the main door at the far end of the hall, the servants moving between the tables, and the kitchen hatch to my right, beyond my father’s shoulder. When people stood to sing in front of us they blocked the hatch, which was why Nerla disliked songs between courses. She had to send someone round with each dish to discover whether we were ready for it.

The cushions on the settle had new covers. I had sewn them in the evenings while my father and Bran discussed cattle. One was a little longer than the other. I turned its loose end against the wall so nobody would see.

Massen sat beside me, still wearing borrowed stockings. The ones lent him belonged to my father and were too large at the ankle. He had folded them under his hose and said he was grateful. My father had received the gratitude as if he had rescued him from the burn himself.

Below us the hall was crowded enough that anyone getting up made three other people move. There were women at the lower tables whom I knew only by the children on their laps. A young man from the western road had put on a blue coat too fine for him and kept checking its sleeves for grease. The musicians had been given a bench by the south wall, out of the draught only when the door remained closed. One had wrapped a blanket round his knees. Another was eating with both hands to make up for the time he expected to lose playing.

I was happy. I did not name it while it lasted. I was too busy watching for the things that might go wrong.

The mutton came up hot, surrounded by onions that had collapsed into a sweet brown mass. A dish of turnips followed, then bread, then a smaller plate of fried liver my father claimed nobody else liked. Lady Catrane helped herself to half of it. Massen asked for the rest. My father looked at the empty plate as if an agreement had been broken.

Eitha brought the first wine. She leaned past me to fill Massen’s cup, and I saw the little curl again. It had been wetted and put straight. He moved his hand to give her room without looking up from what Catrane was saying.

I should have been satisfied. Instead I noticed that Eitha’s sleeve brushed the table.

“Take care,” I said.

She drew back. No wine had spilled.

“There are dishes waiting at the other end. Let Nerla do this.”

Her face changed only a little. She gave the jug to the younger girl beside the hatch and went down between the tables. I watched her pass the musician with the blanket. He raised his eyes to her and she shook her head. I thought they were making a joke about me. I knew I was probably wrong and disliked them for the possibility anyway.

Massen asked if the smoke troubled me.

“No.”

“You’re frowning.”

“It’s the face I have.”

“I’ve seen others.”

I turned towards him. He was smiling, but he let the smile go when I did not answer it. That was worse. I reached for his cup, filled it myself from the jug the girl had left, and asked him about his sister’s horse. I made the question more interested than it deserved to be. He was kind enough to answer at length.

Across the table Bran was cutting his meat into small pieces. He had lost a back tooth and did not like people to know that certain food troubled him. My father had forgotten twice and offered him hard crusts with an enthusiasm that sounded deliberate even when it was not. Bran had put them beside his plate. One by one they disappeared beneath the table to the dog.

Lira sat beside Catrane. I had put her there because she had lived among Lorns and could answer questions about them without making the questions seem important. She was doing it well. When Catrane asked whether the western track remained bad beyond the birches, Lira described a new drain and a turn where carts had to take the uphill wheel carefully. Massen interrupted to say the drain needed clearing again. Lira said it had been cleared a week before he last used it; perhaps his cousin had been looking at the wrong ditch.

Gedran heard his name and demanded to know what he had done now. Everybody gave him a different answer. He protested with increasing pleasure as he discovered nobody was angry.

Then my father began about the cattle.

He did not say he was beginning. He asked whether the red cow with the broken horn had been brought up from the lower meadow, and Massen said it had, and my father said it would do well on Lorn ground if somebody there knew enough to keep it out of the bog. The conversation moved from the cow to the number of animals travelling with me, and from that to the winter grazing they would have. Men who had been talking to someone else grew quiet without turning their heads.

“We’ve agreed the southern strip,” Massen said.

“For the first winter.”

“For the first winter, and the drove through the upper passage after thaw.”

“If the track holds.”

“We’ll repair it.”

“With my men.”

“With men we’ll pay.”

My father looked at Bran. Bran was still cutting meat.

“Your people will have work,” Massen added.

“They have work.”

“Then we’ll bring our own.”

That was said pleasantly. I could feel the room becoming colder around the pleasantness.

“My father’s asking who chooses them,” I said.

Massen looked at me. “Are you?” he asked my father.

My father took a drink. “I’m asking whether a man may know who’s walking across his own ground.”

“Of course.”

“And whether he may turn them away if he dislikes what they bring.”

I felt Massen’s knee move beside mine. He put down his knife.

“If they’re carrying sheep, I hope he’ll say which sheep he dislikes.”

The laughter was grateful. Even my father accepted some of it. Bran reached for the bread and said the upper track would not be carrying sheep before the thaw whatever anyone wrote down, and Catrane asked him how many bridges stood on it. He answered at once. She asked whether that counted the crossing near the ruined bothy. He said it did not, because it had never been a bridge, and for several minutes they disputed the name of an arrangement of stones that had apparently served as a bridge for thirty years without deserving the word.

I ate a piece of turnip. It had gone cold.

Massen leaned closer. “Was that the right answer?”

“Which one?”

“Any of them.”

“You’ll know tomorrow.”

He laughed softly. His hand found mine below the edge of the table. I let it stay.

The first song began at the lower end, without permission from the musicians. A woman whose husband had been telling the same story since noon sang three lines about a miller and stopped before the part everyone knew. Her husband objected. Someone supplied the next line. The whole table took it up, and the musician by the door wiped his hands and began to play.

It was a bad song. It had always been a bad song. I loved it with an unreasonable tenderness as the hall found its separate ways into the tune. My aunt sang the words from forty years before and refused to change when corrected. A child struck a spoon against a cup until Nerla took the spoon. My father leaned back in his chair and bellowed the last line so inaccurately that Bran stopped pretending to sing and laughed at him.

Massen had a better voice than I had expected. It was low and carried without being pushed. He knew a different verse. When the time came he sang it alone, confident that the rest of us would enjoy the surprise, and reached a word he had evidently never considered saying in front of Lady Catrane.

He stopped.

She supplied it.

I laughed so hard that I had to put my face against his shoulder. For a moment I could not stop. Each time I tried, I saw his expression again. He put his arm round me and took the laughter as if he had earned it honestly, which he had.

Later there was dancing. I had said there would be none until the following night because the tables would have to be moved, but nobody asked me once the fiddler had begun. Benches were lifted. Cups were rescued. My father was taken by both hands into a dance he claimed his knee would not permit. His knee permitted a considerable amount before he remembered it.

Bran danced with me. He had taught me the steps in this room when I was too young to reach his shoulder. Now he held me at a careful distance and asked whether I remembered what came after the turn.

“I’ve done it before.”

“You’ve married before. We still expect you to pay attention.”

I trod on his foot. He said nothing, which was how I knew I had hurt him.

When Massen took his place, the difference in their hands startled me. My uncle’s were familiar through years of touch. Massen’s still seemed to require a decision. He held me closer than Bran had. Not much. Enough that the room became something moving beyond his shoulder.

“Are you cold?” he asked.

“No.”

“Your hand is.”

“It’s always cold.”

“Both?”

“You’ll have to find out.”

He missed the next step. I enjoyed that more than I should have.

At the end of the dance he led me to the passage beside the kitchen, where the air was cooler and the music came through the open door in bursts as people passed. I expected him to kiss me there. He waited until I had looked back into the hall, then put his hand against my cheek. The kiss was neither tentative nor particularly accomplished at first; he caught the corner of my mouth and had to move, and I turned too quickly because I had imagined this so often that the real movement felt delayed.

Then we managed it.

His beard was rougher than I expected. I put my hand on his wrist. There was a short, foolish interval in which I thought about the grease I had put on my burn and whether he would smell it. He did not seem to mind. When he drew away, his forehead remained close to mine.

“Come outside,” he said.

“It’s freezing.”

“I know.”

I shook my head and laughed. I wanted him to ask again. He kissed the place beneath my ear, where nobody had touched me for four years, and my amusement left me so abruptly that I gripped his coat.

Someone came down the stair.

We stepped apart. Lira passed carrying a small lamp and a folded cloth. She looked at us once, then went towards the kitchen without a word. I called her name too sharply.

“What?”

“Where have you been?”

“Upstairs.”

“I can see that.”

“Then why did you ask?”

She went on. Massen watched her disappear through the kitchen door.

“She’s tired,” he said.

“So am I.”

He turned back to me. There was a question in his face I was not ready to answer. I took his hand and led him into the hall, where my father was insisting that Bran had promised to sing.

My uncle stood before the hearth with his cup in his hand, refusing in a manner that made everybody ask again. Beyond his shoulder I could no longer see the kitchen hatch. Eitha was at the far table, collecting dishes. The musician with the blanket said something to her as she passed, and this time she smiled.

I sat on my new cushion. The fire warmed my back through the wood of the settle. Above us, in the room being made ready for my marriage, someone walked from the window to the bed and back again.

I thought it must be Druven. I did not ask.

Chapter 6 — What was put away

I woke before light with the taste of wine still on my tongue and the certainty that someone had called me.

The room was dark enough that the shutter made no separate shape in the wall. I lay listening. Below, a log shifted in the kitchen fire. A man coughed in the passage, then there was the scrape of his heel as he went down the stair. It was not my name. I had been dreaming of Massen, which embarrassed me even alone, and whatever I had heard belonged to the dream.

I put my feet out of bed and found one stocking with my toes. The other had fallen behind the chest. Retrieving it required a candle. By the time I had lit one from the little banked pot of embers Nerla left for me, I was fully awake and cross with the day for beginning before I had agreed to it.

My hand still hurt where I had burned it. I touched the place with my thumb and watched the skin blanch. Then I remembered Massen’s mouth beneath my ear and ceased to feel anything else for a while.

Downstairs the kitchen was only beginning. Nerla stood by the hearth in a grey shawl, pushing the embers together with the end of a broken tool. Eitha slept on a bench, wrapped in her cloak with both feet drawn up. Her cap had fallen off. Without it she looked younger, and the curl I had disliked was part of an untidy mass of pale hair pressed against her cheek.

“Don’t wake her,” Nerla said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You’re about to walk into that pan.”

I stopped. In the candlelight the pan was barely distinguishable from the floor. Nerla moved it with her foot and pointed me towards a stool.

“There’s water.”

I drank. It was cold enough to hurt my teeth. Through the open hatch the hall lay in heaps of sleeping people, benches and abandoned clothing. The fire at its far end showed one red eye under the ash. Someone had laid a hand above a blanket with all the fingers spread. I could not see who the hand belonged to.

“Was Druven upstairs last night?” I asked.

“For a while.”

“After the dancing?”

“I wasn’t watching the dancing.”

“I thought I heard her.”

“Then you probably did.”

Nerla knelt to blow gently at the fire. The light strengthened along one side of her face. I had seen her do this every winter I could remember. When I was a child she would let me hold a scrap of bark ready for the first flame, but never let me blow, because I blew too hard and scattered ash into the room.

“Did Bran open the recess?”

She sat back on her heels.

“He had the chest moved.”

“And?”

“And put back.”

“Did Druven see it?”

“I don’t know.”

The answer came more slowly than the others. I waited for something to follow it. Nothing did. Nerla put the bark into the little flame and stayed bent over it until it caught.

There was bread left from supper. I cut some and held it near the fire on the point of a long fork. One side blackened before the other had warmed. Nerla took it from me, turned it, and gave it back without looking at my face.

“You can still eat it.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t make that face.”

“What face?”

“The one you’ve been making since you came down.”

I ate standing up. The bread cracked at the edge and was soft within. When I took a second piece, she pushed the butter towards me.

The third day of the gathering ought to have been easier. Most guests had arrived, the gown was nearly finished, and the marriage terms had been read so often that even those who could not follow the writing could repeat their favourite objections. But there were still the beds, the men coming from the western holdings, my father’s decision that we lacked a proper head of meat, the missing buckle for my cloak, and a quarrel between two women over whose child had wet a borrowed blanket. I knew all these things before the light came.

I went to the store room to fetch the last cups. The blue cloth was folded beside them. I took the small repaired one in my hand and found it cold, as it had been the day before. The silver mend had darkened. I rubbed it with the corner of the blue cloth until a little brightness showed, then put the cup on the tray.

Eitha woke while I was carrying them into the kitchen. She sat up so quickly that her cloak fell off the bench. She looked first towards Nerla and then at the window, as if someone might accuse her of having stolen daylight.

“There’s no hurry,” I said.

She rubbed her eyes. “I wasn’t asleep.”

“Nobody said you were.”

She picked up the cloak and folded it. I wanted to say something about the night before. The words formed themselves into an explanation of how busy I had been, which I knew would make her look at me with that perfectly obedient face. I asked instead whether her hands were sore from the washing.

“A little.”

“Use the grease.”

“I do.”

She put on her cap and went to the pump. I was annoyed that being kind had not relieved me.

By the time the eastern windows grew pale, Lira had joined us. She carried the finished sleeve as if it were something killed after a long pursuit. The gown could be put on after breakfast, she said, but I must not sit in it until she had checked the back seam. Nerla told her to eat. She said she had eaten. Nerla put a bowl in front of her anyway.

There was a streak of dark mud above Lira’s shoe.

“Have you been outside?” I asked.

She looked down.

“Yesterday.”

“You changed those shoes yesterday.”

“Then after I changed them.”

She began to eat. Her hand shook once as she lifted the spoon, and she steadied it against the edge of the bowl. I thought of the lamp and folded cloth I had seen her carrying on the stair. I thought of Massen’s hand on my face. I decided that asking another question would sound like punishing her for having seen us, and left her alone.

Bran came in as the hall began waking. He was dressed for outdoors and had his gloves tucked into his belt. He took bread from the board, kissed Nerla on the top of her head as he passed, and was struck with the back of her spoon for it.

“Druven’s cupboard?” I said.

“Dry.”

“I asked whether she’d seen it.”

“You asked about a cupboard.”

“Uncle.”

He sighed, but lightly. “I opened it. The lower joint is old and dry. There’s no smoke getting through. I’ll show her when she comes down.”

“Thank you.”

“We could put your father in it if that would reassure you.”

“It would improve breakfast.”

He smiled and went out through the yard door. Lira watched him go. Her spoon remained in the bowl until Nerla told her the food was not getting warmer by being observed.

I spent the morning carrying things out of rooms and discovering that they had been carried into different rooms I also needed. My aunt had acquired a second mattress because she distrusted the first. Catrane’s attendant had washed a cloak and hung it across the only place the bridal sheets could be aired. Three little boys had found the basket of small apples and bitten one each before deciding they did not want apples. I ate one of the bitten ones as I went up to the bridal room, furious at the waste and at the sticky patch it left on my hand.

Druven was there, putting the last plaster over the repaired joint. She smoothed it with a flat tool, drew the tool away, looked at what remained and smoothed it again. Bellan knelt on a folded sack, keeping her bowl within reach.

“Did you see the lower place?” I asked.

“I saw the door.”

“Bran said—”

“He showed me a little cupboard. There are shelves at the back. I’d have to take them out to see the footing.”

“Does it need doing?”

She continued with the plaster. “If there’s trouble below, yes. There’s no heat showing through here that shouldn’t be.”

“So it’s safe.”

“I’ve mended the flue I was asked to mend.”

I heard the warning in it and resented her for leaving it with me. There was no smoke, no visible crack in the floor, nothing I could take to my father except another woman’s refusal to promise what he wanted promised. The wedding was tomorrow. I looked at the smooth new patch and asked how long before we could cover it with the hanging.

“Let it breathe today.”

That, at least, was something I could arrange.

I put the apple core in the basket and went to my room for the little box I would take with me. Kennet’s letter lay at its bottom in Bran’s oiled cloth. Above it were two ribbons, a comb with three teeth missing, and the small round brooch I had been allowed to keep because it had belonged to me before I married. My mother had given it to me. I could have asked her where it came from while she was alive. I had asked instead why it was not gold.

I sat on the bed with the box on my knees.

I did not take out the letter. I knew what it said. What troubled me was the effort of remembering how Kennet would have spoken the last line. I could hear his annoyance clearly. I could hear the way he said a horse’s name when it would not stand. But the voice answering me from another room, casual and expecting supper, had become less certain. When I tried to hear it, I supplied something too gentle.

A knock came at the door. Massen asked if he could come in.

I shut the box before answering. He saw me set it aside and made no move towards it. He was carrying the missing cloak buckle.

“Your uncle said you were looking for this.”

It had been cleaned. The little tongue moved freely instead of catching halfway through. I took it and turned it over in my hand.

“He remembers everything.”

Massen leaned against the doorpost. “Nearly.”

“What has he forgotten?”

“That I was meant to give it to you before breakfast.”

“Where were you?”

“With Gedran.”

I waited.

“He wanted to go down to the horses again. I’ve persuaded him to admire them from this side of the water.”

I smiled. He came in then, and sat beside me without touching the box. The bed dipped under his weight. It was a narrow bed, and I was suddenly aware of how many things in the room were placed to suit one person alone.

“Will you miss it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He nodded.

“I still want to go.”

“I know.”

I wanted him to tell me about the room that would be mine. He had described it before, but I asked again, and he did. A window looking towards a hill with no particular view beyond it. A hearth that drew better when the door was open. A chest his sister had said I could use, though she had not emptied it yet. There would be room for my mother’s cups. He offered this last detail carefully, having heard more than I thought through the kitchen hatch.

I leaned against him. He put his arm around my shoulders. We sat like that while below us the household grew louder.

When he left, I put Kennet’s letter into the pocket of the travelling cloak. Then I opened the box again and took out the broken comb. There was no reason to carry it to another house. I held it for a while, found a piece of cloth to wrap it in, and put it back.

Chapter 7 — A man without a token

The stranger arrived with the last light caught in the wet places of his coat.

I was in the forecourt when the porter brought him through. A cart had come up from the lower grazing with the promised head of meat, which proved to be a sheep my father had already decided he owned. The men delivering it disagreed. The sheep, still alive and inconveniently attentive, watched the argument from beneath the cart with a rope round its neck.

The porter waited for a pause. None came. He cleared his throat twice, and then the stranger said he would sit if there was somewhere to sit. It was an ordinary request, made with the flat exhaustion of a man who had spent the last hour promising himself a wall out of the wind.

I pointed to the bench under the eaves. He lowered a wooden chest onto it first, eased its strap from his shoulder and stood looking at the space that remained. I moved a folded sack. He sat.

His boots were good, but one had been repaired with a piece of leather a different colour from the rest. Mud had dried in a high band above both ankles and been wetted again. He had a long face, a short grey beard and eyes reddened by the wind. Nothing about him suggested a person who would be welcomed at the high table. I thought he was a tradesman who had mistaken the gathering for a market.

“Have you come far?” I asked.

“Far enough today.”

He unfastened the cord at his throat. One hand was clumsy with cold. I nearly reached to help him, then remembered that men sometimes preferred to strangle themselves rather than be helped by a woman younger than their daughters.

“There’s warm water inside.”

“I’d be glad of it.”

“Are you expected?”

He looked at the sheep. It had found a loose end of the sack beneath the cart and begun chewing.

“I sent word.”

“To my father?”

“If your father is the laird.”

“He is.”

“Yes.”

That was the first answer he had given me without qualifying it. I stood there long enough to become aware that I was making him account for himself while I had just offered him water. The porter remained beside us. He had the embarrassed expression of someone who knew the question he was supposed to ask and disliked asking it in front of me.

“Who gave you shelter last?” I said.

“A woman below the western road. I paid for my bed.”

“Have you her token?”

“No.”

He said it without defiance. I wished he had sounded defiant; it would have been easier to know what to do. My father chose that moment to free himself from the sheep and come towards us.

“What have we here?”

“A traveller,” I said.

“So I see.”

The man began to rise. My father waved him down and stood over him with his hands inside his belt. He enjoyed this position. It made him look generous before he had decided whether to be.

“Your name?”

“Odran Vale.”

“From?”

“The western holdings, lately. Before that, the coast.”

“Whose man?”

“My own.”

The porter looked away. My father glanced at the chest, then at me, and I knew he was remembering the people who would hear about what he did next.

“We’re not short of men who think that,” he said. “What brings you to my door?”

“I have a matter for your brother. And for you, if he’ll hear it with us.”

“Business?”

“Yes.”

“There’s a wedding.”

“I know.”

My father rubbed his lower lip with his thumb. The sheep bleated behind him. One of the men called out that it had eaten the end of the sack, and my father told him the sack would be deducted from the price if he could prove it had been worth anything.

Odran coughed. It was a dry, painful sound. I went to the kitchen before anybody decided that a cough also required an account of itself.

Nerla gave me a mug of hot water with a little honey in it. When I returned, my father had put a hand on the stranger’s shoulder.

“Come in, then. You’ll be warm before you tell it.”

He said it well. I had heard him admit poor men as graciously when there was no one present worth impressing. He could do a kindness with his whole attention, so that the person receiving it forgot for a moment how much it had cost to ask.

Odran took the mug from me with both hands. His fingers were long and clean under the mud. There was a small cut beside one nail, sealed with something dark.

“Thank you,” he said.

I lifted the chest before he could stop me. It was heavier than I expected. The weight dragged at my burned hand and I set it down again too hard.

“I’ll have someone carry it.”

“I can manage.”

“So can I, if I get someone else.”

He smiled then, briefly, and the smile changed his age. He seemed less old and more tired. I called to one of the men by the cart. Together he and Odran brought the chest into the hall.

The fire had been built up for evening. Its heat met us in a broad wave, carrying the smell of fat and singed wool. Someone had hung damp stockings too close, and Nerla was threatening to let them burn if their owner did not come for them. My father led the stranger towards the hearth with the expansive step he used when he wanted a room to see how much room he had.

“Give him a place.”

There were places at the lower tables. None near the fire remained empty. My aunt had spread her skirts across a bench, and the man beside her pretended not to notice that he could have moved. I looked at my settle and said the stranger could sit there until he had warmed himself.

My father repeated the offer in a voice twice as loud, and several people nodded as though he had thought of it.

Odran sat at the end nearest the hearth. His wet coat began to steam. I watched the moisture darken the new cushion beneath him and wished I had put a cloth down before being generous. He placed the chest between his feet, with one knee against its corner. The man who had helped carry it rubbed his shoulder and asked whether it was full of stones.

“Some,” Odran said.

The man laughed, expecting a joke to follow. None did.

Massen came across from the west door. He had been outside and brought cold with him, clean against the close air of the hall. I introduced the stranger as Odran Vale and discovered that I had no further account of him to give. Massen asked where he had come down from the western road. They spoke for a few minutes about a washed-out bend and a house where a man would lend a horse if a traveller left enough possessions to make returning worthwhile.

“You stayed there?” Massen asked.

“No. I walked.”

“It’s a long descent.”

“Yes.”

The answer was not unfriendly. It ended the conversation all the same. Massen looked at the chest and then at me. I shook my head slightly. He took the hint and asked whether I had seen Gedran.

“He’s avoiding the burn.”

“He’s avoiding his wet coat. He’s put on mine.”

“Does it improve him?”

“I haven’t seen him yet.”

He left us. Odran held the mug close to his face and breathed the steam. The fire lit the underside of his beard. I could see where a patch of hair had grown across a little white scar beneath his chin.

“Will you take your coat off?” I asked.

“In a moment.”

“It’ll dry better.”

“I know.”

I went away before I became angry with a man for wanting to remain warm inside his own wet clothes.

Bran was in the kitchen passage with Lira. They stopped speaking as I approached. That was not unusual; people stopped speaking in front of me whenever they were discussing the gown or the food or whether the marriage was wise. I disliked all three possibilities equally.

“Your visitor’s here,” I said.

“What visitor?”

“Odran Vale.”

Lira looked at Bran. He did not look at her.

“Where?”

“By the fire.”

“Did he say what he wanted?”

“To speak to you.”

He brushed something from his sleeve. “Then I’d better speak to him.”

He went past me into the hall. Lira stayed in the passage, holding the lamp she had been carrying the night before. It was unlit now. A little soot marked its handle.

“Do you know him?” I asked.

“No.”

“Do you know of him?”

She considered the distinction long enough to make me impatient.

“I’ve seen his name.”

“Where?”

“In some work Bran had copied.”

“What work?”

“Old work.”

“That’s helpful.”

She tightened her hand on the lamp.

“I don’t know what he’s come for.”

I could hear that she meant it. I let her go, though I wanted to keep asking, and returned to the hall through the kitchen so I could see the stranger without seeming to follow my uncle.

Through the hatch I watched Bran pull a stool beside the settle. He sat lower than Odran, leaning forward, one elbow on his knee. It was his listening posture. He had listened to me like that when I was angry with my father, and to tenants who had come with a sick animal or a demand they did not know how to make without offending him.

Odran shook his head.

Bran said something I could not hear. The stranger put the mug down and rested both hands on the chest.

“Tonight,” he said.

The word reached me through a brief quiet in the kitchen.

My uncle’s head moved slightly. Odran continued speaking more softly. Bran looked towards the doorway, and I stepped back from the hatch before he could see me watching.

Nerla was standing behind me with a dishcloth.

“What?” I said.

“Are you helping?”

“I brought him in.”

“I meant here.”

She put the cloth into my hand and pointed to the warmed cups. I dried their feet one by one, turning each against the fabric. The little repaired cup caught at a loose thread. I freed it carefully and set it at the end of the row, where it would not be knocked by the larger ones.

“Will he eat with us?” she asked.

“Father’s offered him the fire. I don’t know whether he’s offered supper.”

“He’ll eat, then.”

She went through the hatch with bread and a small dish. I watched her put them on the table in front of Odran. Bran stood aside. The stranger thanked her, and this time took off his coat.

Under it he wore a dark waistcoat mended at one pocket. The shirt beneath was clean enough to show he had expected to be received somewhere, though its collar had frayed. When he lifted the bread his hand trembled. He put it down, waited, and lifted it again more successfully.

I looked away. It felt indecent to keep watching him try to eat.

The light had nearly gone when I next crossed the hall. Odran remained on the settle, but the chest was now beside it rather than between his feet. He had opened a small outer compartment and was taking out a pair of dry stockings. He saw me and closed it again.

“There’s a room for changing,” I said.

“I wasn’t going to do it here.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“I know.”

He smiled without much strength. “My feet are sore. I was thinking how long it would take to persuade myself to stand.”

I showed him the little chamber off the west passage. It had been used for storing folded bedding and contained a stool, a basin and no fire. He thanked me as if it were better accommodation than I had intended to offer. I brought him water warmed from the kettle and left him with the door closed.

As I turned away I heard him make a low sound, not quite a cry. I stopped, one hand lifted towards the latch. There was a pause, then the sound of a boot being put on the floor.

I went on.

At the kitchen door Bran caught up with me. He asked where the stranger had gone, and I told him.

“He shouldn’t be wandering.”

“He’s changing his stockings.”

“Oh.”

He looked towards the closed door. For a moment he seemed about to go there. Then someone called him from the hall and he turned back.

“Keep him by the fire,” he said. “He’s had a hard road.”

I nodded. It was good advice, and kindly given. When Odran came out carrying his damp stockings in one hand, I took them from him and hung them on a peg near enough to the hearth to dry without burning.

He watched me do it.

“My daughter does that,” he said.

“Washes your stockings?”

“Tells me where I’ve put them is wrong.”

I smiled. “Is it?”

“Usually.”

He sat down, lowering himself carefully. I put the stool beneath his feet without asking whether he wanted it. His eyes closed for a moment.

When they opened again he looked at the cushion beside him.

“This is your place.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve wet it.”

“It will dry.”

“I hadn’t thought.”

“Neither had I.”

He looked at me then with more attention than he had given me before. I was glad I had said it. We sat for a little while without speaking, both watching the fire.

Chapter 8 — The verse after the fire

At the last meal before my wedding, the meat was excellent and I hardly tasted it.

The hall had grown too warm. People arriving cold from the yard fed the fire before their own cheeks had thawed, while those who had been sitting beside it began loosening their collars. My father would not have the main door left open. Every time someone propped it for air, he called across the room that we were not heating the hill. The door was closed, the draught stopped, and the smoke settled a little lower beneath the rafters.

I wore the new gown for the first time before everyone. Lira had finished it in the afternoon. The sleeve lay smoothly at the wrist, the back seam did not pull, and the colour, which had looked dull in my room, deepened near the candles until even my aunt admitted it suited me. She added that I must take care not to stain it. I wanted to stain hers.

Massen rose when I came in. He had changed his coat and shaved the untidy edge of his beard. There was a tiny cut beneath his jaw. I wanted to put my mouth against it, which was a surprising response to a shaving injury and occupied me through most of my father’s welcome.

Odran had been given the far end of the high table, still on my settle. I sat between Massen and Catrane on the long bench. It was less comfortable. My father made a little speech about guests and brides and all the good the two houses would do one another, during which he said nothing that anyone could later require him to perform. Massen listened with his hands folded. Bran watched the lower tables.

Nerla came round with the first jug. She had the repaired green cup on a small tray with two others. I saw it because she put it before Odran and he turned it in his hand, examining the silver mend. He did not drink yet. He asked for water first. She pointed to the little ewer near his bread and he thanked her.

My father had invited the oldest man present to begin the meal. This was a mistake. The man had lived long enough to believe that any occasion which might be his last required his complete contribution. He praised our cattle, our women, the weather, the memory of three men nobody at the lower tables could identify, and the strength of a marriage between houses which had occasionally had cause to differ. At that phrase Catrane closed her eyes briefly.

When he had finished, the food came. The room released itself into eating. The sounds were intimate and untidy: knives on boards, breath drawn through teeth at a hot mouthful, the heavy settling of a dish where the table rocked. I heard Massen thank the girl who served him and was pleased by it in a way I would have despised if somebody had explained it to me.

“Are you hungry?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“You’ve been looking at that piece for a long time.”

I ate it to avoid answering. He smiled and turned to Catrane, who wanted to know how the western houses meant to share the cost of repairing the track. I listened while pretending not to. His answer was practical. He knew which stretch needed stone brought up, which could be drained, how many days a team could work before the first deep frost made the labour wasteful. My father interrupted twice. Massen let him finish both times, and then returned to what he had been saying.

Odran listened too.

I became aware of it gradually. He ate slowly, keeping one foot drawn back beneath the settle as if it hurt to rest it normally. His attention moved with the speakers. When my uncle reached for the salt, Odran followed the movement of his hand.

“You’ve worked on roads?” Massen asked him at last.

“On the places beside them.”

“What sort?”

“Houses that are no longer houses.”

My father laughed uncertainly. “We’ve enough of those.”

“Yes,” Odran said.

There was no rudeness in the word. Bran asked whether the western descent had been clear. We had already discussed that, but Odran answered again, describing the washed-out bend without looking at him.

“And you’ll be going back that way?”

“I hope so.”

“Not tomorrow, if the snow comes.”

“No.”

My uncle nodded. I felt the table waiting for the exchange to become ordinary. Nerla arrived with more bread and gave us all something to do with our hands.

At the lower end, Gedran had begun a game involving the naming of horses no one would ever wish to ride. The answers grew worse with the wine. One of the drovers suggested a name that made my father shout approval across the room. Bellan, sitting beside the musicians, asked his mother what it meant. Druven told him it meant the man had drunk enough.

I laughed. Odran smiled into his cup but did not drink from it. It occurred to me that he might be waiting until someone else had tasted the wine, and the thought made me dislike him. He had come into a wedding house, accepted its best warmth and now sat there measuring us. Then I saw him rub his thumb against the base of the cup to warm it, and knew I had invented the suspicion. He asked the passing boy whether it could stand by the fire again. The boy took it to Nerla at the hatch.

Catrane asked me where I would keep the green cups at Lorn.

I looked towards my father before I could stop myself. He was listening to the horse names.

“In the room Massen’s described.”

“Good.”

“They were my mother’s.”

“I remember.”

“She used to say the small one made sour wine better.”

“That sounds like her.”

I wanted to ask Catrane what else she remembered. There was never a good moment to ask people about my mother. They became tender and grave, and told me things they thought a daughter would want, which were usually things no woman had ever been. Catrane took a piece of bread and used it to gather the last of the sauce from her plate. I liked her too much in that moment to risk it.

The old man who had opened the meal stood again. Several people tried to stop him without saying so. He waved away the offered stool, the wine, his nephew’s suggestion that someone else might begin, and announced that he would sing the welcome as it had been sung before any of us were born.

Nerla, at the hatch, set down a dish with unnecessary force.

He stood directly in front of the high table, between me and the kitchen, and cleared his throat. One of the musicians offered a starting note. He rejected it, tried a note of his own, coughed, and accepted the first one.

The verse began pleasantly enough. Fire for fingers, water for feet. He stretched the old melody until the words seemed to be finding their way across a wide room. People joined softly. I could hear Lira singing farther down the table. She knew more of it than I did.

The third line differed from my mother’s version. The fourth differed again. My father corrected him aloud.

The singer stopped.

“That’s not how my father sang it.”

“Your father forgot it.”

“My father heard it from his mother.”

“So did I.”

“Not from mine.”

The room laughed, and for a moment the quarrel remained harmless. Bran rose to speak to Nerla. I saw him go behind the singer towards the hatch. He stood with one hand on the serving board, leaning into the kitchen. The singer moved half a pace, blocking him from my sight.

Massen bent towards me. “Which version do you know?”

“Whichever lets us eat the next course.”

He laughed. Catrane was watching the old man, her expression attentive now. Odran had set down his knife.

Lira sang a line into the gap.

“A way for your leaving—”

My father turned his head.

“No,” he said.

She stopped.

“That belongs to another song.”

The old singer looked at her, then at my father. He opened his mouth and closed it again. The musician laid his bow across his knees.

For the first time that evening I heard the fire distinctly. A pocket of wet in the peat hissed, then burst with a small sharp crack. Someone at the lower table laughed too late at something no longer being said.

Bran came back carrying the wine jug. He set it beside my father and sat down. Nerla followed with the next dish and Odran’s warmed cup. She told the singer that if he wished his portion hot he must take his place, and he obeyed her with the relief of a man offered a way to stop.

“I’m sorry,” Lira said.

My father was looking at the food. He did not answer.

I wanted to tell her she had done nothing wrong. Instead I asked Massen to pass her the bread. He did, reaching across the awkward space between us, and Lira thanked him without looking up.

The room recovered unevenly. Gedran called for the horse game to resume. Somebody objected that he had already used the best name. My aunt asked Odran whether he had family on the coast, and he said he had a daughter inland. She asked if the daughter was married. He said no. She began to advise him about it.

I watched Bran’s hands. He was rubbing one thumb against the other beneath the edge of the table. I thought the old tooth must be troubling him again. He caught me looking and smiled.

“What’s wrong?” I asked.

“Nothing.”

“Does it hurt?”

He understood after a moment and touched his jaw.

“A little.”

“I can ask Nerla—”

“Leave her be. She’s done enough.”

Nerla had returned to the hatch. The repaired cup was before Odran. He lifted it, tasted, and frowned slightly. My aunt was still explaining that a daughter who waited too long would discover that men did not improve in storage. He put the cup down, answered her courteously and reached for the water.

The old singer stood a third time.

“No song,” he said quickly, seeing our faces. “A wish for the bride.”

My father waved him on.

I remember the wish because it was kind and a little embarrassing. He hoped my second house would know when to leave me in peace. People laughed, including my father, who did not appear to think the words concerned him. Massen took my hand beneath the table. I was looking at him when the stranger made the first sound.

It was like a man trying to swallow with his mouth empty.

Odran had one hand at his throat. My aunt stopped speaking. He looked down at his cup, then towards the kitchen hatch, as if the person who had brought it might explain what was happening. His fingers tightened on the table’s edge.

“Water,” I said.

Massen was already moving. He reached across, took the ewer and offered it. Odran tried to drink and could not. Water ran down his chin onto his waistcoat. The stranger shook his head violently, angry with his own mouth.

Someone called for the healer.

He stood. I saw his knee strike the chest beside the settle. The cup fell from the table and hit the rushes without breaking. Wine ran into the new cushion, making a dark, widening mark.

Then he fell against Massen.

For a moment they seemed to be embracing. Massen held him beneath the arms, trying to lower him without striking his head on the bench. My father was shouting for space. The old singer remained where he stood, his hands held out before him. I pushed past him hard enough to make him stagger.

Odran’s face had changed. It was still his face, but the work of breathing had drawn it into an expression I did not know how to look at. His mouth opened. I heard the air catch, thin and high, and felt something in my own throat answer.

Nerla knelt beside him. She loosened his collar and put her fingers beneath his jaw. A man in a dark coat came through the crowd, carrying a bag I had seen under the lower table. I knew he had come with Catrane. I had not known he was a healer.

“Move,” he said.

I moved. Massen did not until the healer touched his arm and told him where to put his hands. Between them they laid Odran on his side. His feet struck the wooden chest. I caught one boot before it could hit again, and found myself holding it with both hands, as if keeping his foot still might help the rest of him.

The heel was wet. His new stocking showed between the boot and the end of his hose.

The healer asked what he had eaten. Four people answered. He asked what he had drunk and everyone looked at the table.

“The green cup,” I said.

“Which?”

I pointed. Eitha had picked it up from the rushes. She was holding it by the foot, away from her apron. A little wine remained inside.

“Don’t wash it.”

She stared at me.

“Put it down. There.”

I indicated the hearth ledge. She set it carefully where I pointed. Her hand was shaking.

Odran made another sound. It was lower than the first. The healer bent close to him, speaking into his ear. I could not hear the words. My father had stopped shouting. The hall was full of people trying not to move, and from somewhere among them came a child asking whether the man was drunk.

I wanted somebody to take the child away. Nobody did.

The stranger’s hand closed on Massen’s sleeve. Massen bent towards him. Whatever he said was lost in the effort of saying it. His eyes moved past Massen to the hearth, and stayed there.

I lost all sense of how long the healer worked. He asked for a cloth, for light, for the people behind him to stand back. I gave him a napkin from the table. It had a little red flower worked into one corner. My mother had sewn half the flowers and Nerla the rest, and I could always tell which was which. I remember trying to see who had sewn that one while the healer pressed it beneath the stranger’s mouth.

At last he stopped asking for things.

Massen looked at him. The healer shook his head.

My hands were still round Odran’s boot. I let go. His leg settled a little farther into the rushes, heavier than I expected.

For several breaths nobody spoke.

Then my aunt said, very clearly, “He’s been poisoned.”

The word moved through the room faster than any of us had moved to help him.

My father stood. He had been kneeling without my noticing. There were rushes caught in his hose and a dark mark on one palm. He looked towards the lower tables, where men were already turning to find their own people.

“Sit down,” he said.

Nobody sat.

He struck the table with his hand. The cups jumped.

“Sit down.”

This time some obeyed. Others remained standing, held by the attention of those who watched them. Bran moved towards the main door and spoke to the porter. I could not hear what he said.

“No one draws steel in my hall,” my father said. “No one answers this with another death. You’re under my roof. Whoever has done this is under it too, until we know what has been done.”

He looked at the healer.

“Was it poison?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then we will find out.”

I looked towards the hearth ledge. The green cup stood where Eitha had put it, tilted slightly on the uneven stone. I went to it and moved it to a flatter place. There was a thread of white grit along the repaired seam inside. I had never seen that there before.

Behind me, the healer asked for a board to carry the body.

I turned. My uncle was coming back from the door, his face grey in the firelight.

“Brin,” he said. “Come away.”

I looked at the wet cushion, the overturned stool and the chest that nobody had moved. Odran’s damp stockings were still hanging from the peg beside the fire. One had fallen against the wall and would not dry properly.

I went to lift it clear.

Part II — The rooms given over

Chapter 9 — What the living ate

The board was the one on which we had carried the cheeses from the dairy. There was a crescent of yellow rind caught in a split near its end. Nerla saw it and picked it out before the men put him down.

They had trouble with his arms. One would stay where it was laid; the other slipped and hung towards the rushes, so that Massen, who had taken that end, had to set his knee beneath the board and put it back. I watched the healer fold Odran’s hands. They seemed larger with nothing in them.

“Where?” Massen asked.

My father looked towards the door behind the high table. It led to the small room where he received people he did not wish to entertain, and there was a good fire there already. I knew he wanted to offer it. Then he looked at Catrane and at the men by the southern door.

“The west room,” he said.

It was the room where I had given Odran a basin and left him struggling with his stockings. Wedding flowers stood in a bucket beneath its window. There was no fire, and the door would not stay open without a wedge. I went ahead of the board to move the basin, because I could do that, and because I did not want the men finding the dirty water and thinking that we had neglected him.

The water was still faintly warm. A smear on the rim showed where he had rested his foot.

I put the bucket of flowers into the passage and carried out the basin. Behind me someone said to take care of the head. They laid him across two stools and a low chest because the bed boards we needed were stored in the loft. His heels projected over the end. The wet stocking was mine to remember, hanging at the hearth, and I suddenly resented him for having left me something so small to remember while everyone else could speak about murder.

I went back for it.

In the hall, the meal was being taken away. A cousin lifted a platter, changed her mind and cut a piece from it before handing it to the boy beside her. He put it into his mouth without looking down. At the lower table, a woman was trying to wake a child who had slept through the death. She called him softly, then shook him harder than she meant to; he began to cry, and her own face crumpled in vexation. She could not pick him up with the basket on her arm. Bellan took the basket.

“There’s room in the kitchen,” he told her.

“There isn’t,” Nerla said, passing him. “Brewhouse. The fire’s going.”

“Brewhouse,” he repeated, and went with them.

The fiddler had wrapped his instrument. His companion sat holding a cup, both hands round it, as if he had forgotten what cups were for. A pale strip of wood shone where a string had rubbed through the dark finish of the fiddle. I had watched those hands all evening and heard almost nothing of the music.

My father stood in front of the main door.

“We’re not putting a bar across it,” he said.

One of his men had brought the iron down from its hooks. He held it awkwardly at his waist.

“No one leaves alone,” my father continued. “If you’ve someone to send for, speak to me or Lady Catrane. If you’ve beasts outside, you see to them with another man. Anyone who draws on a guest answers to me.”

A Lorn near the door made a short sound.

My father turned towards him. “You have something to say?”

“He answered to you.”

The man nodded towards the west passage.

Massen came through the passage behind me. There was a damp patch on his sleeve where Odran’s mouth had rested.

“He said no steel,” Massen told his man.

“I heard him.”

“Then let him hear that.”

The Lorn’s chin moved once. My father watched him a moment longer, then told the porter to put the bar away. He was red about the eyes. The room had gone still for him again.

I found Odran’s stocking almost dry. The toe had been darned in a lighter wool, roughly, with several loops standing proud. Someone had taken trouble without knowing how to do it neatly. I put it with the other one and folded them together.

The cup was gone from the ledge.

I went straight to the hatch. My skirt caught on a bench and pulled me back, and in freeing it I stepped on the hem. I heard a little tearing sound.

“Where is it?”

Nerla looked up from a dish of half-eaten apples.

“The green cup. The little one.”

“Up there.”

She pointed to a high shelf, above the hanging ladles. It stood alone, where none of the boys could mistake it for washing. I had to fetch the footstool to reach it. The grit was still inside, and the wine had dried to a dark skin round it.

“You could have told me.”

“You were carrying a man.”

“I wasn’t.”

Nerla slid the apples into another bowl. She did not answer. I held the cup against my breast and thought about what I had said.

“Keep the jug too,” I told her.

“Which?”

“The one he drank from.”

“We used four.”

I remembered my uncle returning with a jug. I remembered a boy bringing another and Massen pouring for me. All the jugs were brown earthenware except the pewter one reserved for water. I had never paid attention to which wine jug had been filled when.

“All of them, then.”

Nerla stopped her work.

“Brin, if you’ve a reason, give it to me. Don’t stand there frightening the girls.”

I turned the cup so she could see its inside. She came close, wiping her hands on her apron. One thumb hovered above the pale line without touching it.

“Was that there before?” I asked.

“I wouldn’t have put it out dirty.”

“I didn’t say you did.”

“You asked me.”

The kitchen smelled of cooling fat and crushed herbs. Behind her, two girls stood shoulder to shoulder, holding dishes they could not put down because every surface was full. Eitha was not among them.

“I polished the silver this morning,” I said. “I didn’t see it.”

Nerla took a breath through her nose. Then she called for the wine jugs to be set on the small table in the store room, with their cloths over the mouths, and told one of the girls to find Lady Catrane’s healer.

The girl put down her dish with such relief that gravy ran over its edge.

“Take that with you,” Nerla added, and watched her go before looking at me again. “I’ll have the cups kept. But they need to eat, Brin.”

“Who?”

She looked round the kitchen.

The boys who had served us were tearing bread from a loaf on the bench. One had sauce on his chin. The girls’ sleeves were damp to the elbows. Outside the door I could hear somebody being sick, followed by the sound of a bucket dragged nearer.

“Yes,” I said. “Of course.”

Nerla returned to the apples. She lifted out a piece with the tip of her knife and ate it. I had never seen her eat standing in front of me before.

The healer came with Catrane. His name was Hethen, and he had the narrow, reddened eyes of someone who did much of his work in poor light. He asked me to set the cup down. I did not want to let go of it until Catrane drew a stool beside the table and sat.

“No one will wash it,” she said.

Hethen looked inside, then at the repaired seam from the outside. He asked for a clean wooden skewer. Nerla supplied one from the meat tray, thought better of it and broke a fresh shaving from a piece of kindling instead.

“Can you tell?” I asked.

“By looking? No.”

“Would you know if—”

“I don’t put unknown things in my mouth.”

He spoke without impatience. I was grateful that he had not let me finish asking him to taste it. He touched the edge of the grit with the shaving and stopped when it flaked.

“That stays,” he said. “We’ll cover it. Which cups stood beside this one?”

I described the row as best I could. Nerla corrected me: the blue drinking bowl had not been there, it had gone to the musician with the split lip. She remembered the deeper green cup, the two brown cups belonging to the house and a horn someone had insisted on using. The horn’s owner was still holding it. Catrane sent for him.

Hethen asked what Odran had eaten. There was a plate, but several hands had cleared the high table and no one could swear which was his. A girl brought three plates and began to cry before she had put them down.

“Leave them,” Hethen said. “You’ve done nothing wrong.”

That did not stop her crying. Nerla took her by the shoulders and steered her out.

I stayed to tell Hethen about the water, the bread, the old man’s shaking hands. He asked whether Odran had spoken of an illness. I remembered the difficulty with his stockings and the hand he had pressed to his knee. I remembered almost nothing of what he had said while I was helping him.

“He was tired.”

“Most people are, coming up here.”

“I should have asked.”

Hethen spread a cloth over the cup. Catrane tied it below the lip with a length cut from her own sewing thread. It was red, a bright, expensive red I had admired that afternoon where it held a tear in her glove.

“Where will you keep it?” I asked.

“In my room,” she said. “With the jugs. You may see them whenever you wish, with me there.”

“My father will want them.”

“He may see them too.”

She waited until I nodded, then stood. Hethen took the cup. I followed them to the foot of the stair before remembering the stockings in my hand.

When I returned to the west room, the flowers were gone from the passage. Lira had brought the proper boards and clean linen. She was kneeling beside Odran, loosening the knot at his throat. It had swollen with wine or water and would not yield.

“I have scissors,” I said.

“He might want the cord.”

We both looked at him.

Lira shut her eyes. “His people might.”

I fetched my scissors and a fresh cord. When I came back she had unpicked the knot with a pin. She set the pin on the windowsill, straightened it with her thumbnail and put it back in her bodice.

From the hall came the clatter of bowls. Someone had found enough courage to ask for more bread.

Chapter 10 — The body in the west room

Nerla asked whether I had ever washed a man after death.

“My husband.”

“Who helped?”

“His sister.”

She waited.

“His sister did most of it.”

“Then keep near me.”

We had lit three candles. The light ran up the window glass without showing us anything beyond it. Outside, water dripped from the roof into the bucket Bellan had left beneath a bad joint. Each drop made a hollow sound. The flowers had been put in the loft; a few green leaves remained on the floor, their stems bruised to a strong sweetness under our feet.

Lira brought water from the kitchen. She had changed out of her feast dress into an old brown one with a torn fastening at the wrist. I was still in the gown she had fitted. The sleeve pulled when I lifted my arm. After a little while I went to take it off, and met Massen in the passage.

He moved aside for me, then saw where I was going.

“Do you need anything?”

“A gown I can ruin.”

He glanced at the hem I had torn. I expected him to make a joke, something about the expense of marrying me, and for a moment wanted it badly. He only said that he would wait below.

I changed in my own room. The lace had made a red pattern on my breast. I rubbed it with the flat of my hand, and the marks remained. On the bed lay the gloves my uncle had given me, one turned palm upwards. I picked them up and put them in the drawer. I did not want them watching me undress, though the thought was so foolish I would not have admitted it to Lira.

When I returned, Nerla had covered Odran to his waist. His shirt lay in a basin. There were two old repairs at the shoulder, one in fine thread and one in coarse, and a strip of new cloth strengthened the part where his pack had rubbed. Lira was washing his hands.

“There’s a little bag under the coat,” Nerla said. “Put it on the shelf. Don’t open it.”

I found the bag and a plain knife in a worn sheath. Both had lain hidden beneath the coat when we carried it in. The knife’s handle had been polished by a thumb along one side. I placed it away from the bag, because everything seemed to require separating now.

“His key?” Lira asked.

“What key?”

“He was feeling for it when he sat down. Here.”

She touched her own breast.

We found it in a stitched pocket inside his waistcoat: a small silver key, too delicate for any door I knew. Nerla made me set it on the shelf beside the bag. Then she held out the cloth.

“His face.”

I had washed Kennet’s face badly. I remembered his sister putting her hand over mine to stop me scrubbing, and how offended I had been. With Odran I began too gently, moving the cloth over his forehead without lifting the sweat and dust at the edge of his hair.

“He won’t bruise,” Nerla said.

I pressed harder. His beard caught the cloth. There was wine in it, and a crumb near the little scar under his chin. I loosened the crumb with a finger and dropped it into the basin. “Go on,” Nerla said.

I knew three prayers for a dead member of the house, and one for a traveller. The traveller’s prayer had always seemed short beside the others. I began it and discovered that I had forgotten the middle.

Afterwards, we left the key and ring in a wooden bowl. I asked Lira to come with me to Catrane’s room. She took the bowl, and I carried the candle.

At the turn of the passage I heard a boot being pulled off behind the wall.

There was the familiar scrape of heel against wood, a long breath, then a soft drop. I turned my head. The wall was bare stone, hung with a narrow cloth to hide an old stain. Beyond it lay the chimney footing and whatever small cupboard Bran had opened for Druven.

“What?” Lira asked.

“Nothing.”

She looked past me and drew the bowl closer to her body.

We went up the stair together. On the third step I heard the other boot fall.

Chapter 11 — The questions in the kitchen

By the time we reached Catrane, my father had decided that Odran’s chest should be opened in the morning.

“It’ll keep,” he said. “He’s beyond needing anything from it tonight.”

He stood in her doorway with Bran behind him. Catrane had been given the little east chamber, usually occupied by my father’s sister when she came to stay. Her things were still in it: a hairbrush full of pale hair, a cracked mirror, a painted box that smelled of the scent she wore. Catrane had moved them together on the chest and spread her own cloak over the chair. On the table, the covered cup stood among the brown jugs. It looked like a little head tied in a shroud.

“We have the key,” I said.

My father glanced at Lira’s bowl.

“Tomorrow, Brin.”

“His daughter should be sent word.”

“If there’s an address, we’ll find it.”

“In the morning?”

“Yes.”

He was speaking as he had when I wanted to keep a sick lamb in my room, each word carefully separated. I could feel myself becoming younger under it.

Catrane reached for the bowl. “We’ll put these here until then.”

Bran stepped forward.

“I can keep them.”

“They’re with the cup,” she said. “Easier.”

He nodded readily. “Very well.”

His fingers had been going towards the key. They closed instead on the back of the chair. He rubbed a thumb over the wood and asked whether she had enough fuel.

“Enough for tonight.”

“I’ll send more.”

He smiled at me as he passed. I could not smile back. The little sounds in the passage were still in my ears, and I was afraid that if I told him, he would laugh kindly and ask what else I had expected in an old house full of people.

My father went with him. Catrane waited until their steps had descended the stair.

“You think there is reason not to wait?” she asked me.

“I don’t know what I think.”

“Then sleep if you can.”

Lira set the candle down. “The ring is his working mark.”

Catrane looked at her. “Odran Vale’s?”

“Yes. Those two mends. We had an impression from it. One break cuts through a line that ought to run here.” She pointed without touching the face. “The other shows under it.”

“What did you copy?” I asked.

“A welcome. The words used in the lower house.”

“What lower house?”

“The old one.”

“Under ours?”

She stared at me as though I were trying to catch her in a lie. “Brin, I don’t know how much of it is still there.”

“Why were you copying it?”

“Bran asked me. Because I could read the hand.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I did. You said to leave old quarrels alone until after the wedding.”

I remembered a conversation over a basket of linen. I had been looking for a sheet that fitted the bridal bed, and Lira had talked about a lower door and somebody who remembered it. “You may both tell me in daylight,” she said. “If either of you remembers something that cannot wait, wake me.”

“You could have said it plainly,” I told her.

She walked away.

“He’ll take a groom.”

I sat beside him. For a while we listened to the hall. Someone was telling the same part of the same incident to a third person. Each time I heard it, Odran stood a little straighter before falling, and my father reached him a little sooner.

“Did he say anything to you?” I asked.

“When?”

“When he held your sleeve.”

“No.”

“Brin, he couldn’t breathe.”

“I was going to.”

“Good.”

“Lady Brin.”

“No.”

“Nerla.”

“He was at the high table.”

“I know where he sat.”

“I wasn’t serving there.”

“People passing.”

“Who?”

“Everyone.”

Nerla put a hand on the back of Eitha’s chair.

“She’s eaten hardly a mouthful.”

Massen stood. “If you remember anything, tell Lady Catrane.”

I waited until he had gone out before saying I was sorry to have interrupted their meal. Eitha picked up her spoon. She did not look at me.

“You frightened her.”

“I asked her what she’d seen.”

“You asked as though she were keeping it from you.”

“Is she?”

I let go of him.

He looked back towards the kitchen. “Someone put something in a guest’s drink. If she saw a hand, I need to know whose.”

“You don’t know there was poison.”

“Neither do you. Yet you’re keeping cups.”

A man carrying firewood squeezed between us, his eyes carefully lowered. We stepped apart. In the space he left, Massen said more quietly, “I’m trying to help you.”

“I didn’t ask you to do it that way.”

He rubbed the back of his neck. “No. You didn’t.”

I had been ready for another objection. Without it I could hear how loud my breathing was.

“Come upstairs,” I said.

We went to my room. I left the door unlatched, because the household was still awake and because I would not have my father’s first concern in the morning be what his daughter had done while a guest lay dead. Massen stood by the chest until I asked him to sit.

“My hair,” I said. “I can’t find the pins.”

He came behind me. I sat on the low stool, and he worked carefully with both hands, stopping whenever I flinched. He found four pins before the weight began to loosen. One caught in the braid. I took his wrist and guided it backwards, then let him try again.

“There,” he said.

He laid each pin on the chest with its point facing the same way. I watched his hands in the little mirror. His sleeve still bore the dark patch. I turned and touched it.

“You should change that.”

“I will.”

He found the last pin and my hair fell heavily over his fingers. He did not immediately take them away. His hand rested at the back of my head, warm against the tender roots, and I leaned into it.

“I wanted tomorrow,” I said.

“I know.”

I put my face against his arm. He bent until his mouth touched my hair. We stayed like that for some time, awkwardly, his back bowed and my neck turned, until he made a small sound of pain and I laughed into his sleeve.

Outside, a horse struck the yard stones. I heard the gate called for and an answer from below. I thought Gedran had finished with the horses. Massen’s hand moved once over my hair and then was still.

Chapter 12 — The room kept warm

I slept for less than an hour.

When I woke, I could smell apples. There were no apples in my room. The last ones had been carried to the bridal chamber because it was cold enough to keep them and no one had begun sleeping there. I lay still, trying to place the smell among the others: candle smoke, the wool cover pulled up beneath my chin, Massen’s damp sleeve. He had left before I slept. The chair stood empty beside the chest.

Someone coughed in the passage.

It was a small, irritated cough, followed by the click of a tongue against the teeth. My mother had made that sound when a thread caught between hers. It was so exact that I sat up before remembering she was dead.

The candle had gone out. A grey line showed under the door, though there was no window in the passage and it was not yet morning.

“Nerla?” I called.

No answer.

I put my feet into my shoes and took the cloak from the bed. The floor was cold enough to hurt through the soles. At the door I paused, with my hand on the latch, and listened.

A woman moved outside. I heard cloth drawn over wood and the creak of a basket handle. I could have told you which basket: the tall one with the split handle bound in red wool, which my mother used for bringing sheets from the loft. It had broken years ago and been burned in the kitchen fire. Nerla had saved the binding because it was good wool.

I lifted the latch.

The passage was dark. At its far end, a lamp shone on the stair, making the damp in the wall glisten. Lira stood barefoot beside the bridal door.

“What are you doing?” I whispered.

She turned too quickly and put a hand to the wall.

“I couldn’t sleep.”

“Where are your shoes?”

She looked down as though I had told her they were missing. Water shone on the tops of her feet. The hem of her dress was dark to the knee.

“Lira.”

“I know.”

“Have you been outside?”

She did not answer. I took the lamp from the stair. It had been set on a step below her, where one careless movement would have knocked it over. In its light her feet looked bloodless, the small toe on the left bent under as it always was when she stood on cold stone.

“Come into my room.”

“No.”

“Then yours.”

She glanced at the bridal door.

I set my hand on its latch. Lira caught my wrist so hard that her nails hurt.

“Don’t.”

We looked at each other. From behind the door came a faint dragging sound. I thought of a basket across boards, then of a trunk, then of all the things stored in that room which could not move themselves.

“Who’s in there?” I asked.

Lira shook her head.

I called through the door. No one answered. The dragging stopped.

We stood a long time without speaking. The lamp wick hissed. Somewhere below, a man snored and was nudged into silence. Those ordinary sounds made me feel foolish enough to be angry.

“You’ve frightened yourself,” I said.

“Yes.”

She said it with such relief that I could not go on. I took her into my room and sat her on the bed. Her feet left wet marks across the boards. There were no bits of rush or mud clinging to them, nothing from the yard. I looked at the marks while I wrapped her feet in the spare blanket.

“Tell me where you went.”

“I was at the door.”

“The bridal door?”

She nodded.

“Before that?”

“I thought someone had called.”

“Who?”

She pulled the blanket towards her knees.

“Was it my mother?”

Her face changed. “Why?”

“I heard something.”

“What?”

“A cough.”

We listened, each waiting for the other to say more. I remembered how children listen together when one has announced a sound; how soon both hear it, and how impossible it becomes to be the first to stop believing.

“There’s a leak somewhere,” I said. “Your feet are wet.”

Lira began to cry. It was sudden and angry, and she kept wiping at her eyes before the tears could reach her cheeks.

“I know my feet are wet.”

I sat beside her. She shifted away, only a little. I put my hands in my lap and let her cry. After a while she asked for a dry dress.

The one I found had belonged to me before my first marriage. It was too broad through the chest and too short at the ankle. Lira put it on without complaint, shivering so hard that she could not manage the neck tie. I tied it for her. Her skin smelled faintly of water that had stood among stones.

“You can sleep here,” I said.

She shook her head.

“I won’t ask anything else.”

“I want my own room.”

I took her there. She made me wait while she checked the window fastening, then asked whether I would leave the lamp. I did, though it meant finding my way back along the wall. Before her door shut, she said my name.

“What?”

“Don’t go in.”

“The bridal room?”

She nodded.

I said I wouldn’t. I meant until morning. I did not tell her that.

Below, the kitchen fire still burned. Nerla sat on a stool beside it, peeling something small and yellow with her knife. A bowl stood at her feet. There were two more on the hearth: one containing scraps, the other empty. She looked up when I came in.

“Can’t you rest?”

“Lira’s upset.”

“We all are.”

“Did you go upstairs?”

“No.”

“Anyone else?”

“People have been going up and down all night.”

She turned the small fruit in her hand. It was a crab apple, the skin wrinkled, the flesh beneath still pale. She cut away a brown place and put the good piece into her mouth.

I sat on the bench. The heat made my feet ache. Near the door, Eitha slept under a cloak with her face towards the wall. Her bowl had been washed and set upside down beside her shoes.

Nerla reached for the empty bowl on the hearth and set it on the stool opposite hers.

“For whom?” I asked.

She looked at it.

“For—”

She stopped. Her hand stayed on the rim.

I waited. The fire settled with a little rush of ash.

“Nerla?”

“Someone used to sit there.”

“Who?”

“Bressa.”

The name meant nothing to me. She said it again, more quietly, and took the bowl back.

“A girl?”

“A woman.”

“Did she work here?”

“For a time.”

“Where did she go?”

Nerla set the knife on the hearth. “Brin, I’m tired.”

I wanted to tell her about the cough. Looking at her, I found I could not bear the possibility that she had heard it too. I asked for a piece of apple instead. She cut one and handed it to me on the flat of the blade.

It was very sour. My mouth watered painfully. Nerla watched me trying not to show it, and for the first time since the death she smiled.

“You always ask for what you don’t like.”

“I like apples.”

“You like the apples from the south orchard, after a frost, when somebody else has picked them.”

She cut me another piece. I ate it. For a while we sat with nothing to do except watch the fire and listen to the sleeper’s breath by the door.

When Nerla’s head began to nod, I took the knife from her hand. She woke enough to protest, then let me put it away. I made her lie on the bench and covered her with the old brown blanket we kept for the dogs. She complained about the smell. I told her it was that or my cloak.

“Keep your cloak,” she murmured.

I sat where she had sat. The stool opposite me was empty. On the hearth, the bowl she had taken back had a little water in it. I could not remember her putting any there.

By the time the first grey light reached the kitchen window, it was frozen along the edge.

Chapter 13 — The silver key

The morning of my wedding arrived with a man chopping wood badly beneath the window.

He struck, stopped, struck again. Between blows I could hear him moving the log on the block. Somebody called advice from the yard. The next blow missed, and the advice became laughter.

I had fallen asleep with my head against the kitchen wall. Nerla was no longer on the bench. My neck hurt, and a small strip of daylight lay across my knees, showing the grease on my skirt. I put my hands over it before remembering that there was nobody to impress.

Eitha brought me a cup of hot water with a little honey in it. She put it down within reach, neither friendly nor hostile, and returned to the dough she was helping knead. I drank until I could feel my fingers again.

The bowl on the hearth was gone. I wanted to examine it in daylight. I also wanted never to see it again.

In the hall, the older children were eating under the tables. The adults had occupied every bench and left them the floor. A little girl reached up through the gap between two boards to steal from her father’s plate. He slapped at her fingers without looking, then moved a piece of bread closer to the gap.

My father stood at the high table in the coat he had meant to wear for my marriage. The silver fastening shone against the dark wool. Someone had brushed him; a pale hair clung to his shoulder where the brush had left it. I went to take it off.

He caught my hand and held it for a moment.

“We’ll have you married yet.”

“Not today.”

“No.”

He let go. Across the table, Bran was turning a cup between his hands. He looked as though he had slept even less than I had, but his collar was fresh. There was a small cut below his ear. I thought he must have shaved in poor light.

“Did you get any rest?” he asked.

“A little.”

“Lira?”

“I haven’t seen her this morning.”

He nodded, looking into his cup. “She takes things hard.”

I thought of her feet and said nothing.

Catrane came down with Hethen and the wooden bowl. The chest had been brought to the cleared end of the table. In daylight it looked cheap: good hinges fixed to old boards, a leather strap replaced by rope, one corner rubbed almost through. My father had expected something more imposing. He tapped the lid with his forefinger.

“All this trouble.”

No one answered him.

Lira came in from the west passage. She had plaited her hair wet, tightly enough to show the shape of her skull. The borrowed dress was hidden under a shawl. She sat beside Catrane without waiting to be invited.

Massen stood at my shoulder. His clothes were clean. I was glad he had changed the stained sleeve and immediately wished I had not noticed which sleeve was clean. Gedran was absent.

Catrane placed the key beside the chest.

“His property,” she said. “We open it to learn whom to notify and what he came to do. We put back what does not concern us.”

My father frowned at her. “It’s in my house.”

“Yes.”

“I’m not a thief.”

“I haven’t suggested it.”

She waited, and after a moment he gestured for her to proceed.

The key turned with difficulty. Hethen held the box steady while Catrane worked it backwards and forwards. When the lock yielded, it made a surprisingly loud snap. I had expected the lid to spring up. It stayed where it was until she lifted it.

On top lay a folded shirt, a bundle of papers wrapped in skin, and a little packet of dried fruit. Beneath the shirt was a woman’s letter. Catrane unfolded it, read the first lines and refolded it.

“His daughter. A place to send word.”

“Read it,” my father said.

“It’s about his foot.”

“Then read the address.”

She did. Mera Vale, at the fuller’s yard below Dun Cail. I repeated it silently. I had never been there. I pictured a yard full of hanging cloth, and a woman with her father’s forehead looking towards a gate because a traveller had stopped at it.

The letter went back beneath the shirt. A pair of spare laces lay coiled in one corner, tied together at the ends. Hethen took them out to reach the next parcel. The care with which he lifted them made me want him to hurry.

The heavy thing at the bottom was wrapped in dark cloth.

Bran put down his cup.

I looked at him because the small sound carried in the quiet. He had turned towards the doorway. Nerla was there, carrying a tray. She stopped when she saw the open chest.

“Later,” my father told her.

She set the tray on another table and left.

Catrane drew back the cloth. A curved piece of stone lay inside. One edge had been worked smooth by feet, the other broken across. Along the flatter face ran shallow cuts, some filled with dirt. There was no jewel, no hidden compartment, nothing I could understand as worth carrying on a bad foot over winter roads.

Lira gripped the edge of the table.

“You know this,” Catrane said.

“Another piece.”

“Where?”

Bran leaned forward. “In the old work. There were stones kept after the lower wall was taken down.”

Catrane continued looking at Lira. “Is that what you mean?”

“Yes.”

My father gave a short, displeased sigh. “We needn’t pull the whole house apart over a threshold.”

“A threshold?” I said.

He looked at me, then at Bran.

“That’s what it looks like.”

Catrane opened the wrapped papers. Most were folded small. A few bore seals, flattened and cracked where they had been packed against one another. She set them down separately, weighting their corners with the clean cups from her tray. There was no room for my hands; I folded them against my stomach.

The first was a letter employing Odran to examine an old welcome and the ground to which it belonged. Three names appeared at the bottom. Two were marked by witnesses rather than written in the same hand. One of the names was Muirne Fell, at the lower ford.

I knew the Fells. Or I knew where they had used to keep goats, which at that moment seemed enough to say I knew them. My father had called their grandmother a woman who could take the wool off a sheep by looking at it. I had laughed when he said it.

“What have they to do with us?” I asked.

My father reached for the letter.

Catrane allowed him to take it. He read the first part, then handed it to Bran without finishing aloud.

“They’ve been at this for years.”

“At what?”

“Claiming what their fathers lost.”

Massen leaned down to see the next paper. “Passage?”

“Among other things.”

Catrane read aloud. The words were old enough that Lira helped her twice, giving the newer form without lifting her eyes. A house had received certain travellers by its fire. Those received were to have food when there was food to share, shelter through the night and a way out at morning. Names followed. The list filled half the sheet.

“That’s a copy,” Bran said.

“Yes,” Catrane said.

“I had it examined. The hand isn’t the old hand.”

“No. Odran says he made it from an impression kept at Dun Cail.”

“Which may itself be a copy.”

Lira looked up. “The wording is the same as the one we had.”

My uncle turned to her, and she lowered her eyes again.

He did not rebuke her. He said, “There are questions about that one too.”

My father took back the letter from him and laid it among the others.

“Old business,” he said. “The place was closed before my grandfather was born.”

“Why?” I asked.

“A feud.”

“Whose?”

“Brin, there’s scarcely a stone on this hill that hasn’t been in somebody’s feud.”

Catrane opened another sheet. This one was clean and recent, folded only twice. Attached to it was a narrow strip bearing a seal. I recognized the shape of the stamp, though from where I stood I could not see the cut in it.

It named a sum for passage and a second sum for shelter. Below those, in a different hand, someone had written that the shelter had been refused after payment.

My father looked at Bran.

“That can’t be ours.”

Bran held out his hand. “Let me see.”

Catrane gave it to him. He read slowly. I watched his face, willing him to find the mistake.

“The witness isn’t named.”

“Here,” Lira said, pointing.

“That is the complainant.”

“There’s a mark below.”

“A mark anyone could make.”

Massen straightened. “Which road?”

“The western road,” Catrane said.

“Our winter road?”

My father spread his hands. “A demand isn’t a collection. There are people who present a demand as though they’d paid it when they haven’t given a copper.”

“Then the witness can tell us,” I said.

There was a pause. I had meant that the witness would clear the mistake. From the way my father looked at me, I realized he had not heard it so.

“We won’t bring every roadside grievance into your marriage.”

“The marriage is tomorrow or next week or whenever we can bear it,” I said. “He’s dead now.”

Massen’s hand touched my back. I moved away from it.

Bran laid the paper down.

“Of course we’ll ask,” he said. “If Muirne’s still where she was, someone can go to her. But we should find out what killed this man before we decide his papers explain it.”

His voice was so reasonable that I felt relieved. Catrane gathered the loose sheets and put them together without agreeing.

“What did you mean to discuss with him last night?” she asked Bran.

“He wanted them read before the wedding.”

“And you?”

“I wanted my niece to have her wedding.”

I looked at my hands. There was dried honey beside one nail. I picked it away with my thumb.

The chest contained no purse worth stealing. There were two small coins in the bag we had found, a needle case and a piece of soap worn thin enough to bend. Hethen put them back. Catrane returned the personal letter to its place, then closed the lid on everything except the stone and the papers concerning the house.

“Why leave that out?” my father asked.

“To show Druven.”

“For what?”

“To ask where the other piece belongs.”

My father looked as though he might refuse. At last he called for the mason, too loudly, making a child beneath the table stop chewing.

I went to fetch her. On the way I passed the west room. Its door stood open, and someone had put two candles by Odran’s head. The cover lay smooth over his chest. I could see the shape of the dressing at his foot.

I had promised myself I would tell his daughter quickly. I still had no idea what I could say.

Chapter 14 — The dress in daylight

Druven would not give us a history from a stone.

She turned it once on the cloth, squinted at the worn edge and asked where it had been found. Catrane told her only what the papers said. Druven asked whether anyone living had seen it lifted. None of us could answer.

“Then I can tell you it’s been under feet,” she said. “And that whoever took it up didn’t trouble to keep it whole.”

“Does it fit the lower work?” my father asked.

“I’d need to see the lower work.”

“You’ve been seeing it for three days.”

“I’ve been repairing a flue.”

Bellan stood beside her with a bag over his shoulder. He looked from his mother to my father, waiting to discover whether he should put it down.

“The cupboard,” Bran said. “I showed you.”

“You showed me shelves.”

“There is masonry behind them.”

“There generally is.”

My father made an impatient movement, but Massen laughed, once, before catching himself. Druven’s eyes went to him and away. She wrapped the stone again.

“If you want the footing opened, move the things in front of it. All of them. Don’t have me crawling behind your mother’s chest with a candle while somebody says mind the polish.”

“I’ll do it,” I said.

My father began to object, then stopped. He had caught sight of the torn hem of my old dress.

“Have someone do it,” he said.

I went upstairs instead.

I could not have explained why I needed to see the bridal room in daylight. I had told Lira I would stay out until morning; morning was here, and the hall was full of voices that made everything behind the door seem absurd. At the top of the stair the plaster smelled damp and ordinary. Sunlight lay across the landing. Someone had opened the shutter at the far end.

I lifted the latch and went in.

The apples were where we had left them, in shallow baskets against the north wall. The bed stood made, my mother’s cover missing from it, its place taken by a rough grey blanket. My gown hung from the wardrobe door. Lira had stuffed the sleeves with soft cloth to keep their shape. For a moment it looked occupied.

I crossed the room and touched the lace at its throat. A pin pricked me. I sucked my finger and took the pin out.

There was no water on the floor. No basket had been dragged across it. The chest did not rock when I pressed it; the fragment of painted plaster still wedged its foot. I knelt and drew it out. The chest settled with a small creak.

The hand on the plaster lay palm upwards. In daylight I could see a curve beneath it, perhaps the rim of a bowl. I had thought the nails dark when Bellan brought it down; now I saw that the dark lines belonged to whatever the hand held, or to damage in the paint. I turned it over. Its back was rough with old mortar. There was nothing else to read.

I set it on the windowsill, beside the three stone chips.

Behind me, Lira said, “I asked you.”

She stood in the doorway, holding a pair of stockings.

“It’s morning.”

“I asked you not to go in.”

“Come and look. There’s nothing.”

She did not come. I heard the invitation I had made and remembered my own hand on the latch in the dark.

“I wanted the gown,” I said, though until then I had not.

“Why?”

“To put it away.”

Her face altered. “You needn’t do that yet.”

“It’ll collect dust.”

“I put a cloth over it.”

“The cloth fell.”

She came forward then, saw the cloth on the floor and bent to pick it up. I held the gown while she shook it out. Together we laid it on the bed. The empty sleeves spread to either side.

“Don’t fold there,” Lira said.

I moved my hand.

She fetched the sheet we had meant to spread over the wedding chest. It smelled of the rosemary packed with the linen. We laid the gown on it and rolled rather than folded, supporting the lace between layers. Neither of us said anything for a while.

“Did you put a stone in here?” I asked.

Her hands stopped.

“The other piece. The one from the old work.”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

She pointed to the narrow space beneath the window, behind the chest I had moved. A board there had been lifted and replaced. Its headless nails showed as dark holes in the grain.

“Why?”

“Bran said it belonged near the old inner door.”

“This isn’t a door.”

“It used to be below this part. He showed me the plan.”

“What was it meant to do?”

“Keep the room safe.”

“From what?”

She lifted the end of the sheet and tucked it round the skirt. “From things that come into a house when people change places.”

“Who told you that?”

“He did.”

I stood holding the middle of the roll. The gown was heavier than it looked. I wanted to put it down, but there was nowhere that would not crease what we had just protected.

“Lira, why would my marrying make the room unsafe?”

“I don’t know. He said it was done before. He said there were words for it.”

“The welcome?”

“Part of it.”

“Did you say them?”

“Yes.”

“All of them?”

“I didn’t know all of them.”

She began to cry again, silently this time. I saw one tear strike the sheet and spread into a dark spot.

“I wanted it to be right,” she said. “You wanted the room. You wanted him. Everything I tried to do, you had a better way to do it. Bran said this was something I could manage.”

I was still holding the gown. I set it down badly and reached for her, but she turned to the window and wiped her face with both hands.

“I didn’t mean you couldn’t manage.”

“Don’t.”

I stopped.

Outside, somebody shouted for a rope. A horse answered with a short, irritated squeal. The sound travelled up from the yard and died against the heavy bed curtains.

“Will you tell Catrane?” I asked.

Lira kept looking out. “You’ll tell her anyway.”

“I’ll go with you.”

She shook her head. I waited, thinking she meant she would not go at all. Then she said, “I’ll tell her myself.”

She left the stockings on the bed and went out.

I finished wrapping the gown. It took longer alone. Twice I rolled it too tightly and had to begin again. When it was done I could not lift it and keep the sheet from loosening, so I left it where it lay.

On the sill, the painted hand seemed to offer the three dark chips. I moved them apart.

Downstairs, Massen and Bellan were moving the bench from the kitchen passage. Massen had taken the heavy end and was discovering that the lighter end still determined where he could go. Bellan backed through the doorway, steering with great confidence until his bag caught on the latch.

“Stop,” he said.

Massen stopped.

“Not down. Just stop.”

“I have.”

“You’re letting it tilt.”

“I’m thirty-four, lad. I tilt when I stand still.”

Bellan freed his bag and they got the bench through. Druven watched from beside the fire, arms folded, her expression concealing neither amusement nor impatience.

“There,” she said. “Now the chest.”

Massen looked at the large chest beneath the shelves. “Does anything in this house ever leave empty?”

“People,” Nerla said from the kitchen.

He glanced towards her, then took hold of the chest.

I knelt to remove the folded blankets from it. Under them were sacks of salt and a bundle of old harness that had begun to mildew. We carried these out before Massen and Bellan dragged the chest aside. The floor beneath was black with dust except for two pale lines where it had recently been moved.

Bran had kept his promise to move it. He had also put it back exactly where it prevented anyone seeing the wall.

Druven brought the lamp close. Behind the shelves lay a shallow recess, its back made of small stones unlike those on either side. The mortar crumbled when she touched it with a tool.

“Was this what you saw?” I asked.

“No.”

She pointed to a narrow cupboard to the left. Its door stood open. Inside were cups and a cracked serving dish.

I looked at the little cupboard, then at the wall.

“Perhaps Bran misunderstood.”

Druven removed a loose fragment of mortar and set it carefully on the floor. “Perhaps.”

She sent Bellan for another tool. Massen stayed to help lift down the shelves. I went to find my uncle.

He was in the yard, tightening a strap on a packhorse. One of Catrane’s men stood beside him, ready to leave. Bran looked up and smiled when he saw me.

“I’ve found a man who knows the road to Dun Cail,” he said. “We’ll get word to the daughter.”

I had come to ask why he had shown Druven the wrong cupboard. Instead I asked how soon the messenger could leave.

“As soon as you have a letter.”

I looked at the horse, at the clear road beyond the gate. Snow lay only in the hollows. There was no reason to delay except that I did not want to sit down and write.

“I’ll do it now.”

He touched my shoulder.

“Don’t say more than you know.”

I nodded. He returned to the strap. I went inside without asking about the wall.

Chapter 15 — The words sent away

My first letter began by explaining that Odran had been welcomed.

I wrote that he had arrived in good time for the evening meal, that he had been given a place by the fire, that we had attended to his comfort. I described the illness that had taken him, then stopped with the pen over the word illness. The ink gathered at the point and fell, making a dark bead beside it.

I tried again on another sheet.

Your father died last night.

I looked at that for a long time.

From below came the sounds of the hall being put in order for the wake. The trestles scraped. Someone shook a cloth hard enough that I heard it through the floor. In the yard, the messenger’s horse struck a hoof against the paving. He had been waiting nearly an hour.

I added that her father had not been alone. It was true, though I disliked how comforting it sounded. I wrote that his death was sudden, that the cause was being examined, and that we would keep his body and belongings safe until we heard her wishes. Then I put down the pen.

The letter looked disgracefully short. There was so much room on the page that she might think I had cared too little to fill it. I nearly began again. Instead I folded it before I could.

Catrane read it at my request. She did not offer to improve it.

“I’ll send my own account with it,” she said. “She should know who can answer besides the household.”

That stung. I was glad she had said it.

I asked whether the messenger could take both letters. She looked at me with mild surprise.

“He’s my man, Brin.”

“Bran found him.”

“He told him which road was passable.”

I had allowed my uncle to seem to have arranged the whole thing. It was so ordinary a mistake that I might never have noticed it on another morning.

Catrane wrote quickly, blotted her sheet and sealed it. While the wax cooled, I asked whether Lira had come to her.

“Yes.”

“About the stone?”

“Yes.”

“What did she say?”

“What did she tell you?”

I sat down on the edge of the chest. My aunt’s brush had been moved again, its pale hair catching the light beside my knee.

“I don’t want her frightened over something she’s misunderstood.”

“Then let us find out what she did.”

“You think it matters?”

“Two people have brought pieces of the same old work into the house, and one has died. I don’t know whether the pieces matter to his death. I’d rather keep them than throw them away.”

I told her about the wet feet. I did not tell her about my mother’s cough. It seemed too private to lay beside the cup and the jugs, as though Hethen might scrape a little of it onto a shaving and ask whether I had mistaken something ordinary for it.

Catrane listened without interrupting. When I finished, she asked whether Lira was warm now.

“I think so.”

“Make certain.”

That annoyed me more than disbelief would have. I wanted her to tell me whether I ought to be afraid. She sent me to find a dry shawl.

By the time I returned downstairs, the wake had begun to resemble a wake. Odran lay at the far end of the hall, clear of the traffic to the kitchen. A candle burned at each corner of the boards. The flowers from the west room had been tied into a loose branch above him. Their scent was stronger as they faded.

People approached in twos and threes, uncertain what was expected of them. Most had not known him. A man who had argued with him about the western road touched the boards and stood with his head bowed. One of the kitchen boys put a small piece of bread beside the candles. Nerla moved it away from the flame and left it there.

My father greeted each person as though the gathering were still his to conduct. He had removed the silver fastening from his coat. Without it the wool gaped at his throat and made him look older.

“Have you eaten?” he asked me.

“Yes.”

It was not true unless honey water counted. He nodded and turned to tell someone else to eat.

Massen found me near the doorway. There was dust on his sleeve from the recess. He had a splinter in the pad of his thumb and was trying to catch it with his nail.

“Let me.”

I took his hand and brought it to the light. The splinter was dark under the skin. I asked Eitha, passing with a tray, for a pin. She set down the tray, took one from her sleeve and gave it to me.

“Hold still,” I told him.

“I was holding still.”

“You were pulling.”

“I hadn’t begun yet.”

Eitha made a small sound, almost a laugh. Massen looked at her, smiling, and she looked away at once. My grip tightened on his thumb. He glanced back at me.

“That’s skin,” he said.

“Then stop moving it.”

I worked the point beneath the splinter and lifted. It came out longer than I expected. A drop of blood followed it. Massen put his thumb in his mouth while I wiped the pin.

“Thank you,” I told Eitha.

She took it. I could have let her go. Instead I asked whether she would walk with me to the linen room when she had finished carrying the tray.

“For what?”

“To talk.”

Her face closed.

“Not about the cup,” I said, though part of me had already begun arranging a way to ask.

She looked at Nerla. The older woman stood beside the hearth, watching us without appearing to. Eitha said she would come when the tray was empty.

I waited in the linen room. It was too small for a conversation in which anyone might wish to retreat. I saw that only after I had closed the door, and opened it again. There was a stool and no second seat. I left the stool for her and leaned against a chest.

When Eitha came, she remained standing.

“I was unfair to you at supper, before Odran came,” I said.

She looked at me without answering.

“At the table. You hadn’t done anything wrong.”

“No.”

“I shouldn’t have sent you away.”

“No.”

I made myself stay against the chest.

“I was tired.”

She shifted her weight. Her hands were clasped in front of her, the fingers reddened from washing.

“So was I,” she said.

“Yes.”

I waited for some softening. She gave me none.

“I thought you were looking at Massen.”

“I was.”

The answer surprised me enough that I laughed, a short, ugly little laugh I could not take back.

“He asked for water,” she said. “I was waiting to see whether he wanted more.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t.”

“No.”

Outside, somebody called for another cloth. Eitha turned her head towards the sound.

“You can go,” I said, and saw from her face that I had made it worse again.

She left. I stayed until I heard her steps return towards the kitchen, then went out by the other door.

In the passage I met the musician with the split lip. He had the blue bowl in his hand and was looking for someone to take it. I had not learned his name, though he had played at two of our gatherings before. I asked it now.

“Rusk,” he said.

“Were you here last First Snow?”

“And the one before.”

“I thought so.”

He nodded politely. A bruise yellowed the skin below his left eye. I wondered whether it belonged to the same accident as the split lip, and did not ask.

“Eitha says you may want to speak to me,” he said.

“She does?”

“About where I was sitting.”

I looked towards the kitchen.

“Will you come to Lady Catrane?”

“Now?”

“If you can.”

He held up the bowl. “Someone’ll want this.”

“I’ll take it.”

He surrendered it reluctantly, as though it might be charged to him later. We went upstairs. The bowl was still warm from his hands.

Catrane let him choose whether to sit. He remained standing near the window. Hethen was there, examining the inside of one of the brown jugs in the daylight. He set it down while Rusk spoke.

The musician had sat on the bench beside the hatch until the old singer rose. Then a man leaning to hear the song had pushed him against the wall, so he moved towards the kitchen passage. He had seen people at the wine board, but not their hands. The standing singer and the doorpost obstructed his view in different directions.

“Who was there?” Catrane asked.

“Nerla. A boy. The laird’s brother.”

“Together?”

“No. At different times.”

“When you moved, where was Eitha?”

“Other end. Carrying water.”

“Could she see the board?”

He considered. “From the aisle, yes. If nobody crossed in front.”

I pictured the aisle, and the line from it to the warmer. From my place I had seen my uncle’s arm, his hand resting on the hatch before the singer blocked him. Eitha might have seen more. I had sent her to the far end and then questioned her as though she had chosen not to pay attention.

Catrane thanked Rusk. He did not leave.

“Will it be said I accused him?” he asked.

“You’ve told us where you stood.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

She looked at him for a moment. “I can’t promise how people repeat it. I can tell them what you actually said.”

He nodded once. When he went out, I followed him to the stair and returned the bowl.

“I meant to put that in the kitchen,” I said.

“I know where it goes.”

He took it from me. I watched him descend, his free hand lightly touching the wall.

Chapter 16 — Below the shelves

The recess had a smell I knew from the old well: cold stone and damp iron, with something green underneath. Druven had taken down the shelves and removed enough of the small blocking stones to put an arm through. She would not let Bellan enlarge the hole until she had seen what lay above it.

“The room’s full of people,” my father said. “It has been full of people all my life.”

“Yes,” she said. “And I’d like it to remain above them.”

He stood with his hands behind his back, watching her work. I had seen him stand exactly so beside a ewe labouring, until Nerla sent him away before his anxiety made someone pull too soon.

Bran was absent. I asked where he had gone.

“Down to the gate,” my father said. “Someone has to look beyond this hole.”

Druven leaned into the recess with a lamp. Her shoulders almost filled the opening. The flame flattened towards the dark, though the kitchen behind us was warm and still.

“There’s a draught,” Bellan said.

“I feel it.”

“From where?”

“That’s why I’m looking.”

He flushed and held the tools closer. She reached back without turning, and he placed one into her hand.

After a while she withdrew. There was black dust along one side of her face.

“A turn behind it,” she said. “Steps down. I can’t see beyond.”

“Cellar,” my father said.

“No.”

“You can’t see beyond.”

“I can see the steps. They’re worn towards an opening, not down to a floor for storing things.”

“We had stores under there.”

“You may have had stores anywhere you could fit a barrel. It doesn’t tell me what it was built for.”

He opened his mouth, then shut it. Massen, beside me, bent to look through the hole. Druven put a hand against his shoulder before he could lean his weight on the edge.

“Not there.”

He stepped back.

She asked me for the painted plaster. I fetched it from the bridal room, entering quickly and leaving more quickly, with my eyes fixed on the sill. Nothing moved. The gown lay rolled on the bed as I had left it. I was ashamed of my haste and did not slow down.

Druven examined the fragment without touching the paint.

“From this side of the old wall,” she said. “Before the later chimney.”

“What does it show?” my father asked.

“A hand.”

“I can see that.”

“Then we agree.”

Bellan bent over his tools to hide a smile.

My father walked away. I almost went after him; the impulse was familiar, a wish to smooth the expression on his face before it hardened into somebody else’s trouble. Instead I stayed and held the lamp.

Druven sent Bellan upstairs to watch the fresh plaster while she loosened another stone. Massen went with him. She worked slowly, tapping and pausing to listen. Each blow passed through the wall to my hand. The sound had depth beyond the thickness I could see.

“What was the lower house?” I asked.

“A place to stop on the road.”

“You knew it was there?”

“I knew there had been one.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

She looked up. “When?”

I had no answer that did not make her responsible for finding a moment in which I would listen.

“My grandmother called this the high house,” Druven said. “Even when there was nothing below but a blocked way and nettles. I asked her once why she called it that. She told me to fetch her shawl.”

“Did you ask again?”

“I was nine. I wanted to go outside.”

She returned to the stone. I held the lamp higher.

A little fall of mortar struck the floor within. Druven stopped. We listened. Behind the opening, something scraped slowly over stone.

“Rat?” I whispered.

She did not answer. She took the lamp from me and brought it close to the hole.

Beyond her hand, in the narrowing dark, I saw the edge of a stool.

It stood too far away to fit where the wall ought to have ended. One leg was shorter than the others and rested on a wedge of folded leather. A woman’s skirt brushed against it. I saw no feet.

Druven drew the lamp back. The stool was gone. Behind the hole lay a shallow flight of worn steps, just as she had said, ending at a turn we could not see beyond.

Neither of us moved.

From above, Bellan called that the plaster had not changed.

Druven answered him. Her voice sounded almost normal.

“Keep watching.”

I wanted her to name what we had seen. She set the lamp on the floor and took the blocking stone in both hands. Together we put it back into the gap. We did not seal it. She wedged it so it could not fall forwards and told me to step away.

“Did you see—”

“Yes.”

“What was it?”

“I saw what you saw.”

Bellan and Massen returned. Druven asked her son to fetch rope and a board long enough to span the recess. He looked at her face, then at mine, and obeyed without a question. Massen waited until he had gone.

“What happened?”

I told him. I was careful to describe only the stool and the skirt. I held my hands apart to show how far away they had seemed, then let them fall.

Massen went towards the stone.

Druven caught his sleeve. “No.”

“I only want to see.”

“You may stand here and want it.”

He looked at me. I could tell he was making an effort not to smile reassuringly. I disliked him for needing to make it, then saw the worry in his eyes and could not decide whether it was for what I had seen or for me.

“Lira was wet last night,” I said. “Outside the bridal room. There’s no water there.”

He went still.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“I didn’t know how.”

He glanced at the recess again. This time he stayed where he was.

Bellan brought the board. We fixed it across the opening, with the rope passed round the heavy hooks that had held the shelves. Druven tested it by leaning her weight against it. Then she sent Bellan to ask Catrane to come down.

“Don’t let anyone move it,” she told me.

“I won’t.”

“Don’t go in after anyone either.”

I looked at her. There was dust in the lines round her mouth. She wiped it away and left a darker streak.

When Catrane arrived, she listened to the account with one hand on the board. She asked whether the opening could be reached from outside. Druven said there should be a lower end somewhere by the burn, but she would need daylight and another person to find it. Catrane sent one of her men with her. Bellan insisted on going too.

They left through the yard. I watched from the kitchen door as they went down past the washhouse, their heads bending beneath the first thin flakes of snow. Druven walked more slowly than her son. When he got too far ahead, she called him back.

At the far end of the kitchen Nerla was breaking bread into a bowl for a child whose teeth had not come through. Her hands stopped. She looked towards the blocked recess, then resumed her work.

I went to her.

“Who was Bressa?”

Her spoon scraped the bowl.

“Not now.”

“Did she have anything to do with the lower house?”

Nerla looked up sharply. The child reached for the spoon and spilled a little milk on the table.

“Hold that,” she said, giving me the bowl.

She wiped the milk, cleaned the child’s hand and lifted him onto her lap. Only then did she look at me again.

“Bressa worked here before you were born. She had a sister’s boy with her. Your grandfather wouldn’t keep him after a man claimed he’d stolen a clasp.”

“Had he?”

“I never saw the clasp.”

“What happened to him?”

“She took him away.”

“Both of them?”

“Yes.”

“Why would you set a bowl for her?”

Nerla held out her hand for it. I gave it back.

“Because I’m old, and I spent half the night looking at somebody who won’t eat again.”

She fed the child. He turned his head, refusing, then changed his mind and followed the spoon with his mouth open. I waited until he had swallowed.

“Did you hear her?”

Nerla did not look at me.

After a while she said, “Don’t ask me questions you want answered no.”

I sat beside her. The child reached for my finger, closed his wet hand round it and pulled it towards his mouth. I let him. Through the open yard door I could see the snow settle briefly on the dark stone, then disappear.

Chapter 17 — The people outside

Druven returned with mud to her knees and a piece of thorn caught in her sleeve. Bellan carried a broken branch, which he had used to test the ground along the burn. Catrane’s man came behind them, holding his left hand beneath his coat.

“What’s happened?” I asked.

“Slipped,” he said.

His name was Iver. I had heard Catrane use it and remembered because he answered before she had finished speaking. He let Hethen look at the hand. Two fingers had begun to swell.

“The lower end is there,” Druven told us. “Under the west wall. Filled, then faced over. The burn has taken soil from beneath one corner.”

“Can it be opened?” Catrane asked.

“Yes.”

My father had joined us. “Then do it.”

Druven looked at him.

“Not until I know what rests on it.”

“It’s a door.”

“It was a door. Your chimney stands partly on what filled it.”

He went silent. The bridal room lay above that chimney. Above the room were two small chambers and the old tower roof. I pictured them as we had always used them, one room stacked over another, and could not make the weight fit into the little gap I had helped cover.

“There may be another way to reach the old floor,” Druven said. “I’ll look. But nobody takes a bar to that facing.”

She spoke to all of us. Her eyes remained on my father until he nodded.

Hethen wrapped Iver’s fingers together. The man tried once to flex them and was told not to. He took the instruction without complaint, but when Catrane asked whether he could ride, he answered through his teeth.

“I can manage a rein.”

“You’ll stay here.”

“My lady—”

“You can help me more with two useful hands tomorrow.”

He bowed his head. Beside him, Bellan examined his own knuckles, scraped from the stones. Druven saw and held out her hand. He surrendered his reluctantly. She inspected it, kissed the top of his head without warning and told him to wash.

He looked furious with her for the kiss. I nearly smiled. Then I saw that her hand remained against his shoulder until he moved away.

We made rules that afternoon. Not grand ones, and not all at once. Catrane asked people to go together if they left the hall. My father repeated this more loudly and made it sound as though he had ordered it the previous evening, which in part he had. Nerla forbade the children to play on the women’s stair. I took the key to the bridal room, locked it and hung it beside my own at my waist.

Lira watched me do it.

“That won’t stop it,” she said.

“It will stop someone wandering in.”

She considered this and nodded.

We kept the yard door open while there was light. Anyone looking from the kitchen could see the washhouse, the byre door and the path between them. Massen put a man on that path. The man looked offended until Nerla gave him a cup of hot broth and told him where to stand clear of the drips.

For a little while the house became almost manageable. There were places to look and things to keep closed. People fetched what they needed from their rooms before dark. Blankets accumulated in the hall. My aunt complained that sleeping near the kitchen would ruin her lungs, then chose the warmest place and sent a boy upstairs for her pillow.

The dead man lay among us. I had begun to notice when I passed him without looking, and then made myself look too often.

In the late afternoon the porter came for my father.

“There are men below.”

“How many?”

“I saw six.”

“Whose?”

The porter glanced at Massen.

I looked too.

Massen set down the cup he had been holding. “I’ll come.”

My father did not move. “Whose?” he asked again.

“Some of mine,” Massen said.

The porter waited beside the door. I could see the damp along his collar and the small white flakes melting in his beard.

“Why?” my father asked.

“To bring people home safely.”

“They came for a wedding.”

“Yes.”

“And you brought men to take them home from it?”

Massen looked at the porter. “Tell Gedran I’ll meet him at the gate.”

The man went. My father stepped into Massen’s way.

“You’ll answer me first.”

Catrane moved towards them. She did not put herself between the men; she stopped beside my father, close enough that he had to acknowledge her.

“How many were asked to come?” she said.

“Twelve,” Massen answered.

My father drew in his breath.

“Before or after the death?” Catrane asked.

Massen looked at me.

“Before,” he said.

I felt the keys at my waist strike my hip as I turned towards him. I had taken only half a step.

“How long before?”

“Before we came.”

Nobody spoke. Somewhere behind us, the child who had been stealing bread laughed at something under a table.

Massen continued, “They were to stay below the western road. If there was no trouble, they would escort my people through the pass when we left.”

“And if there was trouble?” my father said.

“Then I didn’t mean to have my people shut in here with no one outside to ask after them.”

My father took a step towards him. Catrane said his name. He stopped, breathing through his nose.

“You sit at my table.”

“I do.”

“You take my daughter.”

“I haven’t taken her anywhere.”

I had been thinking about the hand in my hair, the warmth of his arm. While I sat leaning against him, Gedran had ridden out. Massen had heard the horse as I had and known where it went.

“Last night,” I said.

He turned fully towards me.

“You sent for them last night.”

“Yes.”

“While you were upstairs.”

He did not answer quickly enough to spare me the confirmation.

Catrane said, “The men outside need instructions before they receive them from someone else. Massen, you will come with me. Varrak, put nobody on the wall who is looking for a chance to use his bow.”

My father stared at her.

“We have a dead man already,” she said.

He looked away first. I hated that I was grateful to her for making him do it.

They went down together. I followed as far as the door, then stopped. The cold air touched my face. Beyond the yard, the road descended between brown bracken flattened by the wet. I could see the outer gate roof and a horse’s head moving below it. Nothing looked like a siege. Smoke rose from Thessa’s chimney. Someone had hung cloth beneath her eaves, trusting that the small snow would pass.

My father stood beside me.

“You see?” he said.

I knew what he wanted me to see: the Lorn who had smiled at his table, taken his food and prepared against him. I thought of the recent passage demands in Odran’s chest. I did not say anything.

My father put his hand on my shoulder. For once I did not move away. I needed the warmth of a familiar hand even while I distrusted the comfort he meant to give.

“He’ll learn,” he said.

I left him then.

In the kitchen, Nerla was cutting yesterday’s meat into a pot. There were too many people to feed it whole a second time. I stood beside her and began chopping an onion. My eyes watered, which was useful until they watered so much that I could not see the knife.

“Sit,” she said.

“I can cut an onion.”

“Then cut the other half when you’ve stopped crying on this one.”

I sat.

She finished it, put it into the pot and gave me a clean cloth. I pressed it to my eyes. It smelled of warm linen and the cupboard it had come from.

After a while I asked, “Did you know about the men?”

“No.”

“Would you have told me?”

She scraped the board with the back of her knife.

“I might have told him to tell you.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No.”

I waited for her to explain. She lifted the board and tipped the last pieces into the pot. Steam rose round her face.

When Massen returned, he did not come looking for me at once. I heard his voice in the hall, low, answering my father. The answers continued for a long time. I could not make out the words, and did not move closer.

Chapter 18 — A place for Eitha

Eitha came to Catrane after the lamps were lit. She asked for Nerla to be present, and for me not to speak until she had finished.

Catrane sent for me. I was in the west room, folding Odran’s dry shirt. His old clothes had been washed without touching the cup or any of the things Hethen wanted kept. The darn at the shoulder puckered where the cloth had dried. I smoothed it once, left it on the boards and went upstairs.

Eitha sat in the chair with my aunt’s cloak folded behind her back. Nerla stood beside the window. Hethen had set out the cup, still tied, and the four jugs. Massen was not there.

I sat where Catrane pointed.

Eitha began without looking at me.

“The man was singing. The old man. Not the one who died.”

Catrane nodded.

“I’d taken water down the hall. The boy at the far table had spilled his. I was coming back with the pewter jug. Master Bran was at the hatch. I saw his hand over the small cup.”

“Which hand?” Catrane asked.

“The right.”

“What was in it?”

“I couldn’t see.”

“Did he lift the cup?”

“No.”

“Touch it?”

“His fingers were above it. Like this.”

She held her hand over her lap, fingers close together, then drew them back. I had seen my uncle gesture that way while sprinkling sand on wet ink. I refused the comparison so quickly that I felt it as a physical recoil.

“Did anything fall?” Catrane asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Did you see him take something away?”

“He put his hand inside his coat. Then he took the wine jug.”

Hethen remained still. I watched the loose thread on the cup’s covering. It had curled into a small red hook.

Catrane asked where Eitha had stood. The girl described the aisle beside the third table. A drover had moved his stool back, and she had stopped to let him sit. Between him and the singer there had been a clear space to the hatch.

“How long?”

Eitha hesitated. “A breath. Two.”

“Did he see you?”

“I don’t think so.”

“Was anyone else by the board?”

“Nerla had gone to the hearth with the meat. The boy was taking plates.”

Nerla nodded once. Catrane asked her to describe her own movements, and she did. They fitted what I remembered: the dish coming through, my uncle returning with the jug, the old singer refusing to finish.

Then Eitha looked at me.

“You may speak now,” she said.

I had several questions ready, all of them ways to make what she had seen less certain. I could hear them in my own voice before I said them. Was she tired? Had she been angry? Could it have been another cup? I asked only the last, because it mattered.

“It was the little green one,” she said. “The silver showed.”

“Was it still on the warmer?”

“Yes. The cloth was under it.”

“Then how—”

I stopped. I had watched Nerla carry the cup earlier. Odran had held it at the table. If it was on the warmer again, someone had brought it back.

Hethen leaned forward. “Who returned it?”

Eitha looked towards Nerla.

“I did,” Nerla said. “He asked for it to be warmed. His hands hurt. It came back with the boy when I took the dish through.”

“Which boy?”

She named the one who had spilled water at the far table. He had carried the cup before the spill, she said, and she had put it on the folded cloth herself. After the song she returned it to Odran.

I remembered his hands round it, the way he had held them to the steam of the first drink after arriving. I had mistaken warmth for something he wanted to swallow.

Catrane sent for the boy. He came unwillingly, with flour across his cheek from sleeping against someone’s apron. Nerla told him no one meant to punish him for the water. He looked surprised that anyone still cared about the water at all.

He remembered taking the cup to the kitchen. He remembered the silver because he had rubbed his thumb over it. He had not washed it or put anything in it. Nerla had told him to leave it on the board, so he had.

“Then you carried plates?” Hethen asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you go near it again?”

“No.”

They let him leave. He went quickly, without looking at any of us.

Hethen said the other cups showed no similar grit. None of the people who had drunk from the common jugs had developed Odran’s symptoms. That did not tell him what the substance was, or whether it had killed him. It did make the small cup worth keeping apart.

“Could it be from the repair?” I asked.

“I can’t rule that out by wishing,” he said. “Nor rule it in.”

Eitha’s hands were folded tightly in her lap. I wanted to ask why she had not spoken sooner, and was able, this time, to keep the question to myself.

Catrane thanked her and asked whether she wished to return to the kitchen.

“Will he be told I said it?”

“Not tonight,” Catrane said. “I will have to put it to him. Before I do, you will know.”

“Can my brother come?”

“Where is he?”

“Below the ford. With Thessa’s people.”

I had not known she had a brother. I had known which apron she wore when serving and that she had a little curl she took trouble with. I sat still while Catrane arranged for a message to be taken in the morning.

After Eitha and Nerla left, I remained. Hethen covered the cup again, tightening the same thread beneath its lip.

“You’re thinking of your uncle,” Catrane said.

“He fills jugs. He goes to that board all the time.”

“Yes.”

“He might have touched anything.”

“She hasn’t said he put poison in it.”

“But you think it.”

“I think he should be asked what he did.”

I rose and went to the window. The glass reflected the room too clearly to let me see the yard. My face floated over Catrane’s shoulder, pale and pinched, with a loose strand of hair making me look untidy in a way that would have annoyed me yesterday.

“He brought me home,” I said.

Catrane waited.

“After Kennet. They sent my things out in a cart. Not even one of theirs. Bran came to fetch me, and he put feed sacks over everything so no one on the road would see how little there was.”

I had never told her that.

“Did you want them hidden?” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then I’m glad he did it.”

I pressed my knuckle against the cold glass. Below, a lamp moved across the yard. Two people walked beside it, their shadows joining and parting on the wall. I could not tell who they were.

Catrane came to stand a little way from me.

“Don’t speak to him alone about this.”

“He wouldn’t hurt me.”

“I hope not.”

I turned towards her, angry. She did not take the words back.

In the hall, my uncle sat by the fire with a little girl on his knee. She was trying to twist a ring off his finger. He let her struggle, then loosened it with his thumb and pretended astonishment when she succeeded. She held it up, triumphant.

He saw me in the doorway.

“Brin. Come and see what a strong thief we have.”

I walked towards them. The girl put the ring on her own finger. It hung loose enough to fall if she lowered her hand.

“Careful,” I said.

She curled her fingers into a fist. My uncle smiled at me. His right hand rested on her back, broad and familiar, a little burn near the wrist from repairing something at the fire.

I sat beside him because I could not think how to refuse.

Chapter 19 — The road in the morning

I asked to go to the ford before anyone could tell me I ought to stay.

Muirne Fell had been named in the papers, and she lived close enough that a messenger might bring her by midday. But I did not want her brought uphill into our hall, past my father and my uncle, to explain to us what we had misunderstood. I wanted to hear her where she could send me away.

Catrane considered the road from her window.

“Take two people. Not two men who spend the whole walk telling you what she means.”

“I’ll take Iver.”

“His hand needs rest.”

“He can walk.”

“He can. Take him if he agrees. And someone who knows the lower path.”

Thessa’s daughter had come up early with a basket of eggs and a complaint about a missing hen. Her name was Della. I had known that when I was a girl, and forgotten it as I had forgotten that she was not still seven. She stood by the kitchen door while Nerla counted the eggs. I asked whether she was going straight home.

“When someone looks in the byre.”

“For the hen?”

“It came up with the cart.”

“How do you know?”

“Because it wasn’t there before the cart left, and nobody’s found feathers.”

Bellan offered to look. Della gave him a description of the bird so exact that he said he would know it if it introduced itself. She did not smile.

I asked if I could walk down with her. She looked at my cloak, my boots and the two men discussing the gate behind me.

“Are you coming to see Mam?”

“Muirne Fell.”

“She isn’t at the ford house.”

“Where is she?”

“Across. With the willow roof.”

I knew which cottage she meant: the one I had seen closed, with a cat outside. I had assumed it empty because no smoke rose from it.

“We’ll go there,” I said.

Della waited for the eggs to be counted. She had three fewer than her mother had told her to bring; one had broken in the basket and two were wrapped separately to keep them from doing the same. Nerla paid for all of them. Della checked the coins twice before putting them away.

Massen found me fastening my cloak. The clasp caught where the wool had thickened. He reached to help and stopped when I did not lift my chin.

“Where are you going?”

“The ford.”

“Why?”

“To speak to Muirne Fell.”

“I’ll come.”

“Iver’s coming.”

“Then I’ll come as well.”

I looked at him. He was tired, his jaw rough with a night’s growth. I wanted the easy morning at Thessa’s, before the death, before I knew about his men.

“You have people below the gate who need speaking to.”

“They’ve been told to stay back.”

“By you?”

“By Gedran.”

“Then tell them yourself.”

He breathed out and looked towards the yard. For a moment I thought he would insist. Instead he said, “If the ford’s rising, don’t use the upper stones either.”

“I know the ford.”

“I know you do.”

He stepped away. I finished the clasp without help, catching a thread and breaking it.

We left by the outer gate. Iver wore his sword at his hip, his good hand resting near the hilt without touching it. Della carried the empty basket. I had put Odran’s copy of the passage demand inside my cloak, wrapped against the wet. Catrane had kept the original and made me promise not to surrender the copy to anyone who asked for it as though it belonged to them.

The road had softened beneath the thin snow. Our feet broke through to mud at every step. Behind us the hall stood above its outbuildings, smoke rising from the great chimney in a pale, wavering column. From this distance the bridal window was a dark square under the eaves. I looked away from it and watched Della choose the driest ground.

At the gate, two men I knew stood beside a horse. One had a rolled blanket tied above his saddle and a narrow shield slung along its side. The other carried a long parcel wrapped in blue cloth.

“Brin,” the first said. “We heard.”

I knew him as Torm, who had once broken a tooth on a stone in our bread and made a joke about the baker trying to fortify him. He was married to my father’s cousin. His wife had embroidered the cloth round the parcel.

“What’s that?” I asked.

He looked at it, almost surprised.

“For you. A spit. Good iron. We didn’t know whether you’d have one at the new house.”

“Thank you.”

“We meant to come yesterday. The mare cast a shoe.”

Iver waited beside me. Torm glanced at his coat, recognizing Catrane’s colours.

“Are you going out?”

“To the ford.”

“There are Lorns down there.”

“I know.”

“We’ve sent word up our side too.”

“To whom?”

“People who’ll want to know.”

His tone was kind. I looked at the shield, then at the wedding gift.

“Leave that with the porter,” I said. “And go in for food.”

“We’ll wait here.”

“Why?”

He lowered his voice. “Until we see what they’re doing.”

Della had begun walking again. I followed her because I could not think what to say that would send Torm home without insulting the affection that had brought him.

Below the gate, the burn ran dark between white edges. The cottage roofs appeared one by one through the bare branches. Thessa’s door was open. A man sat on the step repairing a harness strap, and a child stood beside him holding the end too high.

Della called to her mother that she had brought visitors and that Bellan was looking for the hen. Thessa came to the door, wiping her hands. Her gaze went to Iver’s sword, then to my face.

“I’ve heard,” she said.

“I thought you would.”

“Some of it.”

I asked about Muirne.

“She’ll be home if she’s not gone to the sheep.”

“Can you send someone?”

Thessa looked at Della. The girl had put down her basket and was pulling off one wet stocking. She stopped halfway.

“No,” Thessa said. “You can knock.”

I felt the rebuke, though she had not sharpened it. I thanked her and went towards the crossing.

Iver stepped ahead of me to test the first stone. I let him. The water struck his boot and divided round it. Snow caught in the seams of his coat. On the far side, the cat I had seen three days before watched from beneath an upturned tub, perfectly dry.

Halfway across, I looked upstream. There was a narrow path along the bank, almost hidden by willow roots. It led towards the hall’s western wall and the blocked opening Druven had found. I could not see the opening itself, only the pale face of stone above the dark run of water.

“Keep moving,” Iver said gently.

I turned back to the next step.

Chapter 20 — The tokens in the cloth

Muirne opened the door with a bowl in her hand. There was flour across the front of her dress, and a strand of hair had come loose and stuck to her cheek. She looked at me, then at Iver.

“If it’s about the lower path, I haven’t blocked it.”

“It isn’t.”

“What, then?”

“Odran Vale.”

Her hand tightened on the bowl.

I told her he was dead. I had thought the letter difficult; saying it in a doorway, with the water behind me and a stranger watching my mouth, was worse. She set the bowl down without looking and missed the table. It struck the edge, tipped and spilled flour across the floor.

Neither of us moved to pick it up.

“How?”

“We don’t know yet.”

“Where?”

“At the hall. The night before last.”

She stepped back and let us in.

The cottage was warm in one half and bitterly cold in the other. A blanket divided the room near the bed. The hearth smoked a little because the wind had turned, and a row of small cheeses hung in cloth above a shelf. There was nothing ornamental except a bright piece of blue pottery set where the light could find it. The cat slipped in behind us and went straight to the warm side.

Muirne fetched a brush for the flour. I bent to help. She took the bowl from me and set it upright.

“Sit.”

I sat.

Iver remained by the door until she told him he could sit too. There was only one more stool. He took it and kept his injured hand inside his coat.

“He was going to speak before the marriage,” Muirne said.

“He didn’t get to.”

“He said he would.”

“I know.”

“No. You know he didn’t.”

She swept the last flour onto a scrap of bark and emptied it into the fire. It flared briefly. I had not seen flour burn before.

I told her about the chest and the papers. I showed her the copy Catrane had made. Muirne read slowly, moving her lips over a few words. When she reached the bottom, she turned it over as though something ought to be on the back.

“It’s not the one I gave him.”

“We kept that safe. This is a copy.”

“Who made it?”

“Lady Catrane of Tor’s household.”

She read it again. This time she nodded.

“That was paid.”

“My father said—”

I stopped.

Muirne looked up.

“What did your father say?”

“That sometimes a demand is shown as though it were paid.”

Her mouth twisted. She went to the bed, knelt and pulled a small wooden box from beneath it. A string held the lid closed. She worked the knot with difficulty, then fetched a pin to loosen it.

“I had three sheep that autumn,” she said. “The old ewe and her two daughters. I sold the younger one. If you ask where the money came from, ask the man who bought her.”

“I wasn’t asking that.”

“You were going to.”

I could not honestly deny it.

Inside the box were several small objects wrapped in faded cloth. She lifted out a piece of wood drilled at one end, a dark stone with a notch along its edge, and a strip of metal so thin that it had bent round whatever lay beside it. She arranged them on the table without ceremony.

“Those were my grandmother’s.”

“What are they?”

“Ways in.”

I picked up the wood. It had been handled until the edges were soft. Three shallow cuts crossed one face. I had seen something like it in a drawer at home, among old gaming pieces and things my father said were not worth keeping but would not let anyone throw away.

“It isn’t a toll token,” Muirne said, seeing my expression. “It’s older. Her people had a place at the lower house.”

“Your grandmother’s people?”

“And before her. She didn’t make it up because she wanted a warm bed.”

I set the wood down carefully.

“What happened to the lower house?”

Muirne looked towards Iver. He sat with his shoulders bent, trying to make himself take up less space. She seemed to decide he was not there to hurry her.

“There was fighting up the road. Families came down. Some were kin to people on the wrong side. Some were only travelling with them. They were let in below, where the old fire was.”

“And then?”

“The lower way was barred. Men came to the other side and called for them. The house gave them out.”

I heard my father’s voice saying a feud. I had imagined armed men, the sort of quarrel in which anyone could have begun it. Muirne touched the piece of wood with one finger.

“There was a girl with a split boot. My grandmother’s grandmother told it that way. The girl wouldn’t come until she found the other shoe she carried for her brother. That’s the part she kept telling.”

“Did any get away?”

“Some weren’t there. Some came later. Some were sent off before the door shut. You want a clean list, I haven’t got one.”

“I don’t.”

She looked at me for a moment. “Odran thought he could make one.”

I asked how well she had known him.

“Long enough to know he’d keep walking after his foot told him to stop.”

She sat at last. The cat climbed onto her lap, turned twice and settled. Muirne put a hand on its back without looking down.

“He said if he brought the stone where everyone could see it, they’d have to listen. I told him a stone never stopped a man closing his ears.”

“Why did you let him bring it?”

Her hand stopped on the cat.

“Let him?”

I looked down at the table.

“He wasn’t mine to keep,” she said. “I asked him to wait for better weather. He wanted your wedding. So many witnesses, he said. Both houses. Someone from Tor. There wouldn’t be another chance.”

Outside, a cart passed slowly on the far bank. We heard the driver urging the horse through the wet. Muirne listened until the wheels were beyond hearing.

“Did he hurt?” she asked.

I could have said that it was quick. It would have been true and insufficient.

“Yes,” I said. “For a little while.”

She stroked the cat again. Iver looked at the floor.

After some time she asked who had been with him. I told her. I told her about the water, Hethen, Massen holding him, the shirt we had put on him. She wanted to know whether we had taken care with the sore. I said Nerla had dressed it again.

Muirne nodded. Her eyes were wet, but she did not wipe them.

I asked about the recent shelter payment only when she began wrapping the tokens again.

“Was it my father who refused you?”

“No.”

“Who?”

“His brother.”

I had expected it by then. Hearing it still made my stomach contract.

“Was there a reason?”

“We’d brought a child from a house they wouldn’t admit. They said leave her at the ford and the rest could come up.”

“What did you do?”

“Went back down.”

“With the child?”

Muirne stared at me.

I felt my face burn. “I’m sorry. I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

She tied the box closed. Iver shifted on the stool. The room had cooled while we talked; the fire needed another piece of wood, and none lay beside it. He rose and asked where he could fetch fuel.

“Behind the door.”

He brought in two pieces and asked which to use. She pointed. He put it on carefully, with his good hand. Then he took off his cloak and laid it over the back of her chair.

“I don’t need that,” she said.

“Wear it until the fire catches.”

She looked at him and, after a moment, drew it round her shoulders.

When we left, Muirne came to the door with us. She kept his cloak. He told her someone would fetch it another day, and she said it would be dry and brushed when they did.

“Will you come up?” I asked her.

She looked past me towards the hall.

“To see him,” I added.

“Not today.”

“Tomorrow?”

“I’ll decide tomorrow.”

I nodded.

At the ford, Iver waited for me to step onto the first stone. His coat was too thin without the cloak. I could see him shivering. I offered him mine and he shook his head before I had finished.

“Lady Catrane would have my other hand.”

“She would not.”

“No. But she’d look at it until I wished she had.”

I laughed, unexpectedly, and the sound hurt my throat.

We crossed slowly. On the far bank, Della stood with her recovered hen under one arm. The bird had lost a feather and looked indignant. Bellan had brought it down himself and was explaining how difficult it had been to catch.

“It doesn’t like being chased,” Della said.

“I noticed.”

She stroked the hen’s breast. Bellan looked at me and stopped talking. I wondered how much he could see in my face.

Above the cottages, a horn sounded once from the gate.

Iver turned towards it. Another answered farther along the western road. Neither was the horn we used to call people to a meal.

Part III — Those who came for us

Chapter 21 — The wedding gift

Thessa took Della by the shoulder and drew her inside. The hen protested at being carried through a narrow door. Bellan looked up the road, then at me.

“Was that the hall?”

“The gate.”

“Should we go back?”

Iver had gone ahead to the bend. He stood partly screened by a willow, his good hand against the trunk. When he returned, he was careful not to hurry.

“There are men on the road,” he said. “Both sides.”

“How many?”

“I can’t see past the gate.”

I wanted to ask him whether it was safe. He could not see past the gate; he had just told me. I pulled my cloak close and began walking.

He caught up. “Let me go first.”

“I know them.”

“That may help.”

I stopped. I let him pass, and Bellan fell in beside me. Thessa called after him to leave his branch. He had forgotten he was still carrying it.

The road rose between banks cut into the hillside. A little farther up, where the ditch had filled with leaves, water spread across the path and froze in a thin skin. We stepped round it single file. I could hear men speaking before I saw them, the voices carried clearly in the cold: one asking where someone had gone, another telling him to lower the butt of his spear before he put it through a horse’s eye. The ordinary annoyance of it frightened me.

Torm stood on the uphill side of the bend with four men. I knew three. One had carried me on his shoulders to see a wrestling match when I was small; I remembered the smell of the oil he used in his hair. On the lower side were Lorns, recognizable by faces rather than badges. The man who had mocked my father’s protection after Odran died stood among them. He wore a different coat and held a shield I had never seen.

There was space enough between the groups for two carts to pass. No one used it.

Torm saw me first.

“Brin. Come this way.”

He held out his hand as though I were on the far side of a flooded ditch. I kept walking up the middle.

The Lorn stepped forward. Iver moved with me, saying Catrane’s name before anyone asked for it. His injured hand remained under his coat. The Lorn looked at that hidden hand, then at the sword on his other side.

“What’s she doing down here?” he asked.

“She is standing in front of you,” I said.

He looked embarrassed, then irritated with the embarrassment. “You should be in the hall.”

“So should you. There’s food.”

“We’ve food.”

A horse behind him shook its head. Its bit rang. The sound made me look at the animal rather than the man, and I saw the feed bag rolled behind the saddle, the extra blanket, the way the rider had prepared to remain outside overnight. Someone had told them what to bring.

“Where is Massen?” I asked.

“At the gate.”

“Then I’ll see him there.”

The man hesitated. Torm took a step towards us. I could feel Iver preparing to move, and the Lorn noticed it too.

“Let her pass,” someone said from behind him.

It was Gedran. He came between two horses, his face flushed from walking quickly. He had lost the loose ease with which he had entertained the table. A strap crossed his chest and disappeared beneath his cloak. When he shifted, I saw the edge of a shield behind his shoulder.

“Brin,” he said. “We were coming for you.”

“I was at the ford.”

“I know.”

“Then you needn’t come any farther.”

He accepted that with a nod and turned uphill. We walked together. The others remained where they were. I had to pass close to Torm, who still held the blue-wrapped wedding gift.

“You haven’t put it inside,” I said.

“No one came to take it.”

“Give it to me.”

“It weighs—”

“Give it here.”

He handed it over. The iron pulled my arms down at once. I shifted it against my hip, feeling the cold through the cloth. Bellan reached for the other end. Together we carried it towards the gate, the gift taking up enough of the road that people had to step aside for us.

I had meant to shame Torm into following us. He did not. At the top of the rise I looked back and saw him speaking to the man beside him, his hand resting on the man’s shoulder, both of them watching the Lorns below.

Massen stood beneath the gate roof with my father and Catrane. My uncle was not there. The space smelled of wet harness and the sweet, hot breath of horses. A lantern swung above the porter’s head although it was still daylight.

My father saw the parcel.

“What’s that?”

“My wedding spit.”

“What are you carrying it for?”

I set it on the bench. Bellan lowered his end more gently.

“Because Torm had other things in his hands.”

My father looked down the road. His expression changed when he saw the men still divided below. He knew them as well as I did. Perhaps he remembered the same shoulders at the wrestling match.

Catrane asked Iver what had happened. He told her without making much of it. I was grateful, though part of me wanted him to explain how close the two groups had seemed while I stood between them.

Massen came to me.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Did anyone—”

“No.”

He stopped. He looked at Bellan instead.

“You should get warm.”

Bellan nodded but did not leave until I told him Druven would want to know he was back. Then he ran uphill towards the yard.

“My men will go back to the western shelters,” Massen told my father. “Yours come inside or go home.”

“My men?”

“The men on your side of the road.”

“They came to see my daughter married.”

“So did mine.”

“Yours brought shields.”

Massen looked at the shield propped beside the porter. My father followed his gaze and flushed.

Catrane said, “Send the orders in front of each other.”

They did. My father called Torm up and told him to bring those who were staying into the hall. Massen sent Gedran back down with his words. The first movement was easy: men who wanted a hot meal and were grateful to have permission to seek one. The next was slower. One Lorn would not leave without his brother, who had gone to see a horse at the cottages. One of Torm’s cousins had already sent a boy farther up the road and would wait for his return. Every delay had a sensible cause when explained on its own.

I stood beneath the lantern until my feet were numb. I could not make the road empty by watching it.

At last Catrane told me to go in and eat. I might have argued if she had spoken kindly. She sounded tired of all of us.

Massen walked uphill with me. For a while neither of us spoke. Snow had settled in the folds of the heather and along the top of the byre wall. The hall windows were beginning to glow, although there was still light enough to see the smoke above them. I remembered coming home from a winter visit and seeing it from this same turn in the road, knowing that there would be a fire, that someone would remark on my red cheeks and put food in front of me before asking anything difficult.

“Did you mean to tell me?” I asked.

“About the escort?”

“Yes.”

“After the marriage.”

I stopped.

He stopped too. “I know.”

“You don’t know what I was going to say.”

“I can guess.”

“Then don’t make me say it.”

He looked towards the hall. His mouth was tight.

“I had people who were afraid to come here,” he said. “Not because of you. Because of what they remembered, and what they believed your father might do if the terms changed once we were under his roof.”

“Did you believe that?”

“Yes.”

I had wanted a gentler answer. The plain one left me with less to push against.

“And the night he died?”

“A man died in front of us, and your father put his men at the door. I told Gedran to bring ours nearer.”

“He said no bar.”

“He didn’t need a bar with men standing there.”

I thought of the porter holding the iron across his waist. I had been grateful to my father for ordering it put away. Massen had seen the men who remained.

“You could have told me upstairs.”

“Yes.”

“While you were taking out my pins.”

His face changed. He stepped towards me and stopped before touching me.

“I wanted those few minutes,” he said.

“So did I.”

“I know.”

I went on uphill. He walked beside me, close enough that our sleeves brushed once. Neither of us moved farther away.

At the kitchen door Eitha stood with her brother. He was a broad-faced young man with the same pale eyebrows, and he had brought her a pair of thick stockings. She held them against her chest while he talked. I waited until they had finished before passing.

She looked at me. I nodded to her brother. He bowed awkwardly, still holding the basket from which the stockings had come.

Inside, my uncle had saved me a place by the fire.

Chapter 22 — What my uncle remembered

Bran had put a bowl on the hearth beside the settle, with a cloth over it to keep the food warm. He drew the cloth away when I came in.

“Nerla says you haven’t eaten.”

“Nerla says a great deal.”

“Generally because the rest of us don’t listen.”

I sat. My legs had begun to shake now that I no longer needed them to carry me. The stew smelled of yesterday’s meat and fresh onion. There was a good piece beneath the surface, one someone had chosen rather than ladled by chance.

Bran waited while I tasted it.

“Better?”

“Yes.”

He sat on the stool beside me. The place had been Odran’s two nights before, when they spoke privately. I watched my uncle settle his bad knee and move his foot out of the draught.

“I hear you went to the Fells.”

“Muirne.”

“How is she?”

“Cold.”

“It’s a cold house.”

I kept eating. He had answered so readily that for a moment the conversation could have remained there, a discussion of roofs and fuel and the difficulty of keeping a lower cottage dry.

“She says she paid for shelter.”

Bran looked into the fire.

“She has said it before.”

“Did she?”

“Pay? Yes. For three people.”

“And brought four.”

He looked back at me.

“You’ve heard her account.”

“A child.”

“A child from a house your father had reason not to admit.”

“Because the child might do what?”

My voice had risen. A man on the next bench glanced towards us. Bran waited until he turned away.

“Because admitting the child admits a claim on the house. Kin come after kin. A place given for a night becomes a place demanded every winter, and sooner or later they ask why their people aren’t represented in the use of the road.”

I put down the spoon.

“Did you give her the money back?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“It was for passage as well.”

“The paper separates them.”

He was silent a moment. I saw him reconsider what I had read, how much I knew. I wished he would become angry. His patience was worse.

“You’ve only seen a copy,” he said.

“Catrane has the original.”

“I know.”

He rubbed his jaw. The movement was familiar enough that I almost asked about the tooth.

“Your father doesn’t always remember what it takes to keep this place open,” he said. “He remembers the nights when there’s enough for everyone. He remembers a man praising the fire. In spring, when the meal bins are low and the track has gone, somebody still has to find men who’ll work on a promise.”

“Was Muirne meant to feed them?”

“No. Muirne was meant to keep to the number she’d agreed.”

I looked towards the kitchen. Nerla stood at the hatch with a jug, listening to a boy explain something with both hands. She waited until he finished, then pointed him towards the yard. Eitha’s brother had taken a place beside her and was cutting bread.

“How old was the child?” I asked.

“I don’t remember.”

“Did you see her?”

“Yes.”

“Was she small?”

“Brin.”

I picked up the spoon again because my hands needed something to do. I could not eat.

He leaned towards me.

“I didn’t send them into a storm. They had the ford cottages.”

“Muirne went back to that cold house.”

“And she’s alive to tell you.”

He heard himself. His face tightened. For a moment the fire was the only sound between us.

“I didn’t mean it so,” he said.

I watched a bead of fat spread on the surface of the stew.

Bran put his hand over mine. He had done that when I was young and furious, keeping me from snatching away whatever I had seized in anger. His palm was warm, the skin rough across the fingers. I let it remain for one breath, then drew my hand out.

“Why did you show Druven the little cupboard?”

He looked surprised by the turn.

“Because that’s what I thought she wanted.”

“You knew there was an opening behind the shelves.”

“A filled opening.”

“And you gave Lira a stone to put above it.”

He sat back.

“She was frightened for you.”

“Before Odran came?”

“She’d been reading old things. You know how she gets when she finds something no one has explained to her.”

“She read them because you asked her.”

“Yes. Because she could.”

I thought of Lira saying the same thing in the bridal room, proud once to have been useful. Bran watched my face.

“It was meant as protection,” he said. “There are old ways of making a room safe when a bride leaves one household for another. Her mother would have known them.”

“Did you?”

“Some.”

“Enough?”

He did not answer at once. That hesitation was the first thing he had given me that afternoon which I believed without effort.

“I thought so,” he said.

A laugh broke out at the lower end of the hall. Somebody had caught a yard hen trying to come in again. Della’s brother—no, Eitha’s brother; I caught myself confusing the two households—went to carry it back to the yard. For an instant everyone watched the bird flap in his arms, and the room was warm and foolish as it had been before the death.

Bran smiled. I did too, unwillingly. He waited until I looked back.

“We’ll put it right,” he said.

“How?”

“I need to see what’s been brought here. And where she’s put the other piece.”

“Catrane has the stone.”

“I know. She may let me look at it if you ask.”

“You can ask.”

His expression did not change much. I knew him well enough to see the hurt, or the attempt to show me hurt. I could not tell which it was, and hated having begun to make that distinction.

“Of course,” he said.

He rose, pressing a hand to his knee. I almost offered my arm. He caught the movement and smiled again, more sadly this time, then went to speak to my father.

I finished the stew after he left. It had cooled to a thick skin, but I was hungry and it was good. I ate every piece he had chosen for me.

Chapter 23 — The empty boards

The first person who went missing was not Lira.

It was my aunt’s youngest boy, a solemn child who had spent most of the gathering counting the carved animals on the backs of the chairs. He had been put to sleep near the kitchen with the other children. His mother went to fetch a dry cloth, returned and found his blanket empty.

At first we did not call it missing. We asked who had taken him to piss. We asked whether he had gone to his father. The boy who was meant to be watching the children said he had only closed his eyes a moment, then began to cry when my aunt struck his shoulder.

Nerla put herself between them.

“Find him first.”

The child was called Rodd. We called the name through the hall, the kitchen, the west passage. I went up the women’s stair before remembering the bridal key at my waist. The door was still locked. I touched the key and felt foolishly reassured.

Massen found him beneath the outer stair, sitting with both hands on his knees.

He was dry. He had no shoes. He said his mother had told him to wait there.

His mother knelt in front of him, gripping his arms. “I was in the kitchen.”

He began to cry because she sounded angry. Massen loosened her hands one at a time. She turned on him, then saw what she had been doing and gathered the child against her.

“Who brought you?” I asked.

Rodd looked at his mother. “You.”

She made a little sound.

“Did you see her face?” Massen asked.

The child shook his head. “She was round the corner.”

“Which corner?”

He pointed towards the wall behind him. There was no corner there, only the narrow triangular space beneath the stair and an old basket full of broken kindling.

We took him to the hall. His mother would not put him down. When her husband asked what had happened, she told him to bring the blanket and stop asking her questions. He brought it. I watched him tuck it round the boy, his hands clumsy, and thought of the men below the gate who had come prepared for danger with shields.

After that, no one laughed at going in pairs. Catrane said that if a voice called from where a person could not be seen, someone else must be fetched before following it. My father repeated the order. He would not speak of the child’s account, but he put his own man at the foot of the women’s stair.

The man asked me for the key to the bridal room. I kept it.

“Then don’t leave it lying,” he said.

I went to find Lira. Her room was empty. The lamp I had left was on the chest, its oil almost gone. The wet dress hung from a peg, stiffening as it dried. Beneath it, her shoes stood together with their toes towards the wall.

I touched the dress. It was still cold.

In the passage I met Bran carrying a bowl. He said Lira had gone to help Nerla. I thanked him, relieved, and went down.

Nerla had not seen her for an hour.

“Perhaps with the linen,” she said.

I checked. Lira was not there. I found her shawl on the bench and took it with me, intending to scold her for leaving it where someone would sit on it.

The west room stood open. Odran’s clothes were on the shelf, folded as I had left them. His body was no longer there because we had taken it to the hall; I knew that, and still looked into the room twice before moving on.

At the far end of the hall the candles burned around empty boards.

For a moment I thought a table had been moved in front of him and hidden him from my view. I walked closer. My mother’s cover was gone. The little piece of bread remained beside one candle, hardening at the edge. Under the boards, where his weight had pressed the trestles into the rushes, two dark marks showed the floor.

I asked the woman nearest me where he had been taken.

“The washhouse.”

“By whom?”

“Your cousin and the old man.”

“Which old man?”

“The one who helped with the benches.”

There were half a dozen men who might fit that description. I asked which way they had gone. She pointed to the kitchen passage.

“Why didn’t you ask them?”

She stared at me. “They live here.”

I went through the kitchen so quickly that Nerla caught my sleeve.

“What’s wrong?”

“Lira’s taken him.”

“Taken who?”

I told her. She put down the spoon she was holding and called for Massen. I was already at the yard door.

The man on the path said two people had carried the body out. He had helped with the turn beside the washhouse. Yes, Lira had been there. Yes, he had thought it strange, but she said Bran wanted the boards brought in for the night so the children would not sleep beside them. He had not liked to question her while she carried the dead.

“Where is Bran?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

Massen came behind me with a lamp. I started down the path and he caught my elbow.

“Together.”

I wanted to pull away. Instead I waited until he had the lamp high enough to light the ground.

The yard was familiar even in the snow. I knew where a stone projected beside the drain, where the path dipped at the washhouse step, how the byre door looked when the bar had not been settled fully into place. We passed these things as we had passed them a thousand times.

The washhouse door seemed farther away each time I looked up.

Massen slowed. The lamp flame bent sharply towards the wall, though the air against my face was still.

“Stop,” I said.

He stopped.

Behind us the kitchen window shone. I could see Nerla standing in it, her face a small pale oval above the sill. Between us and the washhouse, the path lay straight and short. I counted the flagstones. There should have been nine broad ones and a broken half at the threshold. I could see the half, then a stretch of dark ground beyond it, then the door.

Massen said my name.

I could hear water. Not the burn’s usual rush below the wall, but water falling into a deep, enclosed place. Lira’s shawl hung over my arm. I gripped it until the fringe cut between my fingers.

From the washhouse came a dragging sound.

“Lira!”

No answer.

Massen handed me the lamp and drew the yard rope through his belt, leaving its other end in the hand of the man who had followed us. It was an ordinary length used for lowering feed sacks. I had seen it frayed and knotted often enough to trust it more than I ought.

“Don’t pull unless I ask,” he told the man. “Keep it clear of the drain.”

Then he took the lamp back and offered me his arm.

I held it. We walked to the broken half-stone without looking at the door. On the next step my foot met the threshold where it ought to have been.

The door struck inward. Warm, wet air came out, smelling of soap and old linen.

Lira stood at the far end, beside Odran’s covered body. The man who had helped her carry him sat on the floor with his hands over his ears.

Behind Lira, where the washhouse wall should have been, a woman was setting down a bowl.

Chapter 24 — The washing place

I knew the washhouse wall. A crack ran through it above the copper, and we had hung a broken sieve there because the crack looked less troublesome when something covered it. Now the sieve hung beside an open space. Beyond it lay a low room with a stone floor and a fire that gave no light to anything near it.

The woman bent over the bowl. Her skirt was dark, wet along its edge. I could not see her face. She moved with the weariness of someone clearing after a meal, lifting one thing, forgetting why she had lifted it, setting it down where it had been before. The bowl made no sound when it touched the table.

Lira stood so close to that room that the back of her dress seemed to lose its colour in it.

“Come here,” I said.

She did not turn.

Massen stepped towards the man on the floor. The rope at his waist drew taut behind him. He stopped and told the man outside to give him a little more. The man’s answer sounded far away, though I could see his shoulder in the doorway.

I went to Lira.

The floor was wet. It always was in the washhouse; water found its way through the drain badly, and somebody was forever promising to lift the flags and set them properly. I kept my eyes on those flags. The familiar dip beside the copper. The corner worn smooth by the basket. A piece of blue thread caught against the leg of the bench.

“Lira.”

She looked at me then. Her face was almost calm.

“He said if we took him out, it would stop.”

“Who?”

“Bran.”

I reached for her hand. Her fingers were cold enough to make me flinch.

“We’ll talk about it inside.”

“He said the wrong death was under the fire.”

“Come with me.”

She looked towards the woman. “She won’t let him through.”

A stool stood beside the table in the other room. As I watched, a small figure approached it from somewhere beyond the edge of the doorway. I saw a sleeve and the side of a head. The woman reached down and drew the stool away.

The small figure stopped.

The woman put the empty bowl nearer the edge of the table. Her hand passed through the light as though it were beneath water. Then she turned towards a door at the far end and raised both arms to a bolt.

“Don’t look,” Massen said behind me.

I had not heard him come closer. He held the older man beneath one shoulder. The man was trying to stand and could not make his feet stay beneath him. I recognized him now: Harn, who sharpened knives for the household and came to carry things when asked. He had been old all my life. There was a wet patch down the front of his clothes, and his mouth hung open.

“Help him,” Massen said.

I put Lira’s hand into his free one and took Harn’s arm. The old man leaned on me, lighter than I expected and harder to hold. His bones pressed through his sleeve.

“There’s someone outside,” he whispered.

“We know. They’re waiting to help.”

“No.”

He tried to turn back towards the low room. I pulled him the other way. Lira resisted Massen, not violently, only with the stubborn dead weight of someone who had decided where she must remain.

“Odran,” she said.

“We’ll come for him,” Massen answered.

“No. That’s what they did.”

I looked back. The covered body lay on the washing bench where they had set it, my mother’s raised white flowers turned grey in the strange light. The shape beneath was unmistakable. I could not leave it beside that woman and tell myself I had kept the promise in my letter.

“Massen.”

He looked at the body, then at the doorway. He swore quietly.

“Get Harn out first.”

I moved towards the door, half carrying the old man. The threshold was only a few paces away. At each step I felt his effort to stop, and had to murmur into his ear as I once had to a frightened horse. Look here. This foot. Now the next. He kept saying no, so softly that I could barely hear it.

The man outside reached for him. For a moment we pulled in different directions, the old body caught between us. I shouted at him to wait, found Harn’s foot hooked behind the doorpost and freed it. Then they were across, and Harn folded to his knees on the path.

I turned back.

The rope lay along the floor, disappearing into the dark where Massen stood. He had tied Lira’s shawl round her shoulders with one clumsy knot. She was holding the near end of Odran’s boards. Massen had the other. There was not room for them to turn without lifting the dead man close to the opening.

“Under here,” he told me.

I took the side. We lifted.

Odran’s weight surprised me, although I had watched the men carry him before. The boards bent slightly. Under the cover something shifted, and I almost let go. Lira made a sound through her teeth and held on.

We turned towards the door. Massen had to step backwards. The rope caught beneath a foot of the bench. He stopped, unable to see why it would not yield.

“Down,” he said.

“No,” Lira answered.

“Only for a moment.”

“No.”

Her face had changed. She was looking over my shoulder.

I heard the scrape of the stool.

I did not turn. I lowered my side enough to free one hand, keeping the boards against my thigh, and reached for the rope. It had wedged in a split in the bench foot. I pulled; a strand tore and stood out pale. I could not release it without letting the body fall.

“Knife,” I said.

Massen shook his head. “Not the rope.”

“I know.”

He could not reach his knife while holding the boards. I took the little one at my own belt, the one I used for fruit and thread, and cut the edge of the split wider. The blade slipped. I felt it bite my finger and kept working. The rope came free suddenly, striking the bench leg.

We moved.

At the threshold the boards caught. Lira tried to lift her end higher and tipped Odran towards me. I put my shoulder against the covered body, felt the cold through the cloth and had to swallow a cry. Massen told us to stop. He shifted his grip, turned the boards by a hand’s breadth and brought his end through.

The men outside took the weight. I stumbled after it, my hand wet and burning, and found myself on the ordinary path with snow melting on my face.

Lira came last. She stood in the doorway, looking back.

I seized her wrist and pulled.

She struck me across the mouth.

It was a full, frightened blow. My teeth cut the inside of my lip. I let go. Massen caught her as she stepped backwards, lifted her clear of the threshold and set her on the path. She fought him until she saw the kitchen window. Then she stopped so abruptly that he nearly fell.

The washhouse door hung open. Inside were the copper, the broken sieve, the cracked wall. Water ran along the floor towards the drain.

No low room. No woman. No table.

Lira stared at it. I held my bleeding finger in my other hand and tasted blood in my mouth.

“Get inside,” Massen said.

No one argued.

We brought Odran through the kitchen. People moved out of the way as they saw what we carried. The white cover had picked up soot along one edge. Nerla reached to straighten it, stopped when she saw my hand and called for Hethen.

Lira tried to follow the boards into the hall. I caught her sleeve.

“You’re staying with me.”

She pulled free.

“No.”

“You nearly—”

“You asked me to come out. I came out.”

“Massen carried you.”

Her eyes filled. “Then thank him.”

She went past me. I made to follow and Hethen took my wrist.

“Sit.”

I tried to explain that the cut was small. He looked at it and said it would be smaller still if I stopped waving it about. Massen stood beside us while he cleaned it. The rope had scored Massen’s belt and torn the cloth beneath it. He looked down at it as though discovering he had been attached to something.

Hethen bound my finger. When he touched my lip, I drew back.

“Who did that?”

“I caught it.”

He looked at me. I looked away. After a moment he fetched a clean cloth and told me to hold it there.

In the hall, my father was demanding to know who had ordered the body moved. Several people began answering at once. Lira’s voice rose above them, sharp enough to carry into the kitchen.

“Bran told me.”

The other voices stopped.

I stood. Hethen let me go this time.

My uncle stood near the high table, facing Lira. His hands were open at his sides.

“I said it might help to take him away from the hearth,” he said. “I didn’t tell you to go alone.”

“I took Harn.”

“I meant someone who could protect you.”

Harn sat on the bench with Nerla wrapping a blanket round him. He lowered his head.

“Don’t,” I said.

Bran turned towards me.

“He went because she asked him,” I said. “Don’t make him ashamed of helping.”

“I don’t.”

“You just did.”

My father stepped forward. “Enough.”

For once the word did not settle the room. Harn began to weep. He covered his face with both hands, and Nerla sat beside him without trying to stop the sound.

My uncle looked at my mouth, then at my bandaged hand.

“Brin,” he said softly.

I went past him to help straighten the cover over Odran.

Chapter 25 — The thing not said

We slept in the hall that night, if sleeping was what we did.

The benches had been moved to make lanes between the bedding. Catrane walked the lanes once with a lamp, making certain there was space to carry a sick person through. My father resented the inspection until she asked him where the old men would be most comfortable. Then he began finding them cushions, sending for blankets, remembering who could not lie flat and who needed to be near the door.

Massen took the first watch in the yard. I wanted him inside. I did not ask.

Lira lay beside the kitchen wall, with Nerla between her and the nearest children. She had not spoken to me since the washhouse. I sat on a folded cloak a few paces away and watched her pretend to sleep. Once she opened her eyes and found me looking. I turned away first.

My lip throbbed. Every time I forgot and licked it, I tasted blood. Hethen had given me something bitter for the pain in my hand; it had made my mouth dry without making me sleepy. Around me people shifted, sighed and negotiated room for elbows. One woman took off her shoes beneath the blanket, as though the hall might be offended by her feet.

The old singer cleared his throat. Three people told him not to begin. He said he only wanted water.

A little laughter moved through the nearest bedding. I laughed too. Lira’s mouth twitched in the firelight, and I had to look away again before she saw how glad it made me.

After a while Nerla rose to check the kitchen. Lira sat up. I waited for her to speak. She looked at the covered boards, then at her hands.

“You think I wanted this.”

“No.”

“You think I was being foolish.”

“I think Bran told you something he didn’t understand.”

She pulled the blanket round her shoulders. “He said the man had brought the wrong welcome in. That the body had to be outside before we shut the doors for the night. He said Harn would know the way to the cairn.”

“The cairn?”

“We didn’t get past the washhouse.”

I thought of the path beyond it, down towards the burn and the low burial mound whose old stones we used as seats when we were children. I had gone there with Lira to escape lessons. We had hidden sweet things beneath one of the slabs and returned to find mice had eaten through the wrapping. It had never frightened me then.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

She gave me a tired look.

“I know,” I said. “I keep asking that.”

“You’d have said I was making it worse.”

“I might have.”

“You’d have taken over.”

“Yes.”

She looked down. I let the answer remain between us. Across the room my father was helping a man lower himself onto a pallet, his own body bending stiffly with the effort.

“I saw her before,” Lira said.

“The woman?”

She nodded.

“The night Odran died?”

“Through the bridal door. It wasn’t the room when I opened it. There were steps. I thought somebody had taken the lamp down.”

“Did you go in?”

“One step. Perhaps two. Then water came over my feet.”

I remembered the wet hem, the clean soles, the smell of water among stones.

“She had the bowl then?”

“Yes. She put it down for me.”

“Was anything in it?”

“No.”

Lira drew her knees closer. “I thought she wanted me to sit.”

I looked towards the boarded recess beyond the kitchen passage. Two men sat near it, playing a silent game with counters. They had chosen the place because it was warm. They did not know what else they guarded; Catrane had told them only to keep anyone from shifting the board.

“Did she speak?” I asked.

Lira shook her head.

“Then why did you think—”

She looked at me, and I stopped. I had believed my mother stood outside my room because I heard a cough and a basket. Lira had seen a woman set a bowl for her.

“I heard someone too,” I said.

She waited.

“My mother. Or something that sounded like her.”

“When?”

“Before I found you.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

I almost smiled at the question. Then I saw she meant it without irony.

“I didn’t want it to be real.”

She leaned her head against the wall. For a long time we sat looking across the hall. The candles beside Odran had burned lower than the others. Nerla came back and trimmed them, pinching away the dead ends of the wicks. The little flames rose steadily again.

“I’m sorry I hit you,” Lira said.

I touched my lip, which was the wrong thing to do. She looked away.

“I shouldn’t have grabbed you.”

“I was going to follow her.”

I did not know what to say.

Lira rubbed her hands together. “You should have grabbed me. I still hated it.”

I moved closer, leaving enough room between us for Nerla to return. Lira did not move away.

Later, when the hall was quiet enough that I could hear the fire draw, she spoke again.

“Bran had a packet.”

I turned towards her.

“In his chest. With the wax and the little stamps.”

“What packet?”

“Dark wool. A stopper inside. I saw it when I was copying. He shut the drawer before I could move it.”

I remembered the narrow bundle beneath the upper tray, his hand passing over it while he searched for the gloves. I had noticed it only as another thing he kept for work.

“Why are you telling me?”

“Because Eitha saw his hand.”

I went cold. “Who told you?”

“Eitha.”

“When?”

“While you were at the ford. I asked her why her brother had come.”

Lira watched me carefully. “She wanted someone else to know.”

I understood that she meant someone besides the people making arrangements for her. I had been one of those people. I could not resent Eitha for wanting Lira and still found that I did, briefly, meanly, before fear crowded it out.

“The packet might be anything,” I said.

“Yes.”

“He uses things for mending.”

“Yes.”

“Why does it matter?”

“Because when I went for the old plan today, it wasn’t there.”

I listened to the fire.

“Did you look beneath the tray?”

“Yes.”

“The other drawer?”

“Yes.”

“Then you don’t know it’s missing. You know it isn’t where you saw it.”

Lira lay down. “Tell Catrane that.”

I wanted to make her sit up again, to say I was not defending him, only being careful. Instead I drew the blanket over her shoulder. She let me.

Across the hall my uncle slept in a chair beside my father. His head had fallen forward. One hand rested open on his knee. He looked exhausted and harmless, a man who had spent his life finding things when someone asked and putting them away when everyone else had gone.

I watched him until Catrane came down for her turn at the watch.

Then I got up and went to her.

Chapter 26 — The room over the gate

Catrane did not wake my uncle at once.

She took me into the kitchen and asked me to tell her what I had seen in his chest, separately from what Lira remembered. I described the narrow wrapping beneath the tray. I could not tell her its size exactly. I had been looking at my gloves. I knew it was there, dark wool against the pale wood, and that his hand had passed over it. Everything else I was tempted to add belonged to the conversation we had just had.

She listened until I stopped.

“Will you come with me in the morning?”

“To his room?”

“Yes.”

“He’ll know why.”

“I’ll tell him why.”

I looked towards the hall. He still slept in the chair. My father had woken and was rubbing his face with both hands.

“You mustn’t go alone,” Catrane said.

“You keep saying that.”

“I mean it each time.”

I had no answer. She sent me back to the fire and remained in the kitchen with Iver, who had come to relieve the men by the recess.

The hours before morning were the longest I had known since Kennet’s fever. Then I had waited for breaths, for the spoonful he might keep down, for his sister to come back with hot water. Now I watched my uncle sleep. Once he woke enough to see me looking and gave me a drowsy smile. I pretended I had been watching the fire.

At dawn, the hall smelled of bodies kept too close and wool dried too often without washing. Children woke hungry. Someone’s dog had fouled the rushes near the door. My aunt discovered that a boy had slept on the corner of her cloak and demanded to know who would pay for the torn fringe. Ordinary anger returned before anyone had the strength to meet it.

Catrane asked Bran to show us his workroom. She did it in front of my father, with Hethen beside her and Massen within hearing.

“What for?” Bran asked.

“To see the things you used in the lower work.”

He looked at me.

I kept my eyes on his face. He had always been able to make me look away when I was hiding something. This time he looked away first, towards the yard door.

“Of course,” he said. “It’s cold up there.”

“We’ll take a lamp.”

“I can bring the papers here.”

“I’d like to see the room.”

My father put down the bread he had been eating. “What is this?”

“Something that needs looking at,” Catrane said.

“That tells me nothing.”

“I’ll tell you what we find.”

“It’s my brother’s room.”

Bran touched his arm. “Let them look.”

The gesture quieted my father. I remembered how often Bran had done that for me, intervening before an argument became a punishment. I followed them outside with my hands inside my sleeves.

Snow had settled properly during the night. The yard was white except where feet and hooves had churned it black. A lump on the wall resolved into the bucket someone had left upside down. The path to the gate was steep enough that we went slowly, Bran first, then Catrane, Hethen and me. Massen followed at a distance. I had not asked him to come. I was glad of him.

The workroom smelled of leather and the little brazier my uncle used when soldering. Its window admitted a grey light that made everything look abandoned. On the bench lay a buckle half repaired, a strip of harness and the heel of a wooden shoe. A child’s toy horse stood on its side, one leg newly pegged. I picked it up without meaning to.

“For Rodd,” Bran said. “He broke it before supper.”

I put it down carefully.

Catrane asked him to open the chest. He did. She looked at the upper tray, then asked what lay beneath it. Bran lifted the tray and set it on the bench.

The narrow packet was not there.

I felt no triumph. I wanted it found under a sheet of leather or a folded cloth, ordinary and harmless, so I could apologize to him for the whole cold walk.

Hethen asked what substances he kept for his work. Bran named waxes, powders for cleaning metal, a paste for sealing small cracks. He showed each as he spoke. Hethen did not taste or smell them closely. He asked which had been moved recently.

“All of them, one time or another.”

“Since the guest came.”

Bran looked at him.

“I’ve had other things to think about.”

Catrane asked about the dark wool wrapping. Bran said he had used it for a piece of soft metal that marked if rubbed against the tools. Hethen asked to see the metal.

“Used,” Bran said. “On a repair.”

“Which?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Yesterday?”

“Perhaps the day before.”

He turned towards me. “You know how many things people bring.”

I looked at the toy horse. “Yes.”

Catrane asked whether she could inspect the drawers. He stepped aside. She went through them slowly, returning each thing before lifting the next. There was no tearing apart of the room, no hidden spring under the bench. The drawers contained what drawers in a working man’s room contain: useful pieces, failed pieces, things saved because he might one day discover a use for them. A strip of embroidered ribbon had been wrapped round a broken awl. I recognized it from a sleeve I had worn at fourteen.

I had forgotten giving it to him.

In the lower drawer, beneath a sheet of scrap leather, lay a little wad of dark wool. Catrane lifted it by one corner and laid it on the bench.

Bran said nothing.

Hethen brought the lamp closer. Pale grains clung to the weave. The packet had been cut open, or torn; one edge was ragged and the other straight. There was no stopper.

“Is this the wrapping?” Catrane asked.

Lira was not there to answer. I had to lean nearer.

“The cloth looks like it.”

“Looks,” Bran said.

“Yes,” I answered. “Looks.”

Catrane folded a clean sheet beneath it and let Hethen take it. He compared nothing in front of us. He merely covered it and asked Bran where the rest might be.

“In the fire, if it was rubbish.”

The brazier held old ash. Its surface had not been stirred recently enough for me to know anything from it. Catrane looked and left it alone.

Then she asked for his stamp.

Bran’s face hardened.

“For what?”

“The passage demands.”

“They’re mine. I’ve told Brin that.”

“You admit issuing them?”

“I administer the road for my brother.”

Catrane asked again to see the stamp. He took it from the upper tray, a small matrix with a short handle polished by use. One corner of the face was damaged. I had watched him drop it years before and curse as though he had broken a finger. He had never replaced it.

“That mark is on the recent demand,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And Odran came to speak of those demands.”

“Yes.”

“And you asked him not to do so before the wedding.”

“Yes.”

His voice grew firmer with each answer. He had chosen what he would concede and seemed relieved to reach it.

“I asked a stranger not to humiliate my family in front of six houses. If that’s a crime, you can take me for it now.”

Catrane set down the matrix.

“We’re going to ask you about the cup.”

For the first time he looked at Massen in the doorway. Massen had stood there throughout, one shoulder against the frame, leaving room for anyone to pass. He straightened.

“I’ll answer below,” Bran said.

“That was my intention.”

He closed the empty lower drawer too hard. The toy horse tipped on the bench. I caught it before it fell.

We went down together. Bran locked the workroom behind him and put the key in his pocket. Catrane let him. At the foot of the stair he paused, looking over the gate towards the road. Men were still visible beyond the bend, dark against the snow. A horse shook itself, throwing white from its mane.

He turned back to the hall.

“Your father won’t bear this well,” he said to me.

“I know.”

He waited, perhaps for me to ask him to spare my father, perhaps for something else. I kept walking. Behind me the gate hinge creaked in the cold.

Chapter 27 — Before everyone

My father wanted us in his small room. Catrane refused it because there was no space for everyone who needed to speak.

“Who is everyone?” he asked.

She named Eitha, Nerla, Rusk, Lira and Hethen. My father looked at the kitchen passage, then back at her.

“We’ll have the whole house repeating it by nightfall.”

“The house is already repeating things.”

“Then give them less.”

Massen said, “Give them what was said, in front of people who can remember it.”

My father rounded on him. “You’ve given us enough men to remember anything you please.”

Massen did not answer.

I said we would use the hall. It came out more quietly than I intended. My father looked at me as though I had spoken from the wrong place.

“Brin.”

“I’ll hear it too.”

“You don’t need—”

“Yes.”

He stared at me. Then he moved the chair at the end of the table aside with his foot.

“Do it, then.”

We did not gather everyone into a circle. People remained where they had been, some turning their heads, some pretending to continue with work. Nerla sent the children to the brewhouse with two women and Eitha’s brother. Eitha spoke to him near the door, holding his sleeve until he nodded. When he went out she stood very straight.

Bran sat on the bench where he had taught me the first turn of the wedding dance. I remembered his hand between my shoulder blades, the patient counting, the way he let me accuse the musician of going too fast until I learned the step. The floor had been bare then. Now rushes hid the worn board on which my heel had caught.

Catrane began with what was known. Odran had been received alive. He had eaten from shared dishes and drunk from a cup warmed apart from the others. He had died after that cup was returned. His papers concerned recent payments and refusals in the management of the road. The substances in his cup and in the workroom wrapping had not been identified with certainty.

My father seized on that.

“Then why are we here?”

“To hear what people saw,” Catrane said.

Hethen spoke first. He did not name a poison. He described the absence of similar residue from the common jugs and the neighbouring cups, and the fact that no one else who ate and drank with Odran had suffered the same collapse. He refused my father’s demand that he say exactly what had killed him.

“I won’t give you certainty I haven’t got.”

“Convenient,” my father said.

Hethen looked tired rather than offended. “For no one.”

Rusk described where he had stood. He did not look at Bran while doing it. When he finished, Bran asked whether he had seen anything put into the cup.

“No.”

“Then you’ve seen me fetching wine, as I have done in this hall for thirty years.”

“Yes,” Rusk said. “That’s what I said.”

Eitha spoke next. Her voice was low at first. Nerla moved nearer but did not touch her. Catrane asked her to repeat only the part the farther end of the table had missed.

She described the hand over the cup and the movement into the coat.

Bran waited until she finished.

“You were sent from the high table.”

“Yes.”

“By Brin.”

“Yes.”

“Because you weren’t attending to your work.”

“No,” I said.

He looked at me.

“I was wrong to send her away. She hadn’t neglected it.”

A small movement passed through the people listening. Eitha kept her eyes on the table.

Bran leaned back. “Then she had reason to be angry.”

“With me,” I said. “She hasn’t accused me.”

“I haven’t said she accuses anyone. She saw a hand. Mine, she thinks. Over a cup. She doesn’t know what was in it.”

“It was your hand,” Eitha said.

He turned towards her. “You’ve good eyes when you choose.”

Nerla put her palm flat on the table.

“Speak to her properly.”

She put the bar on the table. A flake of iron fell from it.

“That fill is carrying your chimney.”

He looked towards the hearth, then upwards. I saw the rooms arrange themselves in his mind as they had in mine. The bed, the chest, the old roof above them. He shook his head once, refusing the image.

“You repaired it.”

“The flue. I repaired the flue.”

His face had gone grey.

Bellan fetched his mother’s water and set it beside her. She drank so quickly that some ran down her chin. He wiped it with his sleeve, and she let him.

Chapter 32 — The answer beneath the hearth

Druven would not promise destruction any more than she would promise safety.

“We might shore it,” she said. “Given timber, sound footings and time. I haven’t the timber. The ground outside is washing away. And I won’t send my son under that corner to discover whether time is what we lack.”

My father asked about the beams in the byre.

“Then where do the beasts go?”

“Outside.”

“And the people you’ve put in there?”

He looked away.

We crowded round the kitchen table, well clear of the recess. Druven had refused to discuss the work beneath the chimney; my father had followed her to the table without interrupting his objections. He stood at its end. Spilled water had begun to wrinkle the drawing between us.

Druven showed us what could be taken out first without disturbing the load. A facing stone at the lower opening; the later rubble behind it; enough to see the old threshold. Then there would come a point where she could no longer be certain that what she removed was merely blocking a way. She pressed her finger on the drawing and kept it there.

“Before this,” she said, “anyone upstairs is out. Anything you want from those rooms is out. We don’t stop to argue about a chest.”

I thought of my gown, wrapped on the bed.

“And the stones?” Lira asked.

“The pieces must be seen in place. I won’t fit them by a drawing.”

Lira had brought the outer stone from Catrane’s room. It lay wrapped beside the plaster fragment. The inner piece remained upstairs. When she spoke of fetching it, my father objected that it might release something else.

“Leaving it has done well,” she said.

He looked at her, then lowered his eyes to the table.

Catrane asked what we knew, rather than what we hoped. It was a good question and I hated it. Nothing we knew said that putting the threshold pieces where they had been would end anything.

“Could we take them away?” Massen asked.

Lira looked at the wrapped stone. “Odran took this one away from where it lay. I put the other above the old door. We’ve been moving them about and saying it would help.”

“We could leave,” my father said.

Everyone looked at him.

“We can take the people out. Close the house. Let winter have it.”

For one moment I wanted to accept them, wanted the road and any room where I had never heard my mother’s cough.

Nerla said, “We can take them where?”

“The cottages. The lowlanders’ cart.”

“Some. Not all.”

“Then farther.”

“Before dark?”

My father looked towards the window. The light had already begun to thin. He had spent the morning watching his brother admit a murder and the afternoon trying to make the house obey him. There were not enough hours left for either.

Orsett had asked for space beside the byre to unload. His men were there now, moving bales from the cart, lending their shoulders to people who would not have spoken kindly of them two days before. I could hear them laughing at a horse which refused to go under the low lintel. The animal had more sense than all of us.

“We put people under sound roofs tonight,” I said. “Then we see what can be opened.”

My father rubbed his face. “You say it as though roofs grow.”

“No. We go and ask.”

I looked at Massen. He nodded, already thinking of names. That was one of the things I loved in him: the moment a problem became particular enough to act on, he stopped spending breath on what it should have been.

Catrane said she would speak to Orsett about keeping his help separate from any lasting claim. My father began to object. She told him he could come with her and hear every word. He agreed after making it seem his idea.

Lira and I went upstairs for the inner stone.

The bridal room smelled of cold apples and old smoke. The gown lay where I had left it. I stood beside the bed while Lira knelt at the board behind the chest. She worked the loose nails with the point of a small tool Druven had given her. I held the lamp. Neither of us hurried.

The piece beneath was larger than I had expected, dark stone with one smooth edge and one broken face. She lifted it in both hands. Dust remained on the floor in the shape it had occupied.

I waited for a noise, a breath, water over our feet. Nothing happened.

Lira wrapped it in the cloth she had brought. “Carry the lamp.”

I wanted to offer to take the stone, but her grip was sure. We went down together. At the first turn she paused and looked back at the room.

“What?” I whispered.

“The dress.”

“We’ll come for it.”

She looked at me a moment longer, then continued down.

I followed. At the bottom she set the stone beside the other piece. Druven turned them on the cloth. Both had curved, worn edges and a shallow channel, but their broken faces would not meet.

“Two sides of a way,” Lira said, tracing the old writing on the wrapping rather than touching the stone. “Not one whole doorstep.”

Druven moved them apart. “Then we’ll look for the beds they came from.”

Outside, someone began hammering a loose board back onto the cart. The sound reached us steady and practical through the kitchen wall. For a little while I listened to it without hearing anything underneath.

Chapter 33 — Whose beds

We began with the byre because it was nearest, and because everyone agreed that someone could sleep there until we asked who.

The beasts had warmed it all day. Steam rose from their coats in the lantern light. Their breath, the hay, the manure beneath fresh straw: the smell was thick enough to feel against the back of my throat, but the roof was sound and the wind did not come through at floor level. Orsett’s people had hung one of their canvases across the empty end. A woman named Selvene stood on a feed bin securing the top edge while two men argued about whether the rope would hold if a cow leaned on it.

“It isn’t for the cow to lean on,” she said.

“One might.”

“Then move the cow.”

She pulled the knot tight and came down. My father had spent an hour asking how long the canvas would remain his to use. Selvene asked where the people were.

“Not all ready,” I said.

“Get the ones who are. We can work round them.”

I went back to the hall.

The first household I asked had a grandmother who could not bear the draught. The second had a child frightened of cattle. The third had paid for lodging in the village before coming up and wanted to know why they should leave it to the people arriving late. I had meant to arrange bodies under roofs. Each body brought someone who knew why that roof would not do.

Nerla let me exhaust the easy answers before taking my arm.

“Ask who has slept there before.”

We found three men who had spent lambing nights in the byre and knew the warmest wall. One brought his old mother willingly once he saw she could be near the brazier and far from the beasts. A child who had been afraid of cattle became less afraid when Bellan offered to show her how the calf drank. Her mother accepted the place while she watched. Two servants went without being asked, which made me angry until I discovered they preferred a corner together to being scattered among their employers.

I apologized for trying to separate them. They looked puzzled by the apology and continued carrying their bundles.

In the brewhouse there was room for fewer people than I remembered. The vats had not grown, but in my memory they had occupied only the space necessary for a woman to step between them. Blankets needed more. Harn had been put near the wall, where he could watch both doors. He would not sleep behind a hanging cloth. When someone tried to make him comfortable by drawing it, he cried out and knocked over his water.

I took the cloth down.

The man who had hung it said Harn would keep everyone awake if he could see people moving.

“Then let him keep them awake.”

He stared at me. I was too tired to make the words kinder.

Outside, Massen was assigning his men to carrying. He stood with his injured arm held close, the sleeve cut wide enough not to press the dressing. He pointed with his good hand towards the cottages, the yard, the bedding being carried down. A few of the men looked at one another before obeying. Their shields were stacked by the gate. To reach them they would have to leave someone else’s blankets in the snow.

One man asked what would happen if the Dorns came down the road while they were occupied.

Massen looked at the old woman being helped past him.

“Then they’ll find us doing this.”

The man did not like the answer. He took the woman’s other arm anyway.

At Thessa’s cottage the warmth struck me first, then the lack of space. Eitha slept behind the blanket near the hearth. Two children sat on the table because there was nowhere to put their feet on the floor without someone stepping on them. Carlen was splitting kindling outside. Thessa’s husband had come back with the flock and found his bed promised to a stranger.

He was not gracious about it. Nor did he take it back.

Thessa listened while I explained that there might be more people to bring down. She kept turning a cake on the hearthstone, judging its underside with the edge of her knife.

“How many?”

“Four here, if—”

“No.”

“Two?”

“One on the settle. Someone who can get up in the night without climbing over the children.”

I looked at the settle. It had been where Massen sat drying his sleeve after the ford. I had thought the cottage roomy then.

“Iver?” I asked. “He can help with the door.”

Thessa considered, then nodded. “He’ll carry water with the hand he’s got.”

“He will.”

“You can’t promise for him.”

“I’ll ask.”

She turned the cake. It was blackened in one patch. She cut the patch off, ate it herself and gave me the good part. I had not eaten since the morning.

Across the ford, Muirne let me stand in her doorway while she considered the same question. I did not bring up the shelter payment. I did not tell her she owed us nothing. Both would have made her kindness another performance for me.

“Lira,” she said at last. “She can have the bed. I’ll sit up.”

“You needn’t give the bed.”

“I wasn’t offering it to you.”

I felt myself flush. “I’ll ask her.”

“And the dead man?”

“We mean to bring him out before the work.”

“Bring him here.”

I looked past her. There was scarcely room to turn between table and bed.

“The lean-to is cold and dry,” she said. “We can put him there until his daughter answers. He won’t be among strangers.”

I thanked her. She accepted the thanks without making it easier for me.

On the return crossing, snow struck the surface of the water and vanished. I watched a clump cling for a moment to the sheltered side of a stone before the current took it. Above the bank, smoke rose from houses I had looked at all my life without wondering how many people each could hold.

At the gate, my father was arguing over a cartload of meal.

“It is mine,” he said.

Orsett stood beside the horse. “Then say where you want it taken. It cannot remain beneath the tower if the corner falls.”

“I didn’t say it should.”

“You haven’t said where it should go.”

My father saw me and demanded that I tell the captain we had stores enough for our own people. I looked at the sacks, at the snow beginning to soak the outer cloth.

“Brewhouse loft,” I said. “The boards are sound.”

Orsett asked how much they would bear. I did not know. He sent Selvene to ask Druven before they lifted anything up.

My father turned away from both of us. A sack had slipped against the cart rail. He pushed it back with his shoulder, leaving a white smear of meal on his good coat.

Chapter 34 — The things in the chest

Nerla had already packed my wedding chest.

I discovered this when I tried to move it. Two men had come upstairs with me to carry down whatever could be saved before the room was closed. One took the iron handle and made a sound of surprise.

“What’s in it?”

“Linen.”

“Wet linen?”

I knelt to open it. On top lay a quilt I had never seen, patched from pieces of brown and blue wool. Beneath it were three blankets, a child’s cloak, two sacks of spare hose and the bedding from the small room where the kitchen girls slept.

I stared at it.

Nerla stood behind me with an armful of clothes.

“Their room may go too.”

“I know.”

“They couldn’t carry it all loose.”

“I know.”

She waited until I looked up.

“Your sheets are in the little chest downstairs.”

I had been ready to ask that. The fact that she knew embarrassed me more than the question itself would have.

The men lifted the big chest. They had to turn it on its side to get it past the narrow stair, and something inside shifted with a heavy slide. I followed them down two steps, making useless suggestions, until one asked me to move out of the light. I went back into the room.

My gown lay on the bed.

I unrolled enough of the sheet to see the lace. There was a little grey dust on one sleeve. I brushed it off, then kept brushing at a place which would not come clean because it was a shadow in the weave. The room had taken so much preparation: the flue, the bedding, the apples moved, the shutters eased. I had wanted the window open after the wedding night, wanted to wake beneath that old embroidered canopy with Massen beside me and no one entitled to ask why.

I put my face into the cloth. It smelled of rosemary and my own skin from the fitting. I cried without making much sound, irritated by the tears even while they came. No one interrupted me.

When I lifted my head, Lira stood at the door. She had a basket on her arm and the thick shawl wrapped firmly round her shoulders.

“I came for the apples.”

I wiped my face with the back of my wrist. “Take them.”

She set down the basket, came to the bed and helped roll the gown. We worked as we had before, this time without trying to keep every fold perfect. She tied the sheet with two strips torn from an old ribbon.

“It’s still a dress,” she said.

“I know.”

“You can wear it.”

I nearly asked where. Instead I took one end and she took the other. Together we brought it down to the small chest where Nerla had put my sheets. There was room if I took the sheets out. I stood holding them, unable to decide which thing deserved the dry space.

Lira laughed, very softly.

“What?”

“You’ve been telling everyone to choose all afternoon.”

I laughed too, and then had to sit because the laughter had come too close to crying. We packed the gown loosely on top. The lid would not shut. I left it open.

Room by room, the house became untidy in a new way. Cupboards stood empty with their doors thrown back. Old marks emerged on walls where furniture had hidden them: the pale rectangle behind a mirror, a child’s scratched line beside a bedpost, the dark smear where a lamp had once burned too close. I found a comb beneath my mother’s old chest and recognized it before touching it. Half its teeth were missing. She had used it anyway because she liked the weight of the handle.

I put it in my pocket beside Kennet’s letter.

My father wanted the painted board of his father brought down. The men had already carried four loads and were arguing over who had been given the heavier end each time. They looked at the board, then at the chest of books beside it.

“Those too,” he said.

“There are people waiting for their blankets,” I told him.

“This will take a moment.”

“Then you can bring it.”

The men fell silent. My father looked at me, astonished.

I wished I had spoken differently. I did not take it back. He lifted the board from its hook himself. Dust fell over his sleeve. It was heavier than he expected; I stepped forward before remembering I had told him to do it. He shifted it against his shoulder and carried it to the stair.

One of the men followed to help with the turn. My father let him.

Near dusk I found my aunt in Bran’s workroom. Catrane had kept the wrappings and the stamp; the rest remained. My aunt had brought the toy horse from the hall and was turning its newly mended leg.

“He’ll ask for it,” she said, meaning Rodd.

“Take it.”

“Should I?”

I looked at the small wooden head, the mane marked with shallow knife cuts. “I don’t know.”

She put it in her basket. Then she picked up the embroidered strip wrapped round the broken awl.

“Was this yours?”

“Yes.”

“He kept everything.”

I took the strip from her. The embroidery was clumsy. I had been proud of it once. I set it back on the bench, with the awl it had protected.

My aunt watched me. “He’s frightened.”

“So is Lira.”

“I know.”

“You heard what he wanted.”

“I know.”

She left with the basket pressed against her. I stayed long enough to close the drawer properly; a length of thread had caught in it. Then I went out and locked the workroom, putting the key with the others at my waist. Catrane had given it to me to keep. I could feel the new weight against my hip with every step down.

Chapter 35 — The first night outside

We did not get everyone out before dark.

By the time the last safe loads had been carried down, three old people and a woman with a sick child remained in the hall, together with those watching Bran and those too stubborn to admit they needed a bed. The cottages could take the old people, but the path had iced where water crossed it. Orsett’s cart was on the wrong side of the ford. Moving it after dark risked a wheel, a horse and whoever went into the water to free them.

My father proposed waiting until morning. Hethen said the sick child should not spend another night near the recess. The child’s mother said she would carry him herself. She had been awake for most of two nights and could barely stand straight.

Massen called for a door to be taken off its hinges.

My father objected before asking which door.

“The store room,” Massen said. “Four men. Bedding underneath him. We lower him down the path.”

“The door’s oak.”

“Yes.”

It was too heavy. They tried lifting it and discovered this without Druven needing to tell them. Bellan fetched two long poles and a strip of canvas. Selvene showed them a way to roll the edges so the poles could not slip free. They tested it with a sack, then with a man who lay down grinning until they lifted him. The grin vanished as he realized he was depending on four people’s hands.

I watched them take the child. The mother walked beside him, one hand on the canvas, repeating that she was there every few steps. No one told her he could see her.

We carried the old people next, those willing to be carried. The oldest man refused until my father said he would go in the same fashion, and then complained that the laird was making a spectacle of himself. My father walked behind him down the path. I loved him fiercely for those few minutes and was angry that I could.

The kitchen emptied slowly. The fire remained banked because we needed warmth for the people keeping watch, and water for Hethen. Nerla would not leave it until another pair of hands took her place. I took the spoon from her and told her to go to the brewhouse.

“You’ve burned porridge before.”

“This isn’t porridge.”

“You’ve burned other things.”

“Then take it with you.”

She lifted the pot with Bellan’s help. I followed them to the door and watched the steam rise into the cold. Without the smell of food the kitchen seemed already abandoned.

I spent the first part of the night in the byre. Massen had been ordered there by Hethen, who wanted his wound warm and supported. He sat on an upturned box, trying to cut bread with one hand. When I offered help he gave me the knife without argument. That frightened me more than his pallor.

“Does it hurt?”

“Yes.”

I cut the bread and sat beside him. Beyond the canvas a cow shifted, the chain at her neck giving a soft clink. Children slept against their mothers. One of Orsett’s men snored with his mouth open, head tipped back against a sack. Someone had draped a spare cloth across his chest to catch the draught.

Massen ate slowly. I watched him swallow, remembering his hand in my hair. We had not spoken of that evening since the road.

“I can’t do tomorrow with this arm,” he said.

“What do you mean?”

“Lift. Hold a beam. Pull someone out.”

“I know.”

He looked at me. “You keep forgetting.”

I had. Each time a task needed someone strong and willing, I pictured him doing it before remembering the sling.

“You can keep the lower path clear.”

“From whom?”

“From us. From anyone who thinks a cart should stand where people need to walk. From men who decide to argue while someone is being carried.”

He considered this, then nodded.

“I can shout.”

“You needn’t always.”

“No. But I can.”

I smiled. He leaned his head against the wall and closed his eyes. I stayed beside him until his breathing changed. The bread remained in his hand. I took it gently, wrapped it and put it where he could reach it when he woke.

Outside, the stars showed between passing clouds. The hall looked larger with so few windows lit. I could see the bridal room shutter, closed now, and the narrow receiving-room window below. A lamp burned there. My aunt sat inside with him. The guards’ shadows crossed the glass as they changed places.

Catrane stood at the yard gate. She had wrapped a blanket over her coat and was drinking something from a tin cup. She offered me a taste. It was mostly hot water, sharp with herbs and a little spirit.

“Orsett?” I asked.

“Below. His people will help tomorrow. He hasn’t been given the pass.”

“Does he know that?”

“He knows what I’ve agreed to. What his commander chooses to call it later will need answering later.”

I looked down towards the cottages. Their lights shone through the bare branches.

“We can’t put them all back where they were.”

“No.”

I drank again. Catrane let me finish the cup.

Near midnight the wind turned. It brought a smell of damp ash from the hall, though the chimney still drew. A voice called once from an upper window. I did not recognize it. I was grateful for that until I saw the man beside the gate lift his head as though someone had touched him.

I took his sleeve.

“Come and help me fetch more fuel.”

He looked at me, bewildered, then towards the window. I kept hold of him until he came away.

Chapter 36 — What could be returned

At first light Muirne came up with two neighbours and a handcart.

She had brought blankets for Odran and clean boards to lay across the cart. She had also brought Iver’s cloak, brushed as she had promised. He took it with an awkwardness that amused her enough to soften her face.

“It didn’t improve with brushing,” she said. “But I did it.”

He thanked her and put it on at once.

We carried Odran out through the main door. I had wanted the household present, then realized that wanting everyone to witness our care was too close to what my father might have wanted. Those who were there came. Nerla tucked the cover around his feet. Muirne examined the knot without criticism and adjusted one corner against the wind.

His chest went separately, locked, with Catrane’s copy of the contents kept apart. The stones remained with us. Catrane had explained to Muirne why, and Muirne had asked to see where they would go when there was a safe way to show her. We promised only to tell her when that was possible.

As the cart began to move, my father came down the steps.

“Wait.”

I thought he meant to make a speech. He stood beside the body, looking at the white cover, and could find nothing. At last he took off his cap. We waited with him. Then he stepped back and the cart went on.

I watched until the gate hid it.

Druven was already at the lower wall. She had marked the stones she meant to remove with chalk and those she did not want touched with a smear of mud. The distinction was plain enough that no one could pretend to misunderstand. Bellan readied wedges on a dry board. Selvene and two local men lifted loose facing stones away from the burn. Each stone was carried uphill and set clear of the path, not thrown where somebody would later have to climb over it.

The outer threshold appeared under a shallow layer of rubble. Its bed was worn smooth along the middle and broken at one end. Druven fitted Odran’s stone against it. The piece settled without rocking.

She did not speak. She looked at the water running past it, then at the fill above.

I crouched beside her. From here the hall’s wall rose close and sheer. I had never seen its underside. There were roots in joints I had thought solid from above, and bits of wood buried where I had expected only stone. A hair-thin trickle ran from one seam, darkening the chalk on the rock below.

“Can I help?”

“Keep people off the bank above us.”

I went up. At the top I found my father preparing to come down with three men and a bar. I told him what Druven had said. He looked at the bar, then at the slope beneath his boots.

“Ask her where she wants this.”

He handed it to me. I took it down and brought back her answer: leave it by the dry board; she would call for it.

The work took most of the morning. We did not stand watching every stone. There were fires to feed, people to visit, a cart to move out of the way before Massen shouted at its driver. I carried water to the lower team and brought empty vessels back. My cut finger reopened under the handle. Hethen saw the blood and wrapped it more firmly, telling me I was to carry with the other hand. I obeyed until the next bucket was too heavy, then found Bellan and asked him to take one side.

At midday Druven sent for Lira.

The inner stone’s bed had been found at the top of the old steps, beneath a later sill. Lira and Druven carried the piece there together while I held the light. It fitted imperfectly: an old edge had broken away, leaving a gap as wide as my thumb. Druven filled beneath it with small sound pieces so it could not tilt under a foot. She did not carve new marks or ask Lira for words to mend what was missing.

The air changed while they worked.

I noticed it first because I could smell the apples upstairs again. Then smoke, not from our fire, and wool drying too close to heat. Somewhere beyond the turn a child complained that a shoe hurt. The voice was distant and ordinary. Lira closed her eyes.

“Keep looking at me,” I told her.

She opened them. “I’m listening.”

“I know.”

“I can listen without going.”

I nodded and held the lamp steady.

When the stone was seated, Druven stood back. The passage remained the same length. Nothing touched us.

“We haven’t opened the far end,” Druven said.

We went out before anyone could decide to try the bolt again.

In the yard Massen had cleared a route from the lower opening to the path by the burn. There would be people on both banks with lamps, and no cart, beast or pile of salvage in the way. He had sent the last of his men at the ford to help Selvene carry timber. He watched them go without calling one back.

My father came to stand beside me.

“If it falls,” he said, “the roof over your room may go with it.”

“I’ve taken what I could.”

“There are things in the walls you can’t take.”

I looked at him. He was looking at the house, not at me. For once he was not asking me to comfort him. I stood beside him until Druven called us both away from the edge.

Part V — The open door

Chapter 37 — The last loads

We meant to move Bran before the last work began.

I say that because afterwards people asked why he had still been in the hall, and because I asked it too, often enough to wear away the difference between what we had planned and what we had done. Catrane had arranged a place in the gate room, away from the chimney and beside the road by which he would eventually be taken out. Orsett offered two men to sit with ours. My father objected to their taking custody. Catrane said sitting was not taking custody, and made him repeat that distinction in front of them until he was too weary to object again.

The gate room had to be emptied first. Harness, a broken wheel, two sacks of old grain, the blue-wrapped spit Torm had brought me: everything went somewhere else. The sacks were full of mouse tunnels. My father accused the porter of neglecting them; the porter said they had been there since before he came. No one pursued it. We were running out of places for things that had once been easy to put away.

I brought blankets to the gate room and checked the window. A person could not get through it without taking out stone. The door opened onto the covered way, where people passed constantly. It seemed as safe as anything we had left.

On my way back up, I met my aunt carrying a coat and Bran’s shoes.

“He’ll need these,” she said.

“Yes.”

She looked past me towards the road. Rodd was in the brewhouse with his father. She had made certain of that before coming up; she told me twice, as though I might think she had chosen her brother over her child.

“Will they hurt him?” she asked.

“Not if he goes quietly.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

It was not. I told her Catrane would be there and Hethen would see his wrists again. She nodded. We walked the rest of the way together, slower than I would have walked alone.

Inside, the hall was stripped enough to echo. The benches stood along the far wall. Empty hooks marked the places where cloaks and lamps had hung. The high table remained because it was too heavy to move without taking men from more useful work. At its end lay the small wooden horse. I put it into my aunt’s basket; it had been taken out and left there when she came to see Bran the previous night.

She touched its pale new leg.

“Thank you.”

Catrane was speaking with the two guards. Bran sat where he had sat before, hands bound but no longer fastened to the chair. They had loosened that line so he could stand while his coat was put on. Hethen examined his fingers and said the circulation was sound.

My uncle saw me and looked away.

After everything, it hurt to have him refuse my face.

I went to the kitchen, where Lira and Druven were gathering what they needed for the opening. The inner stone was in place. The outer one had been seated, and loose rubble removed around it. A thin wall of packed stone still blocked the passage behind the old door. Druven would remove the remaining support only when everyone was ready, and then the bolt would have to be lifted from within because no one could reach its fastening through the lower fill.

“Can we lift it with a bar from the kitchen?” I asked.

“I can reach it from the turn,” she said. “Someone has to hold the lamp and keep the upper way clear. If the stone above starts to move, we come out at once.”

“Before it’s open?”

“Yes.”

She looked at me until I nodded.

Lira had tied back her hair with a strip of plain linen. Her face was pale but steady. I asked whether she wanted to be below with Massen instead.

“I want to see the keeper lift it.”

“That isn’t a reason to stay in danger.”

“No. It’s why I want to stay.”

Druven handed her a lamp. “Stand where I put you. If I tell you out, out.”

Lira agreed. I held my tongue.

A dull knock came through the floor. Dust sifted from the top of the hatch. We all looked up.

Bellan came in from the yard, breathless.

“A facing stone moved. No one under it. Mam, come.”

Druven went with him. I followed to the door. Outside, the men below the wall had stopped. One was rubbing his shoulder where a small fragment had struck it. Selvene held people back while Druven examined the opening. The whole house seemed to listen.

Behind me, someone cried out in the hall.

I turned.

One of the guards had fallen against the chair. My aunt stood beside Bran, the coat half on him. The rope from his wrists lay on the floor.

For a moment I saw the scene without understanding it: the open coat, the chair sideways, my uncle’s hand gripping the short wooden stick he had used for his knee. Then he struck the second guard across the face and ran for the kitchen passage.

“Bran!” my aunt shouted.

He did not stop.

Lira stood at the foot of the women’s stair, holding the lamp. She turned at the sound. My uncle caught her above the elbow and pushed her into the stair opening.

I went after them.

Chapter 38 — The door’s outward swing

I heard Catrane behind me, calling for the yard to be held clear. The words reached me without meaning. I could see Lira’s lamp moving up the stair, its light jerking across the wall as Bran forced her ahead of him.

He had chosen the stair because Druven and the men were outside, because the lower opening was watched, because he knew the house as well as I did. At the first turn, the old passage ran behind the bridal wall. There was a small door there, opening onto the landing, which we had used for storing empty baskets. Beyond it, before the wall was filled, another stair had gone down towards the refuge.

I had hidden in that cupboard as a child. Bran had found me there once with a stolen piece of cake. He had taken half and not told my mother.

Lira tried to turn on the third step. He drove her against the wall. The lamp struck stone and broke. Oil ran down the steps; its little flame went out under his shoe.

“Leave her,” I said.

He looked back, startled by how close I was.

“Go outside, Brin.”

Lira pulled against his grip. He twisted her arm behind her and she cried out. I stopped because I could see how easily he could hurt her more.

“You can’t do it through there,” I said. “It’s filled.”

“It wasn’t filled to the top.”

He knew. He had known all this time.

“Druven’s opening the lower way.”

“That is what we must stop.”

He spoke with the urgent patience he had used when I was a child about to put my hand near a blade. I wanted to scream at him for it.

“She’s opening it,” I repeated. “Lira doesn’t have to go anywhere.”

“The house needs someone outside.”

“Then go.”

His face changed. He pulled Lira towards the cupboard door. The stick lay against the wall where he had dropped it to manage her with both hands. I could reach it if I moved quickly. I could also strike her by mistake, or lose my footing on the oil and bring us all down the stair.

Lira looked at me. Her eyes went to the door.

It opened outwards. The hinges were on her side, the latch on mine. If he pulled it open while holding her, he would have to step back towards the stair or pin her against the wall.

I moved one step up.

He reached for the latch.

Lira put her foot against the bottom of the door. He pulled harder. For a moment both his hands were occupied: one on her arm, one on the latch. I seized the stick and drove its end against his knee.

I did not strike neatly. The wood glanced off bone and hit the edge of the step. The shock ran into my cut finger. Bran shouted, turned towards me and loosened his grip just enough for Lira to wrench her arm free.

She came down the step against me. I caught her badly. My back struck the wall. Bran reached for her again.

I caught the edge of the door.

It had opened a hand’s breadth. I stepped down and hauled it out across the landing, catching him between its edge and the stair rail. He tried to brace with the leg I had struck. It failed. The rail, old and worn where thousands of hands had rested, broke at its lower fastening.

He fell sideways, one leg caught beneath the door.

The sound he made was nothing like his shouting. It was a high, bewildered cry. I kept pushing until I realized that Lira was free and that I was still hurting him.

I stopped.

For a moment none of us moved. My uncle lay on the landing, his face against the wall, one arm twisted beneath him. His foot pointed the wrong way. I could hear him trying to breathe.

Catrane reached us with the uninjured guard. She took the stick from my hand. I had forgotten I still held it.

“Back,” she told me.

I could not move. I was looking at his shoe, the familiar patch at its side. My aunt had brought it for him ten minutes before.

Lira touched my shoulder. I stepped down with her. The guard went past us and knelt beside Bran, keeping his hands visible and speaking his name until he answered.

Behind the half-open cupboard door, someone moved.

I saw a table through a space too narrow to contain one. A bowl stood near its edge. The keeper reached for it, then stopped, her head turning towards my uncle on the floor.

“Close it,” Lira whispered.

The guard could not move Bran’s leg without lifting the door. Catrane looked at the broken rail, the stair, the dark space beyond. Then she told us exactly what to do, one instruction at a time. Lira held the door steady. I pushed a folded cloak beneath Bran’s shoulder. The guard supported his leg while Catrane eased the edge away.

Bran cried out again. I felt his breath hot against my wrist.

We moved him clear. Lira shut the door.

The latch would not catch. She held it with both hands until the guard brought the fallen stick and wedged it through the handle against the wall.

My uncle looked at me.

“Brin.”

I knelt beside him. I could not bring myself to touch his face.

“Hethen’s coming.”

He swallowed. “My leg.”

“I know.”

“Don’t let them—”

“Lie still.”

He closed his eyes. A tear ran across the bridge of his nose towards the floor. I wiped it with the edge of the cloak because it was there and I could reach it.

Hethen came with two men and a carrying canvas. Behind him my aunt climbed as far as the first turn. She saw Bran’s foot and sat down on the step. No one asked her why she had loosed the rope yet. There was no room for the answer.

Chapter 39 — The person on the canvas

Hethen made me leave the landing while he set the leg.

I stood at the bottom of the stair with Lira. Her arm was reddening where Bran had gripped it. She held it against her body without seeming aware of the posture. I asked whether she could move her fingers. She did, slowly.

“Shoulder?”

“Yes.”

I took her to the kitchen bench. She sat, and for the first time since the chase I felt how badly my own legs were shaking. I sat beside her before they gave way.

Above us Bran cried out. Lira flinched. I put my hands over my ears, then took them away. The next cry came more faintly.

“You stopped him,” she said.

I looked at the blood on my bandage.

“I know.”

She leaned against me, carefully, with her unhurt shoulder. We stayed that way until someone called for water.

The house gave another low knock. A little plaster fell from the passage ceiling. Druven came in from the yard and looked up, then at the stairs.

“Who is still above?”

I told her.

“Get them down.”

“Hethen’s—”

“Get them down now.”

I went to the stair. Hethen had finished binding the leg between padded lengths of wood. He looked at Druven behind me and needed no further explanation. They lifted Bran onto the canvas and began the descent.

The turn was too narrow. They had to raise one end, lower the other, stop while the guard shifted his feet. Bran clutched the edge of the canvas. The knot at his wrist had been replaced; Catrane had not forgotten him even while Hethen worked. My aunt stood against the wall as they passed. He did not look at her.

At the bottom, one carrier stumbled on the spilled lamp oil. I caught his sleeve with my good hand. The canvas tilted and steadied. My uncle’s eyes opened.

“Thank you,” he whispered.

I let go of the sleeve.

They carried him outside. My father stood in the yard. He looked at his brother, then at me, then at the stick Catrane held. He seemed about to ask who had done it. I answered before he could.

“I did.”

He turned away to follow the canvas.

I could not tell whether that was mercy.

Catrane gave the stick to one of the guards and asked my aunt to sit beside the kitchen door, where she could be seen. Then she came to me.

“He’ll live if the injury stays clean. Hethen thinks the foot has blood in it.”

I nodded.

“Your aunt loosened his hands to put the coat on. One guard was called to the door when the stone shifted outside. The other had hold of the rope. Bran struck him.”

“Will you take her too?”

“She’ll give an account. Today we keep people alive.”

I looked towards the gate. The carriers had reached the turn. Orsett met them there and sent his cart towards the brewhouse instead; the gate room had too little space to turn a man on a canvas without moving him twice. Catrane watched, then sent a guard after them with instructions about custody.

Lira came out of the kitchen. Her face was set.

“I’m still helping,” she said.

I began to say no. She saw it.

“You can ask whether I can hold a lamp.”

“Can you?”

“Yes.”

Druven came to stand beside us. She examined Lira’s hand without touching her arm.

“You stand above the steps,” she said. “No lifting. If you can’t hold it steady, say so.”

Lira agreed.

I looked at Druven. “Is it still safe to try?”

“No safer for waiting.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No.”

She took us round to the outer wall and showed us the new crack. It ran from the top of the blocked opening towards the later chimney, a thin black line where the chalk had broken. She could not tell whether it would stand another hour or another winter. She could tell that taking out the rest of the fill would let the old doorway move, and that whatever support it gave the chimney would be lost.

“We can walk away,” she said.

I looked uphill. From the kitchen window came the sound of a spoon tapping a bowl. Nerla was in the brewhouse. No one was in the kitchen.

Druven heard it. She did not pretend otherwise.

“We can,” she repeated.

Lira stood with her eyes on the opening. I thought of the low room entering the washhouse, of Rodd following a voice beyond a wall, of the people sleeping now in outbuildings whose doors I had once thought safely separate from the hall.

“Will it stay here?” I asked.

“I don’t know.”

We stood in the cold. The choice did not become clearer because we waited.

At last I said, “Tell me where you need me.”

Druven took a breath and began.

Chapter 40 — Where I was put

Druven put me at the head of the old steps, with a rope round my waist and the loose end in Catrane’s hands.

“You’re not tied to the house,” she said. “If I tell you out, you come. If you don’t come, she pulls.”

Catrane tested the knot. I looked at her and she said, “I will.”

Lira stood beside the kitchen doorway, holding a lamp in her good hand. A second lamp sat on the floor, protected by an upturned pot with a broken side. We had taken down the shelf above it so nothing would fall directly onto the flame. The small decisions occupied us until there was no small decision left.

Outside, Massen called along the lower path. Voices answered him at intervals: Selvene by the threshold, two men farther along the burn, Gedran at the turn towards the ford. They had cleared the way of beasts and carts. A line of lamps would show it when the light failed. Nobody was to stand in the opening itself once the last stone came free.

My father had been sent to the brewhouse with the remaining household. He went only when Nerla told him the children needed someone they knew. I had not seen him go. I was glad of that. I did not want another look from him before I went down. The porter kept the washhouse turn, where anyone coming out of the kitchen would need directing away from the bank. Bellan was to bring his mother clear, then join him and take down their lamp.

Druven took the bar and entered the recess.

I followed to my place. The air was colder than before but no longer smelled only of wet stone. I could smell cooking, something thick with grain and onions, and the sour warmth of many people sleeping in too little space. A cough sounded beyond the turn. Not my mother’s. A man’s, deep and painful.

Druven paused. I could see the back of her head beneath the cloth she had tied over her hair. A white thread had come loose and moved against her neck as she breathed.

“Still there?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Lamp higher.”

I lifted it.

She placed the bar beneath the bolt but did not lift. Outside, a stone scraped. Then another. The sound came through the passage more clearly than through the wall, as though the lower doorway were already listening to the work beyond it.

We waited for Selvene’s call. Druven had told her the exact stone after which no one was to reach into the opening again. I imagined Selvene’s hands, red with cold, the little white scars across her knuckles. I had only known her a day. She was beneath my house because I had asked what could be done.

A voice said, “Brin?”

It came from the turn beyond Druven.

I knew the voice before I knew the name. Kennet did not sound ill. He sounded mildly puzzled, the way he had when he found me in a room where he had not expected anyone to be. For four years I had tried to remember that ordinary pitch and found instead the dry whisper of the fever. Now it was here, whole enough that my body turned towards it before I could stop.

Druven said something. I did not hear her words.

“Where have you put my coat?” Kennet asked.

I had once moved it because it smelled of rain and hounds. He had searched the whole house rather than ask until he found me laughing in the kitchen. I could see his expression, offended and amused, the wet hair at his temples.

I took one step down.

The rope tightened. I put a hand to it, annoyed by the restraint. Beyond the turn, light moved across a wall. I thought I saw the edge of the old coat, the one his family had sent back to me.

“Brin.”

I wanted to say I was coming. My mouth had already formed the first sound.

Above me Lira shouted, “Jam-toes!”

She shouted it again, furious now. “Jam-toes, get back here.”

I turned. Her face was white in the doorway. We had been children when she caught me eating stolen preserve with my toes tucked beneath my nightdress, so no cold stone would touch them. She had called me that for a summer. I had hated it enough to chase her round the yard.

The rope drew tight again. Catrane had both hands on it.

I stepped back.

My knee struck the edge of the stair. Pain ran up my thigh. I put the lamp down before I dropped it and gripped the wall with my free hand.

Kennet called once more.

I did not answer. Tears ran into my mouth. I could not stop them, and had no hand free to wipe them away.

Lira said, more quietly, “Look at me.”

I did.

Druven waited until I nodded. Then she shifted the bar beneath the iron again.

From outside, Selvene called.

“Clear.”

Chapter 41 — The people at the door

The bolt lifted slowly.

Druven had both hands on the bar. Her shoulders rose, then settled as the iron moved. A little rust fell on the step. I watched it land because I could not bear to look beyond her yet.

The cold came first, but it did not press towards the kitchen this time. It moved past us, along the old way. My skirt pulled towards the turn. The lamp flame leaned with it and steadied.

The low room appeared by degrees. A wall farther away than it should have been. A patch of firelight. The table with its empty bowl. The keeper stood beneath the bolt, arms raised. Where Druven’s bar touched the iron, her hands closed around it.

They lifted together.

I saw the door move.

At first it opened only enough to show a pale line. Then the line broadened. Daylight from the burn entered the room in a shape no lamp could make. Snow lay beyond it, ordinary, blue in the shadow of the wall.

The keeper stood very still.

Someone behind her began to rise. A woman gathered a bundle from beneath the table and tucked its loose cloth under one arm. A small person reached for her skirt. She waited while the hand found a fold to hold.

I could not see faces clearly. When I tried to look straight at one, it seemed to turn or pass behind another. I remember a bare heel, a strip of red cloth tied round a wrist, the top of a head bowed beneath a burden. One man went back for a shoe. Someone near the door held out a hand for him and kept it there until he came.

The keeper moved the stool.

For an instant I thought she meant to take it away again. Instead she set it beneath the lifted bolt, as though it might help her keep the iron raised. The stool was too low. She looked at it, then at her hands. I wanted to go to her. Druven said my name without turning, and I stayed where I was.

The first people passed the threshold.

Outside, no one cried out. Massen’s instructions held. I heard a man breathe sharply and another tell him to stay back. The movement continued down the passage, past the light, out into the snow.

A woman turned twice. Each time someone touched her shoulder and she faced the doorway again. I could not tell whether she was looking for a person or at the room she had expected to remain in. The second time she turned, I saw a child asleep against her breast.

The wall above Druven gave a dry click.

She glanced up. A line of dust slid down the stone beside her face.

“Soon,” she said.

I did not know whether she spoke to me or to the people leaving.

More passed. I could feel the movement through the soles of my feet, though no footfall sounded. The room emptied unevenly. Those near the door did not always leave first. A figure remained beside the table until another came back to take its hand. Someone crouched to lift something too small for me to see.

Then there was only the keeper.

Her hands rested against the bolt. I could see the skin torn at the palms, old wounds that had been made again and again. The iron no longer pressed down on them. Druven held it with the bar, braced against the stair.

The keeper looked towards the table.

I looked too. The bowl was still there, empty.

She took it up. For the first time the movement seemed to belong to her. She held it against her body, walked to the doorway and stopped beneath the lintel.

I thought she would turn back.

Instead she stepped out.

The room went dark, then small. The far wall drew nearer until there was only the passage, the worn floor and the open door showing snow beyond it. Druven sagged against the bar.

“Out,” she said.

I turned up the steps. Catrane pulled the rope, not hard enough to drag me, enough to make me move faster than I wanted. Lira backed into the kitchen with her lamp.

The sound above us was like a great cupboard being overturned.

Druven came up behind me. I felt her hand strike my back and push. The bar clanged on stone. Something hit the step where her foot had been.

We came through the recess together, too close, shoulders catching. Catrane seized Druven’s sleeve. I stumbled against Lira and knocked the protected lamp onto its side. The oil ran inside the pot. I saw flame flare and reached for it before understanding what was happening above the ceiling.

A beam groaned.

“Leave it,” Catrane said.

She pulled me towards the yard door. Lira was already there. Druven stopped, looking back.

“Bellan?”

“He’s outside,” I said.

She did not believe me until she saw him in the doorway, shouting for her. Then she moved.

We ran into the snow.

Behind us, the chimney fell.

Chapter 42 — The room above us

It did not fall all at once.

The upper part broke towards the bridal room. The roof lifted oddly, as though someone had taken it from beneath, and then folded inward. Slates slid in a dark sheet. For a moment the embroidered bed canopy showed through the torn wall, red and brown threads hanging in the daylight. Then dust covered it.

The lower part drove into the hearth. Burning peat burst across the hall floor. The timber above had been drying beside that chimney for longer than anyone alive could remember. Fire found it before we had finished getting clear of the doorway.

Bellan caught Druven and dragged her down the path. She kept turning back towards the wall, as if she could still judge it into standing. Catrane shouted for people to leave the bank. I took Lira’s hand. She let me, gripping hard enough to hurt the cut finger.

The first wave of dust reached us warm. It smelled of old plaster, soot and something sweet from the spilled apples. I coughed and could not draw a full breath. Snow clung to my wet face. Behind the dust, the house made a long series of smaller sounds: glass breaking, wood snapping, stones rolling after the greater fall had stopped.

We reached the byre wall. Massen stood there, keeping people from running uphill. His sling had loosened. Blood marked the bandage beneath it. He saw us and counted with his eyes.

“Catrane?”

“Coming.”

“Druven?”

“With Bellan.”

He looked past me until he saw them. Then he put his good hand on the wall and bent his head for a moment.

From the brewhouse came the sound of children screaming. Their parents were trying to keep them inside while also trying to see the fire. Orsett stood in the doorway telling them where to go if sparks reached the roof. He had taken off his gloves; one hand was black with soot.

My father came out behind him. He looked up at the house and stopped so abruptly that a woman carrying bedding struck his shoulder. He moved aside without seeming to feel it.

“The upper room,” he said.

I knew which one he meant. His father’s painted board had stood there. The board had been carried down; the room had not.

“Come away,” I told him.

He kept looking.

I took his hand. It was cold and dry, the nails rimmed with stone dust. I pulled once. He came.

We moved people from the brewhouse’s uphill end, where sparks might fall, towards the cottages and the lower yard. Selvene had men throwing wet cloths over the roof nearest the hall. They did it from ladders on the safe side, not by climbing beneath the falling wall. Every few breaths someone shouted a warning and everyone looked up.

For a little while all I knew was carrying. A basket. A child who had lost a shoe. A sack that tore and left a pale trail of meal on the snow. Someone put a bundle in my arms and I brought it where they pointed without asking whose it was.

Then Druven began calling Bellan.

I had seen him with her. I had seen him put an arm round her and bring her clear. Now he was gone.

“He went for the porter,” a man said.

“Where?”

“By the washhouse.”

Druven turned uphill. Massen caught her sleeve with his good hand. She struck at him.

“Let go.”

“Show me where.”

She could not. She had not seen him go. Massen released her only when Selvene came beside them with a rope and two men.

I knew the path to the washhouse. I had walked it while the distance changed beneath my feet. I went before anyone could decide to send me elsewhere.

This time it remained the length it had always been.

The washhouse roof had caught a fallen length of timber from the hall. One end had broken through the eaves and pinned the porter against the door frame. Bellan crouched beside him, holding the weight off his shoulder with a short beam wedged under it. He had got it high enough for the porter to breathe and no farther.

“Don’t move that,” he said as we approached.

No one argued.

The porter was conscious. His face had gone grey under the soot. He said his foot was caught. Bellan said he knew and told him to stop trying to pull it out.

Selvene knelt to see the angle. Massen stood back, unable to lift. I saw the shame cross his face and vanish as he began giving the men positions instead. Druven crouched beside her son, one hand on his back.

“You’ve done right,” she said.

He looked at her. For a moment he was young enough to cry. Then he asked for another wedge.

We used the pieces carried from the lower wall. Sound stone, Druven said, not the ones split through. I fetched two. My hands were numb. The first slipped and struck my foot. I picked it up and brought it back.

They raised the timber a little, set a wedge, raised it again. The porter cried out when the pressure moved. His foot was beneath a broken sill. Selvene cut away his boot rather than pull on the ankle. Leather came free in dark strips.

Above us another section of roof shifted.

I looked up. Through a break in the smoke I could see the small chest we had left near the stair, its lid open. The sheet round my gown hung over the edge. A gust lifted it, and the white cloth showed beautifully for one instant against the blackened wood.

Then the wall hid it.

“Brin,” Massen said.

I turned. He needed me to take the porter’s freed foot while the men moved his shoulders. I knelt and put both hands beneath it, feeling the damaged weight, the warmth of blood through the stocking.

We lifted together.

Chapter 43 — The last carrying team

The porter was heavier than Odran had been, and frightened enough to make himself harder to carry.

He gripped whoever came within reach. Once he caught Bellan by the collar and nearly pulled him down. Bellan freed himself gently, saying, “I’ve got you,” until the words became no more than a sound made between breaths.

We put him on the canvas. Massen walked beside it, keeping the path clear, his face pale with pain. Druven had one hand on Bellan’s shoulder as they followed. She did not let go when he tried to move ahead.

At the yard turn my father met us. He had come back after helping people down, and stood for a moment as if unsure whether he was permitted to be useful. Selvene told him to take the rear pole. He did.

I remember his cap lying on the snow. He had dropped it to free both hands. Nobody stopped to pick it up.

We carried the porter to the lower cottages. Hethen had made a place in Thessa’s house for whoever needed him most. Iver moved his bedding out without being asked. Eitha was awake now, sitting beside the hearth. When she saw the canvas she stood and took the pot from the warmest place so Hethen could set his things there.

The room filled with wet clothes and orders. I backed out because I was in the way. On the step, I discovered that I was still holding a piece of the porter’s stocking. I did not know when it had come into my hand.

Carlen took it from me.

“Sit,” he said.

I sat on the low wall. Across the ford, Muirne’s door opened and Lira came out. She had gone down while we were at the washhouse. I was absurdly relieved to see her under a roof someone had freely offered.

She crossed carefully, carrying a cup in her good hand. Half the drink had spilled by the time she reached me. She gave me the rest.

It was hot milk with something sweet in it. I drank too fast and burned my tongue.

“Slowly,” she said.

I began to laugh. I could not stop. Nothing was funny. Lira sat beside me and waited while the laughter turned into something else. I covered my face with both hands. The cup pressed against my knee. She took it before it fell.

I cried for the house first, which embarrassed me even then. For the room, the gown, the window I had wanted open. Then for Odran and the terrible little sound Bran made when his leg broke. Then for Kennet, whose ordinary voice had come back only when it could lead me somewhere I should not go. The reasons moved through me without separating. I could not have told Lira which one I was in when she put her arm round my shoulders.

Above us the hall burned. We could see the light beyond the slope, not the flames themselves. Smoke lay low over the hill before the wind took it east. The cottages remained dark-roofed and whole.

At last I could breathe without crying. Lira gave me the cup. The milk had cooled.

“Bellan?” she asked.

“Safe.”

“Druven?”

“Safe.”

“Massen?”

“His arm’s bleeding again. Hethen will see him after the porter.”

She nodded. We sat until the cold reached through the wall beneath us.

From inside Thessa’s house came an argument about who would sleep where. Someone insisted that a chair would do; someone else said a chair would do for an hour and not a winter. The sound was so ordinary that I began to cry again, more quietly this time.

Lira took my hand and brought me indoors.

Chapter 44 — The borrowed kitchen

I slept sitting up, with my head against a wall that smelled of turnips.

When I woke, a child was trying to take off my boot. She had mistaken it for her mother’s, which stood beside it beneath the bench, and was annoyed that it would not come free. I lifted my foot. She stared at the leg attached to it and began to laugh.

I laughed with her until moving my mouth hurt. Then I remembered the stair, and stopped.

The room was still dark. A red light showed through the crack beneath the oven door. Someone lay asleep beside it, breathing heavily. Near the window, Eitha sat with a basin on her lap, washing a cloth that would not come clean. She looked up when she heard me.

“Hethen says you can have the bed when she gets up.”

“Who?”

“Thessa.”

“I can’t take her bed.”

“Tell her that. She likes an argument before breakfast.”

I smiled. Eitha wrung the cloth and laid it over the basin. Her hands were red from the water. I asked whether there was anything I could do.

“Sleep.”

“I’ve slept.”

“Then drink what’s on the shelf. Not the little brown bottle.”

The drink was cold and tasted of honey and something bitter. I drank half, discovered how thirsty I was and finished it. Through the window I could see a pale line where the road ran uphill. The light beyond the slope had faded. Smoke still moved there.

“Is it out?” I asked.

“The big flames. They won’t let anyone up yet.”

“My father?”

“Brewhouse. With your aunt and the boy.”

“Bran?”

“The cart shelter. Guards with him.”

She said his name without changing her voice. I could not do that yet.

“And Massen?”

“Hethen put him next door. He’s asleep.”

She returned to the cloth. I wanted to ask whether his wound was worse, whether his hand still moved, whether my uncle had fever, whether the porter would walk. Eitha saw the questions in my face and shook her head.

“Ask Hethen when he’s awake. I only carry water.”

“You’ve done more.”

“I know what I’ve done.”

I sat back. After a little while she gave me a clean cloth and asked me to fold it. I folded three while she washed the others. It was enough work to keep my hands occupied and too little to let me pretend I was indispensable.

Thessa rose before full daylight. She stepped carefully over the sleepers, saw me awake and told me to get into the bed before someone else claimed it. I obeyed because I no longer had strength for gratitude or protest. The bedding was warm from her body. I lay with my face towards the wall and slept without hearing anyone call.

By noon, the cottage had become a place people came to ask things. Hethen used the table when he needed light and the bed when he needed a person to lie still. Thessa cooked round him with increasing irritation. Every time he set a bandage where she meant to put a bowl, she moved it without asking. He learned to watch her hands.

The porter’s ankle was badly injured but the foot lived. Hethen said that more than once because the porter kept asking whether he would lose it. Massen had torn the edge of his wound while directing the carrying team; it needed rest now, actual rest, not a promise to avoid the heaviest work. Iver’s fingers were swollen but healing. Lira’s shoulder ached when she lifted her arm. My own finger looked ugly and hurt less than the bruises I had begun discovering elsewhere.

Hethen examined my mouth and asked again how it had happened.

“Lira hit me.”

He looked towards the door, where she stood holding a jug.

“I was frightened,” she said.

“So was I,” I added.

He nodded and gave me something to rinse with. He did not ask us to explain the rest.

Outside, the snow had become a wet crust. The first people returning from the hill carried soot-blackened objects as though they were newly made and might break if held too firmly. A pot. A hinge. The iron end of a spit. I recognized the blue wrapping on one parcel and thought Torm must have saved my gift from the gate room. Then he opened it and I saw a different bundle: the painted board of my grandfather, its lower edge scorched but the face intact.

My father took it from him. He stood looking at it for so long that Torm asked whether he should put it somewhere dry.

“Yes,” my father said. “Dry.”

He let it go.

I went up only as far as the gate. Catrane had forbidden anyone to enter the ruin until Druven judged where a person could stand. The gate itself remained, stained with smoke but sound. Beyond it, the hall had lost its familiar line against the hill. A jagged piece of wall rose where the bridal room had been. Through the gap I could see sky.

I had thought I would cry. Instead I began locating things that were no longer there: the window above the bed, the shelf with the apples, the corner where my mother’s comb had lain beneath her chest. I kept trying to put rooms into the empty air.

Druven stood beside me. Bellan was farther up with Selvene, marking a safe edge with rope. His mother watched him every few breaths.

“Do you think it’s finished?” I asked.

“The falling? No.”

“The other.”

She looked at the lower opening. It stood clear, a dark mouth above the burn, with daylight visible faintly through the turn when the sun reached it.

“I haven’t heard anything.”

“Neither have I.”

We stood a while longer. The burn moved below us, carrying ash along its edge. A bird landed on the broken wall, looked about and flew off. Druven rubbed her hands together.

“Bellan wants to go home.”

“Of course.”

“He won’t say it while there’s work.”

“Tell him there’s work at home.”

She smiled faintly. “There always is.”

I told her I would pay what was owed for the flue, and for the work since. Then I remembered that I did not know where the money was, what my father had kept, what had burned.

“I’ll find it,” I said.

Druven looked at me, not unkindly. “Find out first. Tell me after.”

That evening we ate from whatever bowls had survived the carrying. Nerla set three green cups on Thessa’s table. I touched the nearest one.

“How many?”

“Seven safe. The little one is with Catrane.”

“Eight, then.”

She nodded. I did not ask which three were gone. I could not yet bear to make their shapes in my mind.

The child beside me wanted the green cup because it was prettier than the brown bowl. I let her use it. She drank with both hands and left a crescent of milk above her lip.

Chapter 45 — The daughter’s letter

Mera’s answer reached us the next afternoon.

The messenger came down from the western road with snow packed beneath his horse’s girth and a temper worsened by people stopping him to ask what had happened to the hall. He had seen the smoke from the ridge. Catrane gave him food before asking for the letter, and he became more civil halfway through the bowl.

Mera had written in a small, upright hand. She thanked us for sending word. She asked that her father’s papers not be burned, discarded or given to anyone who claimed them without her agreement. She asked that his feet be covered when he was carried. She would come as soon as someone could take her work at the fuller’s yard.

There was no question about whether he had suffered. I was relieved until I realized she might already have decided the answer.

I read the letter twice and passed it to Catrane. She read only the part I showed her, then gave it back.

“We should send the news of the fire,” she said.

“And Bran.”

“Yes.”

“And that his things are safe.”

“The things that are safe.”

I looked down at the page. Odran’s personal belongings had been kept; some papers used in the examination bore damp at their edges, one had a soot mark where I had set it on an unclean surface. It was small damage. I resented having to admit it, which was why I knew I should.

I wrote at Thessa’s table while people worked round me. A child leaned against my knee. Someone needed the knife I was using to sharpen the pen. Thessa set a plate down on the corner of the paper and apologized without moving it until she had found room elsewhere. It was impossible to write a dignified account in such a room. Perhaps that helped.

I began with Bran’s confession. I named him as my uncle, not merely as the man responsible. I wrote that he was alive, injured and guarded, and that witnesses would be brought before those who could judge him. I wrote that the old passage had been opened and the house partly destroyed. I did not say the death had caused the destruction. It seemed too easy to make Odran carry that as well.

Then I wrote a paragraph about why we had doubted his claims.

I read it. I crossed it out.

There was not enough paper to begin again without taking a sheet needed for Catrane’s messages. The crossed-out lines remained visible. I left them. Beneath them I wrote that I had washed his face and that Nerla had dressed his foot. I told her about the repaired shirt, and the stocking I had put back by the fire. Those were things I had actually done for him. They were not enough; they were what I could report.

I folded the letter and took it across the ford to Muirne.

Odran lay in the lean-to, on the boards she had prepared. The air was cold and dry. Someone had put fresh rushes beneath the trestles. His chest stood at his head, covered against dripping from the roof seam. Muirne let me in alone.

I stood beside him without uncovering his face. After several days I no longer trusted what I wanted to see. I put my hand on the edge of the cover, touching one of my mother’s raised flowers.

“I’ve written to her,” I said.

There was nothing else to say that did not seem intended for someone living to overhear. I remained a little longer, then went back into the cottage.

Muirne read the letter at my request. She did not ask about the crossed-out passage. At the end she put the sheet down and rubbed her forehead.

“He’d be angry about the papers getting wet.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t. He could be a miserable man over a damp page. He’d stand dripping on your floor and tell you your roof ought to be mended.”

She smiled, then wiped her eyes with the side of her hand.

“Send it.”

I put the letter away. Lira sat by the window repairing the torn fastening on her old brown dress. She looked at me over the work and asked whether I needed the borrowed dress back.

“Keep it.”

“It doesn’t fit.”

“Then alter it.”

She glanced at Muirne. “I’ve been offered work doing that.”

“Here?”

“For people here. And farther down, when the road opens. Muirne knows someone who takes clothing from the western houses to mend.”

I almost said she could do better. The words rose so readily that I had to press my lips together.

“Do you want it?”

“I want to try.”

“Then yes.”

She tilted her head. “Yes?”

“I mean, I’m glad you’ve found something.”

She returned to the fastening. After a moment she smiled.

I went back with Odran’s coat over my arm. We would send it cleaned and mended if Mera could not travel soon. There was a loose seam at the shoulder which needed stitching. Eitha took it from me when I entered Thessa’s cottage.

“I can do that.”

“You needn’t.”

“I know. Give it here.”

She sat by the window where the light was good. I watched her choose thread close to the old colour. When I tried to thank her again, she pointed me towards the kettle before it boiled over.

Chapter 46 — The men who would remain

By the third day after the fire, people had begun speaking of rebuilding.

They said it in front of my father as a kindness. A new roof, a straighter wall, a better hall with room for everyone. He listened, sometimes nodding, sometimes looking towards the hill as though he could already see where it would stand. No one mentioned the lower passage while promising him a larger house.

Druven did. She said the old way would remain open if she had any part in the work. She would not build a new footing across it. My father asked whether she meant to dictate the shape of his home. She said he could hire another mason. Bellan stood beside her with their tools packed.

He looked as if he expected another argument. My father looked at him, then at Druven.

“Leave your account with Brin.”

“I have.”

“Then we’ll speak after winter.”

She nodded.

I walked with them to the gate. Bellan had tied the painted plaster fragment into a cloth so it would not break. He asked where I wanted it.

“With Muirne, if she’ll keep it dry.”

He nodded and put it carefully in my hands. His mother had been carrying the tools he usually carried. When he noticed, he took them from her with a muttered complaint. She let him have the lighter bag.

At the road he turned back.

“The hen stayed home?”

“As far as I know.”

“Tell Della I wasn’t chasing it for pleasure.”

“I’ll tell her.”

He grinned, briefly, and went after his mother.

The question of who would remain was harder than the question of who could leave. Orsett’s men had helped save our people and now needed feeding. His commander would expect an account of the pass and an explanation for the cart’s delay. My father wanted them gone before help became habit. Thessa wanted two of them at the ford until the road was safe. Several households agreed with her, including one whose sons had stood with shields below the gate.

Catrane gathered the people who would actually feed and lodge them. The meeting took place in the brewhouse, with sacks piled behind the speakers and a smell of damp grain under everything. My father disliked sitting without a high place. He stood until Nerla told him he was blocking the light.

Orsett asked for twelve nights, stores at an agreed rate and space to keep his people together. My father refused a permanent post. Orsett said he had not asked for one. Catrane had that written plainly, then asked what would happen after the twelve nights if snow still blocked the western road.

They argued over that for some time. I listened until I understood that no wording would make the snow obey us, and that saying so would not settle who paid for the men while it remained.

At last they agreed to send a messenger before the time expired and to discuss any extension with the households providing shelter. Catrane would send her own account to Tor and to our superior house. Orsett would send his to his commander.

Thessa asked who would replace the hay already used.

The men stopped discussing authority and began discussing hay. This took nearly as long.

Afterwards my father sat alone by the cold vat. I went to him. He held his cap in both hands, turning it so the worn part of the brim faced away from him.

“They speak as though I’ve nothing to give,” he said.

I looked at the grain stacked against the wall.

“You’ve given a great deal this week.”

“That isn’t what I mean.”

“I know.”

He rubbed the brim with his thumb.

“I shouldn’t have asked if she’d come back.”

For a moment I did not understand. Then I knew he meant Lira.

“No.”

“I hadn’t agreed.”

“I heard you.”

He looked at me. His eyes were wet, but he did not cry. I wanted to make it easier for him. I wanted him to say more. Neither wish was fair to what had happened.

“Tell her,” I said.

He nodded. I did not know whether he would.

Bran was taken down the road the following morning, when Hethen judged he could bear the cart. His leg lay supported between padded boards. Catrane’s men accompanied him, with two from the households who had witnessed the confession. Orsett did not take custody. He lent the cart and a driver, making certain everyone heard whose cart it was and when he expected it back.

My aunt came to say goodbye. She had given her account of loosening the ropes. Catrane had written it down in front of her. She would have to answer for it later. For now she stood beside the cart with Rodd holding her skirt, and could not decide whether to touch her brother.

Bran asked to see the child. She said no.

It was the first clear refusal I had heard her give him.

He turned towards me as the driver checked the load.

“The gloves,” he said. “Did they fit?”

I had found them among the things brought from my room, folded beneath a sheet. I had put them on that morning because my hands hurt in the cold. He could see them.

“Yes.”

He nodded. His face moved as though he wanted to smile and had forgotten how to ask for it.

I did not take his hand. I stood beside the cart until the driver was ready, then stepped back. My father remained farther away, bareheaded. The brothers looked at each other without speaking.

When the cart had passed the ford, my aunt took Rodd home to the brewhouse. My father put on his cap. I went to help Nerla carry water.

Chapter 47 — The fastening

Massen left two days later.

He would have stayed longer if I had asked. I knew that, and for several hours used the knowledge without making the request. I found things for him to advise on, brought him questions others could have answered, waited beside his pallet while he dressed with one hand. He let me do it until Gedran came to say the horses were ready.

Then he asked me to walk outside.

We went only as far as the sheltered side of Thessa’s cottage. The air was clear and cold. Water dripped from the thatch at intervals, striking a flat stone with a sound I had begun to know in my sleep. Above the hill the remaining wall of the hall showed dark against the snow.

Massen had his injured arm in a proper sling now. Hethen had made him promise to ride slowly and stop if the hand swelled. Gedran had been told separately, which annoyed Massen more than the instruction itself.

“I’ll need to answer for bringing them,” he said.

“Your men?”

“Yes. Some think I should have brought more. Some think I put them under your father’s hand. They won’t all accept the same answer.”

“What will you tell them?”

“What I did.”

I looked at him.

“And why,” he added. “Without improving it.”

I nodded. I still wanted an apology, but he had people waiting below the gate who were owed an account of their own.

“Will your mother be angry?”

“Yes.”

“With me?”

“Probably. At first.”

I smiled despite myself. “That’s honest.”

“She’ll be angrier that she can’t be angry with Lira.”

We stood for a while, listening to the water fall. A hen scratched beside the wall, indifferent to the patch of ice beneath her feet. I thought it was Della’s, then saw a different mark on the wing. I had begun to distinguish them. The discovery made me strangely happy.

My cloak fastening had torn where I caught it before going to the ford. I held the two sides together against the wind. Massen noticed and asked for the clasp.

“It needs sewing.”

“I can see that.”

“With one hand?”

“You have two.”

“One and a half.”

“Then between us.”

I fetched a needle and thread from inside. We sat on the low wall. I held the cloth while he tried to pass the needle through the thick wool. The first stitch went nowhere useful. He frowned at it, drew it out and tried again.

“You’re pulling too much,” I said.

“You’re holding too little.”

I moved my fingers. He made the stitch. We worked slowly, closer than we needed to be for the warmth and not close enough for what I wanted. Once his good hand brushed the side of mine and I forgot to keep the cloth taut.

“When?” I asked.

He knew what I meant. “When we choose it.”

“That sounds like something you say when you don’t know.”

“It is.”

I watched the needle move.

“I can’t leave yet.”

“I know.”

“There are people who came because I was marrying. Some can’t go home until the road opens. Muirne can’t keep Lira’s bedding and Odran’s chest forever. Thessa has half the hill in her kitchen.”

“You don’t have to explain every bed to me.”

“I do to myself.”

He stopped sewing and looked at me.

“Do you still want to come?”

“Yes.”

The answer came easily. I had been afraid it would not.

He bent his head. For a moment he seemed unable to return to the small work in his hand. Then he made another stitch, badly, and we both laughed.

“I wanted that room,” I said.

“I wanted you in it.”

“You would have complained about the draught.”

“I still might.”

I leaned against him, carefully avoiding the injured arm. He put his cheek against my hair. We sat until Gedran came round the corner, saw us and retreated with an ostentatious cough. Massen swore at him without lifting his head.

The clasp held, though the stitches were visible and the two sides sat unevenly. I fastened it. He looked dissatisfied.

“It’ll last,” I said.

“For now.”

“For now.”

At the horses he kissed me in front of everyone who happened to be there. I had thought I would dislike being watched. I did not. His mouth was warm, his beard rough against the healing cut at my lip. I held the back of his coat with my good hand and let the kiss last long enough that Gedran found something else to look at.

When Massen mounted, he needed help. He accepted it. I watched him settle in the saddle, testing the sling, arranging the reins in the hand he could use. He looked less like the man who had come to marry me and more like someone I might know over many years.

He promised to write when he reached home. I promised to answer. Neither of us named a day for the wedding.

The horses went down towards the ford. Dapple walked among them, led by a groom. Massen had asked whether I wanted him left. I had said not yet; there was no feed to spare for a horse I would scarcely ride. He had nodded without making the refusal into a test of affection.

I watched until the last rider passed the bend. Then Nerla called me to help move a table, and I went.

Chapter 48 — Another night

Mera came on a clear morning when the thaw had made the path worse than the snow.

I knew who she was before anyone spoke. She had her father’s broad forehead and his habit of pausing with one hand on a gate before entering. Her coat had been let out at the seams and carefully brushed. She carried a small bag and wore boots intended for work in a yard rather than walking long roads.

Catrane had remained to meet her. We stood together outside Muirne’s cottage while she approached. I had prepared words during the night and lost them all when she stopped in front of us.

“I’m Brin Dorn.”

“I know.”

I could not tell whether she meant she had been told my face or had recognized the person responsible for writing to her.

“Your father is here.”

She nodded. Muirne opened the door of the lean-to and went in with her. Catrane and I stayed outside.

After a time I heard Mera crying. There was no dignity in the sound, no consideration for people who might hear it. I stood looking at the mud beside my boots and let her have it. Catrane touched my elbow when I began to move, and I stayed.

Later we brought the chest into the cottage. Mera opened it with the silver key and examined the contents herself. She lifted the repaired shirt, the letter she had sent him before his journey, the needle case and the thin piece of soap. She turned the shirt cuff between her fingers.

“He always pulled it over his hands,” she said.

“Eitha remembered him saying so.”

Mera looked up. “Who is Eitha?”

I told her. She asked whether she could meet her, and I went to fetch the girl rather than answering for her.

Eitha came willingly. She described helping with the stockings and what Odran had said about his daughter. Mera listened without interruption, smiling once at something I had forgotten. The smile changed her face so much that I could see the woman who had lived before the messenger reached her.

Catrane spoke about Bran only when Mera asked. She gave the place where he was held, the names of witnesses and the people who would hear the charge. She did not promise a particular judgment. Mera asked whether his admission had been written. Catrane showed her the account.

I said I was sorry.

Mera looked at me for a long time.

“Did you believe my father?” she asked.

“Not at first.”

“And after?”

“Too late for him to know it.”

She lowered her eyes to the shirt. I wished I had said it less neatly. It sounded like a line prepared for forgiveness, and I had no right to ask her for that.

“He could be difficult,” she said at last.

“Yes.”

“I didn’t say that for you.”

“I know.”

She began folding the shirt. Eitha moved the other things aside to give her room. I went to the hearth and put another piece of wood on the fire, grateful for something I could do without speaking.

We buried Odran on the slope above the ford, in ground Muirne’s people used and where Mera chose for him. It took men most of a day to open the frozen earth. We brought them hot food and took turns carrying away the stones. My father came. He did not stand at the front or speak for the house. When one of the men needed relief, he took the tool and worked until another took it from him.

Mera kept my mother’s cover with the body. I had offered it, and she had accepted without knowing its history. I did not tell her. The white flowers vanished beneath the boards when the lid was closed.

The papers went with her. Copies of those needed for the hearings remained with Catrane, witnessed and kept dry. Odran’s repaired coat fitted into the top of the chest. His professional ring lay in a little cloth bag inside Mera’s own bag, beside the silver key. She checked for them before setting out.

I watched her leave with Muirne’s neighbour as escort. She had thanked Nerla, Eitha and Hethen. At the gate she turned to me and said she would send word if the hearings were delayed. I told her I would do the same. It was what we could agree to.

Winter continued.

There were mornings when the smoke from the ruined hall still rose thinly after rain reached a buried ember. Druven returned once to inspect the standing wall and found a place where children had begun climbing. We put a barrier there and then another after they found a way round the first. The lower passage stayed open. People avoided it at first. Later, someone used it to keep out of a sudden shower while carrying tools. I heard of that and went down to look, but there was nothing to see except dry stone and water passing beyond the threshold.

My father spoke to Lira. I was not present. Afterwards she told me he had apologized, badly, then tried to give her money for a new dress. She had accepted the apology and refused the dress. She did not say whether she forgave him, and I did not ask.

She stayed with Muirne while she tried the sewing work. When I asked her to help sort the surviving household linen, she said she had work promised elsewhere that morning. I said I would find someone else. That evening she came by with her own mending and sat beside me for an hour. We talked about a woman’s sleeve which had been altered so often that none of the original cloth remained under the arm. Lira was indignant about the workmanship.

The lowlanders’ first twelve nights ended in more snow. They asked for another six. The people housing them agreed to four, and the arguments continued. My father discovered that he could no longer settle them by inviting everyone to his hall. Sometimes he listened longer because of it. Sometimes he left before they had finished and had to be sent for.

I kept seven green cups on a shelf in the borrowed kitchen. The little repaired one remained with Catrane’s evidence. I did not know whether I would ever want it back. The three missing cups stayed missing. Now and then someone brought me a green shard from the hill, and I thanked them and put it in a box, though there was no hope of making those pieces into anything that would hold a drink.

Massen wrote. His mother had been angry, then worried about his arm, then angry that he had allowed her to become worried before explaining himself. He sent that account without improving either of them. I read the letter twice, laughed in the middle and kept it beside Kennet’s in the drawer I had been given.

There were nights I woke certain I had heard a basket in the passage. I lay still and listened. A person turned in a bed, a child coughed, Thessa moved downstairs to check the fire. I learned those sounds. I did not always get back to sleep.

One evening Harn came to the kitchen door with his bundle under his arm. His own roof was sound, farther along the lower road. He had gone there that afternoon, meaning to stay. Now he stood with snow on his shoulders, apologizing before I had asked him anything.

“The fire wouldn’t draw,” he said.

I could smell smoke in his clothes. I could not tell whether that was the whole reason.

Thessa had gone to her sister’s for the night. There was room by the hearth, the narrow settle Iver had used before his fingers healed enough for him to ride. I moved a basket from it and shook out the blanket.

Harn remained at the door.

“I can mend the poker,” he said. “The loose bit. I noticed.”

“You can look at it tomorrow.”

He shifted the bundle from one hand to the other. I put water on the fire and brought down a cup.

“Would you like to stay another night?” I asked.

He looked at the settle. I waited while he unfastened his coat.

“Yes,” he said. “If I may.”

I set the cup where he could reach it and made room for his wet boots.

Appendix — Brin’s household list

Names and directions, set down after the winter. I have not attempted another account of it.

Varrak Dorn. My father. If you are looking for the laird, that is whom you mean. If you are looking for someone who knows where a thing has been put, try Nerla.

Nerla. The hearth, the meals, and a great deal I used to assume would happen of itself. She has other work than answering questions about us.

Bran. My uncle, my father’s brother. I can put the relationship down without difficulty.

Kennet; Massen Lorn. My first husband and the man I mean to marry. They need separate entries. Gedran is Massen’s cousin.

Lira. My cousin, who has lived with both households. A seamstress and a reader of older hands. Ask her about her work; I have had enough practice answering for her.

Eitha; Carlen. Sister and brother. Eitha served at the gathering; Carlen stayed near the ford. I noticed which dress she wore before I learned much else about her.

Catrane; Hethen; Iver. The envoy from Tor, her healer, and a member of her escort. Catrane witnessed my first marriage. This gives her material I should prefer her not to use at the second.

Druven; Bellan. The mason and her son. Both Aeler. They came to work on the flue.

Odran Vale; Mera Vale. Father and daughter. Mera’s direction is the fuller’s yard below Dun Cail. Do not send her questions through me merely because my hand is on this list.

Muirne Fell. Across the ford, with family connections to the old lower house. Thessa and Della are the mother and daughter beside the crossing. The ford is not the higher pass.

Rodd; Torm. My aunt’s young son and a relation by marriage. Different households. Rusk played at the gathering. Harn sharpens knives and helps about the house.

For those arriving from below: a burn is a stream and a byre shelters cattle. The lower house is the older refuge beneath the hall. A welcome token records a claim to admission; it is not a receipt to be thrown away when the toll has been paid.
