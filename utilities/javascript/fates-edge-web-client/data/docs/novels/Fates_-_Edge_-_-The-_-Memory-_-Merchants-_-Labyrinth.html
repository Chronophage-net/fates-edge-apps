The Countess’s Irregulars — Book Four

Part I — Something you would remember

Chapter 1 — The wrong side of the glass

Edrine Salvat had remembered the murder more clearly every day, until she broke her spectacles.

He moved the lamp behind her shoulder. Its reflection had been swimming across the little silver case in her lap, making it difficult to look at her mouth while she spoke. Outside, somebody was unloading empty bottles. Every fourth crate hit the paving harder than the other three.

“Tell me his name again.”

“Helvo. He stokes the furnaces at the Saffron Rooms.”

Ilaro found the name on the page. He had written it correctly.

“And the man who is dead?”

“Jossart.”

“You remember Helvo killing him.”

“Very clearly. That is the trouble.”

He put down the pen.

The woman watched him. She was perhaps sixty, with a narrow, capable face and a band of pale skin where a ring had recently been removed. Her clothes were good and had been cleaned badly. Soap had dried white along the inside of one cuff.

She opened the silver case. It held spectacles, one lens cracked. She did not put them on. Instead she laid them beside his notebook and turned them until the broken lens caught the lamp.

“I remember looking through these. I remember the crack in front of his face. But these were upstairs. I’d left them on my desk. I was washing a stain out of my skirt when they say I saw it.”

The fourth crate fell outside. Ilaro waited for the ringing to subside.

“Who says?”

“Mistress Albel. Two of the buyers. The examining officer. The man who brought me supper afterward. Everyone has been very helpful.”

She said the last word with such care that he looked up.

“I have never forgotten a murder before, Master Selci. I assumed there was a proper way of going about it.”

The Countess’s note had described her as a former correspondent, which could mean anything from a friend to a person whom the Countess had found useful to threaten. Ilaro had received the note with Edrine standing beside its bearer. He had been halfway through copying a shipwright’s bill for Pello and had offered her the only chair without a box on it.

Now he asked her to begin with the stain.

“Pomegranate cordial. A customer’s elbow. Jossart laughed and went down to find a cloth. I followed him because the cordial was working into the seam.”

“Where were you?”

“The old bath below the Rooms. We use the upper part for storage. There’s still a washing basin.”

“How long ago?”

“Eleven days.”

She knew the morning because a carpenter had come to repair a shutter. She had paid him before the customers arrived. The shutter had been making a loose tapping sound for weeks, and she remembered enjoying its absence while she counted out his money.

Then Jossart had laughed at the stain.

Then she was in a bedroom with the curtains drawn, being told that she had been unwell.

“That was what I remembered at first. The rest came back after the treatment.”

“What treatment?”

Edrine looked toward the door. The bottle carriers had begun arguing about the pavement.

Ilaro stood, shut the window and returned to his stool. He sat too quickly; the wall took a little longer to settle than the room. Edrine waited without offering to help him. He was grateful for that.

“We sell recollections,” she said. “Privately. To people who are introduced. Sometimes we keep something a person cannot bear to remember. Sometimes a person wishes to experience a thing they will never do.”

She rubbed a place on the silver case with her thumb.

“I arranged appointments. Packed the vessels. Remembered which people ought not to meet on the stair. I thought I understood the work.”

“Does Mistress Albel perform it?”

“She and Jossart. He prepared the impressions. He had a better hand for keeping the edges.”

“The edges?”

“Where a recollection stops. You ask to lose the sight of a body and discover you cannot remember the walk home. Or the person who walked with you.”

Ilaro left a space after that. He did not know whether she was speaking of herself.

“You were treated after Jossart died.”

“I was told it would let me give evidence without distress. I had already agreed to have the bad part put away. That was what they said. I have my name on the paper.”

“Do you remember agreeing?”

“No.”

Her hands were very still. The troublesome spectacle arm lay open between them.

The restored account was vivid. Helvo stood over Jossart beside the washing basin. He had a knife. Blood ran down his sleeve. Edrine could remember the grinding of her teeth, the taste of something bitter beneath her tongue, even the little blue line in the basin where the glaze had cracked. Helvo said Jossart had cheated him. Then he thrust downward.

“How was Jossart lying?”

“On his side. His face toward me.”

“And you were standing?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

She pointed past his elbow, then looked annoyed with herself.

“Beside the door. The door from the packing room.”

“Could you draw it?”

“I could draw the whole building. I worked there nine years.”

He gave her paper. She made a careful plan, pressing too hard when she reached the basin. The packing room stood above the old bath, reached by three steps. A second passage went behind the basin to the furnace court. A broad ramp carried goods down from the perfume shop at street level. Edrine labeled each before he asked.

“Helvo’s knife,” Ilaro said. “Where is it now?”

“With the watch. He says he cut Jossart’s collar because he thought he was choking.”

“Have you spoken to him?”

“No. They said it would confuse me.”

Ilaro looked at the broken spectacles. “What changed?”

Edrine lifted them. Through the cracked lens one of her eyes became several smaller, angry eyes.

“My sleeve caught the case yesterday. They fell. That is when the glass broke.”

The room grew quiet enough for him to hear the bottle men rolling their cart away.

He asked her to describe the remembered crack. She did. It ran down from the upper rim, forked beside the nose-piece, and ended in a tiny crescent. The crack in the lens matched.

“I may have added it,” she said. “I know people do that. I have heard Mistress Albel explain it to customers.”

“You may have.”

She lowered the spectacles. His refusal to reassure her seemed to give her room to breathe.

“But you’ll look?”

“Yes.”

She began to cry, with no change in her face. He found a clean corner of the cloth beside the ink and offered it. She laughed once at its stiff, ink-black middle, then used the corner.

“I helped put him there,” she said.

Ilaro did not tell her she had done nothing wrong. He wanted to know who had brought her supper, what she had drunk, and where she had been sleeping. Those questions could wait until she had finished with the cloth.

When she was ready, she told him that she had left the Rooms through the furnace court. Her small trunk remained upstairs. A carter who delivered charcoal had taken her as far as the fish market, where she bought the cheapest shawl she could find and walked to the Countess’s house.

“Mistress Albel keeps the original impressions before she improves them. She says you need something to go back to.”

“Do you know where yours is?”

“A white glass cylinder. Blue binding around the stopper. E forty-one. I packed it myself.”

He looked at her.

“She called it a morning on the water. I didn’t know whose morning.”

Edrine turned the little case over. Her thumb had polished a small bright patch through the tarnish.

“They’re moving the collection. A buyer is taking the principal pieces out of Silkstrand. Mistress Albel has always said one must know when a house has become too familiar.”

“When?”

“The boat leaves in three mornings. Helvo goes to the quarry court the morning after.”

Ilaro folded her drawing once, keeping the ink outward. He put it inside his notebook, beneath the page containing the sentence he had got wrong.

Chapter 2 — The fourth recovery

Rosceline disliked the perfume before she had finished climbing the stair.

It smelled of expensive women being asked to believe something about summer. Beneath that lay a sour medicinal trace, partly hidden by the flowers. She stopped on the landing and put her nose to the small vial Celise had given her.

“If she sells this,” she said, “I begin to understand the need to interfere with people’s recollections.”

Celise opened the door. “It is the inexpensive line.”

“Then there is another crime to investigate.”

The others were already in the Countess’s little map room. Daska had a cushion behind her back, although her ribs no longer required one. She had discovered that it improved these meetings. Briska sat beside the open window, dismantling the fastening of a bracelet that did not belong to her. Pello had chosen the chair nearest the door. Ilaro stood over Edrine’s drawing with the Countess.

Six weeks had passed since the carnival. Rosceline’s palm had healed shiny and pink beneath the new skin. The mark hurt when she gripped something too long, which had become a useful measure of how much she wished to continue a conversation.

“Is she staying here?” Rosceline asked.

“Elsewhere,” the Countess said. “Celise knows where.”

Rosceline handed back the perfume. “And what does she want recovered?”

“The unaltered recollection taken from her on the morning Jossart died. The vessel intact, its binding undisturbed. She will decide afterward whether to have it returned to her.”

“She has accused the stoker.”

“She has asked me to help establish whether that accusation is hers.”

Ilaro laid the spectacles on the table. The Countess listened while he explained the crack. She did not interrupt him when he stopped to find the word for the crescent at its end.

Rosceline took a seat beside Daska. “Could the man simply be guilty?”

“Yes,” Ilaro said.

“I thought you might dislike my asking.”

“I’d dislike your deciding.”

Daska reached past Rosceline for the fruit dish. She had ceased turning every disagreement between them into an opportunity to look away. Rosceline had not decided whether she preferred this.

The Countess knew Sevrine Albel by reputation. She had bought no memories from her. She knew people who had, and several who would rather deny it under oath than explain the purchase to their families. Sevrine rented the Saffron Rooms above a perfume shop and used the old baths beneath it for storage and private demonstrations. Her buyers arrived separately. Nobody advertised the business. A man could commission a stolen recollection in the morning and dine with people who hanged mind-readers that evening.

“What protection does she have?” Pello asked.

“Enough to discourage a search on Edrine’s account alone. Not enough to make her remain here once an important customer begins asking questions.”

“Have you?”

“No. The move was arranged before Edrine left.”

Briska held up the bracelet. Its clasp lay in pieces on the table.

“This is supposed to be a recollection vessel?”

“It holds a prepared impression. A demonstration piece.”

“It also holds a spring assembled by somebody with a personal quarrel against springs.”

“Can you put it back?”

“Better than it was. Whether you will remember being six inches taller afterward is outside my competence.”

The Countess smiled. Briska returned to the clasp.

The proposed fee was eighteen hundred silver marks, divided equally. Three hundred in advance; reasonable preparation costs separate. The Countess wanted the cylinder, any written account that identified its treatment, and no sale of either to a stranger who offered more. Successful recovery did not depend on a court believing what the vessel contained. Rosceline listened closely to that qualification.

“And it is the fourth,” she said.

“If you accept.”

Rosceline took the folded agreement from the inner breast of her coat. It had traveled to the mountains and back, been kept dry under a carnival awning, and acquired a soft gray line where her thumb always found the first fold. She put it beside Edrine’s drawing.

“The original vessel. In the original condition. You will have your reader present at delivery.”

“Yes.”

“Edrine may refuse to use it.”

“Yes.”

“A magistrate may be a fool.”

The Countess glanced at Celise. “I have made no promises concerning magistrates.”

Rosceline smoothed the paper. She could feel the others noticing it now. They knew there was an agreement. They had seen its count reduced. She had not invited them to inspect the thing for which she kept returning.

“I will want an introduction,” she said. “Someone with a grievance expensive enough to be convincing.”

“I can arrange a buyer’s introduction. The grievance is usually supplied by the buyer.”

“Mine will be excellent.”

They went through the drawing. The street entrance led through the perfume shop to an upper gallery. Beneath it, a broad delivery ramp descended to a dry packing room. Three steps led from that room to the old bath. The furnace court had its own narrow door onto an alley, but Sevrine had kept it locked since Edrine’s departure. A disused servants’ passage ran along the outside wall. Edrine thought it connected the gallery to the bath; she had not used it in years.

Pello asked about the vessel’s packing case. Briska asked who maintained the lower rooms. Ilaro asked to see the men who had given matching testimony. Celise wrote down the requests without improving them.

Rosceline watched the Countess’s hand rest near her agreement. Four recoveries had once seemed a tolerable way of measuring the future. Now there was an actual morning on which the paper might cease to matter, and she found herself checking for things concealed in its shortest sentences.

The Countess noticed. She withdrew her hand.

“It will be here when you return.”

Rosceline looked toward the others. “Then we should establish whether we can return.”

They agreed to inspect the Rooms that afternoon. No one accepted for another person. Daska wanted to hear Edrine before committing herself, and Pello wanted a look at the alley. The Countess allowed both conditions without treating them as a performance of independence.

As the others left, Rosceline remained beside the table.

Celise set a small book before her.

The brown cover had a cup-mark on it. The blue cloth around the spine had been strengthened since the mountain expedition, with careful stitches that stopped short of the old writing. Rosceline touched the edge of the repair. She did not open it.

“You could have put it away,” she said.

“I could.”

“You find this attractive in people?”

The Countess sat. “Wanting something enough to work for it?”

“Having one place where they can reliably be pressed.”

For a moment the room contained only the noise of Briska explaining the bracelet’s defect to somebody in the passage.

“I have employed you,” the Countess said. “I have kept what I agreed to give you. I do not require you to call that kind.”

Rosceline opened the cover.

Her own cramped hand lay beneath Sarem’s larger writing. The line was an exercise in meter. She had crowded its final word against the edge rather than admit that she had begun too far across the page.

She almost placed her finger beside the little mark that would wake his voice. Instead she read the line. She could still hear where he would object.

“Your reader will be here,” she repeated.

“Yes.”

Rosceline shut the book. Celise reached to put it away, and Rosceline held the cover a moment longer than was necessary before letting go.

Downstairs, Daska was waiting in the courtyard with a pear.

“Did you leave me one?” Rosceline asked.

Daska gave her the pear. It had a bite out of it.

Rosceline inspected the bitten place.

“That is a distressingly literal answer.”

“I was hungry.”

They walked to the gate together. Rosceline ate from the other side.

Chapter 3 — The bath attendant

Miette Avelot kept a doll’s leg in the drawer with her teeth.

Briska discovered this because she had been told to look for a brass pin and had assumed that the drawer nearest the work was the sensible place to keep it. Miette took the leg out of her hand, put it back beside the little porcelain plate, and shut the drawer.

“The other one.”

“You might label them.”

“I know where my teeth are.”

The shop stood above a maker of funeral ribbons. Purple scraps hung from the stair rail to dry, and the room smelled of starch and the warm glue in Miette’s pot. She mended boxes, spectacle cases and the fittings of small instruments. There were three different locks on the door. The innermost had been fastened while Briska and Pello were still taking off their coats.

Miette had worked for Sevrine Albel until the previous winter. The Countess’s introduction had described her as a former binder. Briska had expected books. The objects on the table suggested that binding was being used rather broadly.

A cup with a silver wire around its foot. A hairbrush whose back had been opened and packed with wool. A little brass frame holding a piece of glass the color of weak tea.

“Which of those is dangerous?” Briska asked.

“The glue, if you put your hand in it.”

“And the others?”

“Depends what you do.”

Pello remained by the window. He had loosened its fastening while apparently examining the funeral ribbons below. Miette watched him, then turned back to Briska.

“You can open it. I’m particular about visitors leaving by the door, but I don’t insist.”

Briska found the pin and laid it on the table. Miette had a split thumbnail, blackened where old glue had collected beneath it. She used the nail to hold a collar while driving the pin home.

“What does Edrine’s binding do?”

“Keeps the taking separate from the treatment. If Sevrine’s improving an impression, she wants a clean source. Something she hasn’t already persuaded to behave.”

“Does she remove it from the person?”

“If she means to. It’s different work from making a likeness. A likeness leaves you remembering. A taking leaves somewhere you keep trying to go.”

“Can you make a taking?”

“No. I make the things that stop it getting into the lining of your coat.”

Briska leaned closer to the brass frame. “And this?”

“A way through a house. Useful to somebody. At least it used to be.”

Miette turned it over. The glass held a faint reflection of a door that was not in the room. Briska looked behind her before she could stop herself.

Pello saw. He said nothing, which made it worse.

“Borrowed,” Miette said. “A little carrying. You hold it properly and you remember where the man who gave it to me walked. Not the reasons. Not his whole life. He was very firm about that.”

“Which house?”

“The baths below the Saffron Rooms. Before Sevrine took the upper floor.”

Briska moved her hand away from the frame.

Miette smiled without showing whether she still had teeth of her own. “I thought that might interest you.”

They had come for advice about the cylinder and received a possible route. Briska disliked how neatly the second thing followed the first. She asked whether Miette had offered it to anyone else.

“Edrine asked about the old passage last winter. She thought the lower rooms would make good storage. I said she ought to ask the man who used them.”

“Where is he?” Pello asked.

“Downstairs. Complaining about the color of his ribbon.”

The bath attendant was called Bourin. He came up with a narrow purple strip draped over his wrist and an expression of personal injury. He had bought the ribbon for his sister’s memorial, and the woman below had given him something that became red in strong sunlight.

“She says it’s the same dye.”

“It probably is,” Briska said. “Depending on the mordant—”

Miette coughed.

Briska stopped.

Bourin sat and put the ribbon on his knee. His left foot turned outward beneath the chair. He had kept the baths before their water was diverted to the upper cisterns, and later had watched the empty building for its owner. The morning he had given Miette was a last inspection before he handed over the keys.

“I still remember it,” he said. “People think you sell something and walk about with a hole. I had her keep it because she wanted to know where a pipe came through. Easier than taking her all that way with my knee.”

“Does it include the pipe?”

“Most of it. I looked away at the bend. There was a rat.”

Briska took out Edrine’s copied plan. Bourin put a finger on the servants’ passage and followed it down to the old bath. At the bend he stopped.

“Stair there. Narrow.”

“It isn’t on this.”

“Then whoever drew it didn’t use it.”

Pello unfolded his own smaller sheet. He had spent the morning walking around the block. The furnace court backed onto a lane used by charcoal carts, with an ordinary iron door and a low window above it. The delivery ramp opened through the perfume shop’s side passage. A driver could bring a handcart to the top without entering the shop.

“No water at either exit,” he said.

Briska looked at him.

“I checked.”

She had been about to suggest a boat.

They settled the loan. Bourin wanted the frame returned, and enough money to have his sister’s name embroidered on a ribbon of a color he approved. Miette wanted to know what Sevrine was packing. Briska said she could report what she saw, not everything that might be in a cellar.

“Edrine’s piece first,” Miette said.

“That is the work.”

“Of course it is.”

There was something in the answer that Briska could not put a useful name to. Pello shifted away from the window.

“Do you want something brought out?” he asked.

Miette’s hand rested on the hairbrush with its opened back.

“I wanted things brought out last winter. There were reasons it couldn’t be done.”

“Tell us what.”

She turned the brush face down. “Ask me when you’ve seen the place.”

Bourin wanted to demonstrate the frame before they left. Miette drew a curtain across the window and brought the chair away from the table. The precautions irritated Briska until she touched the glass.

She was carrying a pail.

Its handle bit a groove into the first joints of her fingers. Her left knee hurt. The room had a tiled floor, a high window, and a narrow step that she knew she must take sideways. There was a pleasant certainty about the position of everything. A door she had never seen stood exactly where it ought to stand.

Briska lifted her foot.

The chair struck the backs of her legs. Pello caught the pail that was not there by catching her actual wrist.

She took her thumb off the glass.

The shop returned in pieces. Glue. Purple ribbon. Her own knees, which had no particular reason to hurt.

Bourin nodded. “You do tend to go with it.”

“An observation you might have made before.”

“I thought that was why you wanted directions.”

Miette was laughing. Briska sat on the chair she had nearly stepped through and looked at the frame without touching it.

“I’ll need a cord on this. A proper release. Thumb away, glass covered. No decorative latch.”

“Now you’re speaking my trade.”

They worked at the table for half an hour. Miette held the brass while Briska fitted a sliding leather cover. Bourin disputed the proposed ribbon color with Pello, who listened and eventually suggested that he take the sample outside before buying it.

The old man considered this with the satisfaction of someone to whom a difficult problem had finally been properly presented.

When they left, Briska carried the covered frame in a separate pocket. She had labeled the pocket herself.

Chapter 4 — A satisfactory revenge

The Saffron Rooms had made regret comfortable.

There were low chairs, shaded lamps and small dishes of candied peel. The curtains had been chosen to flatter skin. A woman in a dark green dress sat with her hands folded around an empty cup, smiling at something that had occurred several years earlier to somebody else.

Rosceline passed her without staring. Behind her, Daska inspected the peel and decided against it. Ilaro carried a plain leather portfolio. He had asked for the right-hand side of the gallery, away from the mirrors. Pello and Briska entered separately through the shop with a case of replacement fittings.

The introduction had named Rosceline as a widow with an interest in unusual consolation. She had improved the widow by making her rich, impatient and rather recently bereaved. The resulting woman was recognizable enough that the attendant did not ask many questions.

“My husband’s friends insist I should remember the good things,” she told him. “I do. They are the difficulty.”

The attendant’s name was Teren. He had a soft voice, a heavy key ring and the fingers of a man who had once worked with horses. He asked what sort of experience she wished to purchase.

“I thought you might have something in which a husband receives an adequate answer.”

“Verbal?”

“Let us not close possibilities before we have seen the stock.”

Teren led them to a small table. A buyer behind a pierced wooden screen was protesting that a remembered victory had included an unexpected stomach complaint. The seller replied that it had been a cavalry action and the circumstances were inseparable from its authenticity.

Daska took a piece of peel after all.

Sevrine Albel arrived carrying a tray herself.

She was a broad woman with graying hair cut close beneath a fine net. There was nothing vague about her. She placed the tray where the light would reach it, adjusted a chair that had been left badly, and asked Ilaro whether the lamp troubled him.

“A little.”

She moved it. No comment followed. Rosceline felt Ilaro’s attention sharpen at the courtesy.

“You have been introduced by a careful person,” Sevrine said. “I hope we can be useful to you.”

On the tray stood four shallow boxes. Each held a small object cushioned in dark wool. A button, a piece of green glass, a mother-of-pearl counter and a plain iron ring.

“Are those the memories?” Daska asked.

“They hold enough to allow a taste. The source remains elsewhere.”

“Why?”

“Because a room full of strangers handling the strongest impressions would be an unpleasant room. Also, people steal.”

Rosceline smiled. “That must be tiresome in your profession.”

Sevrine opened the box with the iron ring. The lining smelled faintly of warm metal.

“A man who returned to the officer who had disgraced him. A public correction. No killing. You may be pleased to hear that it includes applause.”

“I may not.”

“Then we should establish what would please you.”

Rosceline let the widow’s polite resentment drain out of her face. It was a small change, chiefly in the mouth. Sevrine noticed at once.

“I have a thing to offer,” Rosceline said.

Ilaro looked toward her. She had told him she might claim to be a seller as well as a buyer. She had not yet told him what.

Sevrine shut the ring’s box. “A recollection?”

“A private satisfaction.”

“Many people believe those more valuable than they prove to be.”

“Then you have been poorly served.”

Behind the screen, the cavalry buyer demanded a different horse.

Sevrine waited. Rosceline enjoyed that she did not help fill the silence. Her face showed professional interest, not reverence. Rosceline would have to earn the latter if she wanted it.

“A man attempted to take something from me,” she said. “He was accustomed to being allowed to do that. There was a moment when he understood that he had chosen badly.”

“Was the understanding prolonged?”

“No.”

Sevrine’s gaze moved to Daska, whose hand had stopped halfway to the dish.

“You were present?”

Daska put the peel down. “I was.”

“That is useful.”

“It wasn’t.”

Rosceline turned her head slightly. Daska looked back at her, neither endorsing the offer nor making a scene in front of the merchant.

Sevrine offered a private appointment the following afternoon. She would hear the circumstances, establish a boundary, and take a trial likeness if they agreed. Rosceline would retain her own recollection. A deposit would give access to the inner viewing rooms during the private sale the next evening. The buyer leaving Silkstrand preferred to inspect principal sources himself.

“Master Carovel,” Sevrine said. “He has an interest in decisive experiences.”

“I expect he has had very few.”

“He has had the money to improve that.”

Rosceline asked to see an inner room. Sevrine did not pretend that the request was unreasonable. She rose and led them down the broad ramp, past Briska arguing with Teren about a damaged brass collar.

“It isn’t a matter of taste,” Briska was saying. “It is cracked.”

“It has always been that shape.”

“That is not the reassurance you imagine.”

Pello stood behind the case with its handles resting against his shins. His eyes met Rosceline’s for an instant, then went back to the ramp.

At its foot lay the packing room. Shallow cupboards lined the walls. A thick door on the left opened onto a square chamber fitted with tall, silver-backed panels. Each panel stood in a stone groove. The floor between them was bare enough to show how often the chairs had been moved.

Sevrine put her palm against a small disk beside the door.

The far wall became a garden.

Rosceline smelled crushed fennel. A boy was climbing over a wall, carrying somebody else’s pears inside his shirt. The garden had the light of a late afternoon in which she suddenly believed she had once been very happy.

Daska caught the sleeve of her dress.

Rosceline looked down. Her foot was beyond the stone threshold.

“Please remain outside,” Sevrine said. “This room is not prepared for company.”

She took her hand from the disk. The garden thinned. For a moment the boy and the chair behind him occupied the same place. Then the chair stood alone.

Rosceline withdrew her foot.

“Whose was that?”

“A gardener’s daughter. She kept the rest.”

“Did she want to?”

Sevrine closed the door. “You would have to ask her.”

They crossed the packing room. Through an open cupboard Rosceline saw white glass cylinders in padded racks. Their bindings differed: red, green, a dark blue almost black beneath the lamp. Sevrine moved between her and the cupboard with no appearance of haste.

Three steps descended beyond the room into the old bath. The basin stood against its far wall. A metal cover had been put over it. The furnace passage opened to the right. On the left, a narrow stair rose into darkness.

“The building supplies more rooms than it appears to,” Sevrine said. “We are careful about who enters them.”

Ilaro looked at the covered basin.

“Does the old water still run?”

“Only when we need it.”

Sevrine led them upstairs again. Rosceline had smelled fennel all the way back to the gallery, although she knew it was not growing there.

Outside, Daska waited until the shop door had shut.

“Rell,” she said.

Rosceline watched a cart pass. “You would prefer I had invented somebody?”

“I’d prefer you told us before you offered him.”

“I offered a meeting. Nothing has been taken.”

Ilaro stood beside them with his portfolio pressed to his ribs. His face was pale.

“We’ll discuss it before tomorrow,” he said. “All of it that affects the job.”

Rosceline wanted to complain about the arrangement, chiefly because she had agreed to it. Instead she took the perfume sample from her sleeve and dropped it into the cart’s passing load of refuse.

Daska watched it go.

“That was probably useful evidence.”

“It was atrocious.”

They walked on. Half a street later Rosceline found herself looking for the boy with the pears.

Part II — The things kept underneath

Chapter 5 — Three witnesses

Helvo wanted his boots.

The watch had taken them because one heel contained a narrow steel blade. He agreed that this was an awkward thing for a man accused of murder to have in his footwear. It remained a poor reason to send him to a quarry barefoot.

“The blade folds,” he told Ilaro. “For cutting twine. You can’t get it open with the boot on.”

The examining clerk sat at the far end of the room eating a plum. He had offered Ilaro the chair with its back to the barred window. Daska stood by the wall, leaving enough room between herself and the door that the clerk did not have to mention it.

“Why keep it in a heel?” Ilaro asked.

“Because people borrow knives. Nobody borrows your boot.”

The clerk removed the stone from his mouth and laid it on a folded paper.

“I have asked for the boots to be returned without the blade,” he said. “The officer who took them is on the western quay today.”

“Then he’ll have comfortable feet.”

Helvo’s own feet were bare beneath the bench. He had wrapped a rag around the right one, where the stone floor had opened a split in the skin. Daska looked at the rag, then away.

Ilaro asked him about Jossart.

The furnace man had found him by the basin after hearing something fall. There was blood from a cut above the ear. Jossart’s face was wet. Helvo had tried to lift him and found him heavy in a way that made no sense until he realized the fine coat was full of water. He cut the collar because the man’s throat seemed swollen and he could think of nothing else to do.

“Did he speak?”

“No.”

“Did anyone come?”

“Teren. He stood on the steps. Then Mistress Albel.”

“Edrine?”

“On the floor behind the door. I didn’t see her at first.”

Ilaro stopped writing. “Which door?”

“The packing room. It opens against the wall. She was behind it, sitting down. I thought she’d fainted.”

“Did you go to her?”

“Teren said to leave her. Then he caught my arm. I still had the knife.”

Helvo looked at the clerk. “That’s when I dropped it. I’ve told you.”

The clerk nodded. “It is in your statement.”

He was called Veyrat, and he had accepted the Countess’s request for an interview without pretending that she commanded the watch. There were three statements against Helvo, a weapon with blood on it and a dead man whom Helvo admitted touching. Veyrat could delay the transfer if supplied with something beyond the accused man’s insistence that his employers were liars.

“Someone who saw differently,” he said. “Something that puts one of these people elsewhere. I cannot tell the court that the Countess dislikes the perfume.”

Daska turned toward Ilaro. He had not realized Rosceline’s opinion had been included in the introduction.

The three witnesses were Edrine, a fur buyer called Lande, and a woman named Perette who kept rooms near the west gate. Each claimed to have stood in the packing-room doorway. Each remembered Helvo saying Jossart had cheated him. Lande supplied the words exactly. Perette insisted the final word had been swallowed by a noise from the furnace.

“People differ,” Veyrat said.

“They do.”

Ilaro asked for copies. Veyrat let him make them at the table. Daska went out and returned with two soft, inexpensive shoes. The guard made her shake them upside down before giving them to Helvo.

He put them on, pressed the toes with his thumb, and said that they would do until his boots came back.

She accepted this as thanks.

They found Lande at a warehouse where fox skins hung from the rafters. The smell was heavy enough to catch in the back of Ilaro’s throat. Lande had no wish to discuss murder before his apprentices, and considerably less wish to discuss why he had visited the Saffron Rooms. He came into the yard with them and stood beneath a dripping gutter.

“I was inspecting a private collection.”

“Of what?” Daska asked.

“Private things.”

Ilaro asked him to describe the scene without referring to his statement. Lande became more confident as he spoke. The bath emerged in painstaking detail: blue crack in the basin, blood on the sleeve, Jossart’s cheek against the wet floor. Then the viewpoint descended. Ilaro could not have said why he noticed it except that Lande bent while describing the man’s face, as if unconsciously returning to a posture.

“Were you standing?”

“Of course.”

“Where was Edrine?”

“Beside me.”

“And Perette?”

“She came after.”

Lande lifted a hand to his mouth. “I had bitten myself. I remember that particularly. I couldn’t get the taste out for hours.”

“Which side?”

He touched the left corner of his mouth.

Ilaro wrote it down.

They left him under the gutter. Daska waited until they had passed the last hanging skins.

“You keep rubbing your mouth.”

“Do I?”

“The same side.”

He lowered his hand. He had been trying to imagine what Lande described and had begun supplying the sensation himself. That irritated him more than the man’s evasions.

Perette would speak only through a half-open door. She had a customer upstairs and a delivery expected. She did not deny visiting Sevrine. There had been something she wanted help remembering, and she did not intend to discuss it with either of them.

“Tell me about the basin,” Ilaro said.

Her description put her at the same low angle as Lande’s. A little blue crescent in the glaze appeared above Jossart’s face. Helvo’s left boot stood in a spill of cordial. When she came to the threat, she swallowed and rubbed her lower lip.

“Had you hurt your mouth?”

“Yes. I must have. I remember the blood.”

“Before the man was struck?”

She looked at him with annoyance. Then her gaze moved past his shoulder to the opposite wall.

“I think so.”

Daska said nothing. A man called from upstairs. Perette did not answer him.

“Which side?” Ilaro asked.

She touched the left corner of her mouth.

The client upstairs called again, less pleasantly. Perette shut the door as far as its chain would allow, leaving only her fingers visible around the edge.

“I told the officer what I remember.”

“I believe you,” Ilaro said.

Her fingers tightened.

He gave her the address where a message could reach him and left before she had to decide whether to shut him out.

By late afternoon his headache had settled behind the right eye. Daska found a covered stall whose owner served thick broth and did not ask people to talk. Ilaro sat with his back to the street. He put the three statements beside his bowl, then moved them out of reach of his spoon.

Daska ate. She had bought bread for him and two additional rolls for herself, which she did not explain.

“Three people may remember the same words,” he said eventually. “They may have seen the same knife. They should not all remember biting the inside of one mouth.”

“Edrine said bitter. Not blood.”

He looked up. “You listened to her this morning?”

“At the house. Before I came for you.”

He turned to her account. The phrase was there: something bitter beneath the tongue. Edrine’s cracked spectacles were another difference. A correction added later, perhaps, as she tried to make the scene belong to herself. He would have to ask what her mouth had felt like when she first woke.

Daska reached across him for the pepper.

“Is this enough for Veyrat?”

“It’s enough for another question.”

“That won’t keep Helvo here.”

“No.”

He wanted to say more. The shape of his next thought had gone while the cart beside the stall was being unloaded. Daska waited, tore a roll in half and put one half on his plate.

When it came back, it was simple enough.

“Someone is still supplying them,” he said. “They haven’t only been told a lie. They’ve been given something they can remember better than the truth.”

He ate before the broth cooled. Daska kept the statements dry with her forearm when the stallholder sloshed water across the next table.

Chapter 6 — The beginning she preferred

Rosceline laid her rapier on Briska’s workbench.

It was an unnecessarily dramatic way of beginning the conversation. She knew this, but the alternative was to arrive carrying it and pretend that nobody would notice what they were discussing. Briska put a rag beneath the guard to keep it from marking the wood.

“We are not testing anything on my bench,” she said.

“No one has suggested it.”

“You brought a sword.”

“People bring swords into rooms quite often.”

“Not into my varnish.”

Pello moved the jar out of reach. Daska sat on the window ledge. Ilaro had brought the copied statements, but he kept them shut while Rosceline explained the proposed deposit.

Sevrine would make a likeness of a short recollection. Rosceline would retain the original in herself. The likeness would remain in the Rooms until the sale, giving Rosceline access as a consignor rather than an ordinary visitor. She could withdraw it before a buyer took possession. They would use the appointment to identify Edrine’s vessel and the cabinet in which it was kept.

“And she sees what happened to Rell,” Daska said.

“Yes.”

“Which part?”

“The end.”

Daska’s heel tapped the wall once. “Where does the end begin?”

Rosceline could have given several answers. She had practiced one on the way to the workshop, something about a mountain already falling around them. In Briska’s room, with the sword lying beside a jar of glue, it sounded worse than it had in the street.

“When he offered terms,” she said.

Ilaro opened his notebook. “What is the risk to anyone else in the recollection?”

“The people beside the ladder may be visible. They were occupied.”

“Names?”

“None in that interval.”

“Your face?”

“It is my view. I am unlikely to turn around and admire it.”

Pello looked at the sword. “She can sell it afterward.”

“Not if I take it back.”

“If.”

Rosceline drew the scabbard a little closer. “I know.”

The admission left an unpleasant gap. She had expected to defend the plan against objections to its taste. Pello’s objection was much more useful and considerably harder to answer.

Briska said she could examine the carrier and close it safely. She could not promise to prevent Sevrine taking more than agreed. Ilaro wanted Daska in the room, with a written account of the agreed beginning and end. Rosceline disliked the written account; she accepted it anyway. Pello would remain by the street entrance. Briska would use the equipment inspection to examine the packing room.

“If she asks for a different recollection?” Daska said.

“I refuse.”

“If you think of a better one?”

“I tell you before I give it.”

Daska looked down at her hands. The fingers injured in the vault had stiffened during the colder week. She worked them open against her knee.

“I won’t tell you it happened differently.”

“I haven’t asked you to.”

“No.”

That was the whole answer. Rosceline sheathed the sword and began putting its rig back beneath her coat. The guard caught in a fold. Briska got up, released the snag without comment, and went back to her varnish.

At the Saffron Rooms the following afternoon, Sevrine gave them a private chamber with a high window and a couch covered in gray linen. There were no mirrors. On the table stood a silver comb, a glass bulb and a small red vessel with a stopper like a closed eye.

Daska inspected the room before sitting. She found an inner door and asked where it led.

“To the viewing chamber.”

“Keep it open.”

Sevrine considered, then opened it. Beyond lay the silver-backed panels Rosceline had seen the previous day. They reflected only the furniture.

The merchant read the agreed interval. She asked whether Rosceline wished to begin when the man approached with his sword.

“He was already there.”

“Then when he threatened your company.”

“When he offered terms.”

Sevrine lifted her eyes. “I am trying to give the buyer a useful beginning.”

“It is mine.”

A little silence followed. Daska sat with the paper open on her knee.

Sevrine asked Rosceline to loosen her collar, take off her gloves and sit with both feet on the floor. She did not offer wine. The comb rested against the inside of Rosceline’s wrist, cold enough to make her fingers curl. Sevrine touched its back with two fingers and told her to remember the light beneath the shutter.

The room smelled of clean linen. Then it smelled of hot metal.

Rosceline felt the ladder rung under her boot. There was blood on Rell’s coat. His sword was low, his free hand open. She had liked the speed with which he named a share. That was there too, an appalling small pleasure preserved beside everything else.

She tried to move past it.

“Stay where you are,” Sevrine said.

The voice came from the wrong room. Rosceline felt irritation rise, then the old anger under it. The sword-point rested on stone. Rell moved left because she asked him to.

She had expected to remember the thrust. What returned most sharply was the small correction of her wrist. The body had offered resistance, and she had adjusted her hand with no more uncertainty than she might use to lift a cup whose handle was cracked.

Her fingers closed on Sevrine’s sleeve.

“Enough.”

The comb lifted.

She was on the couch. Daska stood in front of her, one hand beneath the wrist that had seized the merchant. Rosceline let go. The linen under her palm was wet with sweat.

Sevrine sat back. Blood ran from one nostril. She caught it with a square of cloth and held the red vessel toward the lamp. Something dark moved near its base.

“Very clear,” she said.

Rosceline hated the satisfaction in her voice. She also wanted to know whether it was valuable.

Daska passed her the gloves.

“Can you stand?”

“In a moment.”

Sevrine waited. The blood had made her less composed, but not less interested. When she spoke again she addressed Rosceline as a fellow professional.

“People will pay for certainty. This has a useful absence of doubt at the decisive point.”

Daska folded the paper.

“You have the likeness,” she said. “We’re finished here.”

“I will prepare a sample for Master Carovel. You may inspect it tomorrow before the sale.”

“The source stays as it is,” Rosceline said.

“Of course.”

Rosceline looked at the red vessel. She could still remember the mountain. Nothing had gone from her. That was a relief she resented needing.

She signed the deposit with her Silkstrand name and watched Sevrine put the vessel in a small padded box. Daska copied its number: R twelve. The clerk at the door took the box to the packing room.

On the way out, Rosceline stopped by a cabinet of perfume bottles. She chose one at random, opened it, and smelled sharp green leaves instead of hot blood.

Daska waited beside her.

“Do you want that?”

“No.”

Rosceline put it down. Her hand trembled against the glass. The shop girl courteously looked elsewhere.

Outside, Pello had bought two cups of sour lemonade. He gave one to each of them. Rosceline drank too quickly and coughed. Daska held the cup while she recovered, then returned it without improving the moment.

Across the street Briska emerged from the side passage with her case. She looked pleased, which was usually worth attending to.

“The cabinet is below,” she said. “White glass. Blue binding. I saw the number.”

Rosceline finished the lemonade. She had paid for entry with something she could still feel moving inside her, and at least they had found what they had come to steal.

Chapter 7 — A staircase no longer there

The old man’s knee hurt before Briska reached the stair.

That ought to have reassured her. Bourin had remembered the route in order, with the same dragging ache he had described in Miette’s shop. She could feel the pail pulling at her fingers. Somewhere ahead, a rat was about to cross a strip of daylight.

Pello caught the back of her belt.

“Stop.”

She stopped with one foot in the air.

The stair ended beneath it.

For a moment she saw both things: a worn tread continuing downward and the black space where four steps had been removed. The tread was more convincing. She had placed her foot on it thousands of times, except that she had done no such thing.

Pello pulled her backward. She took her thumb off the glass and let its leather cover fall.

The remaining stair was a stub of stone projecting from the wall. Below it lay broken brick and a dry channel deep enough to break both legs. Someone had taken the missing treads for use elsewhere and left the gap behind a door that no longer locked.

Briska sat on the upper step. Her own knee did not hurt. The absence was so abrupt that she pressed it with her hand.

“Thank you,” she said.

Pello knelt and lowered the hood from his lamp.

She had expected him to say something about testing the ground. He merely held the light where she could see.

They had entered through a narrow upper door from an unused cupboard behind the gallery. During the afternoon inspection Briska had found its frame beneath layers of paint and asked Teren whether his employer expected repairs to work through plaster. He had given her ten minutes and left to settle a disagreement over a sample. She had removed the lower hinge pin, replaced it with a shorter one and closed the cupboard again. Tonight Pello lifted the door out of its seating. There was no lock left to pick.

The Saffron Rooms had shut to ordinary customers. Below them Teren and two packers were preparing the cases for the following night’s sale. Briska could hear crockery moving across wood. She had thought the old passage would let them see the cabinet from behind without crossing the packing room.

It still might, if she stopped allowing a dead arrangement of stairs to carry her feet.

Pello paid out the narrow rope he had fastened around a sound newel at the top. Briska tested the edge of each remaining stone with her tool handle. They descended into the broken channel, crossed it, and climbed onto the lower landing.

The rat never came.

She found herself waiting for it anyway.

At the bottom, a narrow slit opened behind one of the silver-backed panels. Warm air came through smelling of lamp oil and somebody’s hair tonic. Briska looked into the viewing chamber.

Sevrine sat beside a man in a cream coat. He had taken off his rings. His hands lay open on his knees, with a square of glass balanced across them. There were tears on his face. He looked pleased about them.

“A little longer,” he said.

“You’ve had the full interval.”

“I wasn’t looking at her when it began.”

Sevrine took the glass. The man’s fingers tried to follow it before he drew them back.

On the opposite wall a woman stood in a doorway, carrying a bowl. She was ordinary enough to be startling in the rich room. Her hair had slipped from its binding. She was laughing at something the man whose memory they shared had said, and the man in the cream coat watched her as though he would give Sevrine anything to hear it again.

The doorway faded. An empty chair stood where the woman had been.

“You may arrange another viewing,” Sevrine said. “With time between them.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow is the sale.”

“Yes. Of course.”

He rubbed his hands against his thighs. Pello shifted beside Briska, and she knew he was ready to go. She stayed long enough to see Sevrine put the glass into a shallow drawer beneath the reading table. The stronger vessels were elsewhere. A brass fitting connected the table to the nearest panel. Nothing visible drove the image, but the metal was hot enough that Sevrine used a cloth to touch it.

The man in the cream coat rose. One of his rings rolled beneath the chair. He went down on his knees to find it, with an apology that seemed addressed to the woman who had vanished.

Briska withdrew from the slit.

At the next opening she could see the lower cabinet. White cylinders stood in individual hollows, their stoppers upward. E forty-one was in the second row. A blue cord crossed its stopper and disappeared beneath a seal of pale wax. Beside it, in a smaller recess, sat Rosceline’s red vessel.

Teren lifted the white cylinder and read its number against a sheet. He wrapped it in gray felt, put it into a black traveling case and fastened a buckle around the lid. The case had six separate spaces. He filled the others from the same cabinet.

Briska counted by position rather than trusting the labels she could barely see. Edrine’s was nearest the handle, on the left. Rosceline’s red source remained on the shelf.

Teren carried the case toward the bath. He set it in a wall cupboard beside the covered basin, locked the door and put the key back on the ring at his belt.

Pello touched Briska’s shoulder once. He had seen.

They returned up the passage. At the missing steps Briska used the rope without consulting Bourin’s recollection. Its cover stayed down. The remembered pail seemed to drag at her empty hand all the same.

Outside, the night smelled of frying fish and the canal at low water. Briska rested her case against her knee while Pello replaced the hinge pin in the cupboard door. Their ordinary clothes felt strangely light after the weight of the pail.

Miette was waiting at the bottom of the shop stair when they came to return the frame.

She had two men with her. One carried a roll of blankets. The other had a leather satchel that hung stiffly against his side. Briska smelled oil before she saw the stoppered bottles inside it.

“Not tonight,” Miette said to them.

The men waited in the street while she let Briska and Pello upstairs.

“You were going in,” Briska said.

“I was considering it.”

“With lamp oil.”

“You found the source?”

Briska kept her case shut. “We found where they’re moving it.”

Miette took off her coat. Her hands shook a little as she hung it on the peg. She was not calm about what she had been preparing to do, which made Briska less comfortable than if she had been.

“My brother sold a summer,” Miette said. “He thought it was one afternoon. He wanted the money and he signed something Sevrine explained very patiently. There are things I can tell him now that make him look at me as if I’m lying.”

“Is his piece there?”

“No. It went abroad. I paid to have it found. Then I paid to have another piece read. That one belonged to a stranger. Sevrine was sorry. She always has something further to suggest.”

Pello set the covered frame on the table. He kept his hand on it until Miette looked at him.

“What burns with the house?” he asked.

“The holdings. The prepared passages. Her stock.”

“People?”

“We were waiting until they’d gone.”

“Bourin’s inspection says there’s a sleeping room below the furnace stair. Is it empty now?”

Miette did not answer at once.

Briska sat down. The chair was exactly where she expected it to be, and for that she was absurdly grateful.

“We go tomorrow,” she said. “We bring out what we’re paid to bring out. If there is something else you can name, tell us. But nobody pours oil into that passage while we’re beneath it.”

“Then you take one piece and leave her the rest.”

“I haven’t promised to fix every wrong thing in the building.”

Miette looked at her hands. “Neither did I when I went to work there.”

They agreed to meet before the sale. Miette would give them the description of two unsold takings whose owners she could find. She would remain outside until they came out. Pello asked her to say the latter again with her men present. She was offended, but she called them up.

On the way home Briska stopped at a stall and bought a pear. She bit into it while standing beside the brazier. The fruit was cold and gritty, the skin tougher than it ought to have been. She ate it all, including the bruised place, while Pello waited with her tools.

Chapter 8 — Where the witness stood

By the next morning, Edrine had arranged the borrowed room as if she expected its owner to come back and inspect it.

The chair stood square to the table. The washcloth hung with its corners together. A little heap of pomegranate peel lay on a plate beside the bed. Ilaro looked at it before sitting down.

“Celise brought the fruit,” Edrine said. “I thought I might try.”

“Has it helped?”

“It has stained another skirt.”

She showed him the red mark on her thumb. Daska laughed, and Edrine’s mouth moved toward an answering smile before abandoning it.

Ilaro laid the three accounts on the table. He did not begin with the matching sensations. First he asked about the room where she had woken and who had attended her.

Teren brought food. Sevrine brought the treatment. A narrow strip of cloudy glass lay across Edrine’s wrists while Sevrine asked her to follow the scene it supplied. The first time it was difficult. The second time she could see Helvo clearly. After several days she could recall him without the glass.

“Did it change between visits?”

“There was more of it.”

“Was any part different?”

“The knife. At first I thought he held it in his right hand. Later it was the left.”

“Which hand does he use?”

“The right. He hurt the left at the furnace years ago. Two fingers don’t close properly.”

Ilaro remembered Helvo pressing the soft shoe with his right thumb. He had not noticed the left fingers. Daska had.

“He kept them against his coat,” she said.

Edrine looked from one to the other. “Does that mean—”

“It means the hand is wrong,” Ilaro said. “For the account we have.”

He hated how small that sounded after everything she had brought him. He also disliked the relief with which he might have supplied a larger conclusion simply to make her stop looking at him that way.

He unfolded her drawing. The doorway from the packing room stood three steps above the bath. If Lande had been there, he would have looked down across the basin’s near rim. His account put the blue crack above Jossart’s head. Perette described the same angle. Helvo put Edrine on the floor behind the open door.

“Here,” Ilaro said, touching the space beside it. “Do you remember sitting here?”

“No.”

“Was your mouth sore when you woke?”

She touched the left side of her jaw. “I had a cut inside the cheek. They said I’d bitten myself when I fainted.”

The little spoon beside the pomegranate plate lay across the three statements. Ilaro moved it away and drew a line from the floor behind the door to the basin.

“You could see under the cover’s raised edge from there. Someone standing in the doorway would see its top.”

“The cover was open,” Edrine said.

“In what you remember now?”

“Yes.”

He asked her to describe its underside. She knew the iron ribs, the cracked wooden handle, the little hook for holding it upright. She had cleaned those things for nine years. Knowing them did not establish that she had seen them that morning.

Daska took her hands off her knees and leaned forward.

“What was Jossart doing before he went for the cloth?”

“He had asked Mistress Albel for an account of his share.”

“Was that unusual?”

“He’d asked twice already.”

Edrine watched a seed roll across the plate. She stopped it with her finger.

“He intended to leave. He had made a separate arrangement with Carovel. A room at the man’s house, his own equipment. Sevrine said they should discuss it after the customers had gone.”

“Did you hear that discussion?” Ilaro asked.

“No.”

Then, after a moment: “I don’t know.”

They stayed while she tried. There was no sudden scene waiting behind the effort. She remembered the carpenter’s loose shutter, and cordial running along the seam, and the annoyance of having laughed at Jossart’s joke while she was supposed to be cross with him. Trying harder made her head ache.

Daska went to fetch water. Ilaro closed his notebook.

“You can stop.”

“I have stopped for eleven days.”

“This is different.”

She looked at him. He did not explain the difference to her. He was not certain she would find it useful.

When Daska returned, Edrine asked what would happen if the recovered recollection showed Helvo killing him after all. Ilaro said they would have to account for it. If it showed something else, they would have to account for that too. She set the cup down so sharply that water ran into its saucer.

“You make it sound like work.”

“It will be.”

That made her laugh, unexpectedly and without much pleasure. “I used to be good at that.”

Daska pushed the plate toward her. “You’ve still got half a pomegranate to manage.”

They left her eating it with the spoon.

Veyrat received Ilaro’s comparison at the watch house before noon. He listened more carefully when Ilaro explained the line of sight than when he mentioned the traded impressions. He sent an officer to check the bath. An hour later the officer returned without entering it. The owner had required a proper warrant, and the perfume shop’s landlord had required the owner’s consent to anything involving the lower structure.

Veyrat swore with an economy Ilaro admired.

“I can put the transfer back a day,” he said. “I cannot make a murder disappear because the building is inconvenient.”

“Nor can I.”

“You intend to try something.”

Ilaro put the borrowed pen back beside the clerk’s hand. “I intend to find out what is being moved.”

Veyrat watched him. “Do not bring me a vessel full of something nobody else can inspect, and call it a witness.”

“I’ll need the actual witnesses questioned again.”

“I’m doing that.”

“And Helvo’s coat. The knife. The healer’s account of the body.”

The clerk took another sheet. “Those I have.”

Jossart had water in his mouth and lungs. There was a head wound that might have stunned him. The cut at the collar had not killed him. Helvo had said all of this before anyone asked, and the officer taking the statement had treated it as suspicious knowledge.

Ilaro copied the healer’s wording. When he reached the street, he had to sit on the low wall beside the watch house until the ache behind his eye loosened.

Pello found him there shortly after noon. He had brought a small parcel of bread and cold chicken. He sat without asking whether Ilaro needed help.

“Briska found the box,” he said.

“So I hear.”

“Miette has men.”

Ilaro waited.

“And oil.”

He closed his eyes. Pello unwrapped the food on the wall between them.

“We’ll speak to her before we go,” Ilaro said.

“We did.”

“How did it go?”

“She agreed.”

Neither of them found that entirely reassuring. They ate the chicken while the morning watch was relieved and a pair of men on the opposite corner argued over whose dog had bitten the other first.

Chapter 9 — An improved version

The boat was shorter than Pello had described it.

Rosceline stood on the quay and looked down into it. There was room for a useful cargo, two people working comfortably, or five people disagreeing about which of them ought to be considered cargo. Its paint had once been blue. Water lay between the floorboards, reflecting a sky that made the whole affair look more cheerful than it deserved.

“This is the one?”

Pello nodded.

“I had formed an impression of something grander.”

“I was asking about mooring space.”

“That may have been when I stopped listening.”

The seller sat on a bollard eating a boiled egg. He had heard enough of the exchange to revise his assessment of who held the money. He addressed the next sentence to Rosceline.

“She’s sound where it matters.”

“I have heard that said of people shortly before they become expensive.”

Pello went down into the boat. He tested a rib with the handle of his knife and looked beneath the aft bench. The seller resumed eating. Rosceline watched Pello’s hands move over wood he wanted to own and felt briefly indecent, as if she had opened a letter not addressed to her.

He had asked her to come because the seller knew her widow’s name and might be less inclined to disappear with a deposit. It was a reasonable use of her acquaintance. She found it more disturbing than being asked to lie.

“Do you have enough?” she asked when he came back up.

“For the hull.”

“And the inconvenient remainder?”

“After this job.”

The seller quoted a price. Rosceline let him finish, asked him to repeat what he had said about the stem, and watched him correct the figure without quite admitting that he had. Pello named a lower one. The man complained about the price of timber. Rosceline looked at the pale patch where a fitting had been removed and allowed him to continue.

They agreed on a small deposit, with the balance due after another inspection. Pello counted out the money himself. Rosceline witnessed the seller’s mark.

Walking back, he asked what the Countess had promised her.

She could have said it was private. It was private. He had not asked to inspect it or make a decision about it on her behalf.

“A book.”

He waited.

“Someone I knew used it for lessons. There are pieces of his voice in it.”

“Does he speak to you?”

“No. It is always the same.”

She watched the water moving beneath a bridge. A little raft of peel turned in the eddy, went under, and came up again farther out.

“He was kind to me,” she said. “At a time when I had no particular reason to expect it.”

Pello stopped to let a woman pass with a basket on each hip.

“Will you keep working?”

Rosceline looked at him.

“After,” he said.

“I have acquired a number of tastes for which I will continue to require money.”

“That wasn’t what I meant.”

She knew. She also knew that a quick agreeable answer would cost her nothing and might be less than he had asked for.

“If you have work,” she said, “you can ask me.”

He nodded. They went on without improving the arrangement.

Sevrine had asked her to inspect the prepared sample before the sale. Rosceline arrived at the Rooms with Daska, who had spent the morning checking whether Miette’s promised two owners knew anyone was coming for their takings. One did. The other had died. Miette intended to return that vessel to her husband, who wanted it kept and had said so without being asked whether this was healthy.

The private chamber smelled of the same clean linen. Sevrine had changed her cuffs. The silver comb lay in a covered box, and a flat red glass sat on the table beside it.

“A tasting piece,” she said. “The source is secure.”

Rosceline sat. Daska remained standing behind the chair.

Sevrine set the glass against Rosceline’s palms.

Rell came toward her with his sword raised.

She knew the room. The ladder. The heat. She knew the shape of his mouth when he said they were both practical people. But his sword was up, and his free hand reached for her throat, and when she killed him the relief came cleanly, with none of the small, ugly pleasure in making him move where she wanted him.

She took her hands away.

Sevrine waited.

Rosceline looked at the red glass. For several breaths she could remember both versions with the same bodily certainty. The false one had a particularly agreeable completeness. There was no loose place at which Daska’s face could enter.

“He put the sword down,” she said.

“The source had some discontinuity. These short pieces require a little support at the edges.”

“He put it down.”

Sevrine folded the cloth over the glass. “A buyer wishes to understand the action. We do not ask him to live through your whole acquaintance.”

Daska came around the chair. Her voice was quieter than Rosceline had expected.

“Show me.”

Rosceline caught her wrist before it reached the table.

“No.”

Daska looked at the hand holding her.

Rosceline released it. “It is wrong. You don’t need another version of that room.”

Sevrine watched them with interest. Rosceline could almost feel the woman learning where to put the next pressure.

“I can prepare the source more gently,” the merchant said. “For your own use. The essential facts remain. People often find that certain unnecessary sensations interfere with their ability to proceed.”

“Which sensations?”

“The resistance. The anticipation. The feeling afterward.”

Rosceline looked at the line of blood that had dried beside Sevrine’s nostril during the earlier appointment. Today the skin was clean. The woman had done this before, hurting herself a little in the work and returning to it because the work was profitable and she was good at it.

“My company will inspect the source before any sale,” Rosceline said. “The source I gave you.”

“Of course.”

“And I will withdraw it if I dislike the inspection.”

“That is your right under the deposit.”

Sevrine did not sound frightened. She had the source downstairs, guards at the entrance, and a buyer for whom a small difference in the movement of a sword might be a selling point.

Rosceline rose. The memory in which she had acted reasonably tried to accompany her. She was surprised by how much effort it took to leave the glass on the table.

In the passage Daska stopped her.

“You could have let me see.”

“I have no desire to watch you being persuaded.”

“That would not have been the end of it.”

“It might have been the beginning of something I couldn’t stop.”

Daska considered this. The door to the private chamber remained shut behind them.

“Tell Ilaro what she changed.”

“I will.”

“And me.”

Rosceline drew a breath. She described the raised sword, the reaching hand, the clean relief. Daska listened to the end.

When Rosceline had finished, the monk touched the seam of her sleeve where the sword rig sometimes caught.

“He put it down,” Daska said.

Rosceline nodded once.

They went downstairs. In the shop, a young couple were choosing a perfume neither could afford. The girl liked the green one. The man asked for another smell of the cheaper bottle. Rosceline waited behind them instead of making them move.

Chapter 10 — What will fit in the case

Briska had made a carrier with room for four vessels, and everyone wanted it to hold five.

It lay open on her bench, a wooden box fitted with wool-lined dividers and a broad strap. The dividers could be moved. The size of the box could not. She explained this twice, once with an illustration involving Ilaro’s ink bottle, before Rosceline stopped suggesting that something might be slipped into the corners.

“Edrine’s. Yours. The two Miette can name. Four,” Briska said. “The altered sample goes in a separate padded pocket. Flat things and round things require different arrangements.”

“I had hoped geometry might make an exception for urgency.”

“It hasn’t in my experience.”

Miette sat beside the window with the descriptions of the other takings. One belonged to a cooper who had sold the recollection of a winter spent caring for his wife and then discovered how much of her laughter had lived inside it. The other belonged to a dead woman whose husband wanted the afternoon before her last voyage. Neither would help them with a lock.

The cooper had authorized the recovery. The husband had paid Miette what he could toward the same work. Briska had checked both descriptions against the cabinet she had seen. They were in the outer shelf, away from Edrine’s packed case. They would take them if the way remained open. There was no promise to empty the room.

Miette disliked the qualification. She accepted it.

Her men would wait in the lane with a covered cart. The oil would remain in her shop. Pello had gone there himself and put the bottles in the stove cupboard. He had not taken away her key.

They would enter during the private sale. Rosceline would insist on seeing her original beside the improved sample. Sevrine wanted Carovel to buy it and would bring the red source to the viewing table. Daska would stay with Rosceline. Ilaro would attend as her adviser, carrying the copied account of the agreed interval. Briska and Pello, already employed to replace the cracked fitting before the stock traveled, would go down the delivery ramp with their tools.

The key to the bath cupboard hung at Teren’s belt. Pello had studied its shape and the way the man kept his coat unbuttoned indoors. Briska had prepared a plain substitute of the same weight. They needed the genuine key once. They would put it back if they could.

The planned exit was the furnace court. Pello had seen its inner bolt while the charcoal was brought in. He could open it from inside. Miette’s cart would wait beyond the alley turn, out of sight of the shop. The old stair was a connection between levels, not an exit; its missing treads remained missing.

Ilaro asked everyone to show him their route on the drawing. He marked two places where he had mistaken an upper landing for a lower one. Briska started to explain the whole building again.

“Those two,” he said. “I have the rest.”

She stopped. “The first is above the packing room. The second comes out beside the bath.”

“Thank you.”

Outside, somebody was calling for a knife sharpener. Daska took the carrier off the bench and tested its weight with the practice bottles inside. She gave it to Pello, who shortened the strap.

When the others had gone to eat, Briska checked the latch for the third time. She had the unpleasant feeling that it was too ordinary for the place it would enter. Then she remembered the absent stair and left it ordinary.

At the Saffron Rooms, Teren met her on the ramp.

He had slept badly. His collar had been turned to hide a dirty edge, and the heavy keys made a small, persistent noise against his thigh. He looked behind Pello before letting them pass.

“Carovel’s brought men.”

“Buyers often bring people to carry things,” Briska said.

“These don’t intend to carry.”

At the bottom of the ramp, two men in neat brown coats stood beside the black traveling case. One wore a short sword high on his hip. The other held a cudgel as if it were a walking stick he expected someone to believe in. Both watched Pello’s hands.

Briska set down the tool case and knelt beside the cracked collar. It joined the viewing table’s brass fitting to a flat strip running behind the nearest panel. The strip was warm. She had brought a replacement made to the same dimensions, with a split that could be tightened by two small screws.

“Disconnect it,” she told Teren.

He went to the wall and turned a little handle. The warmth began to leave the metal. From the chamber came a faint sound like someone sighing in sleep.

“What does that do?”

“Stops the prepared passages from taking the room.”

“All of them?”

“For this table.”

Briska tested the fitting with a wetted finger. It had cooled enough to handle. She pointed out the cut-off to Pello without giving it a name that would interest the men in brown.

Teren crouched beside her. “Could somebody get through the furnace door with a bag?”

“It depends on the bag.”

“My clothes.”

She looked at him then.

“Why?”

“Because I would like to leave.”

Above them a door closed hard. Carovel’s voice came through the floor, low and irritated. Briska could not make out the words.

Teren took a small folded sheet from beneath his cuff.

“She has an account of treatments. This is from the morning after Jossart. I was told to fetch Edrine, bring the glass, give her something to sleep afterward. I wrote the hours down because she said I kept getting them wrong.”

“What did you see the morning before?”

He looked toward the two guards.

Briska held out her hand. He did not give her the paper yet.

“Can the Countess keep them from taking me?”

“Carovel’s men?”

“All of them.”

She could have said yes. It would probably have got her the paper.

“I can get you to someone who’ll hear you,” she said. “I can’t promise what he’ll decide.”

Teren’s mouth worked. He had the look of a man discovering, late, that the word afterward contained more people than he had accounted for.

Pello crouched on his other side.

“Bring your bag to the furnace passage. Stay by the door. If you come with us, you leave the knife here.”

“I haven’t got a knife.”

Pello looked at Teren’s right boot.

The attendant flushed. He put the folded sheet into Briska’s hand.

She slipped it inside the lining of her case, beneath the spare leather covers. Then she fitted the new collar. It sat well, tightened evenly, and did exactly what she had intended. The pleasure this gave her was brief and quite real.

Teren went to fetch his clothes.

Pello watched the keys move away with him. They had planned to steal those. Now they might have to ask.

Part III — The house remembers

Chapter 11 — The man who bought the ending

Master Carovel had bought courage and was finding it difficult to sit still.

He paced between the viewing table and the door, taking short, energetic turns that brought his coat close to the lamp. Twice Sevrine moved the lamp. The third time she asked him to sit down.

“I prefer to stand.”

“You will see better from the chair.”

“I can see perfectly.”

Ilaro did not think he could. The man kept looking toward the nearest panel as though expecting someone to applaud from behind it. His pupils were large. A silver ring had left a green mark on one finger, and he rubbed at it whenever his restless hand came within reach of the other.

Rosceline occupied the chair facing Sevrine. Daska stood behind her, far enough away to allow the short blade beneath Rosceline’s coat to come free. Ilaro had chosen a place beside the packing-room door. From there he could see the reading table, the ramp, and the three steps down to the bath.

Briska’s repaired collar shone against the older metal. The cut-off handle lay beyond it, beside a panel whose back faced the wall. Pello had pointed it out before the buyers came down. Ilaro had written its position in his notebook and then put the notebook away.

The private sale had fewer people than he expected. Lande was there, accompanied by a thin woman who corrected the way he held his gloves. A man with a cane waited near the entrance. Teren brought chairs for them and set a water jug on the little side table. He carried no bag. His key ring still hung at his belt.

Sevrine began with a morning by a river.

The man with the cane wanted to know whether the person remembering it had understood that this would be his last sunrise. Sevrine said the source’s knowledge at the time was faithfully preserved. Carovel asked whether anything happened after the sun came up.

“A departure,” she said.

“An escape?”

“A departure.”

Carovel waved it aside.

Lande bought a limited viewing of something Sevrine called a reconciliation. His companion insisted on reading the conditions herself. Ilaro watched them leave with their little box and wondered whether the remembered apology would be more convincing than any Lande had managed on his own. The thought was unfair. He was tired enough to enjoy it anyway.

Then Teren brought Rosceline’s red vessel.

Sevrine put the prepared red glass beside it. The source was still sealed. Daska leaned forward far enough to see its number.

“R twelve,” she said.

Rosceline looked at Ilaro. He took out the copied deposit.

“The interval begins with an offer of terms,” he said. “It ends after the weapon is withdrawn.”

Carovel smiled. “A short negotiation.”

“It was,” Daska said.

The merchant touched the sample to the reading table. The nearest panel darkened. For an instant the room smelled of old bronze, and Ilaro felt heat on his shins. He remembered the vault well enough that his body supplied the height of the broken ladder without being asked.

Rell’s sword was up.

Ilaro had not seen the killing from where he stood in the mountain. He knew what Daska had told them afterward. The image now offered him a different account with terrible ease. He could feel Rosceline’s wrist moving. There was an assurance to it he had never possessed in any fight.

Rosceline lifted the sample off the table.

The heat disappeared.

“That is the altered piece,” she said. “I asked to see the source.”

Sevrine held out her hand. “You inspected this afternoon.”

“And found it wrong.”

Carovel’s attention sharpened. “Wrong in what respect?”

“The man lowered his sword,” Daska said.

The buyer looked at her, then at Rosceline. His expression did not become less pleased.

“Ah. That might be more interesting.”

Ilaro saw Rosceline’s mouth tighten. Sevrine had seen it too. She put the red glass in its cloth and moved the source toward the center of the table.

At the packing-room door Teren shifted. Pello had come down the ramp with the empty carrier and a folded piece of felt. He stopped beside the attendant, asked a quiet question, and received the key ring under the felt. Teren’s hand remained closed a moment after it was empty.

Ilaro looked back at the table before anyone followed his gaze.

Carovel had ceased pacing.

“Jossart used to be better at this distinction,” he said.

Sevrine held the red vessel very still.

“He was a capable man.”

“He was to have come with me.”

“So you told me.”

“He told me.”

The man with the cane moved toward the ramp. One of Carovel’s guards stepped aside to let him pass, then shut the packing-room door behind him. The other remained beside the steps to the bath.

Ilaro put the deposit on his knee. There was no useful reason to stand yet.

Carovel took a folded letter from his coat.

“Three days before his death. He had concerns about the sources. The way you kept something once a client believed it had been returned. He wished to make an inventory when he arrived.”

Sevrine set down the vessel. “You should have brought this to the watch.”

“I prefer to settle difficulties with the person who can remedy them.”

“You have brought swords.”

“People steal.”

He seemed pleased to return her own explanation. Ilaro watched the guard by the steps. The man’s attention had moved to Sevrine’s hands.

Carovel offered her a room at his country house, protection from inquiries, and continued employment. He did not use the word confinement. He did explain that the principal sources would travel in his custody and that she would have no need to receive other customers.

Rosceline began to speak. Carovel raised a hand without looking at her.

“Your sale can proceed. This is a larger matter.”

“I rather thought you might say that.”

Teren backed toward the packing-room door. The guard caught him by the shoulder. Pello was no longer visible. Briska stood at the ramp with the tool case open at her feet.

Sevrine touched the edge of the reading table.

“Let him go.”

Carovel’s man tightened his grip. Teren made a small sound. His wrist was being bent behind him, well beyond the point at which the movement could be mistaken for guidance.

Ilaro stood.

The bath water came up through the floor.

It was cold against his ankles. He smelled the sharp cordial Edrine had described, then damp cloth pressed over his mouth. The room beyond the table lengthened into a tiled passage. Its far door stood open on a pale courtyard. A woman was calling for someone to bring a towel.

He knew there had been no water. His foot moved to avoid it anyway and struck the chair beside him.

Carovel’s guard shouted. The cudgel hit stone. Rosceline had come out of her chair with the narrow blade bare, but for a moment Ilaro could not tell which direction she faced. Every panel held a doorway. Every doorway looked as if it led out.

“Stay where you are,” Briska called from the packing room.

Ilaro tried to locate her voice. Water struck his face. He was not wet. His hand, held against his cheek, remained dry.

“The floor is dry,” he said.

Carovel staggered into him. The man’s courage had survived its usefulness. He shoved Ilaro aside and drew a short sword, slashing at a figure that moved across the nearest panel. The blade struck the frame and rang.

Teren screamed. This time the sound belonged to the present room. His arm had broken in the guard’s grip.

Sevrine moved toward the bath steps. Ilaro saw her through a gap where the remembered courtyard did not quite reach the doorframe. One hand trailed along the brass strip beneath the panels.

Pello came out of the bath carrying the black case.

For an instant Sevrine and Pello faced each other above the three steps. Then she lifted her free hand, and the wall behind him became an open quay.

He turned toward it.

“Pello,” Ilaro said. His voice barely carried. He took a breath that seemed full of bath water and shouted again. “Your right hand. Find the wall.”

Pello’s hand struck the actual wall. He stopped with his shoulder against the plaster.

The guard let Teren fall and came toward him.

Ilaro could see the cudgel. He could see Pello holding a case too large to fight with. The water kept rising across the floor that was still dry.

Chapter 12 — A place to put the sword

Rosceline cut the guard across the back of his thigh.

He went down on one knee before he understood that she had moved behind him. His cudgel struck the edge of the bath step. Pello shoved the black case into her arms, caught the man’s coat and drove him sideways into the doorpost. The blow sounded heavy enough to be useful.

“Take it,” Pello said.

She already had. It was far more awkward than the elegant theft she had imagined. One buckle caught in the hidden sword rig under her coat. She tore it free, losing a button, and pushed the case across the floor toward Briska.

The case slid through water that did not move around it.

Briska caught the handle. For a moment Rosceline saw her kneeling on a riverbank. Then the riverbank became the packing-room floor, littered with felt and a dropped screwdriver.

Carovel’s second man came through the viewing chamber with his sword low. This one had recognized that the figures in the panels were not solid. He kept his free hand against the frame and advanced by touching each real thing before trusting the next step.

Rosceline could respect that while wishing he were less good at it.

She retreated toward the table. The panels supplied a mountain stair beneath her feet, broken in the middle. Her ankle tried to turn at a remembered gap. She forced it straight and found the actual flat floor.

Daska met the swordsman from his other side.

He moved well. His first cut kept the monk outside his reach; the second forced Rosceline back against a chair. She could not get around him without leaving Ilaro and Teren behind. He had made himself an ordinary, competent obstacle in a room full of unreasonable ones.

Carovel stood beside the reading table. Sevrine had disappeared into the bath passage. The red vessel remained in its little padded box, a few feet beyond the buyer’s hand.

Rosceline wanted it badly enough that she almost looked twice.

The swordsman saw the first look. He shifted to keep the table behind him.

“You can leave without your toys,” he said.

Daska threw the water jug at his head.

It struck his raised arm and burst. He flinched. Rosceline stepped in, felt the rapier point drag across a buckle and slid it above the belt into the soft place beneath his ribs. Not deep. He shoved her back before she could correct the angle. Blood darkened his coat.

Daska took his sword wrist in both hands. He drove his forehead into her face. Her nose broke with a small, appalling sound. She held him anyway.

Rosceline struck his elbow with the rapier guard. Daska turned. The sword fell, and the two of them went against the panel hard enough to split its silvered backing.

The garden behind it vanished.

For a few feet the floor was plainly stone again. Rosceline saw the table leg, the broken jug, the man’s boot. Then another recollection pressed into the exposed space: blood on bronze, the edge of a ladder, Rell holding his hand open.

Daska let go of the guard to put her forearm across his throat. He stopped struggling when he could no longer breathe. She lowered him to the floor, coughing blood from her own mouth.

Rosceline took the red source.

Carovel caught her wrist.

“I have paid for that.”

“You have offered.”

His grip hurt the tender skin of her palm. She put the rapier point beneath his chin. He kept hold of her for another breath, staring at the blade with an expression that belonged to someone who had bought the feeling of facing one and found the object unexpectedly cold.

“You don’t understand who—”

“I have enough for the present.”

He let go.

She slipped the padded red box into the wide pocket beneath her left breast. The cloth-wrapped altered sample lay beside the reading stand. She took that too and passed it to Ilaro, who had got Teren sitting against the wall.

“The false one,” she said. “Keep it separate.”

He put it inside his portfolio. Teren’s broken wrist hung at an angle that made Rosceline look away before she could decide not to.

From the packing room Briska shouted for everyone to use the furnace passage. Pello had the carrier open. The black case lay beside it, its six compartments exposed. He moved the white cylinder nearest the handle into the carrier, blue binding upward. Briska checked the number. Two smaller vessels followed from the outer shelf. Miette’s descriptions lay beneath her hand.

They had Edrine’s recollection. They had hers. They could leave.

Sevrine stood at the furnace doorway holding its bolt.

She had blood running freely from her nose now. It spotted her collar and the hand she pressed against the brass strip beside the frame. The doorway behind her appeared to open on a long, quiet corridor, with a lamp burning at its far end.

“Give me the case,” she said.

Pello looked at the carrier, then at the black case still open on the floor.

“The sources,” Sevrine said. “All of them.”

Rosceline moved toward her. The corridor acquired a voice she knew.

Rell said they could have done this differently.

She stopped. He stood beside the lamp with his open hand extended. For a moment she could see the throat unpierced, the collar without blood. She knew the terms he had offered. She could almost calculate the share she would have lost by accepting them.

Then the image corrected itself. His sword lifted. The version in which she had been justified came toward her with all the persuasive force of an event she had now experienced twice.

Daska put a hand against her shoulder.

“Here,” the monk said.

Blood from Daska’s nose had reached her collar. She was holding a strip torn from the guard’s sleeve beneath it. Her eyes watered. She looked nothing like the composed witness Rosceline had spent weeks resenting.

Rosceline put her free hand on the actual wall.

The plaster was gritty. A piece came away beneath her thumbnail.

Sevrine’s voice reached them from beside the door. “You know what I can remove.”

Rosceline looked toward her through Rell’s raised arm.

“Step away from the bolt.”

“You could keep the useful parts.”

Daska’s hand left her shoulder. She was going back for Teren. There was nobody to whom Rosceline needed to perform a refusal.

She wanted the better version. She could admit that privately, in the little space the merchant had made around her. She wanted to have been attacked. She wanted the hand on her sleeve to have reached there first. The want was humiliating, and it did not make the false memory less attractive.

Sevrine saw her hesitate.

“We needn’t discuss it in front of them.”

Rosceline drove the rapier through the sleeve of Sevrine’s working arm and into the wooden doorframe.

The merchant screamed. Her palm came off the brass. Rell broke into strips of light, each one briefly attached to a different panel. Rosceline stepped forward, took the bolt with her free hand and pulled.

It did not move.

A locking bar had been dropped into brackets beyond it. Sevrine had secured that before returning to the doorway. The ordinary obstruction remained exactly where she had put it.

Rosceline wrenched the blade free and pushed the merchant away from the frame. Blood ran down Sevrine’s wrist. The woman tried to reach the brass again.

Pello caught her good arm and turned her against the wall.

Briska came beside Rosceline, looked at the locking bar and swore.

Behind them Carovel picked up the fallen sword.

“Ilaro,” Rosceline said.

The investigator looked up.

“Leave the portfolio. Move.”

He dragged Teren sideways. Carovel’s blow struck the place where the attendant’s head had been. The man had paid for courage. It had not supplied him with much judgment about the use of a sword.

Rosceline went back toward him, leaving Briska to the door.

Chapter 13 — The room underneath

Briska could feel the floor sloping toward the sea.

There was no sea beneath the Saffron Rooms. She repeated this once, aloud, and knelt anyway because her balance had begun listening to the wrong place. The furnace door stood in its iron frame. Across it lay a thick bar, dropped into brackets and held down by a wooden retaining piece that had swollen tight.

Rosceline had pulled the bolt free. The bar still bore against the door. Briska put her short pry beneath its nearer end and tested the load. It moved a finger’s width, enough to show that the far bracket had bent.

“Hold her off that strip,” she told Pello.

He had Sevrine facing the wall, her good wrist turned behind her. The wounded arm hung loose. Blood pattered on the stone beside his shoe.

“She’s trying.”

“I can see.”

“No,” he said. “The other way.”

Sevrine had been working her injured hand toward the fitting below the frame. She caught it with two bloody fingers.

The furnace passage filled with summer leaves.

A child laughed somewhere above Briska’s head. She knew that laugh. For an instant she was certain of it, certain that if she looked up she would see someone who had been waiting for her to finish a tiresome piece of work. Her tool slipped. The bar came down on the knuckle of her left thumb.

Pain cleared a small, useful portion of the room.

She hit Sevrine’s fingers with the handle of the pry.

The merchant gasped and pulled them back. Briska did not apologize. Pello shifted his grip and used the woman’s own belt to fasten her wrists in front of her, with room for the injured arm to hang without twisting. Then he set her against the wall well away from the brass.

“Ilaro,” Briska called. “The cut-off. Beside the viewing table.”

“I’m trying to reach it.”

Rosceline and Carovel stood between him and the handle. The buyer had acquired a longer reach by swinging his sword in both hands. It made him dangerous in the way a badly hung beam was dangerous. He struck a chair, broke its back and stumbled through the pieces without stopping.

Rosceline moved around him. She no longer had room for her usual long retreat. Each step tested the floor before taking weight. Carovel lunged toward a doorway that existed only in a panel, caught himself against the frame and turned fast enough to bring the blade across her coat.

The fabric opened at the hip.

Briska saw red beneath it. Rosceline looked briefly surprised, then drove the rapier guard into the buyer’s mouth.

He reeled. She hooked a foot behind his ankle. His heel slid in the water from the broken jug, and he went down hard on the actual stone. The sword struck the table leg and fell beside him.

Rosceline put her point against his throat.

“Stay.”

He stayed.

Ilaro reached the handle. He turned it the wrong way, stopped, and looked toward Pello.

“Down,” Pello said. “Then toward you.”

The handle moved. The brass gave a little settling click.

The summer leaves lost their shadows. Across the room, Rell’s hand remained suspended for a moment without an arm. Then the chamber returned to its ordinary proportions, ugly with overturned chairs and blood on the tiles.

Briska was still certain that the floor sloped. She kept one knee down and worked by pressure through the tool rather than by the picture her eyes supplied.

“Pello. Lift when I tell you.”

He came beside her. She drove a thin wedge under the retaining piece, tapped it with the pry’s heel and split the swollen wood along its grain. Pello lifted. The bar dragged against the bent bracket. She struck the bracket’s outer lip, opening it far enough for the bar to clear.

It fell on the floor with a weight that made her thumb throb.

Cold air came through the furnace door. Beyond it stood Miette, one of her men, and a very ordinary brick wall across a very ordinary alley. Briska could have kissed the brickwork.

“What happened?” Miette asked.

“Move the cart closer.”

Miette saw Sevrine against the wall. Her face changed.

Pello stepped into her line of sight. “We have the two.”

“You got them?”

He pointed to the carrier. “That one stays with me.”

Miette nodded. She turned back to the alley and called to the other man. Neither carried oil.

Daska brought Teren to the doorway. She had one hand under his good elbow and a cloth pressed beneath her own nose. He walked with the careful, astonished steps of someone discovering that an arm could hurt everywhere at once.

“First,” she said.

They helped him onto the cart’s padded bed. Miette folded a blanket under his wrist. He asked whether his bag was still in the passage. Pello fetched it, put it beside him and removed the narrow blade from its outside pocket before returning to the room.

Briska closed the carrier over Edrine’s cylinder and the two smaller holdings. Rosceline drew the red padded box from the wide pocket inside her coat. She gave it over without a remark. Briska checked R twelve, set it in the fourth space, and fastened the lid.

“The false piece?”

“In Ilaro’s portfolio.”

“And the treatment sheet is still in my case.”

Pello took the carrier. Briska put her tools away with her right hand. The left thumb had begun to swell inside its glove. She loosened the cuff and left the glove on until somebody less busy could advise her.

Sevrine watched the closed box.

“You’ll find it difficult to keep those properly.”

Miette looked back from the cart. “I know how to bind glass.”

“You know how to mend what other people have made.”

Miette came one step into the passage. Pello stopped loading the cart and watched her.

Briska expected a blow. She understood the wish for one so clearly that she almost made room.

Instead Miette held out her hand to Pello. He took Teren’s key ring from his pocket and gave it to her.

“Then I’d better keep the packing cupboard shut.”

She went past the merchant into the bath. The black case held five sources they had not come for. Miette closed its lid, fastened the buckle and put it back inside the wall cupboard. She locked it. Then she shut the outer cabinet over the remaining vessels. She could not know every owner or every working. She could keep a curious person from opening a hot piece with bare hands.

They had two wounded guards to account for. One lay on his side beneath the broken panel, breathing noisily. The other was awake and holding his cut thigh. Daska bound the thigh with strips from a clean packing cloth, tightening until the blood stopped coming through. The man tried to speak around his anger.

“You stabbed me from behind.”

Rosceline, who was tying Carovel’s hands with a curtain cord, did not look up.

“You were facing Pello.”

Daska told the guard to keep pressure on the cloth. He did. The second man stirred when Pello and Miette’s companion lifted him. His side wound needed a surgeon. So did several other things in the room.

They could not put everyone on one cart. Ilaro retrieved his portfolio from beside the reading table. He checked the wrapped red sample for damage before taking out a clean sheet. Miette sent the younger man to the watch house with his written request for Veyrat and a surgeon. He carried no magical vessel, only the ordinary address and the account of a fight. Rosceline disliked leaving time for someone else to intervene. Ilaro pointed to the guard whose breath had begun to catch and asked whether she preferred carrying him.

She went to sit on the lower step. Blood had soaked through her torn coat. When Daska knelt to inspect it, Rosceline began a sentence about an unnecessarily enthusiastic buyer and lost interest halfway through.

“Deep?”

“Long,” Daska said. “You’ll want stitches.”

“I seldom do.”

Daska pressed the cloth harder. Rosceline held still.

By the time Veyrat arrived, the first gray of morning had reached the high window. Briska had covered the broken panel with a blanket because people kept seeing movements in it that none of the others could confirm. The cut-off remained down. The floor remained stone. Her body occasionally remembered the sea.

The clerk stopped at the top of the ramp and took in the bound buyer, the wounded merchant and the damaged room.

“I asked for something I could inspect.”

Ilaro handed him the treatment sheet, recovered from Briska’s case. “Start with this.”

Veyrat read. He asked Teren whether the writing was his. Teren said yes. The clerk asked what had happened to his arm. Teren looked at Carovel’s guard, then at Sevrine, then down at the sheet.

“I’ll tell you,” he said. “But not with her beside me.”

The surgeon came down the ramp carrying a case with no supernatural contents at all. Briska moved out of his way.

Pello kept the carrier against his chest while Veyrat arranged the witnesses and prisoners. Miette gave the clerk the cupboard keys and named the people whose holdings they contained as far as she knew them. She did not ask him to burn the building.

When Briska finally stepped into the alley, a baker was carrying trays past the cart. He looked at the blood, adjusted his route, and asked whether anyone could move the wheel off his doorstep.

Pello moved it.

Chapter 14 — What comes back

The Countess’s reader would not open the white cylinder until she had been told who wanted it opened.

Ilaro liked her immediately. Her name was Anette, and she wore an old gray gown with a new patch at one elbow. She had already made Briska uncover her thumb, sent Daska upstairs to have her nose examined, and required the little room’s curtains to be drawn against the morning glare. None of this appeared to be part of her magic.

Edrine sat opposite the carrier. She had not touched it.

The Countess stood at the table while Anette inspected the blue binding. Celise had brought the original commission, Rosceline’s separate agreement and five purses. Beside them lay a copy of Teren’s treatment sheet, certified by Veyrat, who had kept the original. Rosceline stood because the wound at her hip made the chair uncomfortable. A dark spot spread slowly through the new dressing beneath her borrowed skirt.

“The binding is intact,” Anette said. “The number agrees with the register sheet. I find no sign of replacement inside this closure.”

“Is it hers?” the Countess asked.

“I can test the correspondence without returning it. With her permission.”

Edrine held out her hand.

Anette touched two fingers to her wrist and one to the glass. Nothing visible happened. After a while Edrine drew a sharp breath and looked toward the corner of the room.

“The shutter,” she said.

Anette withdrew her hand. “Enough for that question.”

The Countess accepted the recovery. Celise set the five purses on the table: three hundred marks each, the remainder after the advance. Approved costs had been separately written down. Miette’s extra work and the treatment of the company’s injuries would be settled from those accounts where agreed, and from their own money where not. Nobody attempted the calculation while blood was reaching Rosceline’s hem.

The Countess marked the fourth completion on the paper.

Celise brought Sarem’s book from the adjoining room. Rosceline took it with both hands. She looked first at the spine, then at the cover’s old cup-mark. Ilaro had expected her to say something extravagant. She put the book inside her coat and asked for the amended agreement.

The Countess gave it to her.

“Anette can keep your likeness sealed until you decide what to do with it,” she said.

“Destroy the likeness. Keep the altered sample for Veyrat.”

Anette looked at Daska’s empty chair, then back to Rosceline. “That will destroy this copy. It will not change what anyone remembers.”

“I understand.”

They left the red vessel closed until Ilaro could identify the altered sample for Veyrat’s account. Rosceline agreed to that too. She went upstairs with the surgeon’s assistant, carrying the book beneath the torn coat.

Edrine remained.

Ilaro told her what Veyrat had found. The healer’s account supported drowning before the knife was used. Teren had confirmed taking Sevrine’s prepared glass to Edrine and later to the other witnesses. He admitted that Lande and Perette had not been in the bath doorway when Helvo was seized. They had been upstairs waiting for their appointments. He had brought them down separately afterward.

“Did he see Jossart die?” Edrine asked.

“He says no.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I don’t know.”

Teren had heard the struggle, delayed answering because he feared Sevrine’s anger, and found Helvo beside the body. That was his account now. It was better supported than his earlier one. Veyrat intended to ask it again after the surgeon had finished with his wrist.

The clerk had halted Helvo’s transfer. He had not released him while every person in the bath accused another of something. Sevrine and Carovel were being held separately. The two guards had received treatment before questioning. No list of memory buyers had been posted outside the Rooms. Veyrat had enough ordinary violence to investigate without inviting a crowd to destroy the evidence.

Edrine listened. Her hand lay on the table near the cylinder, close enough for her smallest finger to touch the felt around it.

“What happens if I keep it closed?”

Anette answered. “You may continue to reach for the missing part. Knowing where it is can help some people. Others find that worse.”

“And if I open it?”

“I can help it return to the person it was taken from. I cannot promise that you will like it, or that every detail will become certain. You will have this and the account you have been practicing. We may need time.”

Edrine looked at Ilaro. “Will you be here?”

“Yes.”

“Then I want to.”

Anette had her sit with her feet resting on a low stool. She asked Celise for warm water and a clean cloth, then moved the carrier out of reach. The two other holdings remained sealed for their owners. The white cylinder stood alone between them.

When the binding was loosened, Ilaro smelled cordial.

He was certain of it. He did not know whether the smell came from the room or from having been told to expect it. He wrote that down separately.

Anette held the glass near Edrine’s wrist and told her to keep looking at the cloth in her lap. The woman’s breathing changed. She tried to stand, and Anette moved back rather than restraining her.

“Wait,” Edrine said.

They waited.

“I was going downstairs. I thought they were arguing about money again.”

Her thumb found the edge of the cloth. She rubbed it with a little repetitive motion that gathered the fabric into a knot.

“Jossart had a case. He’d put things aside. She wanted to see inside it.”

Ilaro did not ask which things.

“He laughed. He said she could buy her own work back if she wished. I remember being angry with him. She had paid him badly, but he had put some of the takings in his case. He wasn’t only leaving.”

The knot tightened.

“Then he pushed her. She struck him with the basin’s handle. He sat down very suddenly. I thought he’d slipped.”

Anette put the glass on its cloth. Edrine kept talking.

“She called me to help. I went to him. He was still trying to get up. I took his shoulder. She said to hold him. I thought—”

She stopped. Her eyes moved to the doorway. Celise stood there with the water, waiting to be told whether to enter.

“I held him,” Edrine said. “Then she put his head in the basin.”

The room remained quiet. Ilaro could hear the small scrape of Celise’s sleeve against the jug.

“I let go. I did let go.”

He believed that she remembered doing so. He did not tell her he had seen it. He had seen nothing except a woman holding a cloth so hard that her hands shook.

“She hit me when I tried to pull her away. I went back against the door. My mouth was full of blood. I could see his shoe moving.”

Edrine put the cloth down. She looked at it with surprise, as though somebody else had placed it in her hands.

“Helvo came through the furnace passage. After. He shouted for help. That was when I lost it.”

Anette asked whether she wanted the glass moved farther away. Edrine said yes. The reader covered it and carried it to the side table. Celise set the warm water down, and Edrine washed her hands although there was nothing on them.

Afterward she wanted to know whether Jossart’s attempted theft would matter. Ilaro said it would matter to the owners of the takings, and perhaps to the account of the quarrel. It would not turn holding his head beneath water into an accident.

“I thought he was our friend,” she said.

She could have meant either of them. He did not choose for her.

Veyrat came in the afternoon. He heard her account without the cylinder in the room. Then he asked for the source to be retained, the altered pieces kept separate and the physical statements copied before anyone tried another treatment. Anette agreed to the first two. Ilaro had already begun the third.

Perette sent a message before evening. She had tried standing at the bath doorway with the watch officer. From there she could not see the blue crescent in the basin. She wanted that added to her statement.

Lande sent an advocate.

Ilaro folded both messages into different pockets of his portfolio. He had learned enough about where an account began to keep the beginnings apart.

When he went to tell Edrine, she was eating bread in the little room with the shutters half-open. She had asked for the pomegranate to be taken away. Beside her plate lay the broken spectacles.

“Can those be mended?” he asked.

“The lens can be replaced.”

“Would you like me to ask someone?”

She considered it. “Yes. Keep the frame.”

He put the spectacles in their silver case. It was the first thing she had asked him to recover that did not require entering somebody else’s head.

Part IV — In her own keeping

Chapter 15 — The voice in the room

Rosceline had not realized how much she would dislike choosing a place to put the book.

The drawer beside her bed was too shallow. The lower one held things that might stain it. The locked chest had a smell of old leather, and the shelf by the window was exposed to rain whenever the frame swelled badly enough to remain open.

She stood in the middle of the room with the book in her hands and felt ridiculous.

The surgeon had told her to lie down. He had sewn the cut at her hip in eight small stitches and expressed the opinion that she had been fortunate. She had asked whether he charged extra for this assessment. He had said he would if she continued moving.

Now the stitches pulled whenever she turned. She set the book on the table, moved the ink, moved the table away from the window and sat beside it.

Her room was above a shop that sold mourning clothes. Black cloth hung in the courtyard below, taking the sun out of the afternoon. She had once chosen the lodging because the owner asked few questions and kept unusually good keys. Those remained useful qualities. She had never asked whether the place suited something she meant to keep.

Celise had wrapped the book in plain linen. Rosceline folded the linen back. Beneath it lay the receipt for the fourth recovery and the original agreement, marked complete. The Countess had signed a separate transfer of ownership because Rosceline asked for one. There was no ceremonial destruction of the old paper. She might need it if somebody became inconvenient later.

She put the papers in the locked chest.

The book remained on the table.

When she opened it, a little of the old dust rose from the gutter. The repaired spine resisted before settling. She had been so occupied by the prospect of possessing it that she had given very little thought to how fragile it was.

Her finger found the mark beside the familiar line.

“Across the bronze—”

“Burnished,” she said.

“—burnished hills.”

The voice stopped at the same place.

Below the window a woman was refusing to pay for a widow’s veil that had been shortened before she had approved its length. Rosceline could hear every word. She waited until the argument moved into the shop, then touched the mark again.

The same hesitation. The same annoyance. She corrected it again, quietly.

There was no change in the recording to acknowledge that it had come home with her. Sarem did not know what she had done for it. He did not know she was older, or that she could now afford to waste a sheet of paper without first measuring the margin.

She turned to the exercise beneath his hand. Her younger letters crowded toward the edge. She remembered him pushing another sheet across the table and telling her that the paper would survive the extravagance. She had kept using the first one. Waste had seemed like a test to which she did not yet know the answer.

Rosceline laughed. Then she rested her forehead against the heel of her hand and stayed there until someone knocked.

She shut the book too quickly. The movement hurt her hip.

“It’s me,” Daska said.

“That is an unexpectedly modest introduction.”

“Can you open the door?”

Rosceline went to it slowly. Daska stood outside carrying a parcel, with a strip across her nose and one eye beginning to turn yellow at the edge. She had brought the coat Rosceline had borrowed from the Countess’s house. It had been cleaned and folded. The torn one remained with a seamstress who had asked whether the owner had any particular attachment to its existing shape.

“Very little now,” Rosceline said.

Daska came in. She looked at the table, then at Rosceline’s face, and did not ask the question Rosceline had prepared to evade.

“Ilaro says Helvo’s staying in Silkstrand while they question the other two. Veyrat won’t send him upriver.”

“Good.”

“Carovel’s advocate came for the letter. The one from Jossart. Veyrat kept it.”

“Better.”

Daska sat on the edge of the bed. The movement put her nearer the book, but she did not touch it.

“Miette gave the cooper his piece this afternoon. He’s taking it home sealed. He wanted to speak to his wife before opening it.”

Rosceline nodded. The other holding would go to the widower the next day. Miette had spent half the morning naming clients for Anette, and the other half objecting to the reader’s opinion of her packing. They seemed likely to continue working together.

“Did you burn yours?” Daska asked.

“Anette opened the red binding after Ilaro identified the sample. Then she broke the carrier and washed what was left from it. There was very little to see.”

“The source?”

“The likeness I lent. The source is still here.”

Rosceline touched her temple, irritated by the obviousness of the gesture. Daska watched it without replying.

For a moment Rosceline wanted to ask whether the monk thought better of her for refusing Sevrine’s improvement. She knew exactly how to phrase it so that it sounded like a joke. She also knew how much she would resent an answer delivered in the same form.

She said nothing.

Daska’s attention moved to the book.

“Can you hear it whenever you like now?”

“Yes.”

“That must be strange.”

Rosceline looked at the repaired blue spine. “It is becoming clear that I haven’t much practice at having something after I get it.”

Daska considered this, then reached for the parcel she had brought. Beneath the coat lay a flat wooden box lined with clean cloth.

“Briska made it too narrow for her gauge blocks. She says it’s square anyway.”

Rosceline opened it. There was room for the book and its wrapping, without enough space for either to shift much. The clasp was plain and worked smoothly.

“She measured it.”

“I expect so.”

“When?”

“At the Countess’s. While you were arguing about the surgeon.”

Rosceline put the book inside. It fitted.

She was suddenly furious with Briska for having noticed a difficulty she had not yet known she would have. The anger passed almost at once, leaving something less convenient.

“Tell her the corners are acceptable.”

“You can tell her. Pello’s meeting the seller in the morning. He wants us there.”

“I am forbidden unnecessary movement.”

“You could sit on the quay and criticize.”

“That seems to have been my appointed role from the outset.”

Daska smiled carefully around her sore nose. When she rose to go, Rosceline asked her to wait.

She took the book out of its new box and opened it to the marked page. Daska stood beside the table while Rosceline touched the little working. Sarem began the line. Rosceline supplied the correction, and the recording completed its familiar fragment.

Neither woman spoke for a while.

“Was that you, correcting him then?” Daska asked.

“Yes. It didn’t keep my voice.”

“You still know where it goes.”

Rosceline looked at her. Daska was looking at the page, not offering a judgment on anything else.

When she had left, Rosceline played the fragment once more. She did not correct it aloud this time. She listened to the little pause in which her younger self had spoken, then wrapped the book and put it in the box.

There was room for the box on the table. She moved the ink to the shelf.

Chapter 16 — Mallow

Briska objected to the boat’s new name on technical grounds.

“It will be beneath the rubbing strake.”

Pello looked at the space where she had chalked the letters.

“It fits.”

“The letters fit. The fender will rub the first two off.”

He considered the matter. “Put it higher.”

That was an excellent answer, and she found it disappointing that she could not improve it.

Nine days had passed since the fight at the Rooms. Her thumb remained bound to keep it from bending. She could hold a brush but not the small chisel with which she had intended to cut the name. Pello had offered to do the cutting. She had described the required angle until he handed her the brush and asked whether paint would float differently.

The boat was called Mallow. She did not ask why. There was an older name beneath the gray paint, in letters so worn they might have been almost anything. Pello had paid for the hull, replaced one doubtful rib and bought a pair of oars that did not match. Briska had examined the repairs, found enough to criticize to satisfy them both, and brought a proper plug for the little drainage hole beneath the aft board.

Her iron devotional casting hung at her throat. She held it briefly while the fresh paint dried, warming the small ridges between her good fingers. The wood smelled of oil, river mud and a boatyard’s insistence that all three were proof of sound work.

Bourin came to inspect the name. He wore a purple ribbon at his cuff. Outside, in daylight, it remained purple.

“That’s better,” he said.

“The name?”

“The ribbon.”

She gave him back his covered route-frame. The leather slider was scuffed at the edge where she had dropped it against the broken stair. She had repaired the scuff and checked the pin.

“The steps after the turn are gone,” she said. “Four of them. There’s a channel underneath.”

Bourin frowned. “They were good stone.”

“Someone else thought so.”

He put the frame in his pocket. “Did you see the rat?”

“No.”

“Probably gone too.”

He went to find a place on the quay where he could watch without being asked to carry anything.

The others arrived separately. Ilaro came from the watch house with a parcel under his arm. He had new writing in his notebook and the look of someone who would prefer to remain quiet until it had settled. Daska wore a loose jacket that did not catch her bruised face when she pulled it over her head. Rosceline came last, carrying a cushion and walking with a care that made strangers move aside before she had to ask.

“No one mentioned stairs,” she said.

“There are three,” Briska told her.

“I had counted. My objection concerns their existence.”

Pello brought the boat close to the landing. Daska held its side while Rosceline descended. She sat on the aft bench, tested the cushion, and arranged her coat over the dressing at her hip. The plain wooden book-box was not with her. Briska found herself glad she had left it somewhere she trusted.

Ilaro handed Pello the parcel. Inside were Edrine’s repaired spectacles and their silver case.

“She’s at Miette’s this afternoon. She asked whether you could bring them if you’re going that way.”

Pello put the case beneath the bench in a dry pouch. “Helvo?”

“Released to attend the hearing. The accusation against him is withdrawn pending the new account. He has his boots.”

Daska sat on the landing step. “Did he say thank you?”

“He said the shoes were less trouble.”

She laughed and rubbed her nose, regretting the movement a moment later.

Sevrine remained held on the murder charge. Carovel had secured lodging instead of a cell while the assault charges were examined. His guards had been less fortunate. Veyrat had kept the letter, the treatment instructions and the altered sample in separate custody. Lande now remembered very little with confidence. Perette remembered enough to be angry and had retained someone who asked more questions than Lande’s advocate seemed to enjoy.

Ilaro gave the account without pretending it was finished. Briska asked whether the holdings in the Rooms were safe.

“For now. Anette and Miette are identifying the ones whose owners can be found. Some were sold willingly. Some were sold twice. They disagree about what can be returned.”

“Of course they do.”

“She says you’re welcome to help carry.”

Briska looked at her thumb. “In a week.”

Pello passed the forward line to Daska and went to the other end of the boat. The seller had come to watch the departure, chiefly to establish that any damage after this point belonged to somebody else. He offered an unsolicited opinion about the wind.

Rosceline asked whether he had included that in the price. He went back to his bollard.

There was still work before they could move. Briska checked the plug, the seats and the two new bindings around the oar pins. Pello loaded a short pole, a bailer and the old cover he intended to replace after he had earned enough to stop buying other things for the boat. Ilaro chose a place where he could see the bank without facing the glitter on the water. Daska took the forward bench.

Rosceline watched Pello stow the bailer.

“You do intend to row?”

“Yes.”

“An excellent qualification. I mention it because none of us appears to have been engaged for that purpose.”

“I can,” Daska said.

“I was speaking collectively. It made the complaint more impressive.”

Briska stepped down last. The hull shifted under her and she reached with the wrong hand. Her sore thumb struck the gunwale. Pello caught her elbow before she could compensate by stepping somewhere worse.

She sat, breathed through her teeth and waited for the pain to lose its bright edge.

“Thank you,” she said.

He passed her the loose end of the line. She untied it with her good hand and dropped the coil beside her feet.

For several strokes they went nowhere. The line at the bow still held them to the landing. Daska looked down at it, then at the gathered spectators, and reached to free the knot.

Rosceline opened her mouth.

“Don’t,” the monk said.

Rosceline shut it. Her shoulders shook once against the cushion.

The boat drifted away from the stone. Pello set the oars. The unmatched blades entered the water at slightly different angles, and he corrected without comment. Briska watched the new rib take the strain. It held. So did the plug, the seats and the ordinary bindings she had made.

A delivery barge rounded the bend ahead of them, its driver already preparing to object. Pello brought Mallow neatly against the quieter side of the canal and let it pass. The driver looked disappointed to have lost the opportunity.

Rosceline settled back. Daska trailed two fingers through the water. Ilaro kept his notebook closed.

Above the next bridge, laundry hung between windows. A sleeve dripped onto Rosceline’s shoulder as they passed beneath it. She looked up, astonished, and Pello laughed before he could help himself.

It was loud enough to turn the head of the woman at the window.

She drew in the shirt. Pello missed half a stroke, recovered it, and took them out among the morning boats.
