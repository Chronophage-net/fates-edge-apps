Where to begin

Start with The Last Blade to ride out of Tarlington with Riarhin and Elyas. For the family history that made Tarlington matter, begin with Elianor in The Widow’s Hand. For waterfront crime, start with Lyra at work in The Lady Under the Waves. Her earlier appearances give the trilogy a longer history if you already know her.

For a single novel, The Guest Who Brought Death opens at a wedding in Ubral, and The Serpent’s Coil sends three mercenaries into Midh Ahkaz to bring a young woman home. The catalog groups all thirty-nine volumes by series and setting.

The reading paths describe each book’s starting point. Detailed family trees, outcomes and interpretations begin under Spoilers and reference. In individual novels, the optional Reader’s Companion introduces names on the assumption that you have read earlier volumes. Instruments, Positions and Leaves belong to the stories themselves and can be read where they appear.

Reading Riarhin and Belworth together

The two sequences share people and a war. Riarhin can be a long way from home while a letter, a marriage or a campaign changes what she will find on her return. This order gives each side time to tell its part.

  1. Meet the households. Read The Widow’s Hand, A Roll of the Fallen, Count Your Exits, then Paget. The children at Tarlington grow into people whose loyalties matter on both sides of the river.
  2. Take the road with Elyas. Read The Last Blade, The Thread, then The Weight You Carry. Elyas takes the Acasian road in the same spring that Vaelrie’s letters in Paget follow the opening campaign.
  3. Return to the valley. Read Tributary and Estuary here, before The Face You Bear. While Riarhin has been traveling, the Belworth households have lived through the war. Face lets her see the country they now inhabit.
  4. Read the two views of the peace. After Face, read The Names You Take, then The Blood in the Fold, then The Harvest of Leaves. Names follows the company in Theona. Fold stays with Elyas and Lyra at court. Both roads lead to Harvest. Read Face before Fold to discover the changes at court when Riarhin does.
  5. Continue with Riarhin. Pick up The Bones of Empire and follow books VIII–XIII in order. Two optional turns fit here. After Bones, Saikou opens his casebooks on the voyage to Kassamira in The Doors He Holds. After The Kept Place, Lyra’s story continues in The Lady Under the Waves. You can read her trilogy straight through and rejoin Riarhin at The Seed of Ruin.

The Riarhin Cycle

Begin with The Last Blade and follow the numbered sequence through The Mist Walker. If you would like the court’s side of her travels along the way, use the combined reading order.

I–III · Traveling together

  1. The Last Blade. A pursuit takes Riarhin and Elyas from Tarlington to the Acasian roads and the Ykrul steppe.
  2. The Thread. Riarhin, Na’ar, and Aryka follow a documentary trail into Serafine’s Silkstrand.
  3. The Weight You Carry. Sea adventure and a southern expedition lead from Sada’s Taharkan commission into ancient waterworks and an uncanny pursuit through a storm-bound city.

IV–VII · Returning and answering

  1. The Face You Bear. Riarhin returns through Valora and visits Bea’s Rocke kin in Ubral. Years later a message draws her south again.
  2. The Names You Take. In Theona, Riarhin gathers a company and accepts a commission entangled with a marriage, a fleet, and a contested peace.
  3. The Harvest of Leaves. Riarhin’s company must bring the skills they gathered in Theona to bear on a commission in Valora.
  4. The Bones of Empire. Riarhin and Saikou cross Dhahara toward the Himadri, pursued by rival claimants to their burden. River ambushes and dangerous temple machinery share the journey with living households, local obligations and the work of invoking.

VIII–XIII · Kinship and chosen commitments

  1. The Bridge Born. An invitation to a family gathering takes Riarhin back toward the Valewood and the people who remember her there.
  2. The Kept Place. A safe-conduct allows Riarhin back into Valora. What she may do there and what the household expects of her are two different questions.
  3. The Seed of Ruin. An Oracle’s evidence draws Riarhin, a Sumwright and a lantern-warden toward the Valewood’s beautiful, damaged ancient workings.
  4. The Howling Ice. A northern sea-saga of feuding halls, fierce weather, unlikely companions and a rescue into the ice.
  5. The Toll of the Dead. In the Mistlands, a young ward-maker offers protection beyond the bell-line, while missing carriers draw Riarhin into the Direwood.
  6. The Mist Walker. The road beyond the Direwood leads into Obishaal, where Riarhin needs people who know the country and have business of their own.

The Bridge Born and The Seed of Ruin belong to the same Valewood story. Read The Kept Place between them. In Valora, Riarhin has people with claims on her time quite apart from the family she has come to know.

The Belworth histories and war novels

Four different kinds of book bring the households to the opening campaign. A marriage settlement, a family codex, a child’s lessons at a game board and the letters a woman kept each record things the others miss.

The four foundations

  1. The Widow’s Hand. Elianor’s marriages, the settlements, and the difference between acknowledged descent and rumor.
  2. A Roll of the Fallen. Alaysha Renard’s family history, its records, and the inheritance carried into the royal household.
  3. Count Your Exits. Veralyn, Jaennis, Tylan, and Elyas grow up together at Tarlington; friendship meets succession politics.
  4. Paget. Vaelrie’s letters to Jaennis follow an Adrian household into the opening war.

The war and its aftermath

  1. The Belworth War, Part I: Tributary. The northern campaign, the Belworth rebellion, Fairport, and Jaennis’s court converge in a war with many centers.
  2. The Belworth War, Part II: Estuary. Ouen, Cedric, Elyas, and Seniro face the household and military costs of bringing that war to an end.
  3. The Blood in the Fold. Elyas and Lyra hear the petitions that reach Veralyn’s court. A road that looks like good news in the accounts can look very different to the household it passes.

On a court-only reading, go straight from Estuary to The Blood in the Fold. If you are also following Riarhin, save Fold until after The Face You Bear and The Names You Take.

Religion and authority in the Belworth

Public worship belongs to respectable life in the Belworth. Blessings, household lights and feast observances sit beside trade, appointments and royal business. Personal devotion and institutional ambition often overlap; a person can observe the rites sincerely while finding an advantage in being seen to do so.

The Chain-Lanterns are the Temple of Light’s investigators. Their presence runs through the four Belworth prequels, Tributary, Estuary and The Blood in the Fold, often through visitors, servants’ conversations and questions about unusual knowledge or healing. Their claimed powers vary between hunters and remain open to doubt. Their access to households and information is much less uncertain. Thought-readers attract particular scrutiny; ordinary work and courtesy can conceal an inquiry, or let witnesses avoid acknowledging one.

Lyra’s Silkstrand trilogy

Lyra knows what a delayed boat costs the person waiting to be paid. A set of impossible salvage accounts draws her into the struggle for the waterfront. For someone coming from The Thread, this is Serafine’s city seen from the landing where her goods come ashore.

  1. The Lady Under the Waves. Maeron offers Lyra a place at his table and help with the healing Gyllan taught her. The Salt Prince also has work for a merchant who can keep boats moving.
  2. The House of Borrowed Voices. A season later, Tesset and Lyra see the same arrangements from opposite sides of an employer’s desk. Ada needs a boat and people willing to search for a missing man.
  3. The Harbor of Knives. With Zakovian interests pressing in on the harbor, Lyra wants a place in Silkstrand that nobody else can take away. Vaska Rell knows what that kind of security costs.

Lyra before the waterfront

To follow her earlier appearances, begin with The Last Blade, then read The Blood in the Fold, The Harvest of Leaves, The Bones of Empire and The Kept Place. This route skips intervening Riarhin books and carries their outcomes forward. For a first reading of both series, follow the combined order and start Lady after The Kept Place.

Blood and Silk draws on the Velvet War and Millhaven adventures. The Lady Under the Waves draws on The Night of Shattered Screams. House and Harbor continue Lyra’s choices in the novels.

The Lerris trilogy

Markus and his younger brother Lerris belong to the Fenwoods of Tarlington. Here they are scholars in Thepyrgos, where they disagree about the appeal of a dangerous piece of work. Start with the brothers here or come to them from the Belworth books.

  1. Whispers in the Stacks. Markus and Lerris Fenwood encounter dangerous scholarship in Thepyrgos; university life becomes a struggle to get out.
  2. The Ninth Proof. Markus follows his brother back into the work of Thepyrgos. They have different reasons for wanting to know what its scholars found.
  3. Whispers in the Tunnels. In Asselin, Lerris asks his host the name of a woman in a portrait. The Baron remembers fastening her earring after she played the fiddle. Small intimacies like this make him a difficult host to judge. An expedition beneath Aeler follows the visit.

Lerris also appears under the name Levis. Readers who know him from The Witnessed Prey will find his earlier life here.

Companion tales and independent books

Five companion novellas

  • No Bruises. Madam Serafine tells her own rise in Silkstrand. Her account makes a companion to Blood and Silk and The Thread.
  • The Widow’s Walk. A contested telling of Zawadi and Idriss gives another view of the grain agreements behind Sada’s commission in The Weight You Carry.
  • The Diwān of Roads. Safiya al-Fhara’s long leaf follows travel, literacy, possession, and the meanings of the documents that survive.
  • The Daughter of Ahakar. A survivor’s telling joins Ashaani erasure, a borrowed household, and the struggle to retain a name.
  • The Road Kept No Prints. Dusana’s caravan enters Ash-Fenn. A separate road into the setting’s communities, hospitality, and unease.

Standalone novels

  • The Bell That Counts to Nine. It shares the Linn coast with The Howling Ice. Here a road has to survive a winter, and a clerk, her skald mother and her young daughter have different ideas about what a community needs to remember. Either book can come first.
  • The Guest Who Brought Death. In Ubral, the country Riarhin visits in The Face You Bear, Brin Dorn has two households to seat at her wedding before a guest’s death turns her attention elsewhere.
  • What the Mountain Keeps. Aevra, a passage mason, and Drevik, her surface-raised cousin, venture into the Under Realms. Family objects, unfamiliar households and the work of traveling together give the survival story its domestic scale.
  • The Serpent’s Coil. James Dravik, Korvash and Szik take Orun’s money to bring his adult daughter Yara home. Yara has reasons of her own for joining Thaliss’s household. Midh Ahkaz offers James everything he thinks he deserves, from an excellent coat to an appreciative audience. His vanity supplies much of the comedy in a sword-and-sorcery adventure that finds room for a visit to the cobbler on the way to the palace horrors.
  • What the Water Kept. At the Lotus Gate in Heugen, Mirrah cuts a new name into glass for people who have drunk from the wells. An approaching host from the Covenant carries writs in the names those people left behind.

The Doors He Holds

Saikou is sailing from Silkstrand to Kassamira to see his mother. On board he opens three older casebooks. The Doors He Holds can be read for the cases alone. If you are following his travels with Riarhin, read it after The Bones of Empire. The homeward frame discloses the outcome of the mountain expedition.

  • The Lamplighter’s Echo: in Vent-Deep, Saikou and Sanna investigate a warning that the mine cannot safely ignore.

  • The Censor’s Daughter: in Ecktoria, fourteen-year-old Livia and Saikou face a possession inquiry under an approaching public deadline.

Other ways through the shelf

The collection can also be read through desire, presentation, family, humour and the body. These paths cross genres; they do not require treating every domestic detail as a clue.

  • Femininity, performance and desire: The Face You Bear, No Bruises, The Kept Place and The Guest Who Brought Death. Courtly presentation, professional intimacy and wedding anticipation give femininity different meanings; neither a dress nor a weapon settles a woman’s character.
  • Brothers, comrades and care: the Lerris trilogy, Tributary and Estuary. Scholarship, rivalry, military fellowship and practical tenderness offer different approaches to men’s lives.
  • Generations and remembered lives: The Bell That Counts to Nine, The Bridge Born and The Things That Carry Us. Family teaching and local knowledge are lived relationships, sometimes uncomfortable ones.
  • Everyday observances. In The Weight You Carry, a sailor in a red cap tips the last of her drinking water over the rail, a Rainmaiden’s offering to Raéyn. Riarhin would have drunk it. In What the Mountain Keeps, Drevik’s father has a question for the people offering to fetch a Breath Warden. Whose household will the visit go against?
  • The uncanny at home and at work: The Guest Who Brought Death, The Doors He Holds and the Lerris trilogy. Choose a household gothic, supernatural case stories or an unfolding academic and expedition horror sequence.

Learning the work, acquiring the power: Blood and Silk follows developing skill, shared plans and ordinary companionship. Lyra’s trilogy follows a merchant learning what a rival, a patron and an employee each mean by loyalty. Both make progress tangible; their accounts of what people do with it diverge. For waterfront noir with a rising supernatural threat, follow Lady through House to Harbor.

Riarhin and the overlapping campaigns

The Last Blade · Full-book spoilers

Orvain and the River Fellowship

Orvain is the son of a lord in southwestern Istovar, near southern Ethor. Brought to Tarlington late in Elyas’s wardship to keep him company and remind him of home, he becomes a real friend. He has less success finding an independent place at court. Elyas can obtain an invitation for him; that does not make the people inside take him seriously.

The River Fellowship begins with young lords and heirs, many from either side of the Belworth valley, sharing suppers while their petitions wait. Under Merrow’s patronage, copies of grants and useful introductions lead to letters, reports on household crossings and advances for armed escorts. Orvain hopes to win Braedyn’s assent and lead the first escort. His need for standing helps explain why the Vigil can reach him, while leaving his attack his own act. Gyllan distinguishes Fellowship members from Vigil adherents: membership alone does not prove knowledge of the stabbing.

Lyra and Riarhin before the rupture

Their friendship grows through shared amusement over breakfast plums, an evening with Gyllan’s unfinished lesson, and the care of Elyas’s injured gelding. Riarhin listens to Lyra’s knowledge of both Gyllan and the horse; Lyra can laugh with her and seek her judgment. Her later anger follows that affection. At the blindfold crossing, she steadies Riarhin before either has time to negotiate what the help means. “It wasn’t a decision” preserves the instinct to care without settling their disagreement.

Why the Acasian encounters belong to the same journey

The route runs from Tarlington through Pont-de-Tithe and the Scar, into Istovar and along the Thin Road between Ethor and Adria, then through Acasia toward Casta Ferrum and the Ykrul lands. Each encounter changes what the companions can do next. The bridge rescue brings them to Vaul. Challenging Keld’s credit makes Elyas responsible for costs outside his own household. The grain traffic gives them a route to follow. A flooded ford diverts them, and the empty hamlet takes supplies and injures a horse, making the water station’s extortion harder to evade.

Jori’s question at the evening fire is why nobody can mend the bridge by magic. Gyllan remembers a tutor in Thepyrgos who left the place names out of accounts of workings and made the students guess where each came from. The two schools at his own university disagree about what causes backlash. Vaul has had an easier time teaching Jori about Fate. All he needed was a chip of stone and a free hand.

The political inquiry and the uncanny adventure therefore constrain one another without becoming one explanation. Cooperation with Aryka obtains water and a further sighting of the carriers. It does not prove that Keld, the Vigil or Kargath arranged the supernatural danger. Riarhin’s dismissal of the repaired cloth at the fork is an error of attention and inference; her memory cannot supply the significance she failed to give it.

The campaign before Kargath is named

Act II’s local reports gradually suggest a larger undertaking. Jori’s mother remembers raids beyond the hill forts; now Ykrul have taken teams at the lower ford, making the detour around the broken bridge dangerous. Later, a carter reports raiders in orchards the companions passed before reaching that bridge. A smith has been taken for a mustering. The carter demands guards, while saddles, harness work and fresh pack animals fill the old military station. Kargath’s preparations affect the journey before the companions can identify him. The reports do not establish that every raiding band serves him.

Keld deliberately sells Kargath priority at the mills, displacing food already bought for three Aeler holds. The other Edgewalker can wait six weeks or accept a refund that no longer covers replacement purchases; he keeps being outbid without knowing who needs so much food. Keld acknowledges the sale at the lime pits. Her objection concerns Kargath’s further borrowing against her name, leaving her willing assistance distinct from the transactions she disputes. Vaul has no part in the diversion. His refusal to promise an early bridge repair undermines the credit supporting her iron trade, but the meal keeps moving through other sellers. Following it carries the inquiry beyond Keld.

Serekh says Kargath means to take Acasia first and Ecktoria next. Kargath needs the Acasian mills intact to feed the westward campaign. In the fuller’s yard, Riarhin hears his officer report that families are fleeing west into Ecktoria and east into Vhasia. Kargath orders both roads kept open and lets the families carry off grain. He wants to know which crossings the soldiers leave when the towns call them in to guard the bread stores.

Elyas’s games, and the harder negotiation

During the camp’s rest day, Elyas’s Ykrul hosts solemnly recommend wrestling and a mounted ring contest as preparation for speaking to Ernak. He catches on to the teasing and enjoys competing. He nearly earns a wrestling fall, jokes about growing up with two older brothers and three elder sisters, and wins one of three riding runs on a borrowed horse while his injured gelding rests. The children wager buttons. Riarhin watches. These are people enjoying one another’s company before they return to difficult business.

That welcome does not purchase military aid. The gathering refuses it after hearing what intervention would cost particular households: Kargath has settled a blood dispute and protected a well; other families have sons in his service; Zarina will not commit hers. The sixty-three riders who later assemble at the sheepfold come under three commanders with separate obligations. Horse holders require a share, Skilur wants his brother brought home, and payment of arrears does not enlist the recipients under Fenwood. Their cooperation has to be agreed, then tested on the ground.

The victory before and after the duel

The captured accounts show Elyas why Kargath has been paying armed houses on both banks of the Belworth. He wants Viterra and Vhasia fighting while he takes Acasia. Elyas imagines his father feeding the displaced families and sending him across to Harwick when the grain runs short. He recognizes two lords who could come to blows over a grain cart. If even one refused a truce signed by Oswyn and Braedyn, Elyas would have to make a separate peace with him at the ford. Kargath gains if either government loses the power to stop the fighting.

Intercepting the supplies does not settle the campaign. Kargath disputes the captured salt, offers money to the eastern grain house and withdraws the watch protecting a well used by Skilur’s mother’s people. His earlier service to them was real. The coalition has to replace that protection. Elyas contributes his travel money; Skilur pledges his saddle horse and two mares to secure grain. Loyalty becomes something the allies must make possible for one another.

The six-move duel ends Kargath’s life, not all the work around it. The night’s vigil, burial and Rekedim’s decision to remain on the steppe give the departure its human duration. Riarhin’s discovery of the Kalrantha catalog leads toward the southern investigation; Elyas carries evidence and surviving companions home. They leave with different responsibilities.

The board and the unfinished letter

Before leaving Tarlington, Riarhin enters Braedyn’s study, recognizes the position on the dusty Canray board and leaves without moving a piece. At the exchange she begins a note to Braedyn: she misses supper in his study and hearing him complain about the cook. At the final parting, after straightening Elyas’s buckle and saying goodbye to Lyra, she rereads the note and tries to add to it. Alric offers to send it with Elyas’s papers. She keeps it unfinished. The board and letter give the departure a particular household she misses, alongside the companions choosing other roads.

Silkstrand connections · Spoilers for The Thread, No Bruises and Blood and Silk

Returning to Silkstrand brings Riarhin a memory of Kes before he was Old Kes: a black, badly cut beard, three missing fingers, and bread left on the second stair. After Bea’s death, she sometimes slept in his warehouse loft. His care belongs to the years before Ghere found her and took her away; it does not replace her nights on the streets with a settled childhood. When she once returned to an empty stair, Kes had simply forgotten to put the bread down. She remembers how angry she was.

Readers of No Bruises and Blood and Silk will recognize the fence who feeds and shelters young people. Serafine, the Coin’s apprentices and Riarhin know different periods of his life. Later in The Thread, Lena leaves a piece of bread beside Riarhin’s cup while Riarhin complains about the failed plan. Neither identifies their shared connection. The gesture carries the echo without a reunion or an exchange of names; Kes’s exact age and manner of death remain unspecified.

The southern expedition · Spoilers for The Weight You Carry and the opening of The Face You Bear

The southern journey brings Riarhin into Sada’s public duties and a company whose members have different kinds of authority. Inside the ruined city, understanding the waterworks remains essential to finding a route. Something is also following them: dust draws inward, outlines distort, and the weight of their equipment pulls toward an upright emptiness. Whether it hunts the expedition or drives it toward the repository remains unresolved.

Riarhin and the great stair

Kebede discovers that stone has been stripped from the piers beneath the great stair. When the presence blocks the road to the service door, Riarhin uses his warning. She draws it onto the failing steps, jumps clear on a rope held by Na’ar, and climbs as the vaults collapse beneath it. The road opens for the others. Her breathless laugh afterwards belongs to the exhilaration of having done something extraordinary and survived it. The feat gets the company out of an immediate trap; the pursuit later resumes.

Meret’s defenses

Meret is a keeper of the Sealed Gate. An archive clerk who reads echoes has drawn her at a sealed door with a robed judge looming over her. In the margin she asks him to go back and draw the hinges. With chalk, cord, iron filings and her chain of rings, she holds crossings, drives the presence back and shelters the company through the night. Sill attends to the boundaries. These rites give the others time to repair, examine and move; they do not identify what follows them. Her finding about the amulet remains carefully limited: she has found no working she recognizes.

What comes home

Zewdu is taken while helping the exhausted Meret over the last step of the cutting. He has stayed outside it as instructed. Riarhin and Hailu have hold of him when his weight vanishes; Meret’s blessing on Riarhin’s bow keeps her footing but cannot keep Zewdu with them. No body is recovered. They search and wait through another dawn interval before Hailu packs his brother’s belongings. Six people return south: the five city explorers and Hailu.

The loss remains present in the missing imitation of Hailu’s voice, in Riarhin’s custody account, and in the brief naming of Zewdu at Valora in The Face You Bear. Na’ar’s wound still requires care. The meals, affection and final intimacy with Sada retain their place in the return. Kalrantha stays wrapped and unworn; neither its recovery nor the successful defenses establish what the object does or whether it has any connection to the pursuer.

Faith at court · Spoilers for the Belworth histories and war, The Face You Bear, The Blood in the Fold and The Harvest of Leaves
  • Alaysha and Myrmis. Veralyn’s mother believes her daughter blessed by the patron. In Count Your Exits, her private note calls Veralyn “Myrmis’s blessed hand” while she takes her assigned place at the Temple’s public prayers. Her older court circle carries this devotion into royal life.
  • The Everflame faction. Certain canons want to anoint Veralyn Empress of a restored Utar. Their prayer reaches Jaennis in Tributary; in Estuary, Fayd asks whether their offer entails obedience. A restored Utar remains a political project.
  • The Temple or Unity of Light. Already established in Viterra, it seeks wider influence at the Everflame’s expense. Access to imperial schools and control of teaching houses give its good wishes a practical purpose.

Veralyn decides what to accept. In The Face You Bear she crosses out the premature imperial title and allows the Light to send teachers while retaining the houses. In The Harvest of Leaves the rival circles’ claims reach the linen cupboard and the feast arrangements; she mixes their guests and chooses the ordinary blessing. Their influence does not make her decisions theirs.

The wedding clerk in The Blood in the Fold knows every word of the blessing and would like one chapel gift entered twice. Public piety leaves room for that small dishonesty as well as genuine belief.

The crown and Theona · Spoilers for The Face You Bear, The Names You Take, The Blood in the Fold and The Harvest of Leaves

At Harald’s public acknowledgement, Elyas hears the herald call Veralyn “Queen of Viterra and of the Isles of Theona, Duchess of Aberiel and Protector of Vhasia.” He has heard the islands named since childhood. Vhasia is the new addition. Several years after Valdais’s defeat, the Vhasian lords who signed the protection agreements appointed her Protector. The title was on the coins before Harald acknowledged it for Istovar. On Theona the moots elect Conall, the Three-Isles King. Viterra sends a Protector of its own to the islands, where his commission buys little obedience.

At supper in The Face You Bear, Deirdre calls herself the queen’s “good subject” to recover a harbor bond. Her niece asks whether Conall knows she’s left him. By the end of The Names You Take, Veralyn holds much of Acasia and the spring muster points toward Ecktoria. Now the crown addresses “our good subjects in the Isles of Theona” and asks how many horses their ships can carry. In The Harvest of Leaves, the envoy refuses every man and ship. In Valora, Ridow hears people call that treachery. It makes him afraid even to answer a letter from the islands about a grammar book for Lyra.

Na’ar’s lesson, Riarhin’s knife throws and the Midwinter fights · Spoilers for The Face You Bear and The Harvest of Leaves

In Chapter Eight of The Face You Bear, Berent borrows Tavish’s practice swords at Glenruan. Na’ar catches him stepping in every time a point comes near him, then demonstrates against Riarhin. With blades of equal length, his greater reach and short steps keep her outside her own striking distance. When she tries to press him against the trough, he steps sideways and catches her above the elbow. His old leg injury shapes the footwork. Berent’s delighted conclusion is that he can beat her in a fair fight. “Yes,” Na’ar says. “But Riarhin doesn’t fight fair.” Berent then keeps his distance and lands a touch himself.

Berent asks what Riarhin would do if her opponent refused to come closer. “Find something to make him move.” With her right hand she throws Toothpick into a thumbnail-sized knot from twelve paces, then into the same cut from fifteen. Berent examines the wood before returning the knife. Riarhin picks up her basket; Elspeth is still waiting with the eggs. The extraordinary accuracy belongs to an ordinary morning together.

In The Harvest of Leaves, Tylan’s quick recovery brings that touch back to Riarhin. He keeps his point between them, gives little ground and waits for help from the stair. She draws him onto the linen stair, where the uneven tread gives her a moment to get inside his reach. He throws her against the screen and wounds her even there. The practice bout makes the difficulty familiar before the fight becomes lethal.

Veralyn has cleared her room to favor her own reach and speed. Riarhin enters already hurt. At the hearth, she steps sideways along the edge as Na’ar did beside the trough. She remembers the pale knot fifteen paces away, but the right shoulder Tylan injured will not let her raise her arm. She flicks Toothpick upwards from her hip. The bad throw is parried; Veralyn’s point goes up and away, and Riarhin closes beneath it. The remembered accuracy makes the lost movement tangible. Berent’s earlier question also has its answer: the knife can make an opponent move even when it does not land.

The Architecture

The cycle is built like a set of nested boxes.

The Widow’s Hand establishes the legal doctrine. It gives you the Peace of Belworth (785), the Right of Acknowledgment, and the Act of Settlement (797). It shows you how a person who is not a party to a contract can become a term in it — a thing that cannot be struck out without collapsing the whole document. It is the foundation.

A Roll of the Fallen shows you what happens when you live inside that doctrine and discover that you are in the ledger. Alaysha Renard is entered in her father’s codex before she is asked anything. She spends her life trying to read the price column. She cannot. The novella is written as a codex, with five leaves, each one a name and a price.

Count Your Exits shows you what happens when you decide to break the doctrine. Veralyn Everblood is a weapon. She was a weapon at fifteen and she is a weapon at twenty-three, and she says “No” when she is asked to count her exits. The novella is written as a manual for a game — Kon’reh, the game of encirclement and escape — and it teaches you how to read the other two.

Paget shows you what the breaking costs, item by item, in a folder of forty-five documents. It is a correspondence between a widow under suspicion of murder and a girl of sixteen who is not present at her own life. It is also a love story, and it is also a dossier of treason, and the two are the same thing.

The Argument

The cycle is about the difference between being a party and being a term.

A party signs. A term is signed. A party has standing. A term has value. A party can refuse. A term can only be refused. A party is present at the reading. A term is in the schedule.

Every major character is trying to move from one category to the other. Every one of them fails. The failure is the story.

  • Elianor Longblood is Item the Fourth. She becomes a term that cannot be struck. She succeeds. The cost is that she is alone.
  • Alaysha Renard is entered in the codex before she is asked. She tries to read the price. She cannot.
  • Veralyn Everblood is a weapon. She says “No” and rides north.
  • Jaennis Fenwood arranges a room. She collects. She does not display.
  • Vaelrie Longblood puts her hand down anyway.

Part II The Instruments

The cycle is full of documents. These are the ones that matter.

Item the Fourth of the Peace of Belworth (785)

Item. The demoiselle Elianor, daughter of Aymer, Duke of Adria, of the house called Longblood, to be given in marriage to the lord Baldvyn, second son of the king’s body, in surety of the peace among the western cantons and of the quiet of the Belworth crossings; the said marriage to be solemnised at the feast of Saint Ossian following her seventeenth year; and by this the said crossings shall be held open, and the said house of Longblood shall be held faithful, and the crown shall hold the said house in its grace.

This is the clause that makes Elianor a term. She is not a party; she is the surety. She is the consideration. She is the thing being exchanged.

The second paragraph is standard, and it is the clause that makes everything possible:

And if the said marriage be not solemnised, for whatsoever cause, the surety shall be taken in such other manner as the parties shall then agree; and this Item shall not thereby be held to fail.

No one who drafted it, sealed it, or read it aloud gave it a moment’s thought. It is the door through which Elianor walks.

The Right of Acknowledgment

Where a man taketh up a child new-born, in his own voice, before his household, within the hour of the birth, and nameth it his, the child is his.

And this shall not be impeached afterward by any evidence of any kind, nor by likeness, nor by reckoning of days, nor by the common talk, nor by the oath of the mother, nor by the oath of the man himself; but only by proof that the said taking-up did not occur.

And the proof thereof is the household.

Three hundred years old. It exists because a kingdom in which acknowledged sons can be unmade in the third generation is a kingdom that has a civil war every reign. It works. The price of its working is that it is entirely indifferent to the truth.

It has one door in it, and the door is very small: the acknowledgment is a fact in the world, and facts in the world are proved by witnesses. Harald finds the door in four days. Elianor closes it with Clause the Eleventh.

Clause the Eleventh of the Act of Settlement (797)

Whereas the estate of the lord Valdais was acknowledged by the late king in his own voice before his household within the hour of the birth, according to the Right, and was so entered in the chamber book on the same day; the said Right and the said entry are recited herein at length, and the parties do covenant and acknowledge the same; and this present marriage, and the peace of the western cantons and the quiet of the Belworth crossings thereupon depending, are made upon the faith of it.

This is Elianor’s masterwork. She takes a finding of law and converts it into a term of a contract. Now no one can pull on the acknowledgment of Valdais without pulling on the king’s marriage, the legitimacy of every child of it, the title to four hundred miles of border, and the seat under his own duke.

It has never been challenged in a hundred and forty years.

The Codex at Kaerndal

A book, in a locked press, in the muniment room. No prayers. No invocation. A list of names, and beside each name, what was paid.

It has four hands in it:

  1. The house hand, which is obvious and which changes every generation.
  2. A small upright hand that appears eleven times in two hundred years, always for a single line, always to make a correction. It has never been seen to add anything.
  3. A large slow hand that appears four times, always a name with no price beside it.
  4. A set of marks in the gutter of the tenth quire, at intervals, over about sixty years.

The Renards have built a religion out of handwriting over four generations. The truth is simpler: there are four hands because four people wrote in it. The corrector is a clerk of the Mound. The large slow hand is a Cantor. The marks are marks.

The Renards never went to the Mound to ask. It is eleven miles from her father’s hall. A masked man in hammered bronze would have given Alaysha the whole doctrine in forty seconds. She never went.

The ROCKE Leaf

ROCKE

Paid: all of them, and it was not enough.

There is a strike under ROCKE. One fine ruled line, ink over ink. Someone wrote an entry, and someone else struck it through and wrote ROCKE above it.

Alaysha measures the strike. Against the letters above it and below it, and against four other entries in the same hand. Three times, over two days, with a thread. And then perhaps forty times since.

Six letters. ROCKE is five.

She never reads it. The corrector has been in the codex twice in nineteen years. The second time was eleven days after her son’s funeral. There is a word in her own price column now, in the corrector’s hand, six letters long.

Kon’reh / Canray / Kanry / Kanrāy

The game of encirclement and escape. Played before hunting. Five names, five peoples, one doctrine.

The tenets

  1. Count to five. Every plan is a Reforge plan. If taking a thing today cannot be held for five of their turns, it is not a victory, it is a trap you have laid for yourself and walked into first.
  2. Own the ring. The four squares in the middle are a lever. The ring is the road. Boys want the middle. Quartermasters want the ring.
  3. Weld in two plies. One piece to close the file, one piece to print a fork two moves ahead.
  4. Seed is a depot, not a daring.
  5. Exits before enemies. A closed line cuts on their turn.
  6. Respect stagger. A piece that has just spent its second special is rooted.

There is a fourth sentence that is not in the literature because it is not a move. It has no notation. Two people in this account are known to have played it. One of them wrote afterward, in a manual, in ink, that it must never be done.

The Joiner’s Bill

For one chair, a small elbow-chair with an ash frame, taken from the long store, re-seated in canvas and rush, and carried up to the east room over the yard.

Receipted and paid.

The third of Cinaria, 841.

Six days before Vaelrie wrote to Jaennis about Corbin Woodgate.

Jaennis kept it with the letters and numbered it last.

Part III The Women

Elianor Longblood (c. 769–840)

Thrice married. Twice queen. Duchess of Ethor.

She is Item the Fourth of the Peace of Belworth. She is a girl of sixteen in a cold room, sitting on an empty chest, talking to a boy about a stained-glass serpent.

She learns at sixteen that being correct is not a form of power. It is a decoration. She learns that she was never a party to the case. She was the case.

She spends the next fifty years making certain that no one can ever do that to her again. She owns the record. She learns what pepper cost in nine separate years. She builds an instrument that cannot be struck.

And at the end, in a locked house with a guard on the stair, when a young woman asks her what it cost — itemised, in that column — she opens her mouth, and closes it. She does not have the figure. Nobody has ever asked her to add it up.

She never went back for it.

Her question: What is the price of a life’s work, and who keeps the column?

Alaysha Renard (b. 798)

Queen-Mother of Viterra. Keeper of the codex.

She is a girl of eleven who touches the nail and the mare and stops at the press. She is a girl of fourteen who measures a strike with a thread. She is a woman of twenty-three who asks an old queen what it cost, and receives no answer, and begins her own book that winter.

She kills a hound on the night before her wedding and asks for two great warriors and three lovely daughters. She gets three daughters, one of whom is a warrior. She has been doing the arithmetic for twenty-five years and it never comes out.

Her son is the good one. They kill the good one.

She has a word in her price column in a hand she cannot read, six letters long. She has been trying to read it for two years. She could go to the Mound. It is eleven miles from the hall she was born in. She has not gone.

Her question: Which hand answered me — and what happens if it was both?

Veralyn Everblood (b. 819)

Queen of Viterra. Called Blackrose, then the Warrior Queen.

She is a girl of eleven who comes up the Belworth road with nine in the party and a wooden sword she was told to leave at Valora. She is a girl of fifteen who goes to Lence in a supply wagon. She is a girl of fifteen who burns a village and cannot remember the afternoon.

She is a woman of nineteen who kills her cousin with his own dagger on an altar. She is a woman of twenty-three who confirms the sentence of the woman who tried to destroy her, and then does it herself, in one, with the black sword. She wipes it herself. She says: My lords. We have a kingdom to hold. I shall be in the muniment room.

She was taught the board for nine years by a Ykrul sergeant who never once let her win. She learned to own the ring and count the exits and weld in two plies. And after Eryas, the pause is gone. Not shortened. Gone.

She says “No” when she is asked to count them. Once. Out loud. Even if it is nought.

Her question: What does it cost to be the one holding the sword — and what happens when you stop taking the pause?

Jaennis Fenwood (b. 817)

Widow of Baldvyn III. Keeper of the file. Collector of women.

She is a sickly girl of nine who cannot go out. She is a girl of thirteen who does the arithmetic and arrives at a certainty that the thing she is for will kill her. She keeps a locked box of dried preparations and has been quietly, carefully, expensively obtaining items from it for eleven years.

She is a woman of twenty-three who marries a king and buries him three months later. She is a woman of twenty-four who is put in a house forty miles from anywhere, with four women and a cat, and who keeps every letter she receives and makes press-copies of her own.

She teaches a girl of sixteen how to arrange a room. She asks for a morning when the house is empty. She sends the date to her uncle by her own rider, with a covering note she writes herself, and she does not hesitate.

She keeps a joiner’s bill for a chair, dated six days before the girl was in trouble. She tells the girl, in the fourth month, with everything still to gain, that someone put the chair there.

I am not going to be one of, those women who collects girls the way other people collect saints the’ bones.

She collects. She does not, display.

Her question: What is the difference between collecting and displaying — and does it survive contact with the text?

The Fifth Woman: Vaelrie Longblood (b. 824)

Called Paget. Called Anne Corr. Called V. Longblood.

She is a girl of sixteen who writes a condolence letter she did not compose and is found out in nine days. She is a girl who moves a chair in her father’s business room and watches a man sit in it and stay two hours. She is a girl who gives away a mare to a drover’s daughter on the road and cannot explain why.

She tells a woman in a letter that the house will be empty on the fifth of Lavius. She gets on a boat and is on the water when thirty-one men die at a gravel bar.

She writes: I am coming.

She lives in the house with the chair by the window for the rest of the period covered by any record. She keeps the household books from 844 and keeps them very well. She signs herself V. Longblood, plainly, in every hand she ever wrote again — including in the ledger, where a clerk is not required to sign at all.

Her question: What does it mean to stop being a Paget — and is the answer a happy ending or a tragedy?

Part IV The Motifs

The Chair

  • The empty chest in the side room at Belworth, on which Elianor sits at sixteen and is told not to.
  • The chair with the good light at Valora, which Elianor arranges for herself and which has no second seat.
  • The chair by the window at Valvano House, bought on an ordinary Hammer day, six days before Vaelrie was in trouble.
  • The chair moved closer to the fire by Sir Harlan Tasford, in a room Vaelrie had arranged, which is the first thing she ever does that works.

Someone put that chair there. You should know, always, and about everybody, and especially about me.

The Pause

  • Elianor counts the buttons on her wedding dress. Twenty-three eyes. She counts them again at forty-two.
  • Alaysha measures a strike with a thread. Three times, over two days. And then perhaps forty times since.
  • Veralyn takes the pause before the middle of a game. After Eryas, it is gone.
  • Jaennis counts her fingers at mass. Three and three and one. Her mother did it before her.

She does not need the pause. Or she has stopped being able to take it. These are not the same and I cannot tell them apart from the outside.

Rekedim Many-Scars, in the margin of a game sheet

The Stone

  • Elianor places the first earth on Gyllan’s grave carefully. She does not carry a stone for anybody else.
  • Alaysha buries a hound with a folded ear outside a wall at Valora before dawn on the morning of her wedding. She has been back ten thousand times. She knows the place. Twenty-seven years. No stone.
  • Mourne Renard carried one for a cousin. For a tenant. For a horse. I am keeping the count.

Afterwards, who carries the stones?

The Nail

  • Seven nails in eleven miles, marking houses where judgment may be sought. Alaysha counts them herself when she is nine.
  • The nail in the lintel at Kaerndal, driven in crooked, which the smith twice offered to draw and reset and was twice refused.
  • The nail in the lintel at the disused tack-room at Valora, driven by Vaelrie on the night before her wedding, with four blows, which she had practiced so that it would be four.
  • Eleven nails drawn by Wyllmot Everblood’s tenure rulings, over four years, legally, without one death.

The Button

  • Eleven small covered buttons at each wrist. Four women fasten them in pairs, two at each arm, working inward. The sound is very small and very regular, like something eating.
  • Elianor undoes one button at her left wrist before her second wedding. It is noticed. It is meant to be noticed.
  • Baldvyn sees it. He laughs out loud, once, badly, as though the machinery were rusted. The court decides he has been overcome.

The Strike

  • The strike under ROCKE. One fine ruled line, ink over ink. Thorough. Six letters long.
  • The strike in Alaysha’s price column. A word in the corrector’s hand. Six letters long. She has been trying to read it for two years.
  • The strike of the blade at Valora, in the yard, at the sixth hour, with three hundred people present.

The Room

  • The press-room at Avimont, where Vaelrie’s mother’s clothes were, which is empty and has been empty for seven years.
  • The east room over the yard at Valvano House, with a window that gets the morning light, and a chair.
  • The muniment room at Kaerndal, with a locked press and a fire and a bed brought in for a dying man.
  • The locked house with a guard on the stair, where Elianor is kept, and where the fodder accounts of the northern garrisons continue to arrive, because nobody has ever given an order about it either way.

The Hand

  • The Red Hand is war as it is suffered: the burning thatch.
  • The Cold Hand is war as it is made: the drill and the supply line and the siege geometry.
  • The flat hand on the table is what Valdais does when he is thinking about something bad. If you see the flat hand, you should not speak.
  • The hand that strikes and the hand that writes it down are the same act. Myrmis accepts neither answer.

Part V The Questions

The cycle leaves a great deal open. This is deliberate. These are the questions, and what the cycle does and does not tell you about them.

Who was Valdais Longblood’s father?

The cycle does not say. The Right of Acknowledgment does not admit the question. Clause the Eleventh forecloses it. Rumor names Baldvyn; the annals record four lines and a psalter and a road that made no sense.

Valdais himself used the same answer to the question all his life. At Sennet, in 841, at the third jug, a man asked him about his father and the table went silent. He said: Which one? And the whole room came apart laughing.

Vaelrie lay awake afterward, because she had heard him do that four or five times in her life and it is always the same answer and it has never once been a joke about him.

What are the six letters?

The cycle does not say. The strike under ROCKE measures six letters. The word in Alaysha’s price column is six letters long.

The obvious candidates are ROCKES, RENARD, ALAYSE, and any number of six-letter names. The cycle gives you none of them.

The doctrine, which any monk of the Mound could have given Alaysha in forty seconds, is that there are two hands, and the question which hand answered me is not a hard question. It is a malformed one. It is, precisely and exactly, the disclaiming.

She never went. Nobody in her family had gone in four generations.

What did Jaennis Fenwood mean by “the betrayal of the court and the queen’s body”?

The cycle gives you four readings and declines to choose. The editor says: The editor has a preference and is keeping it.

There is a fifth reading, available only to a reader who has noticed that Ylena of Coldmorrow was in Jaennis’s household from 837, and that her name appears on nothing after the fifth of Lavius, 841. The editor says: It is left where she left it.

Did Jaennis Fenwood know the ford at Kettling would be held?

She sent the date. She wrote the covering note herself. She did not hesitate.

She says: I am not going to tell you I agonised. I did not.

What she does not say is whether she knew what the date was worth, or whether she knew it when she asked for it, or whether she asked for it because she knew.

She says: I knew what it was worth. Both of those are true at once and neither cancels the other.

What became of Ylena of Coldmorrow?

The cycle tells you what became of the woman of the household. She was taken, tried, convicted, and sentenced. The sentence was carried out on the fifth of Lavius, 841, in another place and at an hour set down in its own register.

The cycle does not tell you whether she did it. The evidence was sufficient and never conclusive. She never confessed. She was condemned in a hall in which every single person had learned four days earlier whose daughter she was.

Veralyn Everblood understood all of that perfectly. She had written it down in Floralis. She had it in her own hand in the muniment room ninety feet away.

She confirmed the sentence.

What became of Vaelrie Longblood?

She lived in Jaennis Fenwood’s house for the rest of the period covered by any record here. She did not marry. She kept the household books from 844 and kept them very well. She signed herself V. Longblood, plainly, in every hand she ever wrote again.

She was called Paget by nobody after 841.

Whether that is a happy ending or a tragedy is not for the cycle to say, and it does not say.

What did Elianor Longblood’s life cost?

She could not produce the figure. She opened her mouth, and closed it. Nobody had ever asked her to add it up. She had never once opened a column for herself.

She thought about it for nineteen years. There is a line in a letter of hers, written at seventy to a man in a priory at Ancourt, that is the nearest she ever came:

I have been trying since the spring of the third year to draw up a particular account, at the instance of a lady who asked me for it very civilly and has never mentioned it since. I find I cannot arrive at a figure. I find I cannot arrive at the first entry. You will laugh at me; do, I should be glad of it.

What is the cycle for?

It is about the difference between collecting and displaying.

It is about the difference between a relic under a cloth and a relic on a table.

It is about the difference between a term that cannot be struck and a party to the contract.

And it is about the moment when someone lifts the cloth, and tells you what is on the table, and waits to see whether you put your hand down anyway.

Vaelrie put her hand down.

She wrote: I am coming.

And the joiner’s bill is dated six days before she was in trouble, and it is receipted and paid, and it is the last item in the folder, and it does not prove anything at all.

It just says the chair was there.
