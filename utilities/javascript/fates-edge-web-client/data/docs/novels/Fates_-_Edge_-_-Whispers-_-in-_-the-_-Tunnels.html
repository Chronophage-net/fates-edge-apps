A Lerris Fenwood novel — Book III

Part I — The Guest

Chapter 1 — Beyond the River

The ferryman wanted the dog off his boat.

The dog had already crossed twice that morning. It belonged to nobody in Dusana’s company and had acquired the habit of traveling with whoever brought food to the landing. When the ferryman advanced with an oar, it went beneath the wagon. When he went round the wagon, it came out by the wheel and looked at him from the place he had just left.

“You feed it,” Dusana said.

“I don’t.”

“Your wife does.”

“Then let it cross with my wife.”

The woman holding the lead horse laughed. Lerris was standing beside her with his bag against his knees. The Black River moved beneath the landing in broad folds, shouldering the little boat sideways against its ropes. He had been watching the water rather than the argument. At each movement of the hull he felt the remembered pull beneath his feet, although the planks remained solid and the ferryman had no difficulty standing on them.

“Take your bag over,” the woman said. “You aren’t helping it by watching.”

He stepped down. The boat shifted. He gripped the upright and stayed there longer than he meant to.

Behind him the horse snorted.

“Go on, master.”

He went on. At the far side he put his bag beneath a bench, then took it out again because a little water lay there. The ferryman noticed and told him where to put it. He sounded tired rather than reassuring. Lerris was grateful until he saw that the dog had boarded beside him without hesitation.

They crossed with the wagon, two horses, six people, and the dog. Dusana remained on the landing for the next load. Lerris watched her diminish, her bright scarf still distinct when her face had become impossible to read. A man beside him began talking about the river higher up, where a new bank had carried away three fields and left the boundary stones standing in water. Lerris listened. He asked a question about the owner, and the man explained that ownership had become less useful to discuss than who would pay for the boat needed to reach the remaining orchard.

Halfway across, the dog vomited.

The ferryman looked at it.

“There,” he said to everybody.

Lerris laughed before he could stop himself. The man with the orchard story laughed too. When the boat struck the far landing, he had forgotten to hold the upright.

They spent most of the day getting the company across. Wheels had to be lifted over a broken place. A bundle came untied. The ferryman’s wife brought food, fed the dog, and denied this to her husband while still holding the piece it had not finished. By the time the last wagon stood on the northern road, the shadows had passed the good part of the afternoon.

Lerris expected them to hurry.

Instead they stopped at a house a little way from the landing, where the woman with the horse was owed money.

He sat outside beneath an apple tree while the debt was discussed. Through the open window he heard two people who knew each other well arrive, with increasing energy, at incompatible accounts of a coat. Dusana joined him carrying a heel of bread and a little cheese wrapped in cloth.

“Does this happen often?” he asked.

“What?”

“Business on the road.”

She looked at him until he heard himself.

“All right.”

She divided the cheese. He took his portion.

The tree had dropped small hard fruit into the grass. One lay split beside his boot, a wasp working in it. He moved his foot carefully away.

“You can still go south,” Dusana said.

He stopped chewing.

“The boat will be there tomorrow. You could sleep in that house if you paid. They have a room.”

“Have you asked?”

“I’ve slept in it.”

“Would you prefer I went?”

“I’d prefer you stopped asking me to want things for you.”

Inside, the woman raised her voice. Someone had introduced the price of dye.

Lerris looked down at the cheese. He had been hoping the choice would become clearer as the keep grew nearer. The road had provided ferries, money, meals, and a painful place on his heel. None of these had altered the name he had seen in the glass.

“Will you tell me what you know about him?”

“What would you do with it?”

“Decide.”

“You’ve decided several times.”

“I could decide differently.”

Dusana ate the last of her bread. For a while he thought she would leave him with that.

“My mother’s brother took cloth north,” she said. “He knew where he was going. He knew what would be paid. He left his good boots with her because he meant to buy better ones.”

“Did he come back?”

“Yes.”

She folded the cheese cloth into a square.

“He wouldn’t take the boots. She put them beside him. He asked whose they were. They argued. She thought he was making some joke she’d failed to understand, and then she thought he was ashamed of having no money. She was cruel to him over it.”

“What had happened?”

“He didn’t tell her.”

“Was he unable to?”

“I wasn’t there.”

The answer annoyed him, then made him ashamed. He had been about to turn her uncle into a question about a power.

“Did she keep them?”

“For years. They didn’t fit anyone else.”

The argument inside ended with a chair being moved. Dusana stood and brushed crumbs from her skirt.

“You can sleep here,” she said again.

That evening he wrote a letter. He told Markus they had crossed safely. He described the dog because his brother would enjoy it, then found himself writing that the journey was proceeding without difficulty. He crossed that out. There had been no attack, no false door, no voice from a dead person. He did not know whether this was useful evidence.

I am still going, he wrote instead.

He had not received an answer to his admission about the sleep-story. It could scarcely have reached Markus yet. He knew that and nevertheless looked for a letter whenever anyone approached from the south.

The woman settled her debt with money and half the disputed coat, which had been cut into useful pieces some months before. She came out carrying both with an expression of satisfaction Lerris could not reconcile with what he had heard through the window.

They left in the morning.

For the first mile he could see the apple tree behind them. The upstairs window caught the sun. He had noticed the bed while the householder showed him where to wash: a narrow bed beneath a sloping ceiling, with a quilt worn white along its folded edge.

He continued walking until the house disappeared behind the bend.

Chapter 2 — The Last Delivery

The northern road did not become empty. That was what Lerris remembered afterwards.

There were carts of firewood, people coming back from a fair, a boy driving three reluctant pigs. At one village they waited behind a funeral until the bearers had cleared the narrow street. A woman following the coffin stopped to mend her shoe with a strip torn from her underskirt, and the others waited without looking impatient. Dusana took off her headscarf as they passed. Lerris did the same with his cap.

He kept expecting some visible boundary to explain why she had not wanted to come.

The fields grew smaller. Stone gathered at their edges in long heaps with grass between the pieces. At the inns the shutters were closed earlier, though the evenings remained warm enough for Lerris to wish them open. People answered questions about the keep by explaining the road to the next village. They were not unfriendly. One woman packed him an extra egg after discovering how much he had paid for his supper elsewhere.

On the seventh morning Dusana stopped beside an abandoned tollhouse.

Part of its roof had fallen. The standing end sheltered a heap of road gravel, and somebody had used the old barrier as a rail for drying harness. Beyond it, the road divided. One branch followed the stream; the other rose between bare slopes toward trees.

“This is where you wait,” she said.

He looked at the broken roof.

“For whom?”

“The men from the village.”

“You’ve sent word?”

“The innkeeper did.”

“When?”

“Last night. You were there.”

He had been there. He remembered a conversation across the room in a language he followed badly. He had asked about it afterwards and been told they were discussing a delivery.

“Me.”

Dusana tightened a strap beneath the wagon bed.

“Your bag. Yourself. The note you wanted taken ahead. Yes.”

He had given her the note. His name and request for an audience were written in his own hand. No hidden intelligence was needed to know a Fenwood scholar approached the keep.

“Will you wait?”

“We have somewhere to go.”

“How will I find you?”

She pointed down the branch beside the stream.

“The cooper’s yard. Ask for us there. If we’ve left, they’ll know the next market.”

“How long?”

“Three days before we move on. Unless the cooper has lied about being ready.”

Lerris set his bag on the tollhouse step. He had imagined a farewell containing useful last advice. Dusana was occupied with the strap, which would not pass through its buckle. He offered to hold the loose end. She let him.

When it was fastened, she tested it with both hands.

“Have you money apart from what you’re carrying?”

“A letter of credit.”

“Where?”

“In the case.”

“Leave a copy of the name with the cooper. If you come out without the case, you don’t want to begin by persuading strangers you were rich.”

He took out a scrap and wrote the name. She tucked it into her own papers.

“Anything else?” he asked.

She looked at him then. The others were beginning to climb onto the wagons. A child called for something she had left at the last lodging, and an adult answered with the exhausted patience of someone who had already explained where it was.

“If you want to leave, say you want to leave.”

“And if they won’t let me?”

“Then you’ll know something you didn’t when you arrived.”

She went to the leading wagon. He watched her speak to the driver, touch the horse’s neck, and step up. She did not look back at once. When she did, she lifted her hand as she might have done to anyone remaining beside the road.

He stayed on the step until the sound of wheels faded.

The tollhouse smelled of damp grain, though there was none inside. He found initials cut into the doorpost and tried not to make them significant. Two birds disputed a place in the remaining roof. He could hear insects in the long grass by the ditch.

The riders came from the upper road shortly after midday.

They wore dark red and blue. Their horses were ordinary horses, sweating beneath well-kept harness. One rider dismounted to examine a loose shoe before greeting him. The other remained in the saddle, looking at the bag.

“Master Fenwood?”

“Yes.”

“Your note reached us.”

The man held it out. Lerris recognized the seal, broken through the center rather than peeled away.

“We take you to the village,” he said. “You can eat there. Then we ride.”

“How far?”

“Three nights.”

“Nights?”

“The upper road is ours after sundown.”

“What does that mean?”

The rider examining the hoof stood up.

“It means the charcoal carts have stopped coming down it.”

He sounded amused. His companion did not.

They had brought a spare horse. Lerris had ridden before, sufficiently often to resent discovering how long it had been since the last time. The rider adjusted a stirrup, waited while he mounted, and handed him the reins without comment.

At the village they ate in a room above a store. A girl brought broth and bread, then returned with a folded blanket for the bench. She asked whether he wanted to sleep until evening.

“I’d rather go on.”

“The horses won’t.”

He ate. The men sat at another table, speaking quietly. He could not hear enough to tell whether they discussed him.

When the girl cleared the bowl, he asked her name.

She told him it was on the reckoning if he wanted to leave a tip.

“I meant—”

“I know.”

She gave him a tired smile and took the tray downstairs.

He lay down on the bench with the blanket beneath his head. Through the open window came the sound of a hammer. Someone was putting a shoe on a horse. Each blow was followed by a pause long enough for him to expect the next.

He slept badly and woke in a dark room.

For several breaths he could not move. The old chair beneath the Library seemed to be waiting just beyond his outstretched hand. He could smell soup and hot metal.

Then the hammer sounded again. He heard a horse stamp. The bench was hard beneath his hip, the window square and open above it.

He sat up. His shirt had stuck to his back.

One of the riders knocked and entered when he answered. He carried a lamp.

“Ready?”

Lerris almost said yes before looking at him.

“I need a moment.”

The man waited by the open door while he put on his boots.

Chapter 3 — The House Above the Trees

On the second night the road passed above the clouds.

They lay in the valleys, pale enough to see by, hiding the scattered lights Lerris had watched from lower down. The horses walked. Loose stones shifted under their shoes and fell away with small diminishing sounds. He kept his eyes on the tail of the animal ahead.

The rider behind him began to whistle.

“Would you mind?” Lerris asked.

The tune stopped.

After a while the man said, “Does it trouble the horse?”

“No.”

“Ah.”

He did not begin again.

They slept by day in houses belonging to people who had prepared for them. There was water to wash in, food left warm, a bed or pallet in a room whose shutter had been closed. At the second house an old woman scolded the taller rider for bringing mud over her floor. He apologized and cleaned his boots on the step. Lerris found this comforting until he wondered why the comfort seemed so necessary.

The woman offered to wash his shirt.

“I don’t have another dry one.”

“You can wear that while it dries.”

She pointed to a coat hanging beside the hearth. Its sleeves would have reached his fingers. He accepted. In the afternoon he sat wearing the coat and his breeches while she shelled beans into a bowl. One of the riders snored in the adjoining room.

“Do you work for the Baron?” he asked.

She looked up.

“I live here.”

“Of course.”

“His men mend the roof. I keep beds. My son brings flour when he comes.”

“Does he live near?”

“Nearer than he visits.”

She handed him a handful of pods. He helped badly at first, then discovered how to break them along the seam.

Above the hearth hung a small painted panel of a woman holding a branch. Smoke had darkened the upper half. He could not identify her. The old woman saw him looking and said her husband had painted it before they married.

“Who is she?”

“Me.”

He bent over the beans.

“I was trying to work out the branch.”

“So was I. He said flowers were too much work.”

She laughed. He laughed with her, relieved enough to be indiscreet.

They reached the keep before dawn on the third night.

It stood above the last trees, its outer wall built into an older wall whose stones were larger and less regular. There were signs of recent work: fresh lime at a corner, scaffolding against a tower, a cart with one wheel removed. Somebody had covered the cart’s load against rain. Lerris could smell wet wool and the stable before the gate opened.

He had expected a ruin. The keep needed more servants than any ruin could support.

In the court two men were loading a chest onto a low sledge. The younger lost his grip. Something shifted inside with a musical sound, thin and prolonged. Both men stopped. The older checked the lid before letting them lift again.

Lerris followed his escort up the steps.

The man who received him wore a dark coat cut close at the wrists. His hair had gray in it. A narrow mark showed above his collar when he turned: a chain ending in a little lamp.

“Michel,” he said when Lerris asked whom to thank. “Your bag can remain here.”

“I’d rather keep it.”

“As you wish.”

He kept the bag.

They passed through a gallery of portraits. Some were good enough to make him slow. A man seated with one glove off seemed on the point of turning toward somebody outside the frame. A woman looked directly at the painter with an expression of patient dislike. Beneath each hung a brass plate. The names had been scratched away.

He stopped before the woman.

“Was there a fire?”

“No,” Michel said.

“The plates.”

“I see them.”

Farther along, the varnish had yellowed. The faces were still young. He thought the same hand had painted several of them, but the light was poor and he had once mistaken a wife for a saint because she held a branch. He went on.

The Baron stood by a table on which three open cases had been arranged. A servant waited beside him with a list. He finished examining a little silver vessel before acknowledging Lerris.

“That one travels,” he said.

The servant made a mark.

“Master Fenwood.”

He came forward. He was tall, dark-haired, broad enough through the shoulders for the pale silk of his shirt to pull slightly when he extended a hand. A small scar marked his left cheek. Lerris had seen worse scars acquired in foolish arguments about horses.

“My lord.”

“You’ve had a poor journey.”

“Not especially.”

“You look as though you have.”

Lerris smiled because he could not think of anything else to do with his face.

On the Baron’s breast was an embroidered device he almost recognized. A figure, a broken curve, something descending through it.

“The Lost Angel,” Asselin said.

“An order?”

“Once.”

The servant shut a case. The click seemed loud in the room.

Asselin’s gaze moved to Lerris’s collar, beneath which he had tucked the small iron emblem purchased before crossing the river. The woman who sold it had assured him it was old. He had no evidence of that, nor of the several protections she attributed to it.

“Who recommended the iron?”

“No one you would know.”

“How fortunate for her.”

Lerris did not answer.

“Have you come to hunt me?”

The question was asked without a change of tone. Behind Asselin, the servant lifted another object from its nest of cloth.

“No.”

“Then you are my guest.”

“I’ve come to ask about a document.”

“Guests may ask questions.”

The Baron took a step closer. Lerris held himself still. He could see the fine stitching at the edge of the embroidered figure and a little worn place where the thread had parted.

“Hunters also ask questions. I find it useful to establish which I have admitted before feeding them.”

Lerris thought of the road behind him, the sleeping houses, the horses going back without him. He had brought a knife for bread and cord. It lay in the bag, under a folded shirt.

“A guest,” he said.

Asselin inclined his head.

“Good. Michel, see that he sleeps.”

“I would like to know when I might—”

“Afterwards.”

The Baron had already returned to the cases. He lifted the silver vessel again, frowned, and told the servant to put it among the things remaining.

Michel led Lerris out by a different door.

His room had a bed wide enough for two, a basin, a little table, and a window looking over the trees. A fire had been laid but not lit. Michel asked whether he wanted one.

“No. Thank you.”

“Food?”

“Yes.”

“Anything you cannot eat?”

He answered. Michel listened, then set the room key beside the basin.

“Is there a lock on the outside?” Lerris asked.

“On most doors.”

“Will it be used?”

“Not by me.”

After he had gone, Lerris tried the key. It worked. He turned it back, opened the door, and looked along the passage. Empty, with a runner laid over the stone and a vase of dried stems at the farther end.

He closed the door again.

The bedcover had been mended near one corner. He put his bag there, washed, and sat on the mattress to remove his boots. His feet had swollen. For a moment he was occupied entirely with easing the leather over his heel.

When he looked up, daylight had reached the tops of the trees.

He had meant to examine the window fastening. Instead he lay down beside the bag, still holding one boot, and slept.

Chapter 4 — A Place Set for Him

Michel woke him with a tray.

Lerris sat up too quickly. His heart had begun its old frantic work before he recognized the bed. The servant waited while he put down the boot.

“What hour?”

“After noon.”

“I didn’t hear you.”

“You were asleep.”

“The door was closed.”

“It wasn’t locked.”

Lerris remembered leaving the key in the basin. He looked. It remained there, beside a gray ring where water had dried.

Michel set the tray on the table. There were eggs, bread, a dish of stewed fruit, and a folded napkin beneath a knife. Lerris watched him arrange these as though the order might explain something.

“When can I see the Baron?”

“This evening.”

“I’d like to send a letter.”

“Give it to me.”

“Can I take it to the village?”

“Not and be here this evening.”

“I could leave it with the men who brought me.”

“They’ve gone.”

He looked at the food. He had been about to ask another question whose answer would leave him in the same room.

“I want to arrange my journey back.”

“I’ll tell him.”

“I’d like to arrange it now.”

For the first time Michel stopped moving things. He stood with the spoon in his hand.

“Then write that down too.”

He laid the spoon beside the fruit and left.

Lerris ate because he was hungry. The fruit was sour enough to make his mouth water. He finished it, used the bread to clean the dish, and disliked discovering how much pleasure he could take in breakfast while frightened.

He wrote two letters. The first was to the cooper’s yard, asking that Dusana be told he had arrived and wished to know where to find the company after his visit. The second went to the custodian named in his papers. He gave the date, the keep, the names he had been given, and nothing about a supernatural cause he had not established.

He folded them without sealing them. Then he sealed them. He was not sure which act would seem more foolish to anyone who read them.

Outside his room the runner ended at a stair. He followed it down, found a small court with a cistern, and stood in sunlight for the first time since arrival. The stone was warm beneath his hand. Somewhere nearby a woman was singing while she worked, repeating one line whenever the task required her to pause.

A door opened. A servant came out carrying a bucket of dirty water.

“Which way to the gate?” Lerris asked.

She showed him.

He reached it without obstruction. A man sat beneath the arch repairing a strap. Beyond him the road ran down between the trees.

“I’d like to walk.”

The man glanced at his shoes.

“I wouldn’t go far.”

“Why?”

“The rain takes the edge away. Keep to the inside.”

He went through. Nothing caught at him. The air did not change. He walked until he could no longer see the court, then farther, to where a stone had fallen across part of the road. Below it the ground dropped steeply into trees.

He could leave.

On foot, without food, carrying none of his papers, three nights from a village reached by a route he had followed in darkness. He put his hand against the fallen stone and felt a foolish desire to push it out of the way, as if clearing this one thing would improve the rest.

A hoof struck rock above him.

The woman on the horse wore riding clothes darkened at the knee with mud. Her hair was white, drawn back loosely enough that strands had escaped against her cheek. She pulled up beside him and looked down at his hand on the stone.

“Ambitious.”

“I was considering the road.”

“It is usually there.”

She dismounted. The horse shifted close to her shoulder and blew through its nose. She rubbed the place beneath its mane with two fingers.

“You’re the visitor.”

“Yes.”

“Has he fed you?”

“Yes.”

“Then you’re doing better than the last one.”

He looked at her. She smiled, with no evident pleasure in his alarm.

“The last one arrived during a fast and insisted on observing it with everyone else. He complained until midnight.”

She began leading the horse uphill. After a moment Lerris followed.

“Do you live here?”

“When he’s here.”

“And when he isn’t?”

“I prefer the horses.”

The climb left him short of breath. She noticed without slowing enough to embarrass him. At the gate she passed the reins to the man with the strap and told him the near shoe needed looking at.

“Again?”

“You can argue with the horse if you like.”

The man laughed and led it away.

She turned to Lerris.

“Come to supper. Wear something you can spill on.”

“Will the Baron be there?”

“He said this evening, didn’t he?”

“Yes.”

“Then he may.”

She went inside, leaving him with the letters still warm in his pocket.

He found Michel in the gallery. The servant accepted both envelopes, examined the addresses, and said the lower rider would take them when he went out.

“When is that?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Can I speak to him?”

“You just did.”

Lerris glanced toward the gate.

Michel tucked the letters into his coat.

“Your clothes have been brushed,” he said. “The left cuff was coming loose. I’ve had it sewn.”

“Thank you.”

The servant nodded. Lerris wanted to ask whether he could be paid to carry a third letter privately. He could already hear how the offer would sound.

He went upstairs and put on the mended shirt.

Chapter 5 — The Woman at Supper

There were four places at the table and three people to use them.

The woman from the road sat with one boot stretched toward the hearth. Michel stood beside the empty place. Lerris hesitated until she pointed to the chair opposite her.

“He isn’t coming yet.”

“Should we wait?”

“I haven’t.”

She had a piece of bread in her hand. He sat.

The room looked west. Light entered through deep windows and fell across the table in broad strips, catching scratches in the polished wood. A bowl of flowers stood near the empty chair. They were beginning to brown at the edges. Somebody had turned the freshest faces toward the room.

Michel served fish. He put a small dish of sauce beside Lerris, then another near the woman.

“Not that,” she said.

He exchanged it for a different dish without apology.

Lerris watched him go.

“Does he know everything?”

“No. He remembers everything he’s been blamed for.”

She tasted the fish. Her face changed slightly, and for a moment he thought she would send it away.

Instead she took another mouthful.

“Do you prefer this to hunting?” he asked.

Her fork stopped.

He had not intended to say it so bluntly. He had seen the marks on her hands when she passed him the bread: old cuts across the fingers, the hardening where a weapon had rested. He had seen her assess the road and the gate. None of that entitled him to the word.

“What makes you think I hunted?”

He told her badly.

She listened, then set down her fork.

“I could have been a soldier. I could have married one and carried his things until he forgot they were heavy.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You could have asked.”

“Did you?”

“Hunt him? For seven years.”

Lerris looked toward the door.

“Does he know?”

She laughed. It was a low, easy sound. He wanted, against his better judgment, to make her do it again.

“Oh, yes.”

She took the wine and filled her glass. He could see the liquid through it, dark red against the light.

“Where did you begin?”

“Too far south.”

“Why?”

“Because somebody was certain.”

She ate for a while. He forced himself to do the same. The fish had been cooked well, but he was conscious of every small bone.

“There were five of us at the start,” she said. “One went home before winter. We said he had lost his nerve. He had a sister who needed him. I remember being very fair about that.”

“What happened to him?”

“He went home.”

She looked at him until he stopped expecting another part.

“Of the others, one died of a wound we thought clean. One drank. One married a woman who kept an inn. I heard of that years later and was angry with him for longer than I’d been angry about the drinking.”

“And you kept going.”

“Yes.”

Michel entered with a covered dish. She waited while he removed the fish plates.

“You can leave that,” she said when he reached for hers.

He withdrew his hand.

There was a little flesh remaining near the tail. She worked it free while Lerris watched the space where Michel had stood.

“Did you find him here?”

“No.”

“Where?”

“In a house belonging to someone who had assured me he was dead.”

She put down the knife.

“I had no money left. My horse was lame. I remember that more clearly than the door opening. I was wondering whether he’d have the horse killed.”

“Did he?”

“No. He sent for someone to look at it.”

He had wanted a cruelty he could recognize at once.

“He was kind?”

“He was prepared.”

She pushed her plate away. Michel returned almost immediately, as though he had been waiting where he could see her hands.

The next dish was spiced meat beneath a crust. Lerris cut into it and released a smell of onions, pepper, and fat. The woman watched the steam rise.

“Do you still enjoy food?” he asked.

This time she did not laugh.

“Yes.”

He felt himself redden.

“Not as much as I remember,” she added. “But I can’t tell whether the food has changed.”

For a little while he could think of nothing to say. She seemed to appreciate that. She ate slowly, turning her glass between her fingers.

At last she said, “You’re frightened of me.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Is it?”

“It keeps you attentive.”

“It also makes me stupid.”

Her eyes lifted to his.

He had meant it lightly. It had come out with the force of a complaint.

“When I’m very frightened,” he said, “I hear half of what someone tells me. Then I remember the other half as though I’d heard it too.”

She regarded him with a new interest. He wished he had not offered it.

“You can learn to use that.”

“I’d rather learn to hear.”

The door opened.

Asselin stood there in a darker coat, one glove in his hand. The woman looked toward him. Her face altered so quickly that Lerris could not follow the change, only recognize that she had made room for his pleasure before he spoke.

“Will you ride tomorrow?” she asked.

“If the cases are ready.”

“They could be ready afterwards.”

“They could.”

He crossed to her chair and rested his bare hand briefly against the back of her neck. She became still. Lerris looked at his plate.

“Your guest has been asking about me,” she said.

“A sensible choice of company.”

Asselin took the empty chair. Michel poured wine. The Baron did not touch it.

“My lord,” Lerris began, “I would like to arrange—”

“The document. Yes.”

“My departure.”

The woman took up her fork.

Asselin looked at him. The silence lasted no longer than a courteous person might allow before answering an unexpected question.

“Have you had an answer already?”

“No.”

“Then why leave?”

“I’d like to know that I can.”

“You walked out this afternoon.”

“As far as the bend.”

“You were not stopped.”

Lerris could feel the woman eating opposite him, each small movement made careful by the conversation. He wanted her to say something, then disliked himself for wanting to recruit her.

“I’ll need a horse and someone who knows the road.”

“Of course.”

“When?”

“When we have concluded our business.”

“And if I conclude it now?”

The Baron laid the glove beside his plate.

“Then you will have traveled a considerable distance to decline the thing you requested.”

“Yes.”

Michel had stopped behind Asselin’s chair. The woman put her knife down, very softly.

At last the Baron said, “Speak to me tomorrow. I would dislike having sent a tired man into danger because he mistook exhaustion for judgment.”

He rose before Lerris found an answer. His wine remained untouched.

The woman watched the door close.

“He’s going away,” she said.

Lerris stared at her. “I know,” he said.

“Not tomorrow. Soon.”

“Are you going with him?”

She pushed the meat beneath its broken crust.

“He hasn’t said.”

The flowers near the empty chair stirred in a draught Lerris could not feel.

When Michel came for the dishes, she asked him to leave the wine.

Chapter 6 — What the Window Held

Lerris locked his door that night.

He had watched himself do it, watched the key turn and the tongue enter its recess. Then he had written the action down. He regretted that almost at once. A line in his own hand would not prove he had remained awake afterwards, nor that the lock could not be opened from elsewhere. It would only give him another thing to examine in the morning.

He left it uncrossed.

The room was warm. He opened the window a little, listened to the sound of a horse being led through the court, and sat down with the letters he had kept. Markus’s last was creased at the places his thumb habitually held it.

Ask me about other things too, his brother had written.

He knew the words without looking. He read them anyway.

Something tapped the outside of the shutter.

Lerris stayed still. The room was above the court, higher than a person standing on the paving could reach. There might be a branch. He had looked out before supper and had seen no tree near the wall.

The tap came again, impatient now.

“Open it.”

A young voice. Not one he recognized.

“Who is there?”

“I’m cold.”

He went to the window and put his hand on the latch. He could see a narrow piece of sky through the gap. Then a finger appeared against the edge of the shutter.

It had too many joints.

He stepped back so quickly that he struck the table. The candle shook. Wax ran onto Markus’s letter.

“Oh,” the voice said.

The shutter moved inward. Lerris had not lifted the latch. He watched the iron remain in place while the wood passed it, then found a boy sitting on the sill with his knees drawn up beneath his chin.

He might have been sixteen. His clothes were old-fashioned in a way Lerris could not date. One sleeve had been turned back and sewn in place; the other hung over the hand. His face was quite ordinary until he looked directly at Lerris. Then the distance between his eyes seemed to require more room than his head contained.

Lerris looked at the sleeve.

“Did Michel send you?”

“No.”

“The Baron?”

“No.”

“Then why are you here?”

“You have a light.”

The boy shifted. His foot reached the floor without his body leaving the sill. Lerris could not bear to follow the movement. He fixed his eyes on the candle, on the wax creeping into a familiar letter’s fold.

“Please stay there.”

The foot withdrew.

For a moment neither spoke.

“Do you have a name?” Lerris asked.

“Do you need it?”

“I’d like something to call you.”

“You could say please again.”

He sounded pleased with the answer. Lerris might have laughed if his stomach had allowed it.

The boy looked at the table.

“You’ve spoiled that.”

“I can lift the wax when it’s cold.”

“It takes the ink.”

“Sometimes.”

“I know.”

His expression sharpened. “I used to know.”

Lerris left the letter alone. The room seemed smaller with someone occupying the window. He could still see the door. The key was on his side.

“What did you know?”

“Where to put things.”

“Books?”

“Anything. A house. A man. If you put him in the right place he can’t hurt you.”

The boy began moving one finger against the stone sill. There was no tool in his hand. A pale line followed the finger.

“Don’t,” Lerris said.

It stopped.

“You haven’t seen what it does.”

“No.”

“How can you know you don’t want it?”

“I don’t want it in this room.”

The boy studied him. Then he rubbed the line with his sleeve. It remained visible, but no longer lengthened.

“I did three,” he said.

“Three what?”

“His. Before he came.”

Lerris could hear his own pulse. The boy was speaking with the pride of someone remembering work done well.

“Did he do this to you?”

The boy looked down at his hands.

“He stopped it.”

Lerris waited.

“It kept going. Everything I looked at had another part behind it. If I shut my eyes there were more. I couldn’t eat without knowing where the food would go. I couldn’t put my hand down.”

He held it above the sill. The fingers trembled.

“Now I can.”

He laid his palm flat. He watched it rest there, smiling a little.

In the court below, somebody called to a horse. Lerris heard the scrape of a pail. He wanted those people in the room with him. He wanted to open the door and call Michel and disliked the name that had come first to mind.

“Are you cold still?”

“Yes.”

“There is a blanket.”

“Will you bring it?”

Lerris took the spare blanket from the bed. He approached slowly, trying not to look at the places where the boy’s shape failed to agree with the space around it.

“May I put it here?”

The boy nodded.

He laid it beside him and stepped back. The blanket unfolded, lifted, and settled round the narrow shoulders. For a moment the shape beneath it was easier to see.

“That’s better,” the boy said.

Lerris sat in the chair. His legs had begun to shake.

“Does he let you leave?”

The boy looked toward the court.

“I go out.”

“Beyond the wall?”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you want to go farther?”

The blanket tightened around him.

“You ask the same thing in too many ways.”

“I’m sorry.”

The boy drew his knees closer. His face seemed young again, tired and annoyed.

He moved the candle away from the letter.

“Would you like me to stop talking?”

“Yes.”

They sat. Lerris listened to the keep settle around them: a door, footsteps, the faint ring of a vessel placed against another. Once the boy adjusted the blanket and the candle bent sideways without going out.

After a while Lerris found that he could look at the sleeve without trying to understand the hand inside it.

The door handle moved.

He stood at once. The boy was already looking toward it.

“Master Fenwood?” Michel called.

“Yes.”

“Open, please.”

Lerris went to the door. He paused with his hand on the key.

“The window,” Michel said through the wood. “Has someone come?”

Lerris looked back. The sill was empty. The blanket lay folded on it, although not as he had folded it. One corner hung beyond the wall.

He opened the door.

Michel entered carrying a little iron pan and a folded cloth. He went straight to the window. His face changed when he saw the pale line.

“Did you finish that?”

“No.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

Michel examined the line without touching it. Then he placed the cloth over the sill and set the pan on top.

“Leave this until morning.”

“What does it do?”

“It prevents someone putting a hand there by mistake.”

Lerris looked at the blanket.

“He was cold.”

“Yes.”

“Does he have a fire?”

“Yes.”

“Then why—”

Michel turned. For an instant Lerris thought he would be rebuked.

“He forgets which room,” the servant said.

He lifted the blanket by its two nearer corners, brought it inside, and draped it over the chair. He did not offer to take it away.

After he had gone, Lerris closed the window as far as the pan allowed. He scraped the cold wax from Markus’s letter with the edge of his thumbnail. A little ink came away with it.

He could still read the words beneath.

He put the flakes in the cold hearth, folded the letter, and left the blanket on the chair.

Chapter 7 — Michel’s Work

In the morning the pan was still on the sill.

Lerris had slept with the candle burning. It had guttered into its own wax, leaving a scorched smell that made him examine the table before anything else. The mark on the wood would not rub away. He put the candlestick over it, then moved it aside again.

When Michel arrived, he showed him.

“I’ll pay for that.”

The servant looked at the small burn.

“There is no need.”

“I’d rather—”

“I said there is no need.”

He took the pan away. The pale line beneath it had faded to something resembling a flaw in the stone. Lerris kept his hand off it.

“Have my letters gone?”

“Yes.”

“Both?”

“Both.”

“Thank you.”

Michel nodded. He had brought no tray. He told Lerris to come downstairs when dressed, then waited outside while he changed. Lerris put his papers in the case, checked that the little knife remained beneath the shirt, and took the bag with him.

Michel glanced at it.

“Are you leaving?”

“I intend to ask again.”

“Bring it, then.”

They went down a narrow stair, through a pantry, and into a room where the household’s traveling goods stood in rows. The musical chest was there. Lerris recognized its iron corners. Beside it were ordinary trunks with labels tied to the handles, rolled bedding, a bundle of fishing rods.

Michel lifted a tray from a shelf. It held two cups, a pot of hot water, bread, and a dish of butter. He set it on a closed trunk.

“The dining room is being cleared.”

Lerris sat on another trunk. Michel remained standing until he was asked, then took a stool from beside the door.

For a while they ate without speaking. The butter was too cold to spread. Michel warmed the knife against his cup before using it. Lerris copied him.

“You were a hunter,” he said.

The servant looked toward the open door.

“I was an inquisitor.”

“The mark.”

“Yes.”

“Did you come here alone?”

“I ended alone.”

Michel held the cup in both hands. The fingers were ordinary, the nails neatly cut. Lerris remembered the hand placing the pan over the sill.

“Were there others?”

“There are always others if you count far enough back.”

He drank. Lerris waited, uncertain whether the answer had been a refusal.

“A woman kept our horses,” Michel said. “A man brought letters. Two men came into the last house with me. I sent them out before I opened the crypt.”

“Why?”

“I thought one of them had spoken to somebody he shouldn’t.”

“Had he?”

“I never found out.”

Beyond the pantry, someone dropped a dish. The sound made both men turn. An irritated voice followed it, then another, laughing.

“I’d destroyed three of the Baron’s household,” Michel said. “People called that success. It made it difficult to tell them I could no longer sleep in a room with a covered mirror.”

Lerris set his cup down.

“How did he find you?”

“He owned the house.”

“The house with the crypt?”

“The one where I went afterwards. I had chosen it because a man I trusted recommended it.”

“Was the man his servant?”

Michel shrugged.

“You want a list you can check.”

“Yes.”

“So did I.”

He rose to inspect one of the labels. The cord had loosened. He retied it, keeping his back to Lerris while he worked.

“The Baron will offer you what you came for. Ask for your return journey at the same time.”

“Will he grant it?”

“He takes pleasure in receiving guests properly.”

“He didn’t answer me last night.”

“He answered.”

“That isn’t the same.”

Michel pulled the knot tight.

“No.”

Lerris waited until he turned back.

“You could come with me.”

The words felt generous for the brief interval before he heard them in that room, among the things Michel was packing for another man.

The servant looked at the bag beside Lerris’s feet.

“On your spare horse?”

“I don’t mean—”

“You haven’t one.”

“I could arrange something.”

“Do that before you offer.”

Michel sat again. Lerris wanted to apologize and knew it would only require him to receive the apology.

“Is there anything I can actually take?”

The servant’s gaze moved toward the door.

“There is a box.”

“Whose?”

“A woman’s account. Sister Aris. The Baron keeps it with the papers he shows people who ask about consequences.”

“Is it hers to give?”

“She is not here to ask.”

“Do you know where she is?”

“No.”

Michel emptied his cup. At his wrist the sleeve had ridden back. Beneath an old scar lay a fine gray line. It continued beneath the cloth, too regular to be a vein.

Lerris looked away too late.

“Does that—”

“Yes,” Michel said.

He pulled the cuff down and began gathering the breakfast things.

“Don’t offer to cut it.”

Lerris had not reached for his bag. He was ashamed of how little that mattered.

The servant took the tray to the pantry. When he returned, he carried the little iron emblem from Lerris’s discarded traveling shirt. He laid it beside the bag.

“You forgot this.”

“I thought it was useless.”

“For what?”

Lerris picked it up. The cord had left a red mark on his neck. He put it in the outer pocket rather than wear it.

“Will I see you again?”

Michel listened to footsteps passing outside.

“Yes,” he said. “I’m taking you to him.”

Chapter 8 — Bread and Salt

The Baron had breakfasted before them.

A plate stood beside his chair with a folded napkin over it. He was reading a letter when they entered. He finished the page, turned it over, and only then gestured to the place opposite him.

Lerris sat with the bag against his leg.

“You’ve packed.”

“Yes.”

“Michel says you wish to leave today.”

“I do.”

“Before the weather changes. Sensible.”

He had prepared arguments in the passage. None belonged to this conversation.

Asselin folded the letter. Behind him hung another portrait of the woman from the gallery, older here and looking away from the painter. One of her earrings was missing. The artist had painted the little hole in the bare lobe.

“Did you know her?”

The Baron followed his gaze.

“Yes.”

“Is she—”

“Your question was about your father’s papers.”

Lerris opened his case. He took out the extract he had used before the mirror, the notation referring to an account passed between houses. He had kept it separate from everything concerning the Proof. Asselin read it without taking it from him.

“That reached your father late.”

“You recognize it?”

“I recognize the description. The original has changed hands more often than the account admits.”

“Is it here?”

Asselin looked at Michel. The servant went to a cabinet, unlocked it, and brought out a small box. Its outer covering was dark wood, its inside lined with dull gray metal. It was heavier than its size suggested when Michel set it before Lerris.

“Lead?”

“Do not scrape it,” Michel said.

Asselin touched the lid.

“You may read it elsewhere.”

“Why not here?”

“Because you are leaving. You have made that quite clear.”

Lerris withdrew his hand from the catch.

“What do you want for it?”

“Must I want something?”

“Yes,” Lerris said, then wished he had chosen another word.

Asselin smiled.

“I want you to remember where you obtained it. You may write that down if it comforts you.”

“And the journey?”

“Arranged.”

“By the road?”

“By a shorter way.”

Lerris looked at Michel. The servant stood beside the cabinet with the key still in his hand.

“What does it require?”

“Walking,” Asselin said.

“And?”

The Baron regarded him for a moment, then reached for the bread. He cut a slice with a narrow knife and put it on Lerris’s plate. Salt stood in a little dish beside the loaf.

“Eat. You will be hungry before you reach the river.”

Lerris did not touch it.

“Does eating that agree to something?”

“You have eaten in my house since you arrived.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

The Baron set down the knife. Michel moved his hand from the cabinet key.

“No new condition,” Asselin said. “Bread.”

Lerris ate a piece. His mouth was dry enough to make swallowing difficult. The Baron watched him, then broke off a piece for himself.

“You think I am arranging a trick.”

“I don’t know what you’re arranging.”

“The Witness keeps cautious servants.”

Lerris looked up.

“Is that what you think I am?”

“Isn’t it?”

“I agreed to keep certain things. I didn’t agree that every journey belonged to it.”

“Then you may discover you were chosen before you consented.”

He said it pleasantly. Lerris had heard the same pleasure from men explaining his family’s intentions for him. For a moment the resemblance steadied him.

“Who told you?”

Asselin’s face lost none of its warmth.

“No one.”

“Then you don’t know.”

The room was quiet. Lerris could hear a horse outside and the small sound of Michel turning the key between his fingers.

At last Asselin laughed.

“You will be difficult to disappoint.”

“I’ve had practice.”

“Not enough.”

The Baron rose. Lerris did too, leaving the unfinished bread.

“Michel will take you to the entrance,” Asselin said. “You will be told what is known of the passage before you enter it.”

“If I don’t wish to?”

“Then you may remain until the road party returns.”

“When?”

“I expect them within the week.”

“And if they don’t return?”

“Then we shall both have cause for concern.”

Michel lifted the box and waited. Lerris put away the extract with hands that had become clumsy. He could stay. He could ask again tomorrow, and the day after. The woman at supper had waited for an answer about riding that she might still be waiting for when the cases were loaded.

He took the bag.

At the door Asselin called his name.

“You are welcome to return.”

Lerris looked back. The Baron had resumed his chair. His hand rested on the letter, hiding its last lines.

Michel opened the door wider.

Lerris went through.

Chapter 9 — The Shorter Way

They did not go to the stable.

Michel led him across the court and through a little gate beside the unfinished tower. The scaffold stood empty. A mason’s apron hung over its lowest rail, one pocket turned inside out. Lerris had noticed it from the window and had imagined watching men work there while he waited for a horse.

Beyond the gate a path followed the rock behind the keep. There was no railing. Michel carried the box under one arm and walked without difficulty. Lerris kept one hand free for the wall.

“Does he ever go this way?”

“Yes.”

“Does it take something from him?”

“I haven’t asked.”

They stopped before a shallow recess in the rock. Water darkened its lower edge. Nothing distinguished it from several they had passed.

Michel set down the box.

“The Ways Between don’t preserve everything carried through them. Most people arrive with their goods. Some discover an absence afterwards.”

“What kind?”

“A taste. A face. Part of a song. I know of a man who could remember a house except for the room in which he slept.”

“People have died?”

“Yes.”

Lerris looked back. He could still see the scaffold.

“Is this what you meant by a shorter way?”

“I didn’t call it shorter.”

“No.”

“The Baron did.”

Michel stood aside. He made no movement toward the rock.

Lerris sat on a low stone. His legs were shaking again. He was tired of deciding while his body behaved as though the decision had already gone wrong.

“Can I wait here?”

“For a little.”

He took out the water flask. Michel had filled it before they left. He drank, offered it, received a shake of the head, and put it away.

The court was quiet behind the wall. He could hear no wheels. The Baron was not leaving yet. There would be another supper, another conversation in which the means of departure might become available if Lerris accepted that his host knew when he was fit to travel.

He thought of Othrel asking whether someone would tell him if the people he waited for were dead.

“How long inside?”

“It isn’t the same each time.”

“How will I know where to go?”

“Keep to the passage with a draught. Do not follow anyone you see beyond its turns. I can show you the first part. I can’t come all the way.”

“Why?”

Michel looked toward the keep.

“I have work.”

Lerris could have hated him for the answer. Instead he put the flask away, rose, and adjusted the bag so its strap lay across his chest.

“All right.”

“Look at me.”

He did.

“You may go back now.”

“I know.”

Michel kept looking at him until he said, “Open it.”

The servant put his palm against the rock.

The recess became deep enough to hold a door. Lerris had not seen the stone move. One moment water ran over its surface; the next the water gathered along an iron band and dripped from the end. Michel lifted the latch.

Air touched Lerris’s face. It smelled of a place shut for a long time, although it moved steadily past him.

He took the box. Its weight pulled at his wrist. Michel helped him put it in the bag, wrapped in the shirt he had not worn, and fastened the strap himself.

“Both hands free,” he said.

Inside, there was enough light to see the wall. Lerris could not find its source. He put his hand where Michel showed him and followed the pressure of the air.

For several steps he could hear the servant behind him.

Then Michel said, “Here.”

The passage divided. One way sloped down toward a window. Through it Lerris could see the court at home, a table being carried across it, somebody complaining that it would not pass the door. The voice was so ordinary that he turned before he recognized it.

“Not that,” Michel said.

He faced the other way. Air moved against his damp palm.

“Will I see you?” he asked.

There was no answer.

He did not turn again.

The passage had changes of level. He found them with his feet, lowering himself carefully when the next step was not where he expected. Once the box struck his hip and made him cry out. The sound returned from somewhere ahead in a voice almost his own.

He stopped until he could feel the draught.

Later he was standing in a doorway with his hand raised to knock. He did not remember reaching it. A woman’s voice spoke inside, too far away for words. He knew the cadence. He had listened to it impatiently while a servant tried to find a missing glove, while a carriage waited, while he had already promised to be elsewhere.

He lowered his hand.

The air came from behind him.

He followed it back into a passage so narrow that his bag scraped both sides. His fingers hurt. He had been pressing them against the wall without noticing.

When he came out, he fell on his knees in wet grass.

A river lay below. The light was low, the sky streaked with clouds. He could hear someone calling to an animal on the opposite bank. He tried to answer and coughed instead.

The box was still in his bag. The flask was there. His papers were dry.

He checked them twice. Then he sat with the bag between his feet and tried to picture the woman behind the door.

His mother had turned toward him. He could remember her expression when she saw he was impatient, the little delay before she continued speaking. He could remember the fine lines at the corners of her eyes.

He could not remember their color.

He looked away, waited, tried again. He remembered a dress, the light on a window, her hand closing round the recovered glove. Her eyes would not settle. Brown was a word. Gray was a word. Neither belonged to the face he knew.

He opened Markus’s letter, as if something about her might have been written there by chance.

The damaged words concerned himself.

Across the river the person called again. Lerris rose too quickly, stumbled, and put out his hand to catch a tree. He held it until he could stand.

Then he began walking toward the voice.

Chapter 10 — The Cooper’s Yard

The man at the river wanted to know whether he had fallen from a boat.

“No,” Lerris said.

“Where’s your horse?”

“I haven’t one.”

The man looked at the steep ground behind him. He had a goat on a rope and a bundle of cut branches tied across his shoulders.

“You’ll want the ferry.”

“Which bank is this?”

The man told him. Lerris knew the name from the journey north. It was several days from the keep, on the southern bank. He had thought the river lay to his left when he entered the path behind the tower. He could not make himself care about the error for long enough to calculate it.

“Is there an inn?”

“There’s my sister.”

They went there together. The goat resisted at every patch of grass. Lerris carried the branches after the man asked him to, and found the weight helped him walk without stopping to examine what he could remember.

The sister gave him water, heard part of his explanation, and sent her son to fetch a neighbor who knew Dusana’s company. They had passed through the previous morning. The cooper had indeed failed to finish his work, and the delay had become a source of vigorous satisfaction to several people with whom he had previously argued about payment.

Lerris laughed. It came out badly. The woman put food before him and stopped asking questions.

He slept in a room beside the kitchen. Twice he woke and tried to find his mother’s eyes in a memory of another day. Each attempt left the rest of the memory painfully clear. He could remember being bored by her conversation. He could remember answering without looking up.

Toward morning he stopped trying and lay listening to the woman prepare the fire.

He opened the box after breakfast.

Inside lay folded sheets, their edges smoke-darkened. He put them on the table with permission and weighted the corners with clean cups. One side bore figures from a grain manifest. The account had been written across the other in an uneven hand.

The woman was called Aris. There had been a child. There had been a bargain to restore the child’s life, and consequences named for people who would come afterwards.

He read slowly. At one point the woman of the house came to clear the dishes, saw his face, and asked whether he needed someone fetched.

“No. Thank you.”

He moved a cup so she could take it. Beneath it the writer had described warmth returning to the child’s hand.

The paper gave him no way to establish when the account had been written, whether its later predictions had occurred, or why Asselin had kept it. It gave him the sound of someone insisting that she had received what she asked for. Further down, the same hand had pressed hard enough to tear the paper while describing a thread she could not cut.

He stopped before the end, went outside, and sat on the step.

The woman’s son was splitting kindling. He offered Lerris the hatchet in jest. Lerris took it before realizing this, and the boy showed him where to strike.

He did three pieces badly. The fourth split cleanly.

When he went back, he finished reading, folded the sheets along their existing creases, and put them in the box. He wrote the circumstances of receiving them on a separate page. He did not add that he had found his father’s secret.

Dusana came before midday.

She stood in the doorway while the woman explained where he had been found. Lerris rose. He had prepared an account containing the Baron, the captives, the box, and the passage. What he said was, “I can’t remember my mother’s eyes.”

Dusana set down the bundle she was carrying.

“Her face?”

“Yes. I can remember that.”

“Her voice?”

“Yes.”

She came inside and sat. The woman took the boy out to fetch something that did not seem to require them both.

Lerris showed her the box without opening it. He told her how he had left, what Michel had said, the doorway he had almost knocked upon. He could not explain why losing so little had frightened him more than several things he had seen in the keep.

Dusana did not ask him to.

“Will it come back?” he said.

“I don’t know.”

“Your uncle—”

“I don’t know.”

He put his hands flat on the table. There was a small cut beneath one thumbnail. He did not know where he had acquired it.

“I thought I was choosing the safer way.”

“Were you?”

“I don’t know that either.”

They sat until the boy returned, carefully alone, with a bundle of onions. He looked at his mother through the door and was sent away again.

Dusana smiled despite herself. Lerris found that he could smile too.

She had brought his letter of credit’s name and a message left with the cooper. The message was from the keep, telling the company that their passenger had been received and would be assisted on his onward journey. His own letter had not arrived.

“It may come later,” she said.

“Yes.”

He paid the household for his stay. The woman accepted, counted it, and gave some back because he had paid for more meals than he had eaten. He tried to insist. She told him she could count.

Dusana took one end of his bag while he fastened the heavy box more securely. They walked to the cooper’s yard by a road busy with people who knew where they were going.

The company had spread its work beneath a long open shed. A wheel lay on its side. Someone was kneeling inside it, reaching for a tool. The child who had lost something at the last inn came to inspect Lerris, decided he had brought nothing interesting, and ran away.

He sat beside the wagon where his bag had rested before the tollhouse. There was room for it again once a basket was moved. The woman who owned the basket moved it herself.

That evening he wrote to Markus.

He described the lost color. He asked whether his brother remembered it, then crossed out the question so angrily that the pen caught in the paper. After a while he wrote it again. He could bear to be told. He could not bear pretending the answer would be the same as remembering.

Below it he wrote about the old woman’s painting, the branch instead of flowers, the horse whose shoe would not stay sound. He told his brother he was coming south and would ask before visiting.

There was still a little light outside. Dusana was drinking beside the shed with two people he did not know. One of them was telling a story that made her put down her cup before she spilled it.

Lerris sealed the letter. He went to sit where he could hear.

Chapter 11 — The Room He Had Asked For

Markus was trying to move a pear tree when Lerris arrived.

It was young enough to have been sold to him as portable and old enough to dispute this. A laborer held the trunk while Markus worked at a root with a narrow spade. The hole had filled with water. Both men were muddy to the knee.

Alayse met Lerris at the gate.

“He said it would take an hour.”

“When?”

“Before dinner.”

She took his hand, examined his face without pretending not to, and kissed his cheek.

“Your room’s ready. Go and stop him before he cuts something he wants.”

Markus looked up at the sound of their steps. For a moment he remained bent over the spade. Then he gave it to the laborer and came out of the hole, slipping on the edge.

Lerris caught his arm.

They embraced badly because of the mud and then properly because neither cared about the mud for long. Markus held the back of his coat. Lerris could feel the strength of the hand and, beneath it, a slight tremor that had not been there when they were young.

“You came.”

“I said I would.”

“Yes.”

The laborer pushed the spade into the ground and waited for them to finish. Markus released his brother, looked at the tree, and seemed to remember the day he had been having.

“It won’t bear there,” he said.

“Where are you putting it?”

“By the wall.”

“Wouldn’t it be easier to move the wall?”

Alayse laughed. The laborer did not; he appeared to be considering the proposal.

They finished before supper. Lerris carried water, held a stake, and was told twice not to tread on the exposed roots. When the tree finally stood by the wall, its branches took up much less space than the work had required. Markus looked at it with an expression Lerris remembered from a completed comparison, before the lost pages and the years beneath the Library.

“I shall expect fruit,” Alayse said.

“Not this year.”

“I have already waited since dinner.”

They went inside to wash.

The room Lerris had asked for looked across a little yard. There was a bed, a chest, a chair with a folded towel on it. Nothing had been placed across the door to wake someone if he opened it. He set the lead-lined box in the chest, locked it, and kept the key. Then he sat on the bed until he could go downstairs without letting his face make the household responsible for every object it had given him.

At supper they talked about the tree.

Markus believed the nursery had sold it too late in the season. Alayse believed he had delayed buying it while corresponding with three people who owned entirely different trees. Lerris asked whether the fruit itself had been consulted. His brother threw a piece of crust at him, missed, and had to retrieve it because it had gone beneath Alayse’s chair.

Later, when she had gone to see somebody about a leaking roof, the brothers remained at the table.

“I received your letter,” Markus said.

Lerris put down his cup.

“Which?”

His brother looked at him.

“The first one.”

“Yes.”

“The story.”

Lerris waited. He had imagined the conversation so often that he feared answering something Markus had not said.

“I tried to remember telling it,” Markus said. “I couldn’t.”

“You were asleep.”

“I know.”

There was a little mud beneath one of his nails. He rubbed it with his thumb, unsuccessfully.

“I wondered what else you had written down.”

Lerris felt the question in the room before it came.

“Several pieces. Not all of them stories.”

“Where?”

“In my case.”

“Bring them.”

He went upstairs. The case lay where he had put it, beside the bed. He found the folded leaves quickly; he had arranged them for just such a request, although in his imagined version Markus had wanted to know whether they contained anything useful.

His brother took them without looking at the first page.

“Copies?”

“No.”

“All of them?”

“All that I kept. I remember some.”

“I can’t take that back.”

“No.”

Markus folded the packet again. He did not burn it. He put it in the drawer of the table and turned the key.

Lerris had wanted an instruction. Destroy this, preserve that, write to someone, make the injury small enough to fit inside work. Markus rested his hand on the drawer for a moment, then picked up his cup.

“Were you going to offer her another?”

“If the first hadn’t worked?”

“Yes.”

“I don’t know.”

His brother nodded as though he had expected no better answer.

“I won’t use them again.”

“Tell me if you do.”

“I said—”

“I heard.”

They sat without speaking until Alayse returned. She looked at them, at the two cups, at the key still in the drawer. She asked whether either of them wanted more food. Lerris was surprised to discover he did.

The next morning Markus told him about their mother’s eyes.

He tried two words, rejected the first, then spoke of a color changing with the light, of the little darker marks near the pupil. Lerris could understand the description. He could have given it to a painter. It did not enter the place where he remembered her looking at him.

“You remember that?” Markus asked.

“No.”

“I may be describing it badly.”

“No.”

His brother stopped. Beyond the window the laborer was collecting his tools. One struck another, a bright little sound.

After a while Markus said, “She used to look at your hands before your face when you came indoors.”

“I was usually carrying something.”

“Or hiding it.”

Lerris remembered a broken bird’s egg, a stolen pear, a small cut he had been determined not to have washed. He laughed, then found he could not continue.

Markus remained beside him. He made no further attempt at the color.

Lerris stayed twelve days. They read the confession on the sixth, after he had asked whether his brother wished to. Markus questioned its date, its chain of ownership, the certainty with which Aris described events she could not yet have seen. He did not dispute the warmth of the child’s hand.

On the ninth day they went to buy a better support for the pear tree. Markus spent too much. Alayse said so when they came home and then made them move it until the tree stood straight.

When Lerris left, his brother gave him a small parcel of fruit bought from a neighbor.

“Next year,” he said, “you can complain about mine.”

Chapter 12 — The Report with No Monster in It

By the time the letter from Aeler reached him, Lerris had spent two months failing to make a useful account of the keep.

He could describe a room. He could record an answer. Whenever he wrote what either had meant, he found himself borrowing the Baron’s confidence. Michel’s useful warning became a law. The consort’s years became a lesson. He crossed these things out and was left with pages that looked unwilling to make their own acquaintance.

He sent the keepers in Thepyrgos what he could establish about the passage and the confession, with a note separating both from the Proof. The reply acknowledged receipt. It asked him not to send an unexamined supernatural object through ordinary post.

He had not intended to. He resented the instruction for a day, then put it with his other correspondence.

The Aeler letter came through a scholar who had read his account of the stacks. It bore three signatures, one overwritten where the pen had failed, and contained no promise that his experience uniquely fitted him to answer it.

A maintenance crew had reported hearing instructions after the signal line was disconnected. One worker had opened a gate against orders and could afterwards give no account of why. There had been no loss of life. The lower district remained closed to freight pending inspection.

They wanted him to speak to the worker.

A second page described his fee, travel expenses, and the person to whom he would report. Captain Mira Deeproad had added a sentence below the formal invitation.

If you cannot come below, say so before traveling. We can send the man up.

Lerris read that line several times.

He wrote asking whether the worker had been removed from the affected place, whether anyone else had heard the instruction, and what treatment had been provided for injuries or sleeplessness. He asked what they had found wrong with the gate.

The reply came with a sketch.

The gate had opened correctly.

That evening he went through his things. He set aside the papers he would need, then found he had included half the material he most wanted someone to admire. He put most of it back. He kept the custody terms, a clean account of the escape from the stacks, and the names of people who could correct him. The accounts of the keep stayed with his private papers.

The lead-lined box presented a difficulty. He did not want to leave it in a rented room. He did not want to carry it into an unfamiliar underground place and discover that it had arrived with interests of its own.

He arranged secure storage through the keeper’s correspondent, paid for it, and obtained a receipt that described the closed box without reproducing the confession. The clerk wanted to know whether he wished its contents copied in case of loss.

“No.”

“It is usually advisable.”

“Not this one.”

The clerk wrote the refusal down. Lerris checked it, signed, and left without opening the box once more to reassure himself it was the same.

He bought boots suited to wet stone. The seller made him walk in them, listened to his objections about the stiffness, and said he could prefer his old ones if he liked falling over. He bought the boots and wore them about town for six days before setting out.

On the last of those days a letter came from Markus.

The pear tree had lost leaves. Alayse said this was what trees did. Markus thought it had begun too soon. Beneath that was a question about the proposed journey: Who else is going?

Lerris answered with the names he had been given.

He had met none of them. Writing them in a letter made that harder to overlook.

Part II — The Lower Works

Chapter 13 — Bread Before the Bell

The first person Lerris met beneath Aeler asked him to move his luggage.

He had stopped inside the entrance to look at the ceiling. A wagon was coming up behind him, its driver walking beside the team. The passage curved so gently that Lerris had not understood how much of the road continued out of sight.

“Against the wall,” the driver said. “Not that wall.”

He moved again.

The wagon passed with a scraping of iron at the wheel rims. Beyond it came two women carrying baskets, a man with a sleeping child against his shoulder, and a porter pulling an empty handcart at a speed that suggested freedom from the load was temporary. Warm air touched Lerris’s cheek. Somewhere ahead bread was baking.

A guard checked his letter and sent a boy to find the person who had agreed to receive him.

“Will I be staying below?” Lerris asked.

“If that’s the room they’ve paid for.”

He watched the boy disappear among the traffic. The guard had already returned to a disagreement over a cartload of charcoal.

Rennik Stoneheart arrived carrying a loaf under his arm.

He was shorter than Lerris and considerably broader, with a beard bound close enough to keep clear of his work. Flour marked his sleeve. He held out his free hand, introduced himself, and asked whether Lerris had eaten.

“On the road.”

“That was hours ago.”

“Not many.”

“Enough.”

He took the smaller bag before Lerris could decide whether to object. They went down a stair opening beside the passage into a square warmed by a great oven set into one wall. People stood with dough waiting in covered bowls. A girl was arguing that her household’s loaves had been placed too near the back. The oven-warden showed her a mark on a board. She examined it, remained dissatisfied, and began a different argument.

Rennik found a place at a table.

“Sit.”

The bench was low enough to make Lerris’s knees awkward. Rennik noticed and drew over a wooden stool, higher than the bench.

“For when your knees start hurting.”

“They won’t.”

“They do.”

He cut the loaf. Someone brought a bowl of soft cheese and a dish of onions, asked Rennik about a woman Lerris had never heard of, and went away before the answer was finished.

“My sister,” Rennik explained. “She’s marrying someone who thinks he can repair a roof.”

“Can he?”

“He can repair a roof. He thinks this means he can repair hers.”

Lerris laughed. Rennik looked pleased and pushed the cheese toward him.

Around them the court continued its business. A woman settled a complaint without raising her voice. Two men compared burns on their forearms with an air of competitive disappointment. A boy tried to balance a crust on his brother’s head. The brother endured it until the bread fell down his collar, then retaliated efficiently.

Lerris had expected stone and silence. He had not expected to be asked to move his elbow because someone needed the end of his table to mend a strap.

“Is the gate far below?” he asked.

“Far enough. We aren’t taking you there today.”

“I’d like to see the plans.”

“You will.”

“Before I speak to the man.”

Rennik looked at him over the loaf.

“Why?”

“So I can understand what he describes.”

“And if you start putting the plan into his mouth?”

Lerris stopped. He had been about to explain how carefully he asked questions.

“Then somebody should stop me.”

“Good.”

Rennik tore a piece of bread rather than cut it.

“I’ll show you the approach. Dorven can tell you what he heard first.”

“Is Dorven the man?”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t put his name in the report.”

“He asked us not to.”

They finished eating. When Lerris stood, his knees had stiffened. Rennik said nothing while he straightened them.

The lodging was two passages away, a room with a vent above the door and a bed built into an alcove. The woman who kept it showed him how to adjust the air shutter, then told him not to adjust it past the scratched line.

“Even if it seems cold?”

“Especially then.”

He put his things down. Through the vent he could hear the court faintly, the calls altered by distance until they sounded almost like singing.

Rennik waited at the door.

“Do you need a room above?”

Lerris looked at the bed. It was too short until he saw that a board at the foot folded down.

“No. This will do.”

“All right. If it won’t, say so.”

He left Lerris to unpack.

For a few minutes the room was unfamiliar enough to be pleasant. He arranged his shirts, set out the letters, found a place for the lamp. Then a voice passed along the vent, indistinct but close, and he stopped with his hand inside the bag.

It passed. Someone laughed elsewhere.

He took out the rest of his clothes.

Chapter 14 — What Dorven Heard

Dorven had brought his lunch to the interview.

He put it on the table between them, wrapped in a cloth stained through with grease. Captain Mira Deeproad asked whether he wished to eat first. He said he wished to finish before it went cold.

“Then eat,” she told him. “We can wait.”

He unwrapped it with the angry care of someone who suspected kindness would cost him time later.

Lerris sat opposite. Rennik remained by the window, which looked into a shaft rather than outside. Mira had placed her chair where she could see both the table and the door. She wore a short sword at her hip, its sheath tied close enough not to catch when she moved.

Dorven ate a little meat folded in bread. He had broad hands and a healed cut across one knuckle. His left boot was split at the seam. Lerris found himself wondering whether that had happened below, then stopped inventing a history for it.

“Tell me where you were,” he said when Dorven looked ready.

The man swallowed.

“South check. By the gate wheel.”

“Alone?”

“No. Kelset was at the upper stop.”

“Could you see him?”

“Not from the wheel.”

Lerris made a mark.

Dorven watched the pen.

“Is that wrong?”

“No. I’m writing it down.”

“I’ve told them three times.”

“I know.”

“Then read it.”

Lerris set down the pen.

“I have. There are things I can’t tell from it.”

“Like whether I’m lying.”

“No.”

Mira shifted slightly. Lerris heard his own answer.

“Among other things,” he said.

Dorven looked at him for a long moment. Then he took another bite.

The instruction had come while he waited for Kelset to test the upper stop. He had heard his name, then the order to lift the gate by a hand’s breadth. The voice sounded like Rennik’s. It had come from the speaking pipe.

“The pipe had been disconnected,” Mira said.

“I know that now.”

“You knew before.”

“I’d forgotten.”

Rennik turned from the window.

“He had four jobs. We’d moved one while he was working the others.”

Mira nodded. She did not withdraw the question.

“What happened when you lifted it?” Lerris asked.

“Nothing.”

“Water?”

“It was a gate. Yes, water.”

“How much?”

“Enough to show it was lifting cleanly. I lowered it when Kelset came down shouting.”

Lerris asked him to repeat the words he had heard. Dorven could remember some exactly and others only as instruction. He grew irritated when Lerris tried to distinguish these, then supplied the distinction himself in the next sentence.

“And after?”

“They took my key.”

Mira said, “After you were out of the check.”

“They still took it.”

He folded the cloth over his food.

“Have you heard the voice since?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

“Anything else?”

Dorven looked at Rennik.

The engineer came to the table and sat down.

“You can tell him.”

“I can tell him plenty. I don’t know what it does to me afterwards.”

Mira’s hand rested on the closed report.

“You’re still being paid.”

“This week.”

The captain did not answer at once. Lerris was glad she did not promise what she might not control.

Dorven rubbed the healed knuckle.

“Sometimes I know someone’s about to come through a door. Before there’s a sound. Before there could be a sound.”

“How long?”

“Since I was young.”

“Always correctly?”

“No. Or they change their mind. How would I know?”

“Has it changed lately?”

“Yes.”

He pushed the wrapped food away.

“Now I think they’re waiting on the other side after I’ve opened it.”

No one reached for a pen. Lerris had put his own down and found that he did not want to be the first to pick it up.

“Do you see anyone?”

“No.”

“Does it happen here?”

“Once. In the washing room.”

“Do you want someone to come with you?”

Dorven gave him a look of such exhausted dislike that he understood the offer too late.

“To every door?”

“No. I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

They stopped soon afterwards. There were questions left. Dorven had finished his food, and his hand had begun to shake when he tried to fold the cloth back along its old lines.

Mira told him when he would next be needed. He asked whether he was allowed into the stores. She said yes, with Kelset. He asked whether he might go there without an escort when he wanted another shirt.

“Kelset needs to fetch his own things,” she said.

Dorven put the cloth in his pocket.

“Of course he does.”

After he had gone, Lerris looked at the report.

“I’d rather not call that madness.”

“Neither would I,” Rennik said.

Mira rose.

“Write what he told you. Then tell me whether I can let him open a gate.”

“I can’t answer that yet.”

“I know. I still need it answered.”

She took the report and left them at the table.

Rennik watched the door close.

“She had a man die below last winter,” he said. “Not like this. A fall. He’d asked to finish the last inspection himself.”

“Did she let him?”

“Yes.”

The engineer gathered the empty cups. Lerris began putting his notes into order, then stopped at the word voice. He had written it without adding whose voice Dorven believed it had been.

He made room and put Rennik’s name beside it.

Chapter 15 — Three Kinds of Line

Ivradan disliked the new plan before he had unfolded it.

“It will omit the older shafts.”

“It includes every shaft we use,” Rennik said.

“That isn’t what I said.”

He spread it beside a sheet already on the table. The older drawing had been copied onto fresh paper, but its missing portions had been left blank. Lerris appreciated this until he discovered how much was missing.

Ivradan was narrow for an Aeler, with an old burn along his jaw and spectacles he took off whenever he wished to look severely at someone. He had been responsible for the district’s historic records for longer than Rennik had been responsible for its gates. Neither seemed inclined to let the other forget it.

“Show him the water first,” Mira said.

Rennik placed a finger on the new plan.

“Here. Upper collection. The water comes through this throat, then divides. Most goes out by the west run. A smaller amount comes down to serve the workings.”

“What kind of workings?” Lerris asked.

“Washing. Cooling. Drainage from above. Things people would notice if I stopped them.”

He followed the lines down.

“Three checks let us isolate a section. South check is where Dorven was. If the lower run fills, these close in order. Water takes the west relief instead.”

“And if that closes?”

“It shouldn’t.”

Ivradan removed his spectacles.

Rennik looked at him. “Because it isn’t controlled by those checks. Not because I’ve decided to be hopeful.”

He drew a little separate line on a scrap of paper.

“Independent release. You can operate it at the upper gallery. There is another below, but we don’t use that unless the upper one fails.”

Lerris looked from the little drawing to the crowded plan. The explanation had seemed simple until he tried to fit it into the mountain. His finger stopped at a junction that looked like three others. He would have to recognize it with a lamp shaking in his hand.

“Which of these is a place I could stand?”

Rennik glanced at Mira, then turned the plan toward him.

“These broad lines. Everything narrower may be a pipe, a drain, or a passage you’ll regret entering.”

He marked the walking route on another sheet. Lerris asked for the turn toward the upper release, then the next place where he could retreat if water entered from the south. Rennik added both without impatience. He appeared to enjoy a question with somewhere definite to put the answer.

Ivradan waited until he had finished.

“Now the part beneath it.”

His older plan did not fit exactly. Some differences were due to copying, he said. Others were not. Chambers abandoned after a flood had been retained as sumps. A flight of steps had been blocked when an adjacent wall was strengthened. The works now occupied the upper portion of a much older complex.

“A hold?” Lerris asked.

“A palace, in one account. A treasury in another. Those are not mutually exclusive.”

“Who lived there?”

“Valdris is the name attached to the last occupation.”

“A king?”

“Called one.”

Rennik leaned back in his chair.

“He means we can’t charge the king for repairs.”

“I mean you cannot date a wall by the most important person someone says stood beside it.”

Mira tapped the plan near the walking route.

“Show him the stones.”

Ivradan pointed to marks Lerris had mistaken for pillars. They lay at intervals along the old work, some incorporated into later walls, others standing clear. The maintenance summaries called them binding stones. Inspection required cleaning their faces without disturbing the scored lines, checking their seats, and noting changes in sound when the water rose.

“What are they binding?”

“That is the question,” Ivradan said.

“Not the wall?”

“Among other things.”

He brought out a rubbing. Its edges had been mounted on cloth. Beside a drawing of interlocking strokes ran a line of characters Lerris could not read.

“Thane’s workshop,” Ivradan said. “Or a workshop claiming his methods. We have instructions for keeping the faces clear. We have accounts of what was brought for the first working. We do not have a complete account of what they were afraid would happen if they stopped.”

“A drake,” Rennik said.

Ivradan looked at him.

“That’s what the men call it.”

“I know.”

“Then let him hear the word before he goes below and thinks we’re hiding it.”

Lerris examined the rubbing. He could see where somebody had stopped pressing the paper into a damaged recess, leaving the uncertain shape unfilled.

“Does anyone say they’ve seen it?”

“People say many things,” Ivradan replied.

“Anyone whose account you can show me?”

The scholar put his spectacles back on.

“Yes. Two. Neither returned to the same occupation afterwards.”

They spent the afternoon with those accounts. One described a sound beneath deep water. The other described a face appearing in polished stone, then corrected the word face three times. Neither supplied a creature Lerris could draw.

Near the end Mira returned from another meeting and stood looking at the table.

“What have we got?”

“A route,” Rennik said.

“An older route,” Ivradan said.

“And an instruction that may not have come through the pipe,” Lerris said.

The captain rubbed her eyes.

“Tomorrow we look. We don’t improve anything while we’re down there.”

Rennik opened his mouth.

“Not even the thing you’ve already noticed needs improving.”

He closed it.

She put a second report beside the plan. Two workers from the recent west clearance were missing; the search had stopped at the third check when its controls failed. Neither had been found on the accessible upper routes.

“If we hear someone calling, we come back with the position. We don’t follow them off this route.”

When the others left, Lerris remained with the two accounts. He had started a list of similarities to the stacks. Voices. Water. Instructions offered in a familiar hand, or something close enough to one. He turned the page over before the list grew longer.

On the other side he copied Rennik’s walking route. It had three places to stop, and he wanted to know all of them without looking.

Chapter 16 — Kelset’s Turn

Kelset came to fetch him for supper carrying a boot.

“Is that mine?” Lerris asked.

“No. Yours are still pretending they’ve never met a foot.”

The man sat on the threshold and pulled the boot on. One of its hooks was missing. He looped the lace round the next and stood to test it.

“Rennik says you know about voices.”

“I’ve heard some.”

“I hear Rennik every day. Nobody pays me extra.”

They went toward the oven court. Kelset walked with the easy attention of someone who could continue a conversation while moving a dangling cord out of a passerby’s way. He greeted three people, returned a borrowed awl, and stopped to collect money from a man who insisted he had already paid.

“You paid my wife,” Kelset said.

“Then you’ve been paid.”

“I’ve been informed.”

The man laughed and refused again. Kelset accepted this with no sign of grievance. Lerris could not tell whether he had expected the money.

At the table Dorven sat beside a space being kept for them. He had changed his shirt. A small girl stood behind his chair attempting to plait one narrow section of his hair. Whenever he moved, she pulled it straight again.

“Your daughter?” Lerris asked.

“Mine,” Kelset said. “He sits still.”

“I don’t have a choice,” Dorven said.

The girl said he did, but it would look worse.

Kelset went to collect their food. Lerris sat at the edge of the bench. Dorven looked at him, then at the empty place where an interview paper might have been.

“Not tonight,” Lerris said.

“I wasn’t asking.”

“No.”

They sat awkwardly until the girl dropped her tie. Lerris found it beneath the table and handed it back. She asked whether he knew how to plait. He admitted he did not.

“Then hold that.”

He spent the next several minutes holding an end of Dorven’s hair while being corrected about the tension.

When Kelset returned, he examined the result with admiration that made the girl blush angrily.

“Fine work. He’ll be the envy of the shift.”

“He isn’t going on shift,” she said.

The table quieted for a moment. Kelset put down the bowls.

“He can envy us at home, then.”

Dorven laughed. It sounded as though he had decided to, but the second laugh came more easily.

They ate a thick stew with bread softened in it. Lerris burned his tongue and was advised not to rush an oven court’s chief product. Kelset’s wife joined them late, kissed the top of her daughter’s head, and took the bowl her husband had protected from everybody else.

“What did he tell you?” she asked Lerris.

“About what?”

“Anything.”

“That somebody owed him money.”

She looked at Kelset.

“You’ve been paid.”

“So I’m told.”

She ate without answering. Her daughter finished the plait and announced that Dorven could now move his head. He bowed it gravely in thanks, which spoiled the lower part and caused a brief renewal of the work.

Later the child fell asleep against her mother’s arm. People were still arriving from a shift below. They smelled of wet stone and lamp oil. One had wrapped a cut finger so thickly that he could not lift his spoon properly, and accepted mockery until Kelset cut his bread for him.

Lerris asked what the lower work sounded like when nothing was wrong.

Kelset considered that while chewing.

“Wrong in a way you know.”

Dorven shook his head.

“He means something’s always loose,” he said. “Water talks differently when a board moves. You learn which things can wait.”

“Like him,” Kelset said.

“Like your boot.”

Kelset looked down. “That boot has another year in it.”

“It has another foot in it, if you don’t mend the side.”

Lerris laughed with them. Beneath the table Kelset flexed the boot, making the split side gape, and nudged Dorven with it.

On the way back Kelset asked whether the captain meant to keep Dorven above.

“I don’t make that decision.”

“But she’ll ask you.”

“Yes.”

The man stopped beside a vent grille. Warm air lifted the hair at his temples.

“He heard things before. We knew. Sometimes we’d ask him whether somebody was coming because it saved going to look.”

“What do you think changed?”

“He listened when he shouldn’t.”

“That could happen to anyone.”

“I know. That’s what worries me.”

They stood aside for a woman carrying a sleeping baby. She nodded to Kelset, glanced at Lerris, and continued.

“He’s careful,” Kelset said. “You should have that in whatever you write.”

“I’ll put what you said.”

“He is careful.”

“Yes.”

Kelset looked unsatisfied but took him the rest of the way to his room. At the door he reminded him of the early meal and told him to sleep with his boots where he could find them.

“Do you expect trouble?”

“I expect you to be late.”

Chapter 17 — The First Inspection

They stopped before the third turn because the air was wrong.

Lerris had noticed nothing. Rennik held up a hand, waited, and moved the lamp a little lower. Dorven, who had been permitted to accompany the inspection under Mira’s eye, put his own palm against the wall.

“Back,” Rennik said.

No one argued. They returned to the last recess. Kelset shut a narrow door behind them and set its catch. The captain counted the company while Rennik tested the air at the vent above the bench.

“Is it the voice?” Lerris asked.

“No,” Mira said.

Rennik sent Kelset to a handwheel in the wall. He turned it, waited, and turned it again. Something moved overhead with a clatter. A draught reached the recess.

“Shutter stuck,” the engineer said. “They’d opened the loading door above.”

Lerris looked at the others. They were annoyed. Ivradan took out a little cloth and wiped dust from his spectacles. Dorven leaned back against the wall with his eyes shut.

“What did you notice?” Lerris asked.

“The lamp,” Rennik said. “And the air on my neck.”

“Two measures,” Mira said. “Enough to stop.”

They waited before going on. The pause cost them part of the morning, and nobody tried to reclaim it by walking faster afterwards.

At the south check, the disconnected speaking pipe lay on a folded cloth. Its two ends had been capped separately. Kelset showed Lerris the place where he had been working when Dorven opened the gate. From there he could see the indicator but not the wheel.

“I saw the rise. Thought he’d mistaken my signal.”

“What signal?”

Kelset struck the railing twice with a small hammer. The sound traveled through the chamber and returned from several directions.

“That’s wait,” Dorven said.

“And open?”

“A spoken order. Once I was down.”

Lerris walked between the two stations. The distance was not great. It was quite enough to leave a person alone with an instruction.

“Would it be possible to keep each other in sight?”

“Not while working that stop,” Rennik said.

“Another person?”

Mira looked at him.

“For the inspection,” he added. “I don’t know how many people you have for every shift.”

“We have enough today.”

He suggested repeating an instruction aloud and having the person giving it answer from where they could be seen. Kelset asked whether they should do this even if it was Mira telling him to move his foot. Mira told him she would move it herself if he took too long.

They tried it with the gate left alone. The procedure felt foolish on the first exchange and less foolish on the second. Lerris could see how readily they accepted the small delay. He liked being the reason for it.

At the farther wall Ivradan examined the first binding stone. Its face had been cleaned. Water had left a pale deposit around the base, but the scored surface remained dark and dry.

“Someone’s been polishing it,” he said.

“We clean it,” Rennik answered.

“This is polished.”

He showed them a worn place near the lower line. Rennik crouched, looked, and called Kelset over. The worker said no abrasive had been used during his shifts. Dorven said the same.

Ivradan made a rubbing without filling the worn place.

While he worked, Lerris heard somebody speak behind him.

“Lift it now.”

He turned.

Rennik stood beside Mira several paces away. Both were looking at Ivradan’s hand on the paper. Neither had spoken. Dorven had gone still.

“Did you hear that?” Lerris asked.

The captain faced him immediately.

“What?”

He repeated the words. He did not move toward the wheel.

Rennik shook his head.

“Not me.”

Dorven let out a breath so sharply that it seemed to hurt him.

“Yes,” he said. “That’s it.”

Mira brought them together in the middle of the chamber. She asked each person what they had heard before allowing them to compare answers. Kelset had heard water. Ivradan had heard the words, but thought Lerris had spoken them. Mira had heard nothing until Lerris turned.

They withdrew with the gate unmoved.

Above, Lerris recorded the incident while it was fresh. He wrote that the visible confirmation had prevented action on an unverified instruction. That was true. He paused over the next sentence, in which he had begun to say the method provided a means of working safely.

Mira read over his shoulder.

“It worked,” she said.

“This time.”

“Yes. Write both.”

He did. He was pleased enough with the result that the qualification did not diminish it.

Chapter 18 — The Gate That Shut

They returned two mornings later with equipment to inspect the upper release and a different kind of lamp.

Ivradan had found an older account of wear on the binding stones. The marks did not match theirs, but the writer had described a change in sound before the water rose. Rennik wanted to measure the level on both sides of the south gate at the same time. Mira agreed to a brief inspection, with a return party waiting above and no freight admitted to the lower district.

Lerris sent a note before he went. He gave the names of the company and the expected hour of return. He left his private papers in the lodging and took only what he needed to record what happened.

Dorven had volunteered to return.

Mira asked him in front of them whether he understood that refusing would not end his pay. He said yes. Kelset looked away while he answered, giving him the little privacy available in a room full of people who wanted to know.

The first part went well.

The air held. The levels agreed within the difference Rennik expected. Ivradan found no new wear on the face. Lerris stood where he could see Dorven and the gate wheel, repeating instructions that the others no longer found amusing.

Rennik sent Kelset up to check the upper indicator. Mira went as far as the turn where she could see both stations. It left Lerris beside Dorven, Ivradan at the stone, and the engineer kneeling over a shallow inspection channel.

“Higher,” a voice said.

Lerris looked at Rennik. The man had not moved.

“Not confirmed,” Lerris said.

“I heard it,” Rennik answered. “Leave the wheel.”

Dorven’s hands were already clear. He held them against his thighs as if someone might otherwise mistake an intention.

The sound came again.

Not a word this time. It began in the wall, passed beneath their feet, and rose into the ironwork with a violence Lerris felt in his teeth. Ivradan dropped the rubbing paper. Mira shouted from the turn.

The upper gate fell.

It struck once, enormously. Rennik rose from the channel. A lever came back across the space where he had been kneeling and caught his lower leg before he could clear it. He fell without a cry. The lamp beside him broke.

Water lifted through the channel.

Lerris went toward him. Dorven caught the back of his coat and pulled him aside as a second bar tore loose from its mounting. It struck the floor where he had been about to kneel.

“Wait,” Dorven said.

He was looking at the broken lever, not listening to anything else.

Together they reached Rennik from the other side. The engineer was trying to sit up. His leg would not come with him. Blood ran dark into the rising water.

“Don’t pull it,” he said.

Lerris had already taken him beneath the arms. He loosened his hold.

Mira came down the steps. She shouted for Kelset. The worker answered from above, then appeared with a length of timber he had taken from the rail.

“Under there,” Rennik said.

His voice remained steady. Lerris watched him point and found that he could follow the hand even while the rest of the room became incomprehensible.

Kelset drove the timber beneath the lever. Mira put her weight on the far end. Iron rose a little. Dorven knelt in the water and eased Rennik’s boot clear while Lerris pulled only when told.

They had him.

The water was bitterly cold. Lerris had expected underground water to be cold; he had not expected pain so quickly through the new boots. His feet seemed to belong to someone slower.

“Upper way,” Mira said.

Kelset and Lerris lifted Rennik between them. He tried to help with his sound foot, failed, and told them to put him down because he was making it worse. They did, briefly, on the lowest step. Ivradan gathered the lamps that remained lit. Dorven tore a strip from the engineer’s coat and pressed it where the blood came fastest.

They climbed.

At the first turn Mira stopped.

Beyond her the upper passage ended in water.

It should have risen toward the waiting party. Lerris knew that. He had walked it twice. Now a dark surface lay almost level with the third step, moving gently toward them. Something pale passed beneath it and disappeared against the wall.

“Relief gallery,” Rennik said.

Mira turned at once.

“The lower one?”

“The crossway. Above the old sumps.”

Kelset looked back toward the chamber. Water had reached the gate wheel.

“We can’t take him through there.”

“We can if we go now,” Rennik said.

They went back down.

Lerris had the engineer’s arm across his shoulders. Kelset took the other side. With each step Rennik made a small effort to keep weight off them, and with each effort the injured foot touched something it should not. At last Mira told him to stop helping.

He swore at her.

“Good,” she said. “Keep doing that.”

The crossway opened behind the binding stone. Ivradan reached it first and held the lamp above the water. The scored face beside him had begun to shine. Lerris saw his own hand reflected in it, then another hand resting over his.

He looked away.

At the doorway Dorven stopped so abruptly that they nearly struck him.

“What?” Mira demanded.

He was staring into the dark beyond Ivradan’s lamp.

“Someone’s there.”

“Can you see them?”

“No.”

“Then move where I put you.”

She took his shoulder and set him against the jamb. He held the lamp while they passed. Nothing touched Lerris except wet stone and Rennik’s weight.

The crossway rose. They left the water after twenty or thirty steps, and Lerris nearly fell when his boots ceased to resist each movement. Kelset caught him against the wall.

“Not yet.”

“I know.”

“Then up.”

They reached a dry landing with a heavy door at its far end. Mira and Ivradan opened it together. Beyond lay a storage recess, three empty shelves, and a bench fixed to the wall.

They laid Rennik on the bench.

He caught Lerris’s wrist before he could move away.

“The upper gate shouldn’t have done that.”

“I know.”

“No. Listen. The balance lifted while the gate was still caught. I saw the chain go slack. Then it fell.”

Lerris looked toward Mira. She was securing the door. Kelset had gone to examine the vent.

“Write it,” Rennik said.

Lerris took out the small book. His fingers were too cold to separate the leaves at first. He dried them on the inside of his coat and wrote what the engineer had said.

Rennik watched until the words were there.

Then he let go of Lerris’s wrist and began to shake.

Chapter 19 — The Dry Bench

Mira cut the boot.

Kelset objected before he could stop himself. Rennik laughed once, a sound with no breath behind it, and told him there would be another argument about boots if they got home.

“If,” Kelset said, then looked furious with himself.

“When,” Rennik answered. “Cut the damned thing.”

The captain worked along the seam with a short knife. Lerris held the lamp. He could see the leg now: torn cloth, a wound above the ankle, the foot lying in a direction it would not have chosen. He had seen injuries before. Familiarity did nothing useful to his stomach.

“Higher,” Mira told him.

He lifted the light.

“Watch me if you can’t watch that. Just keep it still.”

He watched her hands. They were broad, quick, careful when quickness would have hurt. She asked Rennik whether he could feel her touch. He answered. She asked again in another place.

Ivradan had opened the supplies. He read labels aloud until she told him which packet to give her. Dorven sat beside the engineer’s head, supporting his shoulders when they needed to move him. Nobody asked Dorven whether he was hearing anything now.

The wound bled less under pressure. Mira used clean water sparingly and covered it. She bound the leg between two boards Kelset took from a shelf, padding the hard edges with a folded shirt. Rennik tried to tell her how tight. She listened, then told him to stop looking.

“You’d look.”

“Yes.”

He turned his face toward the wall.

When it was done, she sat back on her heels. Sweat had darkened her collar. Lerris realized he was sweating too, above clothes still wet to the waist.

“Can you heal it?” he asked Ivradan.

The scholar looked surprised.

“No.”

“You know the bindings.”

“I read the accounts of them.”

Lerris closed his mouth. Ivradan offered no further correction. He found a dry cloth and began cleaning the captain’s knife.

They took off what wet clothing they could spare. Kelset wrung water from his shirt in the doorway, then put it back on with a gasp. Mira gave Rennik her outer coat. He protested until she told him she would take it back when he had finished needing it.

The vent above the shelves drew faintly. Kelset held a strip of cloth beneath it and watched the movement. He checked the door, listened, and came back.

“No rise yet.”

“How long?” Mira asked.

He shook his head.

They had five sound lamps and oil for the intended inspection, not an absence whose length they could guess. Mira had two extinguished. The room became smaller. Lerris found this almost a relief; there was less darkness pretending to be within reach.

Rennik wanted the plan.

“After you’ve drunk,” Mira said.

He drank, spilled a little, swore, and asked again.

Lerris spread it on a shelf. Ivradan held one corner. The wet had reached the lower edge, but the route remained legible. Rennik raised himself enough to see and stopped with his teeth clenched.

“Bring it here.”

They did. He followed the crossway with one finger.

“The waiting party can reach the top of the west relief. If it’s still open, they can lower the collection level. We need to get to its inspection gallery.”

“Through here?” Mira asked.

“No. Lower.”

She looked at him.

“Not into the water. The old stair behind the stores. We sealed its lower flight, not the approach.”

Ivradan was already searching his copied plan.

“This?”

Rennik looked, nodded, then shook his head.

“That joins farther down.”

“How much farther?”

“I don’t know.”

His hand stayed on the paper after the answer.

“We wait for them to lower it,” Kelset said.

“If they can,” Mira answered.

“They know we’re here.”

“They know where we went. I don’t know what they can reach.”

Nobody said anything for a while. Beyond the door water made a small irregular sound, touching stone and withdrawing. It did not seem hurried.

Mira sent Kelset to examine the dry passage beyond the recess, with Dorven to keep him in sight. They were to return at the first turn they could not see beyond, or sooner if the measures changed. No gates, no handles, no answering voices. Kelset repeated the instructions without joking.

When they had gone, Rennik closed his eyes.

Lerris thought he slept. He began writing a short account of the gate’s movement, leaving space for the things only Kelset could have seen from above. Beside him Ivradan rubbed his hands together, looked at the door, and did it again.

“The balance,” Rennik said.

Lerris raised his head.

“It can’t have lifted while the gate was caught.”

“You told me it did.”

“I saw it.”

He opened his eyes. They were clear and angry.

“It must have had force from the other side.”

“Water?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Rennik looked toward the vent. His face changed, not much, but enough for Lerris to stop writing.

“What?”

The engineer lifted one hand.

From the passage came Kelset’s voice, calling their names before he opened the door. Dorven followed him in. They had found a dry stair, a locked grille, and air moving through it. The grille would take work, but the hinges were on their side.

Mira asked Ivradan to compare the old plan. Kelset knelt to look at the injured leg. Dorven remained by the door, his head turned toward the sound of water.

Rennik touched Lerris’s sleeve.

“Don’t write this yet.”

Lerris waited.

“I think I know how it lifted.”

“Tell Mira.”

“I want to see it on paper first.”

His hand had stopped shaking. He reached for the little book.

Lerris held it, unwilling to make a refusal out of a thing that might help them. Then he saw where Rennik was looking.

Not at the page.

At the strip of cloth beneath the vent, hanging still now in air that no longer touched Lerris’s face.

“Captain,” he said.

Chapter 20 — The Hinge Side

Mira held her wet fingers beneath the vent. Then she stood on the bench beside Rennik’s good foot and put her cheek to it.

“Kelset. The grille. Take Ivradan and a lamp.”

Kelset was already gathering the tools.

“Lift the pins?”

“If you can. Don’t spend time saving it.”

She stepped down. Lerris had expected an order to hurry, something that would give the fear in him permission to move. Instead she asked Dorven to show her the shelf from which they had taken the splints.

It was deep enough for sacks. Its remaining boards were too short to carry a man.

“The ladder,” Rennik said.

There was a narrow wooden ladder hooked beneath it. Kelset had used it to reach the upper shelf. Mira pulled it out, tested the side rails against her knee, and laid it beside the bench. Its feet extended beyond Rennik’s head; the hooks would clear his injured leg. Dorven took off his coat.

“Over the rungs,” he said. “Keep hers over him.”

“She’s cold,” Rennik said.

“So am I. Move your arm.”

They spread Dorven’s coat across the ladder, keeping Mira’s aside to cover Rennik after the move. The uninjured leg could be secured with a belt, the splinted one supported between rolled cloth. Lerris tried to work out where to put his hands before anyone asked him. When they lifted, Rennik made a noise that stopped him.

“Keep going,” Mira said.

He obeyed. Rennik’s hand found his collar, pulled it tight against his throat, then released it. They lowered him onto the coats. For several breaths he did not speak.

“Sorry,” Lerris said.

Rennik shook his head against the wood.

They had no strap broad enough to hold his shoulders. Dorven opened the tool roll, emptied it, and passed its ties beneath the rails. The familiar pockets lay against Rennik’s chest. He picked a small file from one that Dorven had missed and held it out.

“Don’t want that in me as well.”

Mira took it. Lerris tried to smile and could not.

From the passage came three quick blows, a pause, and another. Ivradan said something too far away to distinguish. Lerris started toward the sound.

“Here,” Mira said.

He came back to the ladder.

“Rennik’s bag. Your own things. Nothing off the shelves.”

The little book lay open on the bench. He closed it over the wet page. Rennik watched him put it away.

“I still need to work that out.”

“When we’re through.”

“You’ll forget what I asked.”

“I won’t.”

He wanted to add that he had written it down. The engineer knew.

They carried the ladder head first. Mira took the front, Lerris the rear; Dorven walked beside the splint wherever the passage permitted. At the first corner Lerris’s knuckles scraped the wall. At the next, the ladder would not turn until Mira lowered her end and he raised his. Rennik shut his eyes. Sweat ran into the hollow beneath his ear.

The passage smelled of wet mortar. Behind them the recess door gave a single heavy knock. Dorven looked back but did not stop.

At the grille, Ivradan was holding a lamp in one hand and Kelset’s discarded jacket in the other. His mouth was bleeding. Kelset knelt beside the lower hinge with a punch and hammer.

“What happened?” Mira asked.

“Pin came out,” Ivradan said.

“Upper one jumped,” Kelset said. “I told him to stand back.”

“You told me to lift it.”

“I told you to lift the light.”

Mira put down her end. Lerris did the same. The relief in his hands hurt almost as much as carrying had.

“Can it come off?” she asked.

“One more.”

Kelset struck the punch. Nothing moved. He spat on his hand, shifted his grip, and struck again.

Lerris looked through the grille. The steps beyond rose into a broad landing. Air touched his face between the bars. He leaned closer, wanting more of it, and saw the clean crescent where the gate had recently rubbed the floor.

“Someone’s opened this.”

“Good,” Kelset said. “Then it can open.”

“It wasn’t on the inspection route.”

Kelset looked up at him.

“Hold this.”

He gave Lerris the punch. The place for its tip was visible beneath the hinge, no mystery to it. Lerris knelt, placed it, and wished his hand did not shake.

“Keep your thumb down.”

The blow went through the tool and into his wrist. The second did too. With the third the pin rose far enough for Kelset to grip it with the pliers.

They lifted the grille together. Its weight came suddenly onto Lerris when the upper corner cleared. He stumbled; Ivradan caught the side before it struck Rennik. They leaned it against the wall, leaving a gap wide enough for the ladder.

Mira went up first with a lamp. On the landing she stopped and listened. Then she crouched, holding the flame low.

“Bring him.”

There were seven steps. They rested after the fourth, with Mira and Kelset taking the upper end and Lerris below. Rennik asked them to wait. His eyes were wet and his voice had become very quiet.

Dorven put a hand on his shoulder.

“We’re waiting.”

Lerris stood with the rail pressing his thigh and looked at the old whitewash above Rennik’s head. Someone had once painted around a bracket rather than remove it. The bracket was gone; its shape remained, two little horns and a square.

Rennik nodded.

They carried him to the landing.

The draft there was strong enough to stir Lerris’s hair. Kelset went back for the bags while Ivradan pressed the inside of his lip with his tongue and counted the lamps. Below, a thin sheet of water crossed the place where Lerris had knelt.

“Leave the grille,” Mira said.

Kelset had not tried to lift it. He was looking at the water.

“My father put that in.”

Then he picked up the last bag and came upstairs.

Chapter 21 — The Lower Exchange

Beyond the landing they found a cart.

It stood beneath an arch in a recess made for it, two wheels blocked with wedges, handles propped on a stone. Dust had settled in its bed. One side was lower than the other; Kelset crouched to examine the axle while Lerris held the light and tried not to hope too quickly.

“The tire’s loose.”

“Will it carry him?” Mira asked.

“Until it doesn’t.”

“Look again.”

Kelset did. He took the wheel off, examined the hub, and put it back. The iron tire shifted when he hit it with his palm, but the spokes held. He wedged a strip of wood beneath its rim and asked Dorven for the small hammer.

They laid the ladder across the bed. It overhung at both ends; Rennik would ride at an angle, with his head above the handles. Mira packed the space beneath it with their bags. She made Lerris push down on each corner before she allowed them to lift him.

Once aboard, Rennik opened his eyes.

“Terrible springing.”

“Complain to the proprietor,” Kelset said.

“Who is he?”

“Dead, I hope.”

Rennik laughed, then held his breath against the pain. Kelset waited until he had recovered before moving the handles.

The wheels sounded loud in the passage. Lerris walked beside the loose one, watching the little strip of wood travel round. Above his head ran a row of hooks, some still supporting lengths of chain. The walls widened. There were alcoves with stone counters, a gutter down the middle, and chalk marks too worn for Ivradan to read.

“Loading exchange,” Dorven said.

“Before the west incline,” Rennik answered from the cart. “You’d bring the loads across level and transfer them here.”

Lerris could imagine the labor. He found himself looking for a place to buy something warm.

There was a smell of frying fat.

He stopped so abruptly that Ivradan walked into him.

“What?”

“Do you smell that?”

The scholar sniffed. Dorven nodded. Kelset kept his hands on the cart but turned his head toward a shuttered opening in the wall.

“Don’t,” Mira said.

“I wasn’t.”

They went on. The smell followed them for a little way, sweetening into something Lerris could not identify. His stomach contracted. He had eaten before the inspection, an egg and bread, and regretted leaving the crust because he had been in a hurry to be ready.

At the next arch, light crossed the floor.

Mira stopped the cart well short of it. She and Dorven went ahead, remaining in sight. Lerris saw her put one hand against the wall, then withdraw it and wipe her fingers on her trousers.

She came back.

“There are people.”

Kelset looked past her.

“How many?”

“I can’t tell.”

“Workers?”

“No.”

They left the cart under the arch, where Rennik could see the opening but not what lay beyond it. Mira asked Lerris to come with her. Ivradan came too, uninvited; when she looked at him he held up the old plan.

The hall was long, with stalls along both walls and lanterns suspended over the aisle. Their light had a faint green cast. Under one a woman folded cloth into squares. At another a man weighed something that made a dry little clatter in the pan.

They wore broad collars fastened at the shoulder. Lerris had seen such a fastening in Ivradan’s drawings. He looked down at the old plan, then back at the woman.

Through her bent wrist he could see the edge of the cloth.

Mira’s fingers touched his sleeve. He had moved forward without intending to.

“No farther yet.”

At the nearest counter, a man sat with his arms folded. Small bones lay before him, each drilled at one end. Some were worked into combs, others into toggles or narrow handles. He raised his head. His eyes were there, dark beneath pale brows. Lerris had expected empty sockets and disliked discovering what a foolish comfort that would have been.

The man spoke.

Ivradan answered, slowly. The man repeated himself, louder, and pointed at Mira’s sword. It hung at her hip, still sheathed.

“He says edged goods are sold in the farther aisle,” Ivradan said.

“Tell him we aren’t selling.”

He did. The man’s expression changed to one Lerris knew from stalls in Thepyrgos.

Then he looked behind them.

Rennik had raised his head above the cart’s side. The merchant unfolded his arms.

This time Lerris understood a few words. The language was close enough to the speech he had heard in the oven court to tantalize him, far enough away that the next phrase escaped him. He heard feet, road, and something that might have meant a debt or a wound.

“He says there’s a place for him to lie,” Ivradan translated.

“Where?”

The merchant pointed along the hall.

Mira waited.

“Ask what he wants.”

Ivradan spoke. The man answered at considerable length, touching the counter twice, then holding out his palm.

“Copper.”

“How much?”

The scholar listened again.

“He wants to see it.”

Lerris took a small copper from his purse. Mira caught his wrist before he could put it down.

“Show him.”

He held it between finger and thumb. The merchant leaned close. His face was solid; his collar was not. Lerris could see the vertical grain of the stall through it.

The man said something sharp and impatient.

“He doesn’t know the stamp,” Ivradan said.

“Tell him the metal hasn’t changed.”

Ivradan looked at Mira, then translated. The merchant considered this. At last he nodded toward the cart and held up two fingers.

From behind Lerris, Rennik said, “Don’t buy me anything until you’ve seen the bed.”

The merchant smiled at the sound of his voice. Lerris wondered how much he had understood.

Chapter 22 — Two Coppers

The bed was a stone bench with a wooden back, under an awning at the end of the first row. Mira inspected it with her lamp while the merchant waited. There was room beside it for the cart. Two passages left the hall nearby; both rose, though one was blocked by fallen masonry beyond the second lantern.

The merchant tapped the bench and said something to Ivradan.

“He says nobody may trouble a sick man here.”

“For how long?”

The answer was until the lamps were trimmed.

Mira looked up. Above the bench, a wick burned inside a pierced metal bowl. No reservoir was visible. She asked Ivradan whether the man had said who would trim it.

He had not.

“We use our own watch,” she said. “We don’t sleep.”

She took the two coppers from Lerris and put them on the counter herself. The merchant gathered them up. His fingers made a scraping sound against the stone.

They brought Rennik forward. The other merchants watched without leaving their stalls. The woman folding cloth lifted a strip of red material and called to him. He shook his head.

“Your sister’s wedding,” Kelset said.

“Don’t.”

Kelset’s mouth closed.

They helped him onto the bench. The back supported his shoulders; for the first time since the flood he could sit without someone holding him. Dorven loosened the cloth at his neck and gave him water. He drank carefully, watching the hall over the rim.

Lerris wanted to ask whether the voices were louder here. He could almost hear how the question would sound, with everyone waiting for an answer. He sat beside the bags instead.

Mira sent Kelset a short way up the clear passage. He returned after looking through an open doorway into another dry chamber. No current works marks, no bell cord, no sound of a waiting party.

“We eat,” she said.

Their food had survived in a waxed bag. It was bread and hard cheese. The bread smelled faintly of Rennik’s lamp oil but tasted like bread when Lerris put it in his mouth. He chewed until the ache in his jaw eased.

Ivradan held his piece without eating. He was watching the stalls.

“Do you know where we are?” Mira asked.

“I know what the plan calls it.”

“And?”

“The Bone Market.”

Kelset glanced at the drilled toggles on the nearest counter.

“Could’ve told you that.”

“It might have been named for the trade.”

“Then we’re all right.”

Ivradan turned toward him, saw his face, and looked away. He took a bite of bread.

Dorven was trying to loosen the plait Kelset’s daughter had made in his hair. Kelset reached across and undid the end for him. They worked at it together, blunt fingers made clumsy by cold. At last the little knot loosened.

“She’ll say you cut it,” Kelset said.

“Keep it, then.”

He put the short thread in Kelset’s hand. Kelset folded it into the pocket inside his coat.

Lerris finished his food. The hall’s lanterns remained steady. Their own lamp, set beside Mira’s boot, had burned noticeably lower. He asked Ivradan how long they had been there.

“Ten minutes.”

Kelset said, “Longer than that.”

None of them had a timepiece. Mira checked the oil, then the wound beneath Rennik’s dressing. There was a small fresh stain, no spreading flow. She covered it again and went to speak with the merchant.

Lerris followed with Ivradan.

They asked for a route to the west relief gallery. The merchant did not recognize the words. Ivradan tried the older name on his plan. The man looked at the paper and shook his head. He called to the cloth seller, who answered without looking up.

An argument followed. Lerris understood an insult, or thought he did from the way Ivradan pressed his lips together. The merchant dismissed the woman with a gesture and pointed at a line on the plan.

“That’s the blocked passage,” Ivradan said.

They told him it was blocked. He shrugged. After a moment he held out his hand again.

Mira did not move.

“No payment for that.”

His voice sharpened. Ivradan answered, then stopped.

“What?” she asked.

“He says we’re under his roof.”

“We paid for the bench.”

Ivradan repeated it. The man looked toward Rennik, still sitting within the reach of the awning, and smiled with his mouth closed.

Mira stepped between him and the bench.

“Get the cart,” she told Lerris.

He went quickly, angry with himself for the relief he felt at having a task that took him away. Dorven rose when he saw him coming. Rennik asked what had happened.

“We’re leaving.”

They got the cart alongside the bench. As Lerris reached to support Rennik’s leg, a hand came down on the other end of the ladder.

He had not seen anyone approach. The cloth seller stood beside Dorven. Her sleeve ended in a narrow cuff that looked new enough to have been sewn that morning. Below it the hand was almost transparent.

She spoke to Rennik.

“I don’t know what you’re saying,” he told her.

She tried again, slower. Lerris caught the word for wheel. Rennik heard it too.

“What wheel?”

She pointed at the cart, then up the clear passage. With her other hand she made a turning motion, stopped, and pressed her palm flat against the ladder.

Ivradan hurried over. Behind him Mira and the merchant were still facing each other across the counter.

The woman spoke to the scholar. He asked her to repeat something. She did, impatiently, tapping the cart wheel each time she said the word.

“The way rises to the old turning house,” he said. “She says the lift doesn’t work. You can’t get him up it.”

“Is there another?” Dorven asked.

“She says a stair. Too narrow for this.”

Lerris looked at the ladder. They could abandon the cart, carry him as before. Dorven was already looking at Mira to ask.

The woman held up a hand. From beneath the folded red cloth at her stall she took a thin wooden board and a piece of chalk. She drew a shaft, then a smaller room beside its foot. Inside the room she marked two circles. One she crossed out.

Rennik leaned forward.

“Let me see.”

She brought it closer.

He touched the smaller circle without quite touching the board.

“A hand drive?”

Ivradan translated. The woman nodded.

“Where’s the handle?”

She answered. Ivradan listened and frowned.

“Taken for the forge. A long time ago.”

At the counter the merchant struck the stone with his palm. The woman did not look at him. She wiped a patch of chalk away with her thumb and drew an arch beside the room, with three marks above it.

“What does she want?” Lerris asked.

Before Ivradan could translate, she pointed at the lit lamp beside their bags.

Mira came back. She looked at the drawing, then at their light.

“Oil?”

The woman shook her head. She pointed again, this time at the flame, and covered the bowl of her own hanging lantern with her free hand.

Its green light showed through her fingers.

Ivradan asked a question. Her answer was very quiet.

“She wants to see her cloth,” he said. “In that light.”

Lerris lifted the lamp. Mira did not stop him.

He carried it to the stall and held it above the red strip. Under its yellow flame the cloth was brown, worn thin at the fold, its edges eaten into scallops. The woman smoothed it with both hands. For a moment Lerris could see every finger.

She put the chalk board down where they could take it.

Nobody spoke while she unfolded the rest.

Chapter 23 — The Turning House

The woman folded the cloth with its worn edges inside. She did not look at Lerris again. When he lowered the lamp, her hand stayed on the topmost fold for a moment before she reached for another piece.

Ivradan took the board. He thanked her in the older speech, and she made a small motion toward the clear passage.

Behind them, Kelset and Dorven had settled Rennik in the cart. The bone merchant watched from his counter. He said something as they passed, addressing Rennik rather than the others.

“What was that?” Rennik asked.

“Leave it,” Mira said.

“I asked him.”

Ivradan adjusted his grip on the board.

“He says you can come back.”

They pushed the cart beneath the arch. Lerris kept his eyes on the wheel. The little wooden wedge made a soft click whenever it passed close to the axle housing. Once clear of the hall, he looked behind him. He could still see the awning, the bench, the woman arranging her cloth. The lamps did not follow them.

The passage rose gently. Tracks had been worn into its paving by wheels narrower than theirs. Kelset tried to keep the cart between them, but at a bend the loose wheel dropped into a groove and Rennik cried out.

“Stop. Stop.”

Everyone stopped.

Lerris knelt at the wheel. His impulse was to lift it out at once, to undo what had hurt the engineer. Rennik was holding the side of the cart so hard that his nails had gone pale.

“Tell me before you move it.”

“We will.”

Kelset put his hand beneath the axle. Lerris took the rim. They counted, lifted, set it down. Rennik kept his eyes closed until Mira asked him to open them.

“How bad?”

“I don’t want to answer that again.”

She nodded and checked the splint instead.

They went more slowly. Lerris walked ahead at each bend, pointing out the worn channels. He could smell the oil in his lamp, his own wet wool, and something mineral, like a warm stone wetted by rain. He found himself trying to remember whether the egg he had eaten that morning had been salted. The question persisted after he knew it did not matter.

The turning house occupied a chamber tall enough for him to straighten without looking up first. One wall was open to a shaft. A wooden platform rested level with the floor, enclosed on three sides by a rail. Heavy chains rose from its corners into the dark. Beside it stood a drum and a toothed wheel on a stone plinth.

Kelset set the cart down well away from the edge.

“No one steps on it.”

Rennik lifted his head.

“Show me the chain.”

Mira carried a lamp to the nearest link. Lerris expected the metal to be red with rust. There was black scale on it, but the places where the links touched shone. A damp thread of oil hung from the bottom curve.

“Someone maintains it,” Kelset said.

“Or uses it,” Mira answered.

There were stairs beyond the plinth, steep and narrow, wrapping round the shaft. Kelset went up far enough to examine the first turn. He came back rubbing his shoulder.

“We’d have to stand him almost upright to turn the ladder.”

Rennik stared at the steps.

“No.”

“We’re looking,” Mira said.

“No.”

She left it there.

Ivradan laid the woman’s board beside the plinth. The two circles matched the large drive wheel and the smaller gearing beneath it. A squared iron shaft projected from the smaller assembly. Its corners were polished, and one had a fresh burr.

“That takes the handle,” Rennik said.

“Can we fit a bar?” Kelset asked.

“Across what?”

Kelset crouched and examined the shaft. He tried the jaws of his largest wrench. They were too small.

“The handle has a socket,” Rennik said. “And a collar to keep it on. You turn that without the collar and it walks off under load.”

Dorven had gone to the shaft’s edge. He lay down with his belly on the floor and looked up, holding his lamp beyond his head.

“There’s a landing.”

“How far?”

“Two house heights. Perhaps three.”

“Can you see a door?” Mira asked.

“A rail. Nothing behind it.”

He drew himself back. Dust had made a gray patch on his wet shirt. He brushed it without much effect.

Rennik asked for the lamp again. He wanted to see the drum from below. They rolled the cart closer, stopping short of the platform, and angled it so that he could inspect the teeth and the retaining pawl. He sent Kelset to test a pin, then another. The engineer’s voice became more familiar as he worked; he interrupted, demanded a better light, called Kelset a cabbage when he touched the wrong place.

Kelset grinned at the insult. Lerris looked away before either of them saw how much that grin had relieved him.

“The platform’s down,” Rennik said at last. “We can inspect its bearers. If those hold, and the top wheels turn, we might get it up.”

“With all of us?”

“Not the cart. Perhaps three at a time.”

“And if the wheels don’t turn?” Mira asked.

“We don’t force them.”

From the shaft came a small metallic note. Everyone stopped speaking. It sounded again, higher, as though someone above them had tapped a chain with a tool.

“Anyone there?” Kelset called.

Mira caught his arm.

He looked at her, then down at her hand.

“Sorry.”

They waited. Water dripped somewhere below the platform. The chain did not sound again.

Mira asked Dorven to look up once more. He could see no one. She had him come away from the edge and stand where she could see him.

Lerris tried to remember precisely how the first note had sounded. A signal, two taps, a pause. It could have been the chain moving as it cooled. It could have been a person above with as little courage to answer as they had to call.

Rennik was looking at the squared shaft.

“The handle,” he said. “We need the handle before any of this matters.”

The arch marked on the board stood across the chamber. Three short strokes were cut above it. Ivradan raised his lamp, revealing a fourth, longer stroke nearly lost beneath a crust of mineral deposit.

He looked back at the drawing.

“Perhaps it used to be covered.”

“Does it say forge?” Mira asked.

“No. A maker’s mark, I think.”

He took out the rubbing of Thane’s workshop seal. The lower strokes matched. The upper portion had been worn away on the stone.

Mira examined the distance between the arch and the cart. Then she picked up her bag.

“We take him with us.”

Rennik began to object.

“We’ve found you a better road,” she said. “You can argue about it while we push.”

Chapter 24 — Tools Left Warm

The passage to the forge was paved with broad slabs. The cart ran easily. Lerris stayed beside the damaged wheel, but the packing strip held, and the silence between its small clicks grew comfortable enough for him to hear the other sound.

Someone was crying.

Not loudly. There were long pauses during which he hoped it had stopped, followed by a breath drawn painfully through the nose. Once it became a low moan. It seemed to come from ahead and a little to the left.

Dorven heard it too. He kept turning his head.

Mira halted them outside a pair of open stone doors. Heat moved across Lerris’s face. He could not see a fire.

The room beyond contained benches, racks of tools, and three low anvils. A shallow channel ran from each anvil to a basin in the floor. Dark liquid gathered slowly at their edges and fell, drop by drop, into the channels. The crying rose when a drop struck the nearest basin.

Ivradan whispered something.

“What?” Mira asked.

He showed her the rubbing. Its seal was cut into the lintel above the largest bench.

“Thane.”

“That tells us who worked here.”

“Yes.”

“Does it tell us whether we can go in?”

“No.”

Rennik shifted in the cart. “Lamp at the floor first.”

Mira crouched. The flame leaned toward the room, then stood straight. Dorven tasted the air and spat into his handkerchief. He shook his head when Lerris looked at him.

“Metal. Not enough to turn us.”

They waited at the threshold while the captain inspected what she could reach without leaving their sight. The paving was dry. A woven mat lay beside the nearest bench, stiff with dust. She prodded it with the tip of her scabbard and watched it break along a fold.

“Cart by the door,” she said. “No one drinks anything. Nothing touched without saying what.”

They moved Rennik inside.

The heat increased as Lerris passed the lintel. His wet collar felt hot against his neck. Yet when he laid his hand against the door to steady himself, the stone was cold.

“Don’t lean,” Mira told him.

He took his hand away.

Kelset found the handle almost at once. It hung on two hooks behind a bench: a long crank with a square socket at one end and a wooden grip at the other. Beneath it were smaller sockets, punches, and several collars arranged by size.

He pointed without touching.

“That’s it.”

Rennik asked him to bring a lamp closer. The engineer could see only the lower half from the cart. Mira moved him a little, checked the wheel blocks, and stood beside him.

“Looks right,” he said. “Measure it.”

Kelset took a scrap of cord from his pocket. He had used it at the turning house to carry the width of the shaft. He stepped toward the bench.

A man stood on its other side.

Lerris could not have said when he arrived. He wore a leather apron over a close-fitting shirt. One sleeve had been rolled above the elbow; the other hung empty and was tucked into his belt. His remaining hand rested on the bench.

He spoke to Kelset.

Kelset stopped.

“Ivradan.”

The scholar came forward, keeping clear of the channels. He answered with a formal inclination of his head. The man repeated himself.

“What does he want?” Mira asked.

“To know who’s measured the load.”

“Tell him Rennik has inspected the lower gear. We haven’t tested the upper wheels.”

Ivradan translated. The man looked at Rennik. His eyes were bright and wet, like the eyes of someone who had been too close to smoke. He asked another question.

“The bearers,” Ivradan said.

“Sound at the ends we can see,” Rennik answered. “We haven’t put anyone under it yet.”

Another question followed, then another. Ivradan stumbled over one term. The man struck the bench with his palm. A drop fell from the nearest anvil, and Lerris flinched at the sob that followed it.

“Get him to show you,” Rennik said. “Don’t guess.”

The man drew with a piece of pale stone on the bench. A chain, a wheel, a suspended rectangle. His lines were quick and clean. He tapped the space below the rectangle.

“Counterweight,” Kelset said.

Rennik nodded. “We haven’t seen it. Tell him that.”

Ivradan did. The man pointed to the door.

Kelset swore.

“We can’t see the damned thing until we lift the platform.”

Rennik was silent for a moment.

“There’ll be an inspection cover.”

“We didn’t find one.”

“We weren’t looking for it.”

Lerris watched Mira. She had been attending to the man’s hand, his face, the distance from his bench to Rennik. At last she asked Ivradan to tell him about the flood.

The scholar spoke at length. The man listened without moving. When Ivradan mentioned the south check, his hand tightened. When he described the rising water, the man turned toward the back of the workshop.

There were stone panels on the wall, set between racks. Each contained a different arrangement of cut lines. Several had been broken across the middle. Others had no lines at all.

He spoke more quickly now. Ivradan asked him to slow down. He did not.

“Something about the lower seat,” the scholar said. “A bed. A bearing bed, perhaps.”

“Ask him to draw it,” Rennik said.

The man took up the pale stone again. He drew a curved chamber beneath the lift, a channel entering it, and a row of upright marks along one wall. Halfway through the last mark he stopped. His hand went to his face.

He was crying.

The sound was scarcely louder than what they had heard in the passage. He wiped his eyes on the back of his wrist, inspected the wetness, and resumed drawing. The line would not come straight. He rubbed it away with his palm and tried again.

Lerris felt himself lean forward. He wanted to tell him that the mark was clear enough, that they understood. He did not understand it at all.

The man put down the chalk. He came round the bench and took the handle from its hooks.

Mira moved in front of the cart.

He ignored her. At Kelset’s feet he laid the crank, then a collar and a retaining pin. He touched the pin, held up one finger, and pointed to the socket.

“We know,” Kelset said softly. “We know that bit.”

The man went back to the drawing. Ivradan bent over it beside him. Lerris stayed where he was until Rennik beckoned.

“There’s a clean board in my bag,” the engineer said. “For tracing. Give it to him.”

Lerris found it wrapped with two narrow rulers. He carried it to Ivradan, who set it beside the drawing and began to copy.

The man corrected the position of the channel. Ivradan scraped the line away and drew it again. This time the man nodded.

A wet drop landed on the board. Ivradan looked up, then quietly moved it a little farther from the man’s face.

Chapter 25 — Beneath the Platform

They returned with the handle, collar, pin, and a drawing that Ivradan held flat in both hands. The figure in the workshop had not given his name. The scholar called him Thane once, while explaining the marks, and Mira asked whether he had answered to it.

“No.”

“Then write that too.”

Ivradan looked wounded. Lerris recognized the look before the scholar lowered his head: the wish that someone might let him have the discovery for a little while before asking how much of it was his.

The return passage seemed shorter. The cart’s wheel had begun to squeak. Kelset stopped to adjust the packing strip and found a crack in it. He replaced it with another sliver from the discarded piece in his pocket. His hands had stiffened; twice he dropped the little hammer.

“Let me,” Dorven said.

“I’ve got it.”

“You’ve dropped it.”

“I noticed.”

Dorven waited. Kelset struck the wedge home and stood up too fast. He caught the wall, bent his head, and stayed there.

Mira gave him water. He drank without thanking her, then wiped his mouth and said, “Thank you,” so emphatically that she almost smiled.

At the turning house, the chains were still.

Rennik had them bring him to the edge again. He directed Dorven to clear dust from the paving beside the platform. Beneath it they found a narrow cover fitted flush with the floor. Its lifting ring had been folded into a recess and filled with dirt. Kelset cleaned it out with the file Rennik had found in the tool roll.

The cover opened over a short iron ladder.

Cold air rose from below. It smelled of old grease and standing water. Mira lowered a lamp on a cord, watched the flame, and drew it up again. They waited, checked it a second time, and listened.

“I’ll go,” Dorven said.

Mira looked at him.

“You can keep your head at the top,” he added. “I’d rather fit than be brave about it.”

He took off his tool belt and passed it down separately. The ladder was only five rungs long. At its foot he crouched beneath the platform, turning the lamp until the light showed between the boards above.

Lerris knelt at the opening. Dorven’s face was below him, creased with effort as he looked toward the rear of the shaft.

“Weight’s down here. Stone blocks in an iron cage.”

“Resting on anything?” Rennik asked.

“No. Hanging. Plenty of room below.”

“Links?”

Dorven reached up, then stopped.

“Which ones can I touch?”

“None until you tell me what you see.”

He described them. Rennik asked for more light on the upper connection. Lerris held his lamp over the opening, and Dorven shifted his own. The engineer listened with his eyes closed.

“Is the cage leaning?”

“A little.”

“Toward you or away?”

“Away.”

Rennik opened his eyes.

“Anything against the back?”

Dorven moved farther under the platform. His face disappeared. The light remained, falling in strips through the boards. Lerris leaned into the opening.

“Dorven?”

“Here.”

“Come where I can see you.”

“I’m looking at the thing he asked for.”

Lerris sat back. Mira was watching him.

Dorven returned to the ladder after a moment.

“Broken timber. Wedged behind the cage. I can’t tell what it was.”

“Can you take it out?” Kelset asked.

“No,” Rennik said sharply. “Come up.”

Dorven climbed. His sleeve had acquired a black smear, and there was blood on one knuckle. He looked at it as though it belonged to someone else, sucked it briefly, and accepted the cloth Mira offered.

“If we lift it with that behind the weight,” Rennik said, “we could jam it halfway. Or break the cage.”

“Can we shift the wood?”

“With the weight secured.”

They examined the locking sockets in the shaft wall. Rennik had noticed them on the first inspection but not known how the lower pair related to the cage. Two iron stops lay in a rack behind the plinth. Kelset cleaned them. One was bent; the other fitted its socket.

They would need another.

Mira stood with the bent stop in her hands. Lerris expected her to swear. Instead she set it down carefully and rubbed the bridge of her nose.

“Forge,” Kelset said.

“Yes.”

Ivradan sat on the floor beside Rennik and unfolded the workshop drawing. The engineer leaned over it. Lerris watched him follow the curved chamber with one finger, stop at the row of marks, then begin again.

“I know this,” Rennik said.

“From the plans?”

“No.”

Ivradan looked up.

Rennik pressed his lips together.

“Not the whole thing. The cut at the bottom. There’s something like it behind the lower control.”

“Have you seen it?” Mira asked.

“Part of it. We inspect the access chamber. The seat’s behind the wall.”

Ivradan took out his copied plan. He put it beside the new drawing. Their channels joined if one sheet was turned slightly and the other moved underneath it.

Lerris could see that much. He could not tell whether the joining was meaningful or merely something any two bent lines could be made to do.

Rennik drew the sheets apart.

“Don’t join them yet.”

“I wasn’t—”

“I need the difference left.”

Ivradan withdrew his hand.

For a while they were quiet. Mira inspected the lamps. Kelset put the bent stop in a sack so that its dirty end would not smear the rest of his tools. Dorven ate the last corner of his bread, turning it over several times before he took a bite.

Lerris crouched beside Rennik.

“What did you mean, before? When you said you thought you knew how the gate lifted.”

The engineer looked at him, then at the open inspection cover.

“I could see it happening.”

“You were there.”

“After. On the bench. There was a way the pull could go through the stone instead of the chains. I could see where to put it.”

Lerris waited.

“Does that sound foolish?”

“No.”

It sounded worse than foolish. He kept that out of his voice, but Rennik saw something in his face and pulled the drawing closer.

“There’s a seat behind the control wall,” the engineer said. “That’s real. You can ask anyone who’s worked that level.”

“I believe you.”

“Then don’t look like that.”

Lerris could not think what to do with his face. He looked at the plan instead.

Mira called him over. She wanted him and Ivradan to take the stop to the workshop with Kelset. She would remain with Rennik and Dorven. Three lamps would stay lit, one for the turning house, two for the passage and forge; the others would remain closed and cool in the bag.

“How long before we come looking?” she asked.

“We don’t know how long it will take,” Ivradan said.

“Before you come back and tell me.”

They agreed to return after examining whether the stop could be straightened. No work begun, no side passage, no second errand without coming back.

Lerris repeated the agreement. Mira nodded once.

On the way out he looked back at Rennik. The engineer had tilted the workshop drawing toward the lamp. His finger followed the cut at the bottom, then returned to it.

Dorven stood beside him. When he saw Lerris looking, he moved closer to the cart, making room for Mira to sit.

Chapter 26 — A Hand on the Hammer

The artisan was standing where they had left him. The drawing had been wiped from the bench. A damp patch remained where he had rested his arm.

Kelset took the stop from its sack and laid it down. He explained what they needed before remembering that Ivradan must speak for him. The scholar listened, then began. Halfway through, the artisan turned the iron over and pointed to its bent neck.

“Yes,” Kelset said. “There.”

The man shook his head. He tapped the place again, more firmly, and slid the stop back across the bench.

Kelset took it to the light.

There was a dark line across the inner angle. He scraped it with the file. The line remained.

“Cracked.”

Lerris leaned closer. He could see it now, fine as a hair where the bending had pressed the surfaces together.

“Could it still hold?”

Kelset looked at him.

“No.”

He put it down. A flush had risen above his collar. Lerris was ashamed of the question before he could decide whether it had been unreasonable.

The artisan opened a shallow drawer. Inside were iron blanks laid between strips of oiled cloth. He selected one and held it beside the broken stop. It was longer, with a thicker head, but the neck was straight.

He pointed to the inner end, drew a mark with his pale stone, and picked up a hammer.

Kelset reached for it. The man drew it back.

A question followed. Ivradan asked for the last word again, then answered something on his own behalf.

“What?” Kelset said.

“He wants to know who taught you.”

“My mother. For basic forging. My uncle for locks and fittings.”

“Which house?”

Kelset gave the name. The man did not appear to recognize it. He asked another question, and another. At last he set the hammer on the bench with its handle toward Kelset.

“We have to go back first,” Lerris said.

Both men looked at him. He hated the interruption, the absurdity of leaving now that they had been given what they needed. He could already imagine Mira asking why he had wasted time walking to her with good news instead of doing the work.

“We said we would.”

Kelset breathed out through his nose. Then he let go of the hammer.

“Tell him we’ll come back.”

The artisan listened to the translation. He laid the blank across the hammer handle and turned away.

They walked back together. Kelset took the cracked stop out twice to look at it under the lamp.

“I’d have tried it,” he said at last.

“Rennik would have checked,” Ivradan answered.

“He’s on his back. He’s checking everything through someone else.”

He put the iron away before either could reply.

Mira heard the report. She asked about heat, fuel, the time required. Kelset could answer only the last, and that provisionally: a short job if the metal was good, the hearth usable, and nothing had to be made to fit twice.

“No anvil liquid,” she said.

“I wasn’t planning to wash in it.”

“I know. I want it said.”

Rennik asked for the broken stop. Kelset gave it to him. The engineer ran his thumb beside the crack without pressing on it, then returned it.

“The other one’s seated?”

“Yes.”

“Take the width again before you go.”

Kelset did. Dorven held the lamp while he checked the socket against a new length of cord. This time he took the depth as well.

They returned to find the blank still lying across the hammer. Beyond the bench, a small hearth glowed dull red. Its light had not been visible from the doorway before. There was charcoal in it now, recognizably charcoal, with a powdery white edge to the pieces nearest the mouth.

Kelset held his hand near the opening. He withdrew it at once.

“That’s hot.”

The artisan pointed to a pair of tongs.

There were no bellows. Kelset searched the hearth’s sides, found a shutter in the stone behind it, and looked at the man. He nodded. Kelset opened it a little. The charcoal brightened.

Lerris stood well away with the lamp. The artisan had moved the metal himself, but now he waited. Ivradan translated an instruction. Kelset answered without looking round.

“I can see it.”

The blank took on color slowly. Kelset turned it, adjusted the shutter, and waited. Lerris had expected something more violent. His father had employed smiths whose hammers could be heard across a stable yard; he remembered sparks, men shouting, a horse trying to pull away. Here the fire made a little sucking noise. The crying continued behind them.

At last Kelset lifted the blank and brought it to the clear corner of the nearest anvil. He struck once.

Lerris felt the blow in his teeth.

He swallowed. Kelset struck again, working the end to the mark. The artisan stood beside him. His one hand hovered above Kelset’s wrist but did not touch it.

With the fourth blow Lerris smelled singed wool.

He looked down at himself. His coat was nowhere near the hearth. Ivradan had put a hand to his mouth.

“Do you smell—”

“Yes.”

Kelset kept working. His strokes had become faster. Sweat shone on his forehead. The artisan said something; Ivradan did not translate.

“What did he say?” Lerris asked.

The scholar stared at the hearth.

“My sleeve caught,” he said.

There was no flame on his sleeve. Lerris caught his arm before he could thrust it toward the basin.

Ivradan struggled once, hard enough to hurt him.

“Let go.”

“Look at it.”

“He said water. He said put it—”

“Look.”

The scholar looked. His breathing was loud. Lerris held the cloth away from his wrist, showing him the unburned seam.

At the anvil, Kelset stopped with the hammer raised.

The artisan’s hand lay across his.

For a moment there were two arms extending from Kelset’s shoulder, one bare, one clothed. Lerris could see the worker’s dark sleeve through the older man’s skin. The hammer shook between their grips.

“Kelset.”

“I can’t let go.”

“Put it down.”

“I can’t.”

Lerris seized the hammer handle below his fist. Heat burned across his palm although the wood felt cool. He tried to lower it. Kelset made a choking sound and resisted.

“It’s still in there,” the worker said.

“What is?”

“His hand. In the press. Don’t—”

Ivradan shouted in the older language.

The artisan turned his head.

The pressure left the hammer so suddenly that Lerris struck its head against the anvil’s edge. Kelset fell backwards. Lerris caught his elbow, failed to hold him, and went down on one knee beside him.

The man in the apron stood clear of them. His empty sleeve hung from his belt.

Ivradan was shaking. He spoke again, more slowly. The man looked toward the hearth, then at Kelset on the floor. His face did not change. He took the tongs in his remaining hand and lifted the iron from the anvil.

“What did you say?” Lerris asked.

“The work is cold.”

“What?”

“He kept telling us not to stop. I said the work was cold.”

Kelset looked at his hand. He opened it, closed it, then wiped it down his face.

“Was it?”

“It had gone dark.”

The artisan put the blank back into the hearth. Then he withdrew to the bench.

Lerris wanted to leave. He wanted it with such force that he began gathering the cord and the broken stop before anyone spoke. Kelset watched him.

“We still need it.”

“I know.”

“Then put that down.”

They rested until Kelset could stand. Ivradan drank a mouthful of water and rinsed his split lip. Lerris inspected his own palm. There was no burn, only the pressure mark where he had gripped the handle.

For the remaining work Ivradan stood beside Kelset, repeating each instruction in the current speech. Lerris watched the artisan’s hand. They stopped between heats, even when the figure beckoned them on. Once Kelset left a stroke unfinished and stepped back until the double shadow beneath his elbow became one again.

The new stop fitted their measurements. Kelset cooled it in a clean pail they filled from their own water, a little at a time, while Ivradan translated the artisan’s objections to the waste. He filed its edges, checked the neck, and wrapped it in the sack.

At the door he looked back.

“Tell him thank you.”

Ivradan did.

The artisan was staring at the empty sleeve. He did not answer.

On the return journey Lerris offered to carry the stop. Kelset let him. A little farther on he asked whether Lerris had seen the hand caught in the press.

“No.”

“I did.”

He kept flexing his fingers as they walked.

Chapter 27 — The Upward Road

Mira was sitting on the floor with her back against the cart. Rennik had fallen asleep. Dorven stood beside the open cover, moving his lamp slowly from one hand to the other.

When the three returned, Mira rose at once. She listened to what had happened in the workshop without looking away from Kelset’s hand.

“Can you hold things?”

He picked up the sack containing the stop.

“Can you let go?”

He put it down. His mouth twisted, but he did not argue.

She took his hand and turned it over. There was an old scar at the base of his thumb, the kind Lerris would not have noticed an hour earlier. She pressed each finger. Kelset answered, watching her rather than his hand.

Rennik woke while they were speaking. He tried to sit and caught his breath. Mira put a rolled bag behind his shoulders.

“Better?”

“No. Leave it there.”

Dorven went down into the inspection space. This time Kelset knelt at the opening and passed the new stop to him. They seated it opposite the old one, beneath the projecting lugs on the weight cage. Rennik had them check the depth twice. The stops would catch the cage if it dropped; they could not prevent it rising.

“We take the strain on the handle,” he said. “Then draw the timber clear. No one reaches through the cage.”

Kelset fitted the crank, collar, and pin. Ivradan and Lerris took the long handle while he watched the gear. They turned it until the pawl caught, one tooth at a time. The platform lifted scarcely the breadth of Lerris’s finger. Below it Dorven called that the wood had loosened.

They stopped. Dorven used a hook from his tool belt to pull the broken piece toward him. It scraped the floor, caught, and came free with a hollow clatter.

Lerris could see him now, bent over the timber. There were holes along its edge, regularly spaced.

“Old rail,” Dorven said. “Fell from above.”

They brought him out before moving anything else. His shirt was gray from shoulder to waist. Kelset brushed the worst of the dust off him, then handed him water.

The empty platform went up.

It made a noise Lerris disliked: a long complaint from the upper wheels, followed by a brief silence at each turn. Rennik listened. At the third turn he told them to stop. Kelset climbed the stair to examine what he could see from the side. He returned with a little black grease on his fingers.

“Axle’s turning. Rim’s rubbing the guard.”

“Constantly?”

“One spot.”

Rennik thought about it. He asked how much clearance, whether there was fresh metal in the grease, whether the guard itself moved. Kelset answered, went back to check one thing, and came down again.

They raised the platform to the landing. Nothing broke. When they lowered it, one of the empty hooks on the rail swung against a post and made the same small note they had heard before.

Kelset looked at Mira.

She nodded. “Could have been.”

They left the cart below. Lerris felt an unreasonable reluctance to abandon it, though he had cursed its wheel often enough. Kelset took the little hammer out of the bed. Dorven removed the wedges and put them with his tools. They transferred the ladder and Rennik to the platform, then lashed it where it could not slide.

Mira would ride beside him. Lerris was to go with them and help at the upper landing. Kelset and Ivradan would turn the handle; Dorven would climb the stair, where he could watch the platform’s progress without adding weight to it.

At first there was only motion in the chains. Then the platform rose beneath Lerris’s boots.

He held a post. Cold moved along his legs from the shaft below. The floor of the turning house dropped slowly away, revealing the underside of the stair, a narrow service door, the weight cage descending between its guides. He wanted to look at all of it and keep his eyes on Rennik at the same time.

“Hold the lamp lower,” Mira said.

He did. Rennik’s face shone with sweat. The engineer had one hand over his mouth and was breathing through his fingers.

“You can stop them,” Lerris said.

Rennik shook his head.

The upper wheel complained. Dorven appeared on the stair beside them, crouched beneath a low stone arch. He waited for the platform to pass, then climbed again.

At the landing, Mira signalled for the final movement. The platform stopped an inch below the floor. Dorven dropped two iron catches beneath its frame. He tested each with his boot before anyone stepped off.

Lerris moved onto the landing first. Solid stone under his feet made him light-headed. He put down the lamp and helped Mira lift the ladder clear. They settled Rennik against a wall while Dorven called down that the load was off.

The others came by the stairs. Kelset carried the crank on his shoulder, saying they might need it if the next turn went as well as all the others. He sat beside Rennik when he reached them and pressed his forehead to his knees.

Beyond the landing, a passage ran toward a line of square columns. There were no green lanterns here. A white patch on one column showed beneath their lamps: the remains of a works mark, newer than the carving behind it.

Mira looked at it for a long time.

“West relief approach,” she said.

Kelset raised his head.

“Are you sure?”

“Of the mark.”

He laughed once and covered his face. Dorven sat down beside him. For a moment Lerris feared he would hear crying. Instead Kelset began describing the meal he wanted, impatiently rejecting each suggestion Dorven made until Dorven told him to cook it himself.

Mira let them sit. She leaned beside Rennik, closed her eyes, and rested the back of her head against the stone.

Lerris watched the white mark. He allowed himself to imagine daylight, then a room with a door he could leave open.

Chapter 28 — The Farther Lamp

The works marks continued along the passage. Some had been painted over older signs; one was hidden behind a mineral curtain that Kelset broke away with his knife. Beyond it the ceiling lowered and the paving changed to narrow slabs laid across the direction of travel.

They carried Rennik again. Lerris and Kelset took the first turn at the ladder, then Mira replaced Kelset. The engineer apologized once for his weight. Dorven told him he had always been insufferably heavy and it was too late to change now.

At the columns they found a door hanging open against a wall. Beyond it stood another, shut. Each had a small square inspection opening beside its jamb, covered by a sliding plate that had been left raised. A wheel projected from the wall between the doors.

Rennik asked to be set down where he could see it.

“Pair of pressure doors,” he said. “You shut the first before the second can open.”

“Can we get through together?” Mira asked.

He measured the space with his eyes. It was long enough for the ladder but scarcely wide enough for people to stand beside it.

“Not six with this.”

Kelset examined the wheel and the iron link disappearing through the stone above it. There were words cut beside the spindle. Ivradan read them aloud, translated, and asked Rennik about one term.

“Released,” the engineer said. “As in taken off the catch. Not free to move.”

Mira made him explain it again in relation to the doors.

They could pass through in two groups. The first door would close behind the first group, allowing the far one to open. Once they were out, the far door had to be shut before the near one could be released for those waiting. The catches were worked from within the chamber and from the approaches. A mechanical indicator showed which door was seated.

“Unless something’s bent,” Kelset added. He showed Lerris the small return lever outside the chamber. “Don’t force this if the tab’s down.”

They tested the empty chamber first. Kelset entered, shut the near door, and worked the wheel. Through the inspection opening Lerris watched the far door move. Kelset checked its approach, then came back. He closed it and released the near door.

“Dry passage. Another turn. No one in sight.”

Mira wanted a signal they could distinguish from another borrowed voice.

“Watch the person,” Lerris said. “At the opening. Agree the signal now, and watch them make it.”

“Like before?” Dorven asked.

“Yes.”

He moved to the inspection opening and held up his hand.

“Palm flat for stop. Two fingers, then the whole hand, for ready. We don’t move on a voice alone.”

Mira looked through from the other side. The opening was too small for a shoulder but broad enough for a face and hand. A lamp held beside it lit both clearly.

“We keep saying what we’re doing as well,” she said.

They agreed. Lerris demonstrated the signals for Rennik, who watched without enthusiasm. The engineer wanted a look at the return linkage before they put him through. Kelset showed him the rod and the indicator. He nodded at last.

Mira, Dorven and Rennik went first. Mira took the head of the ladder; Dorven the foot. They eased it into the chamber, leaving room for the wheel. The near door closed. The indicator fell into its lower position.

Lerris watched Mira’s face through the opening. She looked tired, her hair flattened where the helmet band had pressed it. She showed her flat palm while Dorven operated the wheel. Then the far door opened and the two of them carried Rennik out.

Mira returned to close it.

A moment later she appeared at the opening beside the near door. Her lamp was below her face now, making her brow look heavy. She raised two fingers, opened her hand, and nodded.

“Ready,” she said.

Lerris reached for the exterior release. Kelset caught his wrist.

“Indicator.”

The iron tab had not risen.

“It’s stuck,” Lerris said.

He could see Mira. She was waiting for him, her hand still raised. The opening framed the little cut at her chin from the morning’s hurried shave. He had noticed it at breakfast and looked away because it seemed an intimate thing to have observed.

“We wait,” Kelset said.

Then something struck the far side of the door. The figure disappeared from the opening. Mira shouted, but her voice came from farther away than her face had been.

Lerris pulled the release.

It moved a hand’s breadth and stopped. Beyond the stone there was a hard metallic snap. The indicator jumped sideways. Kelset dragged him back as the release struck the wall.

Water came through the inspection opening in a solid gray jet.

They stumbled clear. Ivradan fell against a column. Kelset caught the edge of the door, then let go with a cry as cold water forced itself round the seal.

Lerris shouted Mira’s name.

There was no answer he could distinguish from the noise. The opening was filled with water. It struck the paving, spread to both walls, and began running toward the lift landing behind them.

Kelset seized the release with both hands and tried to return it. Lerris helped. Together they moved it far enough for the flow around the door to lessen, though the jet from the opening continued.

“The shutter,” Kelset said.

There was a sliding plate above the opening. Ivradan reached it with the end of the crank and drove it down. The last inches stuck. Kelset hammered it with the heel of his tool, and the water thinned to a trickle.

They stood panting in the spreading pool.

On the wall, the indicator hung crooked. Lerris touched it. It swung loosely beneath his finger.

From somewhere beyond the sealed door came three blows.

He put his mouth close to the stone and called that they were there.

Three blows again. Then two.

He did not know what they meant.

Chapter 29 — What He Had Seen

Kelset made Lerris take his hands off the mechanism.

He did not say it angrily. He pulled them away one at a time and put the lamp where Lerris could hold it. Then he opened a narrow iron cover beneath the indicator.

The little rod inside had broken at a drilled hole. A second piece lay across it, caught under tension. Kelset did not touch either.

“What happened?” Ivradan asked.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Mira closed the other door,” Lerris said.

Kelset looked up.

“I saw her.”

“Then I don’t know why there’s water.”

He went back to the opening. Lerris held the light where he pointed. There was blood on Kelset’s thumb, running down to the wrist. He had cut it on the edge of the release when it snapped back. Lerris offered a cloth. Kelset took it, wrapped it once, and continued looking.

Ivradan went to the side of the door and tapped with the crank. He repeated the sounds they had heard, slowly. After a pause something answered, too faint for Lerris to count.

“They’re there,” the scholar said.

Lerris wanted to agree. He wanted it so badly that he almost said he recognized Mira’s way of knocking.

“Keep trying,” he said instead.

The water at their feet had begun to drain down the passage. Kelset watched its level against the lowest hinge.

“If it’s falling out there too, we might be able to open this. Not yet.”

“How long?”

“I can’t see the other side.”

The words were sharp this time.

Lerris put the lamp down. He crouched beside the wall, where the noise of the water made it harder to hear himself.

“She gave the signal.”

Kelset sat back on his heels.

“I saw a light. I saw a hand. I was looking at the tab.”

“Her face.”

“I wasn’t looking at her face.”

Ivradan lowered the crank.

“What did you see?” Lerris asked him.

“I was watching Dorven. I thought I could still see his lamp past her.”

Lerris tried to put the views together. Mira at the opening, Dorven behind her, Rennik beyond the far door. The lamp lighting her from below. Her small nod. He remembered each part with the clarity that had made him pull the release.

In the workshop Kelset had felt a hand caught in a press. Ivradan had smelled his own sleeve burning. Lerris had been there. He had held the unburned cloth.

He pressed his palms against his eyes until colors swam beneath them.

“The face wasn’t enough,” he said.

Neither man answered.

“I told her it was. I said we could check that way.”

Kelset unwound the bloody cloth and examined his thumb. He wrapped it again, tighter.

“You pulled against the catch.”

“Yes.”

“I told you.”

“Yes.”

Kelset turned toward the door. For several moments his shoulders moved with his breathing. Then he pointed at the broken linkage.

“You’ll have to tell her exactly what you did. If we get back together. Not just that something fooled you.”

Lerris nodded. Kelset did not look round to see it.

They listened for more blows. None came.

Ivradan took out the plans. The water had reached the corner of the older sheet, and he tore off a loose wet scrap before it could pull farther. They spread the papers on the dryest part of the floor, weighting them with tools.

The chamber appeared as a blank between two walls. A thinner line passed around it and joined the farther approach.

“That could be a drain,” Kelset said.

“Or an inspection passage.”

“Or a drain.”

Ivradan kept his finger on it.

“There’s a door marked at this end.”

Kelset looked. Then he took the lamp and walked back toward the lift. Lerris followed without speaking. Near the last column they found a narrow recess, half concealed by a slab that had fallen from the facing. Behind it was a low iron-bound door with a ring handle.

Kelset knelt and scraped at its sill. Dry dust came away beneath his knife.

“No water this side.”

He examined the hinges, pushed the ring with the blade rather than his hand, and finally pulled. The door opened reluctantly. Air moved out against their faces. It smelled of wet stone, but the floor beyond was dry.

Ivradan brought the second lamp. Its flame remained steady in the opening. They waited, checked again, then looked farther in.

A stair descended between close walls. At its foot they could see a white works mark.

Kelset stared at it.

They returned to the pressure door and left a message cut into a scrap of the tracing board’s wrapping: their names, the passage taken, and the broken release. Lerris added that he had pulled it before the indicator changed. He wedged the note above the mechanism, where water would not reach it at once.

Ivradan tapped one more time.

They waited.

Kelset said Dorven’s name so quietly that Lerris thought at first he was speaking to himself. Then he said it louder, close to the shutter.

When no answer came, he picked up his tools.

Chapter 30 — The Man at the Sluice

The stair ended in a service passage wide enough for a person carrying a bag. Lerris’s shoulders brushed both walls. Their lamps filled it with hot oil smell, and he was glad when it opened into a chamber where three narrow channels ran beneath iron grates.

A man sat beside the middle grate.

He wore a modern works coat, dark at the knees and elbows. A lamp stood beside him, unlit. His head was bowed over something in his hands.

Kelset stopped so abruptly that Ivradan nearly struck him with the crank.

“Vasker?”

The man raised his face.

His beard had grown unevenly, with bare patches at the corners of his mouth. One eye was swollen almost shut. The other fixed on Kelset, then moved to the lamp.

“Put that down.”

Kelset lowered it.

“Where have you been?”

“Here.”

“You were on the west closure.”

The man looked past him.

“Did they send you?”

“No. We came down on an inspection. The gates shut. Rennik’s hurt.”

“Rennik?”

His face altered. He put down the thing he had been holding: a little wheel with four spokes, perhaps part of a gauge. He tried to stand. One leg would not straighten, and he lowered himself again with a grunt.

“Who was with him?”

“Mira. Dorven. They’re on the other side of the pressure doors.”

Vasker glanced toward a low arch at the rear of the chamber.

“Can’t go that way.”

“Is it flooded?”

“No.”

“Then why?”

The man rubbed his mouth. His fingers were wrinkled and pale. Under the nails lay a black substance that might have been grease.

“They’re working.”

Kelset crouched in front of him. Lerris wanted to stop him. Vasker touched a mended place on Kelset’s sleeve.

“You still have that.”

“Of course I do.”

“Told you to ask for a new one.”

“You told me plenty.”

Kelset took out the water flask. He held it where Vasker could see it and asked whether he wanted some. The man nodded. He drank too quickly, coughed, then drank again despite Kelset’s warning.

Ivradan stayed by the entrance. Lerris stood halfway between him and the two workers, looking at the channels. Water moved below the gratings without much sound. A thin copper wire had been wound through the bars of the middle one. It led to a metal cuff fastened around Vasker’s wrist.

“Kelset.”

“I see it.”

Vasker tucked the wrist into his lap.

“It keeps the count.”

“Of what?”

“The turns.”

His other hand found the small wheel again. He held it tightly enough to whiten the skin over his knuckles.

Kelset offered him the last piece of cheese from his bag. Vasker stared at it. For a moment he seemed unable to decide what it was. Then he took it, put it into his mouth, and began to cry without making a sound.

Lerris looked toward the low arch. He could hear something there, a regular scrape followed by a pause. Water, perhaps, or a tool moved across stone.

Kelset asked Vasker how long he had been below. He could not say. He knew the west closure had been ordered and remembered a dispute about leaving tools behind. He named two workers Kelset recognized. When asked where they were, he pointed beneath the grate.

Kelset looked down.

“Alive?”

Vasker shook his head impatiently.

“The tools.”

He had finished the cheese. A little remained in his beard, and he picked it out carefully and ate it.

They asked about the other passage. Vasker said it led to the lower control, then said it went to the old rooms, then insisted that they must not go until the work stopped. Ivradan tried the old name on his plan. The man flinched at it.

“I don’t know those words.”

“You said old rooms,” Ivradan answered.

“I don’t know those words.”

Kelset put a hand on the cuff. Vasker seized his wrist.

“No.”

“It’s cutting you.”

“No.”

Kelset let go. Vasker’s fingers remained around him for a moment, then released one at a time.

Lerris knelt far enough away not to crowd them. He asked whether they could sit with him while they looked at the plan.

Vasker nodded. His eye followed the paper as Ivradan brought it forward.

They spread it on a dry patch beside the channels. Kelset pointed to the pressure doors. Vasker touched the thin line they had followed, traced it back toward their entrance, and then moved his finger to a mark lower on the sheet.

“There.”

“That’s where it comes out?”

“That’s where you shut it.”

“What?”

“The water.”

He looked pleased to have made himself understood. He took the pencil Lerris offered and drew a short transverse line. The tip broke. He stared at it, then held it out apologetically.

Lerris took it back.

The scrape beyond the arch stopped.

Vasker’s head turned. His body followed it with a suddenness that made Kelset draw away. He listened, his lips moving. Then he gathered up the little wheel and began to pull at the copper wire.

“Wait,” Kelset said.

Vasker got to his feet. The leg that had failed him straightened. Blood ran from the cuff and fell onto the grate.

“It’s time.”

“For what?”

He caught Ivradan by the collar and dragged him toward the middle channel.

The scholar fell hard on one knee. Kelset seized Vasker’s arm. Lerris grabbed the back of Ivradan’s coat and pulled. For an instant all four were joined above the grate, the lamp on the floor throwing their shadows across the ceiling.

Vasker let go of the scholar with one hand and struck Kelset in the face. Kelset went down. The worker reached for a long iron hook lying beside the channel.

Lerris had his knife out before he remembered drawing it.

“Leave him.”

Vasker looked at the blade. His face showed no anger. He raised the hook, bringing its curved point toward Ivradan’s neck.

Lerris stabbed his forearm.

The knife went in less deeply than he had expected. Vasker cried out and dropped the hook. Lerris pulled Ivradan back. The scholar’s collar tore. They fell together against the wall.

Kelset had risen. He caught Vasker round the waist and drove him away from the channel. They struck the opposite wall, rolled, and came apart. Vasker clutched his bleeding arm. He looked from it to Lerris.

“Why did you do that?”

Lerris could not answer quickly enough.

Kelset caught his sleeve.

“Move.”

They took their lamps and the plan. Ivradan snatched up the crank with both hands. Vasker remained beside the wall as they backed toward the arch. He was making a low hurt sound through his teeth.

At the arch, Kelset stopped. He took the remaining clean cloth from his bag and threw it within the man’s reach.

“Press it there. Vasker. Hear me. Press it hard.”

The man looked at the cloth. He reached for it with his uninjured hand.

Kelset waited until he had put it against the wound. Then he followed the others through.

Lerris wiped the knife on his own coat. The blade was small enough that he had always thought of it as a tool. He could not get the blood out of the place where the metal met the handle.

Chapter 31 — Across the Water

They stopped where the passage bent. Ivradan put down the crank and touched his throat. There were red marks above the torn collar, four on one side and a broad patch on the other. When he tried to speak, his voice caught.

Kelset gave him the flask. Very little water moved inside it.

“Small mouthful.”

Ivradan drank. He held the water in his mouth before swallowing, then handed the flask back. Lerris reached for it without thinking. Kelset closed the stopper.

“Later.”

He had blood beneath his nose. He wiped it with the back of his hand and looked down at the smear.

“Bastard.”

Lerris did not know whether he meant Vasker. He stood against the wall, afraid that if he sat he would not willingly get up again.

“How long has he been missing?” he asked.

“Closure was six days ago. He wasn’t on my gang. I thought they’d sent him higher.”

“Wouldn’t someone have checked?”

Kelset looked at him. Lerris wished he had not asked it that way.

“He had people,” Kelset said. “I don’t know what they were told.”

Ivradan coughed. He tried his voice again, quietly, testing a word whose meaning Lerris did not catch. Kelset waited until he had finished.

“We can leave directions. When we get above.”

Lerris nodded. He had not asked whether they should go back, though the question had been growing in him ever since Vasker took the cloth. He was grateful to Kelset for answering it and resented him a little for hearing it.

They continued. At intervals the stonework changed, clean squared blocks giving way to surfaces that looked rubbed rather than cut. Ivradan held his lamp close to one. Beneath a skin of moisture lay a shallow groove, wide enough for the edge of a finger. It ran from floor to ceiling without a join.

He did not touch it.

The scrape they had heard at the sluice resumed, somewhere beyond the wall. It came three times, paused, and began again. Lerris concentrated on Kelset’s boots. They made ordinary noises. He could follow those.

The passage opened beside a basin.

Water filled the chamber ahead, black beneath a low ceiling. A narrow ledge followed the near wall to a control wheel, then vanished under the surface. Across the basin another ledge ran higher, behind a rail. There was light there.

Lerris caught Kelset’s arm before he could call out.

The worker turned on him.

“I know.”

They stood at the entrance and watched. The light moved. A figure came to the rail, carrying a lamp, then crouched and disappeared from view.

Lerris could hear speech. It was too faint to distinguish. He wanted to hear Mira so badly that he distrusted the first syllables before they reached him.

Kelset picked up a loose stone and struck the wall with it. Three blows. He waited, struck twice more.

Across the basin the lamp rose.

“Kelset?”

Dorven’s voice. Lerris gripped his own wrist.

Kelset called their names and asked who was with him. Dorven answered: Mira, Rennik. The captain came to the rail. She did not ask for a signal. She asked what they could see.

“A basin,” Kelset called. “Low wheel on our side. Steps drowned beyond it.”

“There’s a stair from our end. Water’s over its foot. Can you see the center post?”

They moved their lamp. A stone post stood above the surface toward the middle, with a narrow iron guide fixed to its side. Kelset described it.

“Yes,” she said. “Don’t turn anything.”

He gave a short laugh without humour.

“We weren’t going to.”

Lerris called to her. His voice sounded thin over the water.

“I pulled the release. The indicator hadn’t changed.”

She was silent long enough for him to hear water touching the wall below his feet.

“Why?”

“I thought you were at the opening. Giving the signal.”

“I was at the far door. Rennik heard the water before we saw it.”

He shut his eyes.

“Is he hurt?”

“He was already hurt.”

“Worse?”

“No. We got him onto the high approach.”

Dorven moved beside her. Between their bodies Lerris could see the end of the ladder. Rennik raised one hand, then lowered it.

Mira leaned against the rail.

“Can you get round?”

Kelset examined the ledge ahead. It narrowed beneath a projection of stone. Beyond the wheel there might have been footholds, but they were under water. The dark surface gave no indication of depth.

“Not carrying him. Not safely on our own.”

“Then we stay here. Rennik wants the wheel described.”

They did it slowly. Kelset took the nearer lamp and inspected the spindle, the guide, the slots in the wall. Ivradan read the marks beside them. Lerris relayed each answer, listening while Dorven carried it to Rennik and returned with the next question.

Twice they had to repeat a description. Once Ivradan corrected a word after Lerris had already called it across, and they waited for the correction to make its way back. He imagined standing beside Rennik, both of them looking at the same part, knowing without this miserable sequence of guesses what the other meant.

Then Rennik asked a question that made Kelset look again.

“Does the spindle enter the wall, or a plate in front of it?”

Kelset scraped away a little deposit with his knife.

“A plate. Four bolts, one socket empty.”

Rennik wanted the lower edge examined. Kelset crouched, shifted the lamp, and drew back.

“Crack. Under the plate.”

The answer crossed the basin. Dorven bent over the ladder for a long time.

When he returned, he asked them to bring the workshop drawing to the edge and hold it up. It was too small to read across the water. Ivradan enlarged it in chalk on the wall, copying the curved chamber and upright marks while Lerris held the light.

“That,” Rennik called suddenly. “The lower cut. Show that again.”

His voice carried surprisingly well. Ivradan retraced the line.

There was a long pause.

“The seat’s moving,” Rennik said.

Lerris looked at the plate. He could see no movement.

Mira asked the question for him. Rennik answered from where he lay, taking breath between phrases.

“The controls are fixed to the facing. The old bearing’s behind it. If the seat shifts, the linkage binds. That’s why it wouldn’t return.”

“Can we drain this?”

“Not from that wheel. It might open. It might take the plate off.”

“What works below it?”

Rennik asked to see the drawing again. Ivradan added the channel and the line of upright marks. The engineer watched from across the water, his head supported by Dorven’s arm.

“Lower chamber,” he said. “The first setting. We need to get to that.”

Mira did not answer at once. She looked along her own ledge, then toward the passage behind the three men.

“Can we wait for the upper party?” Lerris asked.

“They’d have to come through where we came,” she said. “Or find another way. I don’t know how long either takes.”

A faint crack sounded beneath Kelset’s feet.

He stepped back from the wheel. A flake of stone fell from below the plate and vanished into the water. The three of them across the basin heard it too; Dorven’s lamp swung toward the sound.

Mira looked down at Rennik. They spoke too softly for Lerris to hear. When she returned to the rail, she told them to leave the wheel alone and find the downward passage marked on the workshop drawing. They were to report what they could see from its entrance before proceeding.

“And Lerris.”

He looked up.

“If you see me beside you, tell the others before you do anything.”

“I will.”

She stayed at the rail while Kelset searched the wall. Lerris kept his lamp where she could see it. Across the basin Dorven adjusted Rennik’s coat, tucking its edge around the splinted foot with a care that made Lerris look down at the water until he could speak again.

Part III — The Exposed Face

Chapter 32 — A Line Through the Wall

The downward passage began behind a screen of stone ribs. Kelset found it by putting his hand where the drawing showed an opening and feeling air between the ribs. One of them had broken near the floor. He could turn sideways and get through. Lerris could not.

“Bag first,” Kelset said.

Lerris took it off. Without it he could force one shoulder through, then his head, but the stone caught his coat at the ribs. Kelset reached back and freed the cloth. For a moment Lerris stood unable to move either way, breathing against cold stone while someone else’s hands searched for the thing holding him.

Then he was through.

Ivradan followed more easily. Beyond the screen, steps descended round the outside of the basin. A shallow channel ran beneath their right hands, cut into the wall and covered with loose slabs. Kelset lifted one. An iron rod lay inside, joined to another by a collar. When he touched it with the end of his knife, it trembled.

He left it alone.

They went no farther than the first bend. There was dry floor below and air moving up, but their lamps would not reach the next landing. Ivradan crouched to compare the drawing. The curve of the stair seemed right.

Lerris returned to the basin with the report. Mira was still by the rail. She had taken off her helmet. Without it he could see how tightly she had pulled back her hair, a band of exposed skin showing above each ear.

He described the gap. There would be no carrying Rennik through it.

“We don’t need him down there,” she said. “We need you back.”

Rennik asked about the rod. Lerris fetched Kelset, who came out through the ribs and called across. The engineer listened, then wanted to know whether the joints were moving together or against one another.

“I didn’t try them.”

“Good. Don’t.”

They established where the rod went as far as they could: upwards toward the low wheel, down along the stair. It might let them release the water from a different point once the bearing seat was supported. Rennik could not tell them where that point would be.

Dorven had been examining his side of the basin. He called that there was a narrow opening below the rail, perhaps an old speaking channel. Mira knelt beside it. On their side Kelset searched the wall and found a matching hole just beyond the ribs.

He spoke into it.

Dorven answered from across the water, faintly but distinctly. Kelset drew away.

“I don’t want to use that.”

“We can hear you without it,” Mira said.

“Below, we won’t.”

Lerris looked at the little opening. They had come down to investigate a voice from a disconnected pipe. He wanted a reason to reject this one that did not depend on how frightened it made him.

“Use it for descriptions,” he said. “Nothing moved because a voice says to move it.”

Rennik raised himself on one elbow. Dorven helped him before he could lose his position.

“Could you feed a line through?”

The question took them a moment. Then Kelset knelt at the hole again, testing its course with a length of wire. It curved downward, clear for as far as he could reach. Dorven examined his own opening. His side was higher.

They had the light cord used to lower a lamp, a few shorter lengths in the tool roll, and the wire from Kelset’s bag. Dorven tied a small weight to his cord and let it fall into the channel. Kelset listened to it travel. When it stopped short, Dorven drew it back a little and let it go again.

On the third attempt Kelset caught it with the wire.

He grinned before he could stop himself. Across the basin Dorven gave a whoop, then looked embarrassed by it.

“Don’t pull it out,” he called. “It’s all we’ve got.”

They joined the available lengths. The cord was too light to hold a person, but it would carry a folded scrap tied to it. More could be fed into the channel as they descended, provided the lower inspection covers gave access.

“No guessing what a tug means,” Mira said. “Describe what you find. No one turns anything they haven’t examined themselves.”

“And if it sticks?” Kelset asked.

“We stop expecting it to work.”

They sent a test. Dorven’s scrap came through with the knot caught at one corner. It bore a rough drawing of the far ledge, Rennik’s ladder, and a dark mark where water reached the stair. Underneath he had written, Tell me whether I still have the cart on this plan.

Kelset wrote beneath it, You never draw anything to scale.

He sent it back. A moment later Dorven’s laugh crossed the basin.

Lerris looked at the two men. He could not recall the last time he had felt so excluded from something he wanted to keep hearing.

Mira had Dorven add the message cord to the other things he was tending. She would remain with Rennik and watch the water. Kelset was to leave room in his descriptions for uncertainty; Ivradan was to keep the copied marks separate from his translations. Lerris would carry the light and stay close enough to see what they were doing.

She asked whether any of them needed to sit before they went.

Ivradan said yes.

They sat on the ledge for a little while, their knees nearly touching. The scholar coughed into his sleeve. Kelset unwrapped his thumb and examined it, then turned the bloodied part of the cloth inward. Lerris wanted water. He did not ask.

Across the basin Mira sat beside Rennik. Dorven kept hold of the cord.

Chapter 33 — The Rooms Below

The stair followed the basin wall for two turns. At each turn Kelset opened an inspection cover and checked the rod. The speaking channel ran beside it, branching once into a dark hole where he could see neither floor nor end. They drew the cord past the branch with a hooked wire, then continued feeding it down.

Lerris carried one lamp. Ivradan had the other. They tried to keep both flames below their faces, where they could see the steps as well as one another.

The murmuring began before the second landing.

It sounded like a roomful of people attempting not to disturb a sick person. A low voice, a scrape of furniture, someone’s brief attempt at a laugh. Lerris thought of the rooms outside Markus’s, where people used to wait before asking whether he could receive them. He stopped so abruptly that Ivradan touched his shoulder.

“What?”

“Talking.”

The scholar listened.

“Yes.”

“Do you know the voices?”

“No.”

Kelset had stopped by the next cover. He looked back at them, said he could hear water, and waited until they came on.

At the bottom of the stair stood a pair of open doors higher than any they had passed. Their lower panels were hidden beneath pale deposits that had hardened in flowing shapes. Above them Lerris could see carved figures carrying something between them, their hands worn smooth by more than weather.

He found himself wondering whether anyone had come down here simply to look before the water works had taken over the route. A child brought to see the old rooms. Someone bored by a guide. A young couple finding an excuse to fall behind.

Then the light reached the floor beyond.

There was water everywhere except along a narrow walk beside the wall. Columns rose from it in two rows. Between them lay fallen beams, a stone vessel split into three pieces, and a long object like a table turned on its side. Much of the ceiling was too high for their lamps. Here and there a pale surface caught the light and seemed to hang unsupported.

Ivradan breathed out.

“Don’t go ahead,” Kelset said.

“I wasn’t.”

He had already taken a step.

They moved along the walk. The iron rod emerged from its covered channel and entered a later structure built against the older wall: squared blocks, a broad iron plate, a wheel with most of its rim missing. Beside it was a stone frame containing three uprights. Two stood straight. The third leaned toward the water, leaving a dark gap at its foot.

The workshop drawing showed that gap as a fitted joint.

Kelset set down his bag. He did not touch the mechanism. Ivradan held the board beside the frame, comparing one with the other, and began to whisper the old words cut above it.

Lerris watched the water. A light moved under the surface, too deep to be their reflection. It passed beneath the fallen table, paused, then returned.

He stepped back.

The light vanished.

“What did you see?” Kelset asked.

He told him. Kelset looked into the water himself, then moved the lamp farther from its edge.

“Keep watching us.”

Ivradan was tracing the inscription in the air, keeping his finger clear of the stone. He recognized enough to find the start of a sentence and not enough to finish it. There was a prohibition, a reference to an open face or exposed surface, and something about the setting of a load.

“Which load?” Kelset asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Then write what you do know.”

They sent up a description of the frame, the leaning upright, the gap, and the later plate. Lerris drew a section of the old room on a separate scrap. He made the water too shallow at first, scraped out the line, and wrote that they had not measured it.

While they waited for an answer, Kelset tested the walkway with the end of the crank. The stones held near the wall. At the outer edge one shifted. He put a loose piece of rubble on it so that they would not step there without thinking.

Ivradan had found a name on the lintel.

“Valdris.”

Kelset looked up.

“The king?”

“A king. The form says so.”

“Did he build it?”

“It says he renewed it.”

“Did he?”

Ivradan gave him a tired, irritated look. Then he looked back at the water.

“Someone wanted it said.”

The cord moved inside the channel. Kelset drew out the reply and held it where all three could read.

Rennik wanted measurements. Distance from the lower wheel to the frame. Width of the gap. Whether the iron plate bore against the leaning stone or the surrounding masonry. Underneath, in a different hand, Mira had written that the water had risen another finger at the far stair.

They worked carefully. Kelset used the cord from his pocket for lengths and marked them on a scrap of wood with his knife. Ivradan kept the written descriptions. Lerris knelt to put the lamp where Kelset could examine the foot of the plate.

Something touched his ankle.

He jerked backwards, knocking his shoulder into the frame. His heel had slipped off the edge into the water. There was nothing there now, only a wet line across the leather.

Kelset steadied the lamp before it could fall.

“Out of the way a moment.”

“I can hold it.”

“I want you to stand behind me.”

Lerris obeyed. His shoulder hurt where it had struck the stone. Beneath his wet boot, something seemed to press upward from inside the sole.

He took the boot off. There was nothing in it. He put his hand inside, checked the lining, and put it on again while the others pretended to be interested only in the measurements.

The murmuring stopped.

He knew, with a certainty so complete that it frightened him, that Markus had entered the room.

He kept his eyes on Kelset’s bent back.

Chapter 34 — The Unfinished Sentence

Markus said nothing.

Lerris had been preparing for a voice ever since the false signal at the door. He had imagined commands, pleas, a familiar anger applied to some new purpose. Instead there was the small movement of a chair behind him and the sense of his brother settling to work.

He looked over his shoulder.

A chair stood on the dry walk near the doorway. Markus sat in it with a book across his knees. He wore the old coat he used in the garden. One sleeve had been turned back farther than the other.

Lerris said, “I can see my brother.”

Kelset straightened. Ivradan turned toward the door.

“Where?”

He pointed.

The scholar looked, then came to stand beside him.

“I see a fallen post. Nothing sitting on it.”

Markus raised his head. He seemed tired, but not ill. He had the expression Lerris knew from interrupted reading: patient at first, already retaining a finger beneath the line he meant to return to.

Lerris pressed his thumb into the sore place on his palm.

“He’s in his garden coat.”

“Keep telling us,” Ivradan said.

“I don’t want to.”

The scholar did not insist. He stayed beside him until the next message arrived.

Rennik’s instructions were precise about the parts he understood. The iron plate was a later brace, carrying some of the load into the outer wall. The leaning upright had to be returned to its seat before the lower wheel could safely turn. Even a small change might release the bound linkage above. They were to look for sockets beneath the plate where a temporary prop could bear.

After this came a question: Are the cut lines continuous across the joint?

Ivradan crouched. There were grooves on the upright, continuing onto the base. At the tilted joint they missed each other by the width of a small nail.

He wrote that down.

Kelset found the prop sockets. One was empty. In the other lay the rusted remains of a short iron strut. He measured both, looked at the crank they had brought, and shook his head.

“Too long. Wrong ends.”

“The tools in the workshop?” Lerris asked.

“We can’t keep going back.”

He sounded ashamed of the irritation. Lerris was glad of it. The ordinary annoyance held his attention more firmly than Ivradan’s quiet kindness.

They searched along the wall without leaving sight of the frame. There were iron pieces beneath the deposits, most useless, one long bar that crumbled when Kelset lifted it. In a recessed rack they found two square-ended props and a wedge, all black with grease rather than red with rust.

Kelset laid them on the paving and examined the ends. Ivradan translated the marks beside the rack. They referred to the seat, though he could not say whether the equipment belonged to its original making or a later repair.

Kelset sent the dimensions up to Rennik. Then he sat down.

His hands were trembling again.

Lerris sat beside him, leaving the lamp between them. Behind his shoulder he could hear the turn of a page. He tried to breathe at the same pace as Kelset and found the other man was trying to slow his breathing too.

“Tell me about the wedding,” he said.

“Whose?”

“Rennik’s sister.”

“I don’t know anything about it.”

“You were joking.”

“He’s been complaining for a month. I listen to half.”

“Does she know?”

“That he complains? She gives him reasons.”

Kelset wiped his mouth. After a while he said the man she was marrying had once repaired the wrong roof. Two houses, same upper passage, wrong door. Rennik had never let him forget it.

“Was it a bad roof?” Lerris asked.

“Oh, it needed doing. People were delighted until he asked to be paid.”

Lerris laughed. He could not help it. The laugh hurt his throat and then became easier. Kelset’s mouth softened, and for a moment they sat together imagining the repairer at the wrong door.

Behind them Markus closed the book.

Lerris’s laughter stopped.

You needn’t choose every word first.

He had not heard the words. He had understood them, exactly as one understands the point of a sentence before its speaker has finished. He knew which conversations his brother meant: a dozen in which he had stood outside a door trying to decide how much hope sounded like a demand.

He put both hands flat on the paving.

“It’s offering something,” he said.

Kelset turned toward him.

“What?”

“I don’t have to explain. He’ll know what I mean.”

Ivradan had left the inscription and was watching him.

“Who will?”

“My brother.”

“Is that what it’s said?”

“It hasn’t said anything.”

The cord moved. Kelset rose quickly and went to fetch the message. Ivradan stayed.

Lerris could feel Markus waiting. There would be no more false starts, no failed jokes that left them both ashamed, no need for Alayse to find an errand that would let one of them leave the room. He could offer a thought entire. Markus could answer without the exhausting passage through his mouth.

Lerris wanted it with a tenderness that made him feel sick.

Ivradan knelt and took the lamp.

“You can hold this paper for me.”

“I can hold the lamp.”

“I need the paper higher.”

He put the copied inscription into Lerris’s hands. Lerris raised it. The scholar began checking the marks against the stone, asking him to turn the page when the light caught its damp surface.

They worked through a damaged line, then another. Lerris could not read them, but he could find the shapes Ivradan described. One stroke, a hook, a pair of small cuts beneath a long one.

At last the scholar stopped.

“What?” Lerris asked.

“I’ve been using the wrong subject.”

He took the paper and crossed out a word. The sentence concerned the person at the exposed face of the setting. That person was to remain while the seat was brought true. The instruction after it was damaged. Ivradan could read the beginning of a prohibition and the sign for departure, but not the words joining them.

“Mustn’t leave?” Kelset asked. He had come back with Rennik’s reply.

“Perhaps. Or mustn’t be prevented from leaving. I can’t read what’s gone.”

He sounded angry at the stone.

Rennik had approved the props for a cautious trial. They were to set them without driving the wedge, describe their seating, and wait for his answer. Mira had added a line beneath: No one held in place. Come back if the work requires it.

Lerris read that twice.

When he looked toward the door, the chair was empty. The book lay open on it, a finger’s breadth from sliding off.

Chapter 35 — The Trial Setting

The first prop seated cleanly. Kelset fitted it against the plate while Ivradan held the light close enough to show the edges of the socket. He packed the second with a thin iron shim from the rack, then took it out when Rennik’s reply came down forbidding loose packing against the damaged masonry.

He did not argue with the paper. He turned the prop, examined its other end, and found the shoulder he had mistaken for a broken corner.

That end fitted.

They sent up the correction. Lerris took the scrap to the open channel himself, tied it on, and watched it drawn into the dark. He did not leave until the cord had stopped moving.

The water beneath the walk had become very still. Something pale lay just below its surface. As he watched, it took on the shape of a hand resting palm upward.

He looked at the wall and returned to the others.

Kelset had placed the wedge on the paving. He would need to drive it between the prop heads, lifting the plate a little and allowing the upright to move back. Rennik had asked him to mark its starting position. He scratched a line with his knife and showed it to Ivradan.

“No farther than this width until we look again.”

He held up the blunt end of the file.

The answer came from above: the same small movement, then a pause while Dorven watched the upper linkage and Mira watched the water. If anything shifted suddenly, they were to stop. No attempt to catch or hold moving stone.

Lerris stood beside the frame with his lamp.

At the first blow, the murmur returned.

Kelset struck again. The wedge moved a little. Ivradan bent to inspect the scratched line.

A pressure crossed Lerris’s face. It was like putting his head into deep water, though his skin remained dry. His ears hurt. He stepped back.

The plate rang beneath the props. Kelset dropped the hammer and caught Ivradan’s sleeve, pulling him clear.

For a moment all three stood motionless. Then the pressure eased. A thin thread of dust fell from the joint.

“Stop,” Lerris said, too late.

Kelset looked at the abandoned hammer.

“We have.”

They waited. Nothing else moved.

Lerris described the pressure. Ivradan had felt a heat in his injured jaw. Kelset had felt nothing until the metal rang. The wedge had traveled less than half the distance they had agreed.

When Lerris moved nearer to lift the lamp, the pressure returned, lighter this time. He stopped. It remained against his ears.

Kelset watched him.

“Come back.”

He did. The sensation loosened.

They sent the whole account up, including the places where Lerris had stood. While they waited, Ivradan returned to the inscription. He compared the position of the exposed face with the shape drawn in the workshop. Then he walked to the place Lerris had left, keeping well clear of the stone.

He stood there for a few breaths. His face tightened.

“Jaw?” Lerris asked.

The scholar nodded and came away.

“There is something holding it apart,” he said.

“Water pressure?”

“I don’t think so.”

Kelset sat on his heels beside the tools. He looked older in the low light, his bruised face slack with exhaustion.

“Can either of you do anything about it?”

Ivradan did not answer immediately. He rubbed his jaw, then took out the workshop board. On it the artisan had drawn one mark slightly apart from the others. The scholar had treated it as damage to the copying surface. Now he traced it with his finger.

“A place to stand,” he said.

“There’s plenty of those.”

“Before the open face. While the others move the setting.”

Kelset looked toward the narrow space beside the frame. The water came almost level with its outer edge.

“That’s a rotten place to stand.”

“Yes.”

The reply arrived from above. Rennik had felt movement through the higher control but not enough to free it. Dorven had seen the indicator shiver. Mira said the water had stopped rising for the moment. They were not to try again until the crew below described how they meant to stop the plate ringing.

Lerris took the paper. He looked at the empty chair near the door. It was a fallen post again, partly covered in pale deposit.

“I can try the place,” he said.

“No hammer,” Kelset answered at once.

“I mean just stand there.”

They sent that proposal up too. The answer was to limit the attempt to a spoken count and stop at once if he failed to answer. Mira would hold them to it. Ivradan was to stay within reach without blocking his way out.

Lerris put down the lamp.

His hands wanted something to do. He wiped them on his coat, then found the dried blood at the hem and stopped.

Ivradan stood beside the narrow space. He asked whether Lerris wanted him to count in the university speech or the language they had been using below.

“The one we’ve been using.”

He stepped forward.

The water disappeared.

He stood on a dry floor beneath a high ceiling. The columns were whole. Light came from shallow bowls along the walls. People moved between them, carrying tools and pieces of cloth. A man knelt beside the frame with a wedge in his hand, his wrist bent awkwardly beneath the weight of the tool.

“One,” Ivradan said.

The kneeling man looked up. He was Kelset and someone Lerris had never seen, both at once. Behind him another man held a lamp in a hand that had been crushed years before it was lost.

“Two.”

Lerris could feel how the upright ought to fit. The grooves longed to join. He could put them together if everyone would be quiet for a little while.

“Three. Tell me what you see.”

He tried to answer. The knowledge was too large for the words he had. He could have given it to Ivradan entire if the scholar would only step closer.

“Lerris.”

A hand touched his sleeve.

He stepped back, stumbling into Ivradan, who held him without turning him toward the water.

The bowls were gone. The lamps stood on the paving. Kelset was kneeling beside the hammer, his hands clear of it. Nothing had moved.

Lerris drew breath.

“Three,” he said.

“Five,” Ivradan answered. “You stopped answering at three.”

Kelset stood up.

“We’re sending that first.”

He took the paper before Lerris could ask for another attempt.

Chapter 36 — While They Worked

Mira’s answer took longer than the others.

Kelset waited beside the open cover, holding the cord loosely. Ivradan checked the props without touching them. Lerris sat with his back to the wall. The intact room had gone, but he could still remember where a doorway ought to be beneath the broken stone opposite. He wanted to show it to Ivradan. He was afraid the scholar would be interested.

The cord moved.

Kelset drew the paper out and read it aloud. Mira wanted to know whether they could withdraw now. Rennik thought the slight change had eased the load but would not hold. Water had begun rising again at the upper stair. They could wait where they were, return to the lift, or attempt the setting with the limited movement already agreed. He could recommend the last only if the people below could stop and come away.

At the bottom Mira had written, You decide there. Do not tell me afterward you thought I ordered it.

Kelset passed the paper to Ivradan.

“I can move the wedge,” he said. “And leave it.”

The scholar looked at Lerris.

“I came back,” Lerris said.

“You stopped answering.”

“I came back when you touched me.”

Ivradan rubbed the bridge of his nose. He did not look reassured.

“We can put a strap on your arm.”

“No.”

The refusal came too quickly. Lerris saw them both react and forced himself to explain. He would have to step back across broken paving. A strap pulled suddenly could drag him off the edge. They looked at the space and conceded this, though Kelset asked him whether the prospect of being pulled had been what frightened him.

“Yes,” Lerris said.

They said nothing further about it. Ivradan would keep his hand on Lerris’s upper arm. They rehearsed the step back, slowly, with the lamp moved out of their way. Kelset set the hammer where he could take it without crossing behind them. Then they sent the plan up.

The response contained Rennik’s last instructions for the wedge and Mira’s agreement to watch the water. Dorven would pull the message line continuously if they had to leave the high ledge. That would mean only that contact was ending; none of them was to remain below waiting for a more encouraging answer.

Lerris read it, handed it back, and stood.

“Don’t let me ask for more time.”

Ivradan looked at him.

“I mean it.”

“I heard you.”

He put his hand on Lerris’s arm.

The first step brought the pressure. The second brought the room.

This time Lerris kept his eyes on the water. It remained for several breaths, black and faintly trembling. Then light opened beneath it. He saw a depth filled with slow movement, something passing behind something larger, no edge by which he could begin to measure either.

He understood how to put the upright back.

He understood how to lift the gate without touching its chains, how to draw the water through the stone, how to stand beside Markus and give him the whole of what had happened at the keep. None of these things required the clumsy interval of speech. They were all parts of one movement he could make if Ivradan would stop holding him.

“One,” the scholar said.

“Here.”

A hammer struck. The pressure changed, concentrating at Lerris’s forehead. He tried to keep his mouth open so that he could answer.

“Two.”

“Here.”

Markus stood on the other side of the water. He had no book. His hands were empty. He seemed to have been waiting a long time, but without resentment. Lerris felt the shame of having expected resentment and wanted to explain that too.

The hammer struck again.

Kelset swore. Lerris heard the sound from very far away. A small part of him wanted to turn and ask whether the wedge had moved. The rest knew that asking was unnecessary. The upright could be set without them. It could be held forever, if he would remain.

“Three.”

He answered, but not with a word.

Ivradan’s hand tightened.

Markus raised his own hand, palm outward. Lerris saw the old nick across a knuckle. A true detail, the kind he had used to mistake for proof. He hated that he could think this while wanting to go to him.

“Lerris. Back.”

The sound broke through. He tried to lift his foot. It seemed to belong to the floor.

Another blow fell, then a ringing unlike the first. Kelset shouted that it was down.

Lerris could make it better. The fit was wrong by less than the width of a hair. He could feel that error as a tooth feels a seed lodged beside it. One more moment, and they would not have to return with tools and wounded men. No one would have to decide which door to seal above the people left below.

“Back.”

Ivradan pulled at his arm.

Lerris turned toward the pain. He bent his knee, though it felt like bending it the wrong way. The room lost its light. Markus remained a moment longer than everything else.

Then Lerris stepped backwards into the scholar’s chest.

They nearly fell. Ivradan held him, braced against the wall, and Kelset caught the lamp before Lerris’s heel struck it.

The water returned with a sound like someone taking breath after being held under.

Lerris doubled over. Nothing came from his stomach. His mouth filled with saliva, and he spat onto the paving while Ivradan kept hold of his coat.

“The wedge?” he managed.

“Seated,” Kelset said.

“It’s not right.”

“It’s down.”

“It isn’t—”

Kelset knelt in front of him.

“Look at the stone. From here.”

The upright stood in its seat. The grooves joined, though one had lost a small chip at the edge. The plate no longer rang. A thin line of water ran from beneath it into the basin.

Lerris wanted to touch the damaged groove. He could almost feel how easy it would be to close.

Kelset picked up the hammer and put it in his bag.

“We said how far.”

The cord began to move.

Chapter 37 — The Open Stair

It brought a message.

For an instant none of them would touch it. Then Ivradan drew it out, unfolded it with shaking fingers, and held it beneath the lamp.

The upper linkage had moved. Rennik wanted the lower wheel turned no farther than the first stop, while Dorven watched the upper indicator. Mira had moved the ladder away from the rail. The water was falling at the foot of their stair, slowly.

Kelset read it himself. He looked at Lerris, then at the upright. He went to the broken wheel.

The rim was too damaged for both hands. He fitted the crank into the remaining spokes as a lever, protecting the contact with a folded scrap of leather. He did not put his weight on it at once. First he tried the motion, then waited, listening.

Something shifted in the wall. The iron rod turned a little in its housing.

Ivradan stood beside the setting. He watched the gap while Kelset worked. Lerris watched the water, careful not to look beneath its surface.

At the first stop, the wheel would move no farther without lifting a latch. Kelset left it there.

The sound from the channels grew louder. Water began to draw away from the edges of the fallen stone vessel. The pale shape beneath the surface vanished as the current moved.

They sent a report. Before an answer returned, there came a deep knock through the floor. Dust fell from the frame. Lerris backed toward the stair.

“That’s enough,” he said.

Kelset was already removing the crank.

Ivradan took one last look at the cut lines. He started to bend for a loose piece beside the frame, stopped himself, and picked up only his copied drawing.

They left the props and wedge. Kelset marked the position of the wheel with chalk and wrote beneath it that it had been moved only to the first stop. The words were cramped. Lerris could see his hand shaking as he wrote them.

The reply reached them at the lowest inspection cover on the way up. Mira said the stair was exposed. They were moving Rennik down to the near end of the basin. The three below were to return by the same route; no one was going back to the pressure doors.

Lerris laughed when Ivradan read that part.

He could not have said what was funny. The laugh broke into a cough. Kelset put a hand against his back and waited until he could breathe.

They climbed.

At the speaking-channel branch the line caught. Kelset drew it gently, then let it go. It caught again. He opened the cover, freed the knot from a chipped edge, and fed it through. His care with it made Lerris impatient. Mira knew they were coming; they could leave the line. He held his tongue while Kelset recovered the full length he could reach and coiled it around his hand.

At the rib-screen, Dorven was waiting on the other side.

He saw Kelset first. He made a noise Lerris had never heard from him, half a laugh and half an accusation, and reached through the ribs. Kelset gave him the bag and the recovered coil of line. Dorven slipped the coil over his wrist before helping him through. When he emerged, Dorven caught his coat with both hands and shook him once.

“Took you long enough.”

Kelset leaned against him. Neither said anything for several moments.

Ivradan passed out the lamps. Lerris squeezed through last, scraping the sore shoulder. The basin had dropped enough to expose the lower steps. Mira stood beside Rennik, who lay on the ladder with his head turned toward them.

The engineer raised two fingers.

“First stop?”

“First stop,” Kelset said.

“Left the latch?”

“Yes.”

Rennik closed his eyes.

Lerris went to him. He had thought he might apologize, but the words belonged to too many separate things. Instead he asked whether he wanted the lamp moved.

“Anywhere but my face.”

He moved it.

Mira took Lerris aside far enough that Rennik would not have to listen. She asked him how long he had remained at the setting. He did not know. Ivradan answered that he had stopped answering on the third count and stepped away after the order was repeated.

“You’ll write that,” she told Lerris.

“Yes.”

“And the door.”

“Yes.”

She looked at his face for a while. Then she laid a hand briefly on the back of his neck.

“Carry the bags. You aren’t steady enough for him.”

They had a way up on the far side: a service stair that joined the west relief gallery above the pressure-door approach. The lowest flight was wet but clear. Dorven had inspected it only to the first landing while Mira remained with Rennik. He had seen current works marks and the beginning of a bell cord.

Kelset wanted to go first and check the landing catches. Mira agreed. She and Dorven would carry Rennik; Ivradan would light their feet. Lerris took the bags, the copied plans, and the crank, which Kelset finally consented to leave with him.

Before they lifted, Rennik said he needed a moment.

They gave him one. He lay with his eyes closed, breathing slowly. His lips moved. Lerris could not hear what he said and did not bend closer.

Then he nodded.

They crossed the exposed steps. Water still ran over the lowest one. Lerris put his foot where Mira had put hers and kept the bags high. Behind them, from the room below, came another deep knock.

No one stopped to listen.

Chapter 38 — The Landing

The service stair was wider than the lift stair, built for carrying equipment between levels. Even so, the turns were hard. Mira and Dorven brought Rennik up head first, resting the ladder on each landing while Kelset checked the flight above. Ivradan held the lamp near their feet. Lerris came last, banging the crank against his thigh whenever he forgot to lift it clear.

They found the bell cord at the second turn.

Kelset pulled it once, listened, then pulled again in a pattern Dorven recognized. Somewhere above, faintly, a bell answered the movement. They waited. Nothing came back down the line.

“Keep going,” Mira said.

The cord ran through guides beside the stair. At the next landing they found it frayed almost through where a guide had broken. Kelset tied the weak place around an iron hook, making a shorter working length above it. Dorven held the lamp while he did it. Lerris stood below them with the bags and wished they would hurry.

Rennik began shivering. Mira tucked the coat more closely round him, but there was little dry cloth left. He tried to help and could not manage the fastening.

“Leave it,” she said. “I’ve got it.”

Above the landing stood a narrow door. Kelset opened it onto the west relief gallery.

There were lamps in the distance.

These moved together, one stopping while the other came on. A voice called a works challenge. Mira answered. The lights quickened.

Lerris leaned against the wall. His knees had become unreliable all at once. He could hear the approaching people arguing about where to set something down. The annoyance in their voices made his throat close.

A woman reached the doorway first, carrying a hooded lamp. She wore a rescue harness over her coat. Behind her came two men with a folded stretcher and a bag large enough to hold food.

Mira spoke quickly. Injured leg, exposure, possible influence below. Six of them. Lower controls moved, no guarantee of holding. The woman looked past her at Rennik and called back for the healer.

Kelset had gone ahead to move a loose section of rail out of the doorway. The broken length of wooden rail lay across the track of a sliding flood panel, preventing the panel from opening to its full width. He lifted one end, turned it, and dragged it clear so they could carry the ladder straight through.

Lerris saw him set it against the wall.

Then a sound came up the stair behind them, low and long. The floor lifted beneath Lerris’s feet. He struck the door frame with his shoulder and lost one of the bags.

The rescue woman caught Mira’s end of the ladder. Dorven pushed from below. Together they got Rennik through the doorway.

Kelset turned back.

The outer edge of the landing dropped.

There was no great breaking sound. A joint opened with a dry crack, and the stone tilted toward the well beneath the stair. Kelset’s foot slipped. He caught the remaining rail with both hands.

Lerris dropped everything and reached for him.

His fingers touched a sleeve. The cloth slid across his palm. Kelset tried to bring one knee up, could not find an edge, and looked at him with furious concentration.

“Hold still,” Lerris said.

He heard himself give it.

He dropped onto his stomach. Someone caught the back of his coat. His hand closed on Kelset’s wrist, wet with blood from the cut thumb. For a moment he had him.

The rail came away from the wall.

Kelset fell with it. Lerris’s arm was pulled straight; the wrist slid out of his grip. There was a brief metal crash below, then water.

Lerris tried to go farther over the edge. The person behind him dragged him back so hard that his chin struck the floor.

He shouted Kelset’s name.

Dorven came to the gap. He had the light cord in his hand, the coil Kelset had passed him at the rib-screen. He looked at it, looked down, and threw it aside.

“Rope,” he shouted. “A rope.”

The rescuers had one. A man clipped it to his harness while the woman found an anchor above the doorway. Mira held the lamp out over the broken landing. Lerris could see a black pool far below, the shattered rail floating at an angle against a wall, no face above the surface.

Dorven called again and again.

The rope went down. The rescuer lowered himself over the surviving edge, testing the wall with his boots. Halfway down he stopped. Water was forcing through a seam beneath the landing, carrying grit and white pieces of mortar. He called that the lower stone was moving.

Mira told him to come up.

Dorven turned on her.

“He’s there.”

“I know.”

“He could be holding something.”

“I know.”

The rescuer stayed a moment longer, sweeping his lamp across the pool. Then the water rose around the broken rail and spun it out of sight.

Mira pulled on the rope. The woman at the anchor hauled with her. The man came up quickly, one boot scraping for purchase on the wall.

Dorven tried to pass them. Mira caught him round the chest. He did not fight wildly. He pushed steadily against her arm, trying to get to the place where he could see down.

They let him look once more while the rescue woman held the back of his belt.

There was nothing to see but water.

The flood panel began to shudder in its track. Someone shouted from farther along the gallery. Mira brought Dorven through the doorway. He was still calling when they pulled the panel shut.

Lerris stood beside Rennik’s ladder. His right hand was empty. He kept it closed until Mira prised his fingers apart to look at the palm.

Part IV — The Account Returned

Chapter 39 — Above the Closed Panel

The healer arrived with two porters and a bag that smelled of spirits. She looked at Rennik first, asked how long ago the leg had been caught, and accepted nobody’s answer until they admitted they could not give her a reliable hour.

Mira described the injury, the splint, and the times they had moved him. The healer knelt beside the ladder. She spoke to Rennik while she loosened the dressing.

Lerris turned away. He had held a lamp for that leg before. He could not do it now.

A porter took the bags from where they had fallen inside the doorway. One had split at a seam. The copied plans were wet at the edges but still wrapped together. The man brought the crank too, holding it away from his body as though uncertain whether it belonged to the casualty.

Dorven took it from him.

“That’s ours.”

He stood with it until Mira persuaded him to let the porter carry it.

The rescue woman returned from the flood panel. Her name was Heska. Lerris heard someone call it and held onto the sound; he wanted to know the name of the person who had pulled him away from the edge. She had found another inspection opening farther along the gallery. It gave a view of the pool beneath the broken landing. There was no safe access. Two people would remain at the opening while the injured were taken up.

Dorven asked to stay.

Mira said he could remain with them until the next relief, provided he kept behind the rail and went when the watch changed. Heska looked at him, then nodded. He took a lamp from the supplies without answering.

Lerris wanted to stay as well. He could not find a reason that would not sound like asking to be seen staying. While he tried, the porters lifted Rennik onto their stretcher. The engineer made a hoarse sound and reached for Mira, who put her hand where he could take it.

They went up.

At the first rest place someone handed Lerris a cup of broth. He held it between both hands. It was too hot to drink. He kept raising it to his mouth anyway, wanting the steam against his face.

Ivradan sat beside him. A bruise had begun to darken around his throat. He watched the stretcher being carried through the next door, then put his cup down without drinking.

“We left that man.”

“Vasker.”

“Yes.”

Lerris told Heska about him when she came through with the next report. Name, chamber, cuff, injury. The hook he had raised. The cloth Kelset had thrown. She asked whether the way to him was passable. Lerris said he did not know now.

She wrote it down. Her pencil stopped at the description of the cuff.

“West closure,” she said.

“You knew he was missing?”

“Two people didn’t come back. We searched from above until the third check stopped responding. They’d been told to leave the lower tools.”

“Was he one?”

“Yes.”

Lerris looked at her paper. The lines were straight and the names legible. He envied the steadiness of her hand, then noticed that she had braced it against the wall.

She went back down. He drank the broth. The last mouthful had cooled enough to taste of salt and onions.

At the infirmary, they took his coat. He resisted when the attendant reached for the pocket containing his book, and she let him empty it himself. There was nothing in the pockets that had not become damp. He arranged the contents on the bed beside him, ashamed of their smallness: pencil, string, two coins, a handkerchief stiff with someone else’s blood.

The attendant examined his shoulder and washed his palms. The right wrist had begun to swell. She asked him to move each finger.

He could move them all.

He hated the answer.

When she had finished, she gave him a shirt that was too short in the sleeves. He put it on. Someone had mended it beneath the arm with cloth of a different color.

Across the room Ivradan had fallen asleep sitting up. Lerris tried to lie down. The instant his head touched the pillow he felt Kelset’s wrist sliding through his hand.

He sat again and kept his feet on the floor.

Chapter 40 — Her Questions

Dorven came up after the watch changed. His face had the blank, exhausted look of someone who had been answering the same question inside his head for hours. He went first to Rennik, then to the room where the others waited.

“They couldn’t get down.”

Mira asked whether the second inspection was still clear.

“For looking.”

He sat beside the door. Lerris offered him the cup he had been given, then realized it was empty.

Dorven looked at it.

“I’ll get you another,” Lerris said.

When he came back, Kelset’s wife was there.

Her name was Telve. Someone had said it at supper, and he had failed to remember it until he heard Mira speak it now. Her hair was pinned up as it had been at the oven court. She still wore her working apron. One corner had been folded into its belt, leaving a pale patch where the cloth beneath had stayed clean.

She asked which of them had seen him fall.

Lerris put down the cup.

“I did.”

“Were you close?”

“Yes.”

“How close?”

He told her. At the first mention of the wrist, Dorven bent forward with his elbows on his knees. Telve kept looking at Lerris.

“Did he hit the side?”

“I heard the rail strike. I couldn’t see.”

“Did he come up?”

“No.”

“Could you see all the water?”

“Not all of it.”

She asked where the other edges were, whether there were openings beneath the stair, how high the water had risen. Mira brought the rough plan Heska had made after the attempted descent. Telve sat to look at it.

Lerris stood beside her while Mira explained. He wanted to say they had tried. He heard how urgently he wanted that understood and kept silent.

Telve touched the place where the wall had moved.

“Can someone look from here?”

“Heska has people there,” Mira said.

“Not down the same hole. Around it.”

“We’re checking the older passages. Some are under water.”

“Then the ones that aren’t.”

Mira nodded.

For a while Telve looked at the plan. Then she asked what he had been doing when it happened.

“Clearing a rail,” Lerris said. “It was blocking the doorway.”

“Was he told to?”

“No.”

She pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead.

“He’d do that.”

Dorven asked where the child was. Telve said a neighbor had her. She had told her that there had been trouble at the works and she needed to fetch news.

“I haven’t told her anything else.”

Dorven nodded, then nodded again after the answer required it.

Telve looked at him.

“Will you come?”

He opened his mouth. Lerris thought he would refuse; his whole body had drawn back against the chair. Then he stood.

“Yes.”

They left together. At the door she turned to Mira and said she would return in the morning. She wanted someone to tell her where the search had reached, even if it had reached nowhere.

Mira said there would be someone.

Lerris remained standing after they had gone. On the table lay his empty cup beside the full one he had fetched for Dorven.

He carried both to the kitchen. The woman there took them from him and asked whether he wanted food. He said no, then asked for bread. She cut him a piece and put butter on it without asking how much.

He ate it standing beside her table while she washed the cups.

Chapter 41 — The Work That Could Wait

Rennik slept through most of the next day. The healer had cleaned the wound and set the leg again after the swelling allowed her to judge it. A working had checked the worsening damage around the torn flesh; the broken bone still needed rest. She would make no promise about the ankle.

When Lerris visited, Rennik was awake and angry about a cushion.

“It’s always under the wrong bit.”

Lerris moved it. Rennik told him he had made it worse. The attendant came over, moved it almost to where it had been before, and received a grudging sound of satisfaction.

“Leave the clever work to me,” she told Lerris.

He sat.

For some time they talked about nothing below. Rennik wanted to know whether the roof-repairer had come. He had, bringing a basket from Rennik’s sister and asking whether the wedding should be postponed.

“What did you say?”

“Told him I’d endured enough.”

Lerris smiled. Rennik closed his eyes, then opened them again.

“The controls?”

“Mira’s meeting the works people this afternoon.”

“Bring the drawings.”

“They said you should rest.”

“They can say it after I look.”

Lerris fetched the copied papers. He laid them where Rennik could see without lifting his head. The engineer studied the measurements, asked who had written one number, and had Lerris correct it from Kelset’s marked scrap of wood.

At the description of the upright, he stopped.

“Did it fit?”

“It sat down. The lines joined.”

“But did it fit?”

Lerris thought of the small broken edge, the certainty that he could have closed it. He described what they had actually seen.

Rennik listened.

“I still know how to do the other thing.”

Lerris kept his hand on the paper.

“What other thing?”

“Pull through stone. I wake up knowing. Then I try to draw it and there’s nothing where the force comes from.”

He looked toward the door. The attendant was speaking to someone in the next room.

“If I tell them, they’ll take me off everything.”

“They need to know.”

“I know what they need.”

Lerris did not answer. After a while Rennik turned the drawing face down.

“Stay when I tell her.”

“I will.”

The healer did not take the papers away. She asked when the knowledge came, whether it commanded him to act, whether he could sleep when it would not stop. She asked Lerris to describe what had happened at the setting. Then she spoke to Rennik about having someone with him when he studied the lower works and keeping the diagrams out of reach at night until they knew more.

Rennik objected to the last condition. She let him finish.

“You asked me how to get some sleep.”

He looked at the papers.

“All right.”

That afternoon Mira met the wardens and the works officers. Ivradan attended with his bruised throat wrapped in a scarf. Lerris sat beside him with the damaged book and the clean copy he had begun.

The upper route would remain shut. The interruption affected carts, water levels, and two workshops whose draft depended on the closed passage. A man wanted to know when the lower setting could be treated as stable. Mira asked which report had said it was stable.

Nobody had.

There would be more inspection from the dry routes, supports brought from above, and a search continued from any opening that could be reached without sending a person beneath the shifting landing. Telve had already been told where that left them. The officer responsible for the west closure said Vasker’s family had been contacted too.

Ivradan asked to accompany the first team that reached the workshop again.

Mira looked at him across the table.

“Can that wait?”

He began to explain the condition of the inscriptions. Then his hand went to his throat.

“Yes,” he said.

The meeting went on. Lerris wrote down which people had agreed to which work. When someone asked how they knew the Drake was contained, he put his pencil down.

“We don’t know how much of it we held.”

He could hear his own sentence inviting a more impressive one. He described the upright, the props, the first stop, and the water falling. He described the landing failing afterward.

The officer made him repeat the position of the props. That was the part he needed.

Chapter 42 — The Account Returned

Lerris stayed long enough to see the first inspection abandoned and the second reach a chamber from which the pool could be watched. Neither found Kelset. One recovered part of the rail. Telve asked to see it, then asked why anyone thought showing it to her would help.

Mira apologized. The rail went back to the works store, tagged with the place it had been found.

Vasker was not reached. The service bypass had taken water after the lower wheel moved. Heska’s team found the entrance obstructed from the far side and withdrew when the air failed its measures. Lerris put the location of the chamber on every usable plan. He sent a separate account of the wound he had given Vasker, including where and how deeply he thought the knife had gone.

He could not make that estimate useful enough to satisfy himself.

The first fair copy took four days. He worked in his lodging, at the table beside the vent shutter. Once he fell asleep with his face against the page and woke to a smear of ink on his cheek. The lodging keeper brought him a clean cloth and told him that he was paying for a bed as well.

He gave copies to Mira, Ivradan, Rennik and Dorven.

Mira returned hers first. She had marked the pressure-door account heavily. He had written that they had been separated when the mechanism failed. She had underlined the words and written: You pulled before the indicator changed. Put it here, not three pages later.

He moved the sentence.

Ivradan objected to the artisan being named Thane without qualification. Lerris had thought the name harmless as a convenient designation. He changed it to the figure in Thane’s workshop and added what the seal and the man’s knowledge did, and did not, establish.

Rennik’s corrections were mostly mechanical. Beside one passage he wrote, I told you this after you asked the second time. I was not explaining things continuously while being carried.

Lerris laughed when he read it. Then he cut a paragraph that had made the journey sound easier than it had been.

Dorven kept his copy longest.

When he brought it back, he stood in the doorway rather than sit. Lerris offered the chair. Dorven shook his head and opened the pages on the table.

“Here.”

The passage said that Kelset had died ensuring the injured could escape.

Lerris looked at it.

“He moved the rail.”

“Yes.”

“It let us get Rennik through.”

“Yes.”

Dorven laid his hand on the page.

“He thought he was coming through too.”

Lerris said nothing.

“He wasn’t choosing. Don’t give him a speech he didn’t make.”

“There isn’t a speech.”

“You know what I mean.”

Lerris did. He took out the sentence and described the rail, the doorway, the joint opening. He kept the failed grip. Dorven watched him write it.

After a while he said, “You had him.”

“For a moment.”

“I saw.”

Lerris put down the pencil. He had been waiting for someone to say that, and now that Dorven had, it gave him nothing he could use.

Dorven sat at last. He looked toward the vent, then at the bed.

“Telve asked whether he said anything for her.”

“What did you tell her?”

“No.”

Lerris closed his eyes.

“She wanted him to have. So did I.”

They sat until the room had grown cold enough for Lerris to adjust the shutter. He asked Dorven to stay for supper. Dorven said he was expected elsewhere, then stood without moving toward the door.

“The girl did my hair again,” he said.

“I can see.”

“She thinks it’s the same thread.”

The little plait lay against the side of his neck. It was tied with green instead of brown.

Lerris wanted to tell him she might know. He had no reason to think so. He asked whether he would like the corrected pages sent over when they were dry.

Dorven said yes.

Chapter 43 — The Hand He Left Alone

Markus’s reply arrived after Lerris had stopped expecting it every morning.

It was shorter than his own account. He asked after Rennik, asked whether Telve had people nearby, and enclosed a note from Alayse offering practical help if there was something money could actually do. Lerris set that aside until he could ask someone who knew the household.

Markus had marked one claim. Lerris had written that withdrawing his attention had permitted the old binding to close.

You saw the stone move and the water fall. You also say that you cannot remember part of the count. Who saw what your attention did?

Lerris read the question three times. Then he changed the claim. Ivradan had watched his body, Kelset the wedge. None of them had seen attention taking hold of stone. He described where he stood, what he experienced, when the wedge moved, and when he came away.

The next page was the one he had feared sending: Markus across the water.

There were no corrections on it.

Beneath it Markus had written, I should have liked that too.

Lerris went out before answering. He walked to the oven court and bought more food than he wanted. The woman serving asked whether he was carrying it to the infirmary. He said yes and took the long way there so it would cool enough for Rennik to eat.

The engineer was practicing with crutches. His sister stood beside the bed, maintaining an expression of patience that neither man believed. Rennik got as far as the doorway, swore at a stool, and returned.

His sister moved the stool herself. Then she kissed him on the forehead and told him that if he insulted her husband at the wedding she would push him into something shallow.

“When is it?” Lerris asked.

“Still when it was,” she said. “He’s only broken the one leg.”

Rennik took the food. His sister stayed to share it. For half an hour they argued about guests, music, the number of people who would claim they could dance after drinking. Lerris listened. Once Rennik asked his opinion and then dismissed it for being insufficiently informed, which seemed fair.

On the way back Lerris stopped at the works office. The expedition payment was ready. He had been putting off collecting it, though his lodging was using up what money he carried.

He took it. He asked about Kelset’s pay and was told Telve was receiving it while the search remained open. The answer was qualified; everything after a certain date depended on decisions not yet made. He asked who made them and wrote down the name.

He did not go straight to Telve with money. He sent word through Dorven asking whether there was anything she wanted him to do before he left.

The reply came the next day. A letter to the people deciding the pay, if he could keep it to what Kelset had done and what had happened. She did not want another account of the whole expedition.

He wrote it. It was less than a page.

Then he answered Markus. He said the other offer had frightened him because it had not seemed hateful. He stopped there, crossed out a sentence about what that meant, and asked how the pear tree had taken.

Chapter 44 — Another Road

The next report came from a carrier who had heard that Lerris knew about voices.

He brought a piece of pottery wrapped in cloth. Lerris did not let him open it on the lodging table.

“What is it?”

“A jug.”

“Why have you brought it?”

“It speaks when it’s empty.”

Lerris looked at the wrapped shape. It was smaller than he had expected, a household thing with a narrow neck and one handle.

“Does it speak when you’re alone?”

“Sometimes.”

“Does anyone else hear it?”

The carrier’s wife had. So had his sister, though she denied it now. The jug had been taken from a ruined wayside house after a wheel broke nearby. The carrier had wanted water and found none. He had taken the vessel anyway.

“Put it back?” Lerris asked.

“I tried.”

“What happened?”

“I got as far as the place. There’s someone living there.”

Lerris waited.

“She says it was hers.”

“Then why do you still have it?”

The man rubbed his palms on his knees.

“She was buried last spring.”

They talked for a long time. Lerris asked where the jug was kept, what it said, who had touched it, whether anyone was ill. He asked whether the carrier’s family had somewhere else to sleep if they needed it. At last he wrapped the cloth more firmly around the vessel without lifting it and told the man not to bring it into any other house while they arranged a place to keep it.

Mira knew a road warden who had worked the district and a woman who had laid out the dead in its villages for most of her life. The woman would not leave before her niece returned from a visit. The girl had been at the carrier’s house when he brought the jug home; her aunt wanted to question her before they set off. Lerris waited. He paid for the warden’s time, storage for the jug, and rooms for the carrier’s family, who were more offended by the idea of needing rooms than by the speaking vessel itself.

Before departure he visited Rennik. The engineer had been allowed a short walk outside. He was tired and pleased with himself and pretending to be neither.

“Something below again?”

“A road house.”

“Good. Windows.”

“I hope so.”

Rennik asked who was going. Lerris told him. He nodded at the road warden’s name and asked for the woman’s twice, committing it to memory.

“Write when you know what it is.”

“I’ll write what we find.”

Rennik waved away the distinction.

“And whether you ate.”

Lerris said he would.

The morning he left, Dorven met him by the outer gate. He brought the corrected account, folded into a piece of oilcloth. Lerris had given him the copy to keep, but Dorven said Telve had it now and this was the one he had marked.

“You’ve still got the sack wrong.”

“What sack?”

“When you came back with the stop. It was in Kelset’s sack. You say it was in mine.”

Lerris opened the pages against his bag and found the place. He changed the word.

“Anything else?”

“Probably.”

Dorven watched the correction dry. Then he held out his hand. Lerris took it carefully; his own wrist still hurt when he gripped too hard.

Outside, the warden was checking their packs. The woman from the villages sat on a low wall eating an apple. She looked at Lerris’s bag and told him he had brought too much paper.

“Some of it’s blank,” he said.

“That’s the easy kind to leave.”

He laughed and went to help with the packs.

At the first night’s lodging he opened a new book. He had written an elaborate title on a separate sheet and left it behind. On the first page he put the place, the date, and the names of the people who had handled the jug.

The woman sat opposite him, loosening her shoes.

“You’ve put my niece down as my daughter.”

He looked back over the page.

“So I have.”

“And she never touched the thing. I told you she saw it.”

He made the second correction. Then he turned the book toward her and asked whether she would read the rest.

She reached for the lamp. He moved his cup out of the way.
