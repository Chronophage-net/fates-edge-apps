Part I — The people one knows

Chapter 1 — A small alteration

The blade came halfway out and stopped.

Rosceline stood on the stool with one elbow above her head, a foot of bright steel projecting from the silk beneath her arm. She had paid forty-two marks for the dress. The sword had cost a man considerably more, although most of that expense had been incurred before she acquired it.

“Don’t pull,” Briska said.

Rosceline pulled.

Silk parted from elbow to hip. The stool tipped. Daska caught her by the waist and set her back on it without interrupting the cut she was making with the shears.

“There,” Daska said. “You can breathe now.”

“I could breathe before.”

“You were doing it very expensively.”

At the workbench, Pello moved the dressmaker’s bill out from under the dripping oilcan. He examined the total, turned the paper over in the hope of finding an explanation, and put it down.

Briska knelt beside the stool. Her black hair was braided close to her scalp, where it could not enter a gear. A pale burn curled around her wrist. She opened the clasp beneath Rosceline’s arm with a thumbnail and eased the blade clear of the torn lining.

“The catch released. Your sleeve went into the housing. I asked for fitted sleeves.”

“These were fitted, Briska. I stood in them for two hours while a woman with a mouthful of pins assured me of it.”

Daska turned the dangling silk outward. “Keep your elbow up.”

The workshop was above a cooper’s yard on a narrow canal where the water seldom moved faster than a walking man. Through its open window came the smell of wet staves, smoke and the scum that collected beneath the bridge. A boy downstairs was striking a barrel hoop with an iron hammer. He struck four times, paused, then struck five. In the corner Ilaro Selci sat with his face turned from the window and waited for him to finish.

“Will the—will the merchant be wearing armor?” he asked.

“I hope not.” Rosceline looked toward the mirror. “We are going to a party.”

“Then perhaps you could stab him with something smaller.”

The mirror had belonged to a woman who sold assurances of fidelity. A dark stain occupied the place where her customers’ left eyes would have been. Rosceline shifted until she could see all of herself. The split showed a promising length of leg.

She liked the color. In shade the silk was green enough to seem sober. Sunlight brought gold out of it, a slow, rich bloom that made the skin at her throat look warmer. She had chosen it for a woman who bought paintings on behalf of people who could afford to have somebody else choose them. It had no resemblance to the clothes she wore when visiting those people’s creditors.

“I could keep the slit.”

“You will have to,” Briska said. “Unless you can grow another piece.”

Pello looked up. “Can you?”

“Only on someone else’s dress.”

He considered this answer longer than it deserved. Pello was perfectly capable of carrying a false bottom through a search conducted by his own uncle. He had trouble with Rosceline’s accounts of magic. She had never done him the courtesy of making all of them false.

Daska folded the torn cloth back and pinned it. She had large, blunt fingers and a seamstress’s indifference to the body inside the garment. Rosceline had seen those same fingers turn a man’s wrist until he gave back a purse he had not stolen. It had been the wrong man. Daska had apologized, bought him a drink, and gone looking for the right one with a description that was now much better.

“You can carry mine,” she said.

“Your knife looks like a kitchen utensil.”

“It is a kitchen utensil.”

“I have a position to maintain.”

“Then stand still.”

Briska removed the little spring housing and brought it to the bench. Her tools lay on a gray cloth in a remarkably exact disorder. Pello knew which things he could touch because she had put them on his side. He touched one that she had not, and she took it from him without looking.

“This wants a guard over the throat,” she said. “The catch is sound. A lip here will keep the lining out, even if you insist on that sleeve.”

“You can do that by tomorrow?” Rosceline asked.

Briska set a sliver of brass against the opening. “Before you leave. Hold this edge.”

The hammer below struck again. Ilaro shut his notebook.

“Could somebody ask the boy to stop?”

Pello went to the window. Ilaro waited until he looked back.

“For half an hour. I’ll pay.”

Pello leaned out. There was a brief negotiation involving the boy’s skill, his employer’s hearing, and two copper pieces. The hammer stopped. A saw began.

Ilaro rested his forehead on two fingers.

Pello kept the remaining coppers in his palm. He looked at Ilaro, then leaned out again.

Daska laughed. It was an unguarded, belly-deep noise. The monk’s former superiors, whoever they were, had failed to remove that from her. She finished the last pin and helped Rosceline step down.

“You owe the dressmaker another fitting.”

“She owes me a sleeve.”

“Try that on her. I want to hear it.”

There was a knock below, followed by the cooper telling someone that they were upstairs and that he took no responsibility for it. A woman in a dark blue coat came through the door. Her hair was dressed without a single escaped strand. She looked at the sword, the pins, the bill and Ilaro’s closed eyes in that order.

“Madame Rosceline.”

“Celise. Come in.”

Pello cleared a chair. Celise remained standing.

“The Countess will receive you at four. All five of you.”

“She has decided?” Rosceline asked.

“She has a proposal.”

“Those are often the same thing in her house.”

Celise gave that no answer. She took a square card from the inside breast pocket of her coat and put it on the workbench, beyond the oil. There was no name on it. A narrow border, black ink, an address on the quiet side of a bridge whose other side never slept.

Rosceline had been to the house twice. The first time she had talked her way in. The second time she had been expected, which had felt worse.

“Will there be food?” Daska asked.

“At four?”

“I like to know.”

“There can be.”

“Thank you.”

Celise’s mouth changed slightly. “You are welcome.”

After she had gone, Ilaro opened his notebook again.

“You told her about us.”

“I recommended you. With some care, I might add; I wanted her to employ you, not send for someone to look into you.”

“What did you recommend me for?”

“Finding the truth when people have gone to considerable expense to make something else easier to believe.”

“You could have employed me yourself.”

“I could not afford the volume.”

He looked at her for a moment. His left eye watered in strong light; she had learned to wait until he finished blinking before deciding whether he was angry. This time he was amused. A little.

“Four,” he said. “I need an hour before that without the rest of you.”

Pello reached for his cap. Daska took an apple from the bowl on the bench and tossed another to him. Briska was already shaping the guard for the release, one foot braced on the stool. She worked with the pleasure of someone being allowed to correct a person in metal.

Rosceline stayed for the second test.

The harness lay diagonally beneath the dress, holding the slender blade from her left ribs toward her right thigh. A small sheath covered the point. Briska fitted the guard over the release and made Rosceline draw slowly, once, then in a single movement. Steel hissed through the slit. The point passed within an inch of the mirror.

“Again,” Briska said.

Rosceline sheathed it. Drew. This time she turned as she did it, letting the skirt follow the movement. The green silk opened around her knee. In the mirror, a woman of excellent breeding appeared ready to run somebody through.

“That’s better.”

“The guard keeps the cloth clear now,” Briska said. “You still need to draw along the sheath.”

Rosceline kissed her on the crown of her head.

Briska wiped her cheek against her shoulder, although the kiss had not touched it. “You’ll put paint on the work.”

“Then it will be worth more.”

She went downstairs before Briska could answer. At the bottom, the cooper was trying to explain to his apprentice why silence was not part of the price of a barrel. Rosceline left two more coppers beside the boy’s saw and carried her torn, lovely dress out into the afternoon.

Chapter 2 — The price of a voice

The Countess’s door had no knocker. A woman opened it before Rosceline could decide whether that was a courtesy or a measurement.

Inside, the air smelled of jasmine and the dry, sweet dust of paper kept from damp. There was water somewhere behind a screen. A shallow fountain, perhaps, though Rosceline had never seen it. The sound was irregular enough to make her listen.

Daska had brushed her coat and left it open. Her clothes fitted closely at the wrists and ankles, and she had put on earrings the color of ripe plums. Pello had washed. Ilaro wore the same dark coat he wore everywhere, with the collar repaired in thread almost, but not quite, its color. Briska carried her questions in a leather case, together with a short rule.

Celise took them through a waiting room. A woman sat beside the window with a paper crushed between her hands. She was in stage paint, one eye finished, the other bare. Without the second eye her face looked painfully unbalanced.

“He says the rooms belong to the company,” she was telling a younger attendant. “They do. I know that. I pay for them every month.”

The attendant glanced toward Celise.

“Letha,” Celise said. “Come upstairs with me.”

“I don’t mind the rent. He wants me to tell him who comes after the performance. As if I don’t have anything to do afterward except remember men for him.”

Her voice was low and scraped at the edges. A singer who had been arguing instead of resting it.

Celise held out her hand for the paper. Letha let her take it and stood. At the foot of the stair she caught sight of Daska’s earrings.

“Those will pull if you turn fast.”

“I take them off.”

Letha nodded, as though that at least had been settled, and went upstairs.

The Countess waited in the next room. Here, Madam Serafine received as the Velvet Countess. Her veil was dark, fine enough for the outline of her mouth to show when she turned toward the light. A bowl of white flowers stood near her chair. She wore no excessive jewelry. Men who expected a quantity of gold to tell them where the power sat generally found this room confusing.

“Madame Rosceline. Your friends.”

Rosceline introduced them. The Countess gave Ilaro the chair with its back to the window. To Daska she offered a plate of small savory cakes before the introductions were finished.

“I have been told you prefer matters put plainly,” she said to Briska.

“It saves taking something apart to discover what the customer meant.”

“Then I shall try to be precise.”

She laid a drawing on the low table. Briska moved the flowers to make room. The Countess watched her do it.

“Lord Caspian Verre holds his Lantern Gala tomorrow evening. I require a book from his cellar.”

Pello waited. The Countess looked toward him, but he had no question yet.

The drawing depicted a house with a wide entrance hall, a side wing and a canal along its eastern wall. A small separate plan showed the cellar. Briska put her rule across it.

“Are these measurements or paces?”

“Paces.”

“Then I need the person who walked it. I can’t get a door’s width from somebody’s stride.”

“I can arrange an introduction.”

“I’ll want one.”

The Countess touched a rectangle at the end of the cellar passage. “The Sunset Ledger. Brown leather, brass corners, a red band across the spine. The name was a private joke. Caspian liked to conduct this business at the end of a successful day.”

“Whose business?” Ilaro asked.

“His. And a number of people who would now prefer to have conducted less of it.”

She described a customs officer who stopped examining particular cargoes, a watch captain who sent patrols to the wrong quay, a landlord who discovered an old right to increase rents after his tenants refused to vote as instructed. She did not give the names. She gave the amounts.

Rosceline looked toward the stair door.

“And singers.”

“Caspian has recently begun taking an interest in my acquaintances.”

“You object to competition.”

“I object to that woman’s frightened face in my waiting room. I also object to competition. You need not choose which is true.”

Daska put down the cake she had been holding. She had eaten three. The Countess moved the plate closer to her anyway.

The fee was a thousand silver marks, divided equally, with a separate purse for preparation. Rosceline watched Pello avoid looking at anyone. He had been talking about a boat for months. She could almost hear its dimensions expanding.

“The original book,” the Countess said. “Complete. No pages sold to someone you meet on the way out. No copying it for an independent enterprise.”

“Who else has copies?” Ilaro asked.

“Of individual arrangements? Several people. Of the whole? I believe nobody. Caspian has found it prudent to make himself necessary.”

Ilaro let the answer sit before writing anything down.

Briska leaned over the cellar plan. “What’s on the door?”

“Ironwork. A lock whose key is kept in his upper office. A portrait of his late wife.”

“Does the frame conceal the lock?”

“The portrait requires something said to it. A private form of address, perhaps. I have not obtained it.”

“And behind the paint?”

“I do not have a complete account.”

Briska sat back.

The Countess took a cream envelope from a shallow drawer. Its flap was closed with red wax bearing the impression of a small flower. She laid it beside the plan.

“If a claim is made upon removal of the book, this may settle it.”

“May,” Ilaro repeated.

“I cannot promise what someone else will accept.”

“What are you offering them?”

“One place, for one named guest, at one of my private evenings before the season ends. I choose the evening. They enter my house and receive my hospitality. Nothing more.”

Rosceline looked at the envelope. She had once spent seventeen days trying to get a man invited to a supper in this house. The man had died of ordinary causes before she succeeded. She had retained the advance.

“That’s expensive,” she said.

“So is the book.”

Briska lifted the envelope by its edges. “Does opening this bind the opener?”

“No.”

“Does carrying it?”

“No.”

“Can someone use it to enter a bedroom?”

The Countess’s voice changed. “No.”

Briska put it in her case.

The rest was access. Lady Wren, who had a grievance and liked company. A restoration contractor prepared to add two workers to a list. A guard who wished to leave the city. The Countess knew where he drank and had not approached him herself.

“He will trust someone who can be found afterward,” she said.

“Then why ask me?” Rosceline said.

“I asked all five of you.”

They agreed to inspect the arrangements before giving a final answer. Celise returned and led them out, carrying the copied plans. Rosceline remained seated.

The Countess waited until the stair door closed. Then she brought out the book.

It was smaller than Rosceline remembered notebooks being in that house. Brown covers, rubbed pale at the bottom corners. A scrap of blue cloth had been sewn around the spine by a person who could work magic and could not sew. There was a dark crescent on the front where a cup had stood.

Rosceline did not touch it.

“Where?”

“An Ashaani collection sold through three owners. I had the seller’s account checked as far as it could be.”

“Where is he?”

“The latest seller? At home, enjoying his money.”

The Countess turned to a page marked with a thin ribbon. The writing ran uphill and then lost confidence halfway across. Rosceline knew the hand. She had copied beneath it, once, her own letters cramped for fear of wasting the paper.

There was a mark in the margin shaped like the beginning of an ear. She placed her fingertip beside it.

For a moment she heard only the water behind the screen.

Then Sarem said, “Across the bronze—”

“Burnished,” she said.

“—burnished hills,” the voice continued, annoyed, and stopped.

She laughed once. It hurt her throat.

“He always did that.”

The Countess said nothing.

Rosceline touched the mark again. The same hesitation, the same correction. She knew what should follow. Her own voice, young and intolerably pleased to have caught him. The working had kept none of it.

She withdrew her hand.

“How many?”

“Surviving passages? Four that my reader could wake. There may be others.”

“Jobs.”

The Countess sat opposite her.

“Four successful recoveries. Offered separately. Accepted separately. Paid at the agreed rate. The first can be Caspian’s book.”

“And if I refuse the next?”

“You wait for another proposal.”

“If you dislike the manner in which I bring something home?”

“I will probably tell you.”

“But it counts.”

“If it is the thing we agreed upon, in the agreed condition, yes.”

“Put that down.”

The Countess opened the writing box beside her chair. Rosceline watched the nib move. At the end she took the paper, read it twice, and made one small addition above the line. The Countess initialed it.

“You may hear it again before you go.”

“I have heard it.”

She stood too quickly. The Countess closed Sarem’s book with both hands, carefully, and Rosceline hated her for knowing how to handle it.

Celise was waiting in the passage. Rosceline gave her the smile she used for agreeable meetings.

Celise looked at it, then down at the torn seam beneath her coat.

“There is a woman in our sewing room who could mend that.”

“It’s intended.”

“Of course.”

Outside, Silkstrand struck her with heat and the smell of fish frying in old oil. The others waited beneath an awning. Daska had bought a paper cone of cherries. She offered it without asking why Rosceline had stayed behind.

Rosceline took one, bit through the skin and let the sour juice fill her mouth.

“We should do it,” she said.

Chapter 3 — People who will help

Ilaro put a white bean on the cellar vault and a red one on the canal wicket.

“The white is the book.”

Pello moved the red bean aside and bent over the drawing beneath it.

“Leave that visible.”

Ilaro moved the bean to the edge of the paper.

He had slept badly. A pain sat behind his left eye, dull enough to tolerate until something struck it from the outside. The workshop’s upper shutters were closed, leaving a thin stripe of morning on the floor. Briska had moved her hammering downstairs. Pello, who seemed constitutionally unable to sit without shifting his weight, had been given the chair that did not creak.

Daska arrived smelling of river mist and breakfast.

“He’s there,” she said. “Eating very slowly.”

Ilaro closed the notebook he had been reading. On its first page was his old business address. He had crossed it out years ago and continued using the book from the other end. Sometimes he opened it backward and spent a moment expecting somebody to knock.

“Did you speak to him?”

“I bought food. He looked at my hands.”

“Did he look frightened?”

“He looked as though he’d paid for eggs and wanted eggs.”

Pello laughed. Ilaro put his pencil down.

“Come on, then.”

The eating stall stood above a landing where eel baskets were unloaded. Its proprietor cooked over charcoal in an iron pan so worn the middle glowed red through the ash. Hessel sat with his back to the wall and his breakfast divided into small pieces. He had a square head, a close beard and a little hooked scar beside his mouth. His coat was turned inside out to hide its livery. The stitching gave it away.

Daska sat opposite him. Pello took the place nearest the steps. Ilaro stayed on the end, out of the direct sun.

“I was told two,” Hessel said.

“He asks questions,” Daska said, indicating Ilaro. “It saves time afterward.”

Hessel pushed his plate away. “Ask.”

The guard knew the house well. He gave the service-stair turns, the height of the kitchen sill and the position of the key board in Caspian’s office. He knew which maids would object to seeing a tradesman upstairs and which would object to not being helped with a basket. He described the head of security without affection and without contempt.

“Mistress Thorn reads the whole instruction,” he said. “You can’t give her the last bit and hope she likes it.”

“Is she at the gate?” Ilaro asked.

“Everywhere. She starts at the gate.”

Pello drew a narrow rectangle beside the canal.

“Loading door?”

“Upstairs. Linen room.”

“Working hoist?”

“It lifted a crate of lamps yesterday.”

“How many men’s weight?”

Hessel looked at him.

“One at a time,” Pello said.

“You asked about the wicket.”

Pello kept his pencil beside the upper door and waited.

Ilaro watched the guard’s plate. The yolk had begun to harden. Hessel wanted to reach for the bread and would not do it while they were watching him.

“Eat,” Ilaro said. “We are not paying you to leave hungry.”

Hessel tore the bread.

His shift covered the last part of the gala. At the last dance he could turn from the canal wicket to help the kitchen men wheel out an empty wine rack. He would leave its bolt drawn and the landing lamp covered. The scow could take the crew aboard under the noise of a departing guest barge. Hessel would join them, leave his coat in the boat, and be out of the city on a coastal vessel the next morning.

“Before that,” Ilaro said. “The cellar.”

“I go down for the special wine.”

“You leave the stair unwatched.”

“For a little while.”

“How little?”

“Depends how long it takes to find the wine.”

Daska smiled. Hessel did not.

“They tell you to be useful,” he said. “You do a thing and then you’ve done it, and next time they know who to ask.”

The proprietor put more charcoal under the pan. Sparks flew into the bright morning.

“What did you do?” Daska asked.

Hessel looked toward Pello, who was drawing the mooring posts.

“There was a woman in a shop. Caspian said the lease had ended. She had shelves full of little birds. Clay ones. Whistles. I took the shelves down.”

“With the birds on them?”

Hessel rubbed the scar at his mouth.

“Yes.”

Ilaro could have asked which shop, how long ago, who else was there. He knew the usefulness and the danger of an account that ended before anyone bled. Daska let the silence go on until Hessel lifted his eyes.

“I want the boat,” he said.

Pello turned his paper. On the reverse he wrote a vessel’s name and a master’s name.

“The Little Sister. East along the coast. Master Vennick. Your place is booked under Hessel. No clever name to forget when someone calls it.”

Hessel looked more closely at him. “Your people are the Tregards.”

Pello’s pencil stopped.

“I’ve seen your cousin at the confiscation quay,” Hessel said. “The Vilikari family. Everybody in the watch knows the boats.”

Pello folded the paper along its old crease without taking it away.

“I’m not leaving Verre to work for smugglers.”

Pello pushed the paper nearer. “Vennick isn’t family. I’ve bought you an ordinary berth. Nobody’s asking you to carry a parcel, now or when you get off.”

Hessel read the names again. “Good.”

“When?”

“First light after the gala. I’ll give you the passage paper when we get you out.”

“And if you leave early?”

Daska put her hand flat on the table.

“You go with us.”

He looked at her hand, then at her face.

“Say it properly.”

“I’ll get you to your boat, Hessel.”

He nodded. It was the first time his shoulders moved without following a noise.

On the way back, Pello said, “The wicket opens inward.”

“You asked him?” Ilaro said.

“I looked from the landing yesterday. There’s a crescent scraped in the stone inside the sill. People drag it. It will make a noise.”

“Put that on the plan.”

“Under the bean.”

Ilaro stopped. Daska went another two paces before she noticed.

“What?” she asked.

Ilaro held up a finger. The words took a moment to come.

“His shift. He said he relieves the canal guard at the second bell after supper. The Countess’s list says the first.”

“He may have remembered wrong.”

“Then we should not build an escape on his remembering right.”

They went back. Hessel was still at the stall, finishing the bread.

This time it took only a question. The kitchen bell was rung after the servants had eaten. The hall bell marked the guests’ supper, an hour earlier. Both lists were correct and useless without the distinction. Ilaro wrote HALL in large letters at the top of his page.

Hessel watched him.

“You used to be in the watch?”

“No.”

“You ask like one.”

“I used to be paid to notice when they hadn’t.”

At the workshop, Rosceline was waiting with two invitations on thick paper and a smear of lilac paint on her glove.

“Wren?” Ilaro asked.

“Admires my judgment.”

“What did you promise?”

“To admire a painter at her next salon. Apparently he’s been standing beside his pictures for three weeks while everyone talks about the curtains.”

“Can he paint?”

“I have not yet been asked to decide.”

She took the chair opposite and listened while they described the guard. She asked sensible questions about the scow, the moment of departure and Thorn’s habit of reading every instruction. Ilaro watched her fingertip move along the plan from the wicket to the upper loading door. He had known her for two smaller jobs. On the first she had lied to everyone except him. On the second she had lied to him as well, and saved him from arrest by doing it.

He had not decided how to enter that in his notes.

“What does the guard want?” she asked.

“To leave,” Daska said.

“Everyone wants to leave somewhere.”

“He wants to leave this place on this boat tomorrow morning. We’ve promised.”

Rosceline lifted her finger from the plan.

“Then we should be punctual.”

She went out again with the invitations tucked into the inner pocket of her coat. Pello left to confirm the berth. Daska took the cold tea downstairs to empty it without asking Ilaro whether he wanted it reheated. Briska returned with a metal blank she intended to turn into a key.

Ilaro put the white bean beside the canal and began the sequence again, this time from the end.

Chapter 4 — Spare parts

Briska’s parent had sent the lock as a birthday present.

It was a beautiful piece of work. Seven tumblers, a concealed gate, a spring that would hold its temper for longer than most lowland houses stood. The key had arrived a week later with a proposal for distributing the locks along the western coast.

Briska kept the lock. She had opened it before the key came.

Her parent had spent most of her childhood walking between the Aeler holds and the lowland markets. Every homecoming brought samples, contracts and some new reason the workshop could be made more profitable. Briska had declined the last reason in writing. The Edgewalker had sent back a correction to her estimate.

People hearing Aezenbron sometimes expected somebody older, broader and born under a mountain. She was half-Aeler and had grown up beside this canal. Neither fact had ever prevented a customer from asking her to mend an ancient thing whose maker had been dead longer than her family could remember.

The lock stood now at the back of her bench, holding down a stack of drawings she did not want anyone to read. Beside it lay three brass blanks, a shallow tray of dark wax and a thin fold of silver that would keep the wax from adhering to the original key. She had spent the morning learning how much pressure would give her a clean impression through a work glove.

Pello watched her make the fifth attempt.

She lifted the test key. Clean shoulders, no drag at the teeth. The wax smelled faintly of pine resin. It pleased her more than she intended to show.

Pello was looking at the birthday lock.

“Did you make that?”

“No.”

Pello turned it over, studying the finish. “I could sell these.”

Briska gave him the look that had made two masters decline to retain her as an apprentice. “So I have been told.”

He left it alone. Pello had an excellent sense of danger when the danger was within arm’s reach.

Down in the yard, his cousin was attempting to move Briska’s tool frame without touching the dirty side of it. This was difficult because all four sides had been in the workshop. The cousin had a broad face, silver rings in both ears and the patient, proprietary manner of someone who had known Pello when he was small enough to lift by the collar.

“You said a box,” he called.

“It folds,” Briska said.

“So do dead men. People still charge by the length.”

She went down and showed him how the corner braces released. The wooden frame came together around the tools, its canvas cover closing over the top. A pair of leather handles took the load. The cousin lifted it and grunted.

“And five people?”

“Six,” Pello said. “The guard.”

“Plus me.”

Pello nodded and went down to the landing to make room.

The scow waited at the landing beneath the yard. Its paint had been blue in several different years. A broad, shallow hull gave room for crates and passengers; a long sweep hung off the stern, and two shorter oars lay inside. Briska stepped aboard and inspected the gunwale fastenings. The cousin watched her with increasing offense.

“She floats.”

“I’m checking where to lash the tools. They mustn’t shift into someone’s legs.” She pointed at the gap beneath a thwart.

He showed her a sound lashing point, then another. He knew his boat. She liked him better after he stopped telling her so.

Pello counted the oars, touched the spare painter and looked up toward the workshop window. “We need to practice lowering.”

The cousin’s expression brightened. “Him first.”

They used the yard’s lifting beam. Pello rigged a line through a pulley and made a broad sling from a spare sail strap. Briska put the tool frame in it first, then the cousin added two sacks of sand. The beam creaked. The knots held.

Daska arrived while they were lowering Pello.

“You’ve found work for him.”

“He’s freight,” the cousin said. “Costs less if it doesn’t speak.”

Pello reached the scow, unhooked himself and threw the empty sling up. His cousin made him do it again. On the third descent he caught a heel under the window sill and swung hard enough to strike the wall.

“Feet together until you’re clear,” Daska said.

“I know.”

“Then do that instead.”

Briska watched the rope run. Nothing in the arrangement required novelty. Sound rope, a sound anchor, a person who paid attention at each end. She felt a small resentment toward how well it worked.

Ilaro came down when the sun had moved off the yard. He examined the sling without touching it.

“I would prefer stairs.”

“So would I,” Briska said. “We’re making sure.”

He took his coat off and folded it over a barrel. Daska helped him into the strap. Halfway down he shut his eyes and asked them to stop swinging him. They had not known they were.

Pello steadied the rope from the boat. Ilaro descended the last few feet with his jaw clenched and remained seated after they freed him.

“Again?” Briska asked.

He did not answer until she began to reach for the strap.

“No. No, once is enough today.”

She sat on the landing beside him while the others coiled the line. It was evening now. A woman was washing a child on the next stair, threatening to throw his dirty ears into the canal and let him grow new ones. The child offered her his brother’s ears instead.

“Will you manage?” Briska asked.

Ilaro rubbed his palms together. “If you don’t ask me while I’m doing it.”

“I can manage that.”

“And tell me before the drop. People say ‘ready’ when they mean they’re about to release something. I prefer to hear what the something is.”

She wrote that on the corner of the plan after they went upstairs.

Later she took apart the latch-spreader she intended to carry. A tiny screw drove two opposed jaws outward. In an ordinary door it would ease the wood away from a sprung latch long enough to put a shim through. In a well-repaired door it would probably bend itself into an expensive ornament. She packed a thin strip of steel beside it.

Pello had gone with his cousin to arrange the coastal berth. Daska sat on the floor oiling the soles of her dancing shoes. The shoes were soft leather with a repair under the right heel. They had been used, and used hard.

“You like this part,” Briska said.

“The shoes?”

“Tonight.”

Daska pressed the repaired heel with her thumb. “I’ve danced for worse reasons.”

“At the monastery?”

“They frowned on it.”

“Did that stop you?”

“For years.”

The answer came so readily that Briska had to look up.

Daska set the shoe aside. “I was very good at doing what I was told.”

“I find that difficult to picture.”

“So do I, now.” She picked up the other shoe. “They used to give us sealed chests to carry to the winter kitchens. Said curiosity was the self asking to be fed. I got strong carrying them. Didn’t get curious until they stopped sending the kitchens any food.”

Briska tightened a screw that was already tight.

“Did you open one?”

“Eventually.”

Daska rubbed the oil into the leather. Briska let her. After a while the monk asked whether she had a needle heavy enough for a sole, and they talked about that.

When the workshop was quiet, Briska opened a little tin from the top shelf. Inside lay a casting with one bubble near the neck. A narrow, useless thing, her first attempt to make a valve that would close under its own weight. She set it beside the finished tools and rested a fingertip against the flaw.

“First Breath,” she said softly. “One thing I haven’t thought of. Let me see it before it breaks.”

There was no answer. She had never expected one. She inspected the sling buckle again and found a burr that might fret the strap. She filed it away.

By the time Rosceline returned for her dress, the tools were packed, the rope was dry and the candle had burned a little hollow in its own wax. Rosceline came upstairs with a narrow red ribbon folded between two fingers.

“A gift?” Briska asked.

“For someone who is helping us.”

“What does the help cost?”

“Less than the ribbon.”

Briska looked at her. Rosceline smiled, took the mended dress from its hook and held it against herself.

“Have you improved my sword? I ask because it has cost me a sleeve and an afternoon, and I should like to feel one of us has benefited.”

“I have improved your access to it.”

“A useful distinction.”

She turned toward the mirror. Briska put the faulty little casting back in its tin and closed the lid.

Part II — An excellent invitation

Chapter 5 — Almost certainly invited

Rosceline wrote with her left hand.

She could write well with it. Tonight she made the letters thick and uncertain, an educated person’s idea of a laborer’s effort. She misspelled duplicate, corrected it, and left the first attempt visible beneath a single stroke.

Mistress Thorn. Your canal man Hessel is selling keys. Ask him where he means to be tomorrow. You will not find him at his station when the guest barge leaves.

She read it over. Too good. She removed the final sentence and began again on a fresh sheet, replacing it with He has money that is not his pay.

That was true. Daska had given him half his fee against the route. Enough for Thorn to find something when she looked, and finding something would keep her looking there.

The first sheet went into the little fire beneath the kettle. Rosceline held it by a corner until the heat reached her nail.

Below her rented room, a messenger waited beside a flower stall. He had a pitted cheek and the long, careful nails of a man who did no carrying. He had delivered three unpleasant discoveries for her in the past and never asked whether the discoveries were genuine.

She gave him the folded note, tied with the red ribbon.

“Amber Villa. Security entrance. Mistress Thorn herself.”

“Before the party?”

“At the second boarding call from the guest barge. Not the first.”

“I know numbers.”

“I have paid people who didn’t.”

He tucked the note inside his waistcoat. “What if the barge doesn’t leave?”

“Then you bring it back.”

She paid him half and showed him the rest. He looked at the dress beneath her open coat, with the new slit edged in narrow gold braid.

“You going in?”

“I have an invitation.”

“People with invitations usually want things delivered before.”

“Those are gifts.”

She left him with that distinction and walked toward the hired chair waiting at the corner.

Hessel would be in Pello’s scow by the second call. She had left margin for ordinary delay. Thorn would find his absence, his abandoned duties and the money, if he had been fool enough to keep any in his room. The inquiry would follow the departing guard toward the sea. Madame Rosceline would remain a woman who had admired two paintings and refused to buy either.

It was a useful identity. She had spent a year making it useful. She did not intend to lose it because the others were content to leave the direction of the search to chance.

Ilaro waited beside the chair, wearing a small black mask above his ordinary coat.

“Is that all?” she asked.

Ilaro adjusted the small black mask. “The larger one pressed here.” He touched his temple.

She took his arm. “Smile when I introduce you.”

“I have a headache already.”

“Then we must give you a different expression for it.”

The Amber Villa rose above a low wall crusted with flowering vines. Its pale stone had drunk the day’s heat and returned it to the evening in slow breaths. Lanterns swung between carved posts. Within the gates, women moved like brilliant fish through the dark backs of men’s coats. Gold flashed at throats and wrists. A man had brought a small white animal on a chain and was attempting to explain its value to someone who had stepped in its leavings.

The hired chair stopped behind a carriage whose wheels were lacquered crimson. Rosceline gave the bearers a gratuity large enough to be seen, then walked past the carriage before its passengers could emerge.

At the gate a woman in a plain coat examined their invitations. Her hair was graying at the temples. A sword hung high at her left hip, clear of the people crowding past. The fingers holding the paper had old burns across the knuckles.

“Lady Wren’s guests,” Rosceline said.

“I can read.”

Rosceline smiled. “That makes the evening easier.”

The woman looked up. Thorn’s eyes were a pale brown, almost yellow in the lantern light. They passed over Ilaro, returned to Rosceline’s split skirt, and stopped at the line of her left shoulder.

“The coat stays in the cloakroom.”

“I should hope so. It has been trying to slide off me since the bridge.”

“Weapons stay there too.”

“Do you consider opinions weapons?”

Thorn beckoned the cloakroom attendant.

She returned the invitations. Rosceline went through without another attempt to be amusing.

In the cloakroom she surrendered her outer coat and the little knife she carried openly in its pocket. The attendant gave her a wooden token. The rapier’s hilt lay flat beneath her left arm. She kept that arm relaxed. Briska’s harness held without pulling.

The hall opened in a blaze of cut glass. Chandeliers threw light onto gilded pilasters and the wet-looking green of enormous leaves standing in pots. Someone had banked white flowers along the stair. Beneath their scent lay hot wax, perfume and the dense animal warmth of people determined to enjoy themselves in too many clothes.

Rosceline breathed it in.

She had been hungry in cities full of rooms like this. She had entered them through kitchens, windows and a hole beneath a wall once occupied by a much smaller thief. Tonight a servant pronounced her name, correctly, and six people turned to find out whether they should know her.

She let them wonder for a moment.

“Madame Rosceline,” said a man in silver-gray silk. “At last.”

Caspian Verre was handsome in the well-maintained manner of a man whose mirrors were paid to tell him the truth. A little softness beneath the jaw, a strong nose, hair dressed back from his temples. His mask hung from two fingers. He had taken it off so that other people could recognize him.

“My lord.”

“Wren tells me you have an eye.”

“Two, on most evenings.”

He laughed exactly enough. “And Mr. Selci.”

Ilaro inclined his head.

“You know the family?” Rosceline asked, lightly.

“I know a number of people,” Caspian said. “I hope you will like the collection.”

He touched her fingers as he bowed. His skin was warm and very dry.

A servant brought him a glass. Caspian glanced at it and said, “No clove for Lady Vessa. She asked last year. And give the musicians their first purse before the dancing begins. I won’t have them counting my generosity by the tune.”

The servant smiled with relief and went away.

“You see?” Caspian said. “I attend to small things.”

“It must be exhausting.”

“I have people.”

Across the hall, Daska stood with the dancers near the musicians’ platform. She had removed the plum earrings and wore a narrow amber mask. Her hair was bound behind her neck. A wiry woman beside her was demonstrating a step with considerable impatience. Daska watched, repeated it, and added a turn. The woman’s impatience became interest.

Rosceline let Caspian introduce her to a man who owned salt warehouses. While the man described the difficulties of salt, she watched Daska take the floor.

The first movement was restrained. Hands meeting, weight passing, skirts brushing polished stone. Then the drum entered. Daska’s partner stepped across her line to test her; she shifted around him so closely that his sleeve lifted in her wake. He grinned. She gave him the turn back and laughed when he nearly missed it.

Rosceline had meant only to use the distraction. She found herself watching for another measure.

“Difficult business,” the warehouse owner said.

“A terrible business.”

“People imagine the sea does it for us.”

“People imagine all sorts of things.”

She saw Pello on the far side of the hall carrying a rolled canvas. Briska walked ahead of him with the confidence of a woman who had been asked to repair something and intended to make everyone regret the asking. They vanished through the eastern service door.

Ilaro had obtained his introduction to the art steward. He was being shown upstairs. Before he turned, he touched the right side of his mask: the agreed sign that he had the gallery.

Rosceline moved her glass to her left hand. Under the musicians’ platform, a man in a porcelain mask sat beside an empty chair.

There was nothing on the mask. No painted mouth, no eye decoration, only the cut openings and the faint glaze of cream-colored clay. The wearer held a wineglass by its stem. A dark glove covered the hand.

The warehouse owner followed her glance.

“Do you know that person?” she asked.

“One of Caspian’s private guests.”

“Which one?”

He seemed to forget that she had asked. His eyes returned to her face and settled there with renewed appreciation.

“I was saying, the cost of storage—”

Rosceline put her glass into his hand.

“Forgive me. I have seen a painting.”

She left before he could decide which glass belonged to him.

Chapter 6 — The door that stopped scraping

The steward’s name was Orset. He had written it on the repair order in a hand that disliked expenditure.

Briska stood beneath the portrait in Caspian’s office and held up the damaged brass fitting.

“Who used this screw?”

“The previous contractor.”

“The thread is stripped. He’s packed the hole and driven it in anyway.” Briska turned the fitting so Orset could see. “You paid him for this?”

Orset tightened his mouth. Pello, carrying the tool frame, looked innocently at the ceiling.

The office was long and shallow, with two windows toward the street and a door onto the upper gallery. A desk occupied the center. Its surface was empty except for a tray, a letter opener and a little glass bird with a chipped beak. On the wall beside the portrait hung a board with four keys.

Hessel had described them well. The vault key was the shortest: a broad, flat bow, a stem darkened where fingers held it, five teeth with one cut deep into the shoulder. Briska looked once and returned to the fitting.

“I’ll need a new fixing in the wall. Can someone fetch water? Enough to settle the dust.”

“You will not make dust in here.”

“A wet cloth under the fixing will catch it. Unless you’d rather leave the frame loose.”

Orset looked toward Pello.

“I need him to hold the frame,” Briska said.

The steward went to the door and called for a servant. Pello brought the tool frame against the wall, opening its canvas cover between Orset and the key board. Briska reached beneath it, lifted the short key and pressed it into the wax lined with thin silver. Her thumb knew when the teeth had settled. She lifted it straight out, replaced it on its hook and closed her palm over the tray.

Orset turned back before she had put the tray away.

“What is that?”

“Wax.”

“For the wall?”

“For the fitting. The wall gets water.”

She showed him the piece of brass with wax darkening one edge. She had put it there before coming into the house. He inspected it, disliked the evidence of activity, and told them to be quick.

Briska made the repair properly. She would have found it difficult to do otherwise with the screw in her hand. The old fixing had been driven into a crumbly joint. She moved it a finger’s breadth and seated it in sound stone. Pello held the frame while she worked. Sweat gathered under his cap.

The woman in the portrait wore black, with a white flower caught in the opening of her bodice. Her face was less beautiful than the painter might have been paid to make it. The mouth, broad and slightly uneven, interested Briska. So did the wedding ring, worn high above the knuckle where the finger narrowed.

When they lifted the frame clear, Pello saw writing on its back.

“Don’t rub that,” Briska said sharply.

He froze with his thumb beside the letters. “I wasn’t.”

“Hold it away from your coat.”

She took the thin mirror from her kit, ostensibly to inspect the upper fastening, and read the line backward.

They came for you. C.

Below it was a date, old enough for the varnish to have darkened around the incisions. She copied both on the back of the repair order while pretending to note the screw size. Pello watched without asking. He was good when given something clear to do.

Afterward Orset took them to the linen room. Its loading door stood above the canal, and the hoist projected beyond the outer wall under a small tiled hood. A wooden cleat was bolted into a beam inside. The floor was scarred by the feet of heavy trunks.

“Your things out this way,” the steward said. “You will not carry that box through supper.”

“Someone will have to receive it.”

“Arrange it.”

Pello leaned out and whistled twice. Below, his cousin brought the scow out from under the shadow of a mooring stage. A servant stacking linen glanced through the opening and lost interest.

Briska knelt beside the tool frame. Under its cover she put the impression into a wooden clamp and began filing a prepared blank. The coarse work was already done. She needed the deep shoulder cut and the final height of two teeth. She worked by feel as much as sight, hiding the motion beneath the canvas while Pello examined the hoist.

“Old line is rotten near the eye,” he said.

The servant looked up.

“Then don’t use it.”

“We brought one.”

“Use that.”

He did. The rope went through the pulley; the sling took the tool frame; the cousin cursed softly as it came down onto his boat. Pello left their line reeved and coiled the tail behind a stack of spare canvas. Briska kept the small kit in her leather bag and put the finished duplicate into its narrow side pocket.

When she stood, the linen-room door caught on the floor.

She looked at the lower hinge. Two turns would lift it clear.

“Leave that,” Pello murmured.

“It makes noise.”

“It’s made noise for years.”

“We’re leaving through here if things go badly. Do you want noise?”

She lifted the door against her knee and tightened the loose screw. It moved beautifully.

Mistress Thorn came through it less than a minute later.

She stopped with one hand on the latch, pulled the door back and opened it again.

“Who did that?”

Briska kept her bag open. Hiding a thing too quickly was a good way to advertise it.

“I did.”

“Why?”

“It scraped.”

Thorn looked at her clothes, the bag, the clean semicircle on the floor where old scraping had polished the boards. “It did.”

Pello stood by the hoist with the awkward blankness of a man trying not to look toward a rope.

“Name?” Thorn asked him.

“Canaletti.”

“That all?”

“Pello Canaletti.”

“You took something down to a boat.”

“Tools.”

“A large number of tools.”

Pello glanced toward Briska before answering. “Enough for the day.”

She kept her attention on Thorn.

Thorn went to the loading door and looked down. The cousin looked up, waved a bill and demanded to know who was paying for his waiting time.

“Not me,” Thorn said.

“That’s what they all say.”

She examined the new rope. Then the folded canvas. Then Briska’s bag.

“Open it.”

Briska set it on the linen table and laid out the contents. Pliers. Rule. Screwdriver. Files. The little latch-spreader, folded tight. The steel shim. A shallow tin of wax closed over the impression. The duplicate key lay in the side pocket under her finger.

Thorn picked up the spreader.

“What’s this?”

“For easing a stuck latch.”

“Do you get many stuck latches on picture frames?”

“People ask you to fix other things once you’re in the house.”

“Such as my door.”

“That was free.”

Thorn turned the screw. The jaws opened. She inspected them with genuine interest.

“Would it work on oak?”

“On a loose latch-side joint, yes. Sound oak will bend the jaws before it gives. It’s a latch-spreader, not a jack.”

“A careful answer.”

“I’d rather have the complaint before you break it.”

Thorn returned the tool. Briska resisted the urge to check it immediately.

“Orset can sign you out. After that, downstairs. No wandering.”

“We have two frames left in the gallery.”

“Then repair them.”

Thorn stood aside and watched them leave. Briska felt the duplicate key pressing through leather against her finger. On the gallery Pello let out a breath.

“You fixed her door for free,” he murmured.

“Don’t.”

Below, the orchestra changed its tune. They had spent more time in the linen room than she had allowed for every repair upstairs. Briska gave the copied inscription to Ilaro as she passed his quiet chair, then went to the next frame and made herself work at a believable pace.

Chapter 7 — The woman in the other frame

Ilaro had found a chair behind an ugly marble shepherd.

The shepherd held a flute to a mouth too small to accommodate it. From the hall below came the real music, dulled here by a heavy curtain and a fortunate turn in the gallery. Ilaro could see the western stair without being seen from most of it. He had put his notebook on his knee and a glass of water on the shepherd’s plinth.

Orset had left him three sale catalogs and a list of pictures Caspian might consider parting with. The list omitted the good ones. Ilaro knew very little about painting, but the good ones had their prices written lightly in pencil where somebody could alter them before a buyer saw.

He compared the paper Briska had given him with the oldest catalog.

They came for you. A gift to a performer, he thought. The date might identify a season, perhaps a first appearance. Caspian liked women who could bring a room to life. He also liked to own a significant part of the room afterward.

Pello came along the gallery carrying a folded dust sheet.

Ilaro lifted a hand. Pello slowed, then stopped when Ilaro found his voice.

“A moment. Tell me about the picture in the office.”

Pello laid the sheet over the marble shepherd, hiding its tiny face. The improvement was considerable.

“Woman. Black dress. It weighs more than it looks.”

“What else?”

“White flower. Hands crossed.”

“A musician’s hands?”

“What do those look like?”

“Never mind. A ring?”

“Wedding ring. High on the finger. Same as the woman downstairs.”

Ilaro looked up. “Which woman downstairs?”

“In red, above the stair. The picture with all the flowers under it. People keep saying how much she’s missed.”

The public portrait had occupied his left side when they entered. The chandeliers had been on his right. He remembered watching the light break across the spectacles of the man showing him in. He could not remember the painting at all.

“The same woman?”

“Same knuckles. Painter made her prettier downstairs. Didn’t fix the hands.”

Ilaro crossed out the word performer.

“His wife,” he said.

Pello nodded.

“The portrait on the cellar door is his wife. The private words might be these.” He showed Pello the line.

Pello read it. “Looks like he lost an argument.”

“Possibly he enjoyed losing that one.”

From below came applause, followed by a burst of laughter that had Daska somewhere in it. Ilaro listened until the next tune began. He turned the copied date toward the light and checked it against the catalog. The first portrait preceded the second by eleven years. The earlier commission had been paid by Caspian personally, before there was an Amber Villa to decorate.

No cryptic poem. No elaborately concealed declaration. A husband had scratched something on the back of his wife’s picture where only people carrying it would see.

“We should check the writing,” he said. “Against a letter in the office.”

“Briska’s copied it.”

“She copied the words. I need to know whose hand put them there.”

Pello took the paper back. “I’ll tell her.”

“And don’t conclude it’s the answer until we’ve tried it.”

“You want me to tell her it might not work?”

“She knows that already. Tell Rosceline.”

When Pello had gone, Ilaro reached for his water and put his fingers into the glass. He stared at them, irritated beyond proportion. He had known where it was. He had just watched himself reach a little too far and failed to stop.

He dried his hand on the inside of his coat.

A man in a gilded half-mask passed the shepherd and glanced at the covered face. “Restoration?”

“Mercy,” Ilaro said.

The man laughed as though understanding something expensive and continued toward the private rooms.

Ilaro shut his eyes. The dark behind them throbbed. Somewhere below, a glass broke and people cheered the person who had broken it. He thought of his old office on a wet afternoon, the little brass bell above the door, his partner telling him there was someone he ought to meet. The memory stopped before the steps. It always approached the steps as though there might be another route.

“Here.”

He opened his eyes. Daska stood beside him holding two pastries in a napkin.

“I thought you’d gone home,” she said.

“I thought about it.”

“Pello would send for you.”

Ilaro shifted the notebook to make room for the food.

She put a pastry on the notebook. It left a transparent spot of fat beside his reconstructed times. Her own was gone before he had decided whether to object.

“You were good down there,” he said.

“I am good.”

“I meant—”

“I know. Thank you.”

She sat on the floor beside the shepherd, stretching one leg out of sight behind the curtain. There was a red mark above her ankle where the shoe rubbed.

“The little man with the green sash thought he could lift me.”

“Could he?”

“I let him get far enough to find out.”

Ilaro bit the pastry. Pepper, crab and something sweet he could not identify. He had not been hungry until the first mouthful. Daska watched the stair while he ate.

“Hessel?” he asked.

“At his station. He saw me.”

“Did he signal?”

“He scratched his ear.”

“That was the signal?”

“It wasn’t a very convincing itch.”

He nodded and turned to the next page. The small facts were easier with someone sitting beside him who did not require a conclusion every time he wrote one down.

“Which hand does Orset use for the keys?”

“Right.”

“And then?”

“Puts them in his left pocket. He has a ring on that hand. Big green stone. It catches if he tries to reach in with it.”

Ilaro looked at her.

“I was waiting for a door,” she said. “There wasn’t much else to watch.”

He wrote it down. Briska had the cellar key. They still needed the office open again after the repairs were finished, and the steward’s pocket would matter.

The music stopped. For a moment they could hear individual voices below, the shift of serving dishes, a woman’s tired cough. Ilaro let the quiet enter his head.

“Say the pocket again.”

Daska did. Then she sat without speaking until the musicians found the next tune.

When she rose, she took the napkin with the crumbs in it.

“Your white bean’s on the floor.”

He looked down. It lay beside his shoe. He had brought it in his coat and must have dropped it with the notebook.

“Leave it.”

She picked it up anyway and put it in his palm.

“Keep it. You’ve brought it all this way.”

She went down the stair with the swing of a woman whose feet hurt and who would dance again as soon as they were asked. At the foot she spoke briefly to Pello, then caught Orset carrying an empty wine tray. The steward shook his head at her first invitation. She took the tray from him. At the second, he looked toward his employer and let himself be led onto the floor.

Pello collected the discarded tray, offered it to a passing servant and stepped behind Orset as Daska turned him. His hand disappeared under the steward’s coat for less than a beat. Orset was concentrating on his feet.

When Pello came upstairs, he put three small keys in Ilaro’s palm.

“Left pocket,” he murmured. “You can put them back.”

“I can’t.”

“Neither can I once he’s stopped dancing. We’ll leave them where he thinks he dropped them.”

Ilaro put the bean in his pocket and rose. The first key did not fit the office door. The second opened it without a sound.

Chapter 8 — A useful acquaintance

Rosceline found the salt merchant beside a painting of a storm.

He was explaining to a younger woman that water did not behave as the painter had represented it. The younger woman had adopted the inward, peaceful expression of a person planning to leave through a locked door.

“You must tell Lord Caspian,” Rosceline said. “He believes it an exceptional example.”

The merchant swelled. “It is an exceptional example. Of an artist’s ignorance.”

“How fortunate you are here.”

She touched the younger woman’s elbow as she passed. The woman needed no second offer. She slid away behind Rosceline and disappeared into a knot of guests.

Caspian stood near the flowers beneath his wife’s portrait. Rosceline brought the merchant to him as though presenting a rare animal that had performed an amusing trick.

“My lord, you must settle something before I lose all confidence in my judgment. Master Deren has an objection to your sea.”

“Only its movement,” the merchant said. “The color is admirable.”

Caspian looked from him to Rosceline. Something in his face sharpened, then relaxed.

“Deren has spent more time looking at water than most admirals,” he said. “We should listen.”

They went to the painting. It was on the wall farthest from the eastern service passage. Deren began with the direction of the wind. Rosceline stood where Caspian would have to turn his back on the service door to answer her. Across the hall, Pello took a tray from a passing servant and walked upstairs under its authority.

The sea in the picture rose black and green beneath a torn sky. A boat clung to the flank of a wave. Rosceline liked it. She had been on water like that, with a man beside her praying to a patron he had not mentioned before the weather changed. The painter had understood the terrible height of the water when the boat fell away beneath one’s feet. She could not have said whether the foam was correct.

“You have something to add,” Caspian said to her.

“I like the little boat.”

“It is badly placed,” Deren said.

“The people in it appear to agree.”

Caspian laughed. Deren reconsidered whether he had been invited into an argument he could win.

The host gave him a gracious retreat by asking about a shipment. Deren followed it. A minute later Caspian had passed him into the care of a man who needed salt, leaving Rosceline beside the sea.

“That was unkind,” he said.

“He was enjoying himself.”

“I meant to me.”

She turned. “I wanted to see whether you would defend it.”

“Did I?”

“You defended the evening. The painting can fend for itself.”

He moved a little closer. Enough that the warmth from his body entered the cool air near the stone wall.

“Wren has been keeping you for her own amusement.”

“Wren would be very disappointed to learn that she has failed.”

“What do you buy?”

“Paintings, mostly. People tell me where they mean to hang one and what they mean their guests to think of them. The second is rather more useful.”

“And when they want something they ought not?”

“I charge more for the conversation.”

He looked at the slit in her dress without pretending not to. She let him. His attention was exact and practiced; it might have been enjoyable in a man who had not spent the afternoon threatening a singer’s rooms.

“There are people I could introduce you to,” he said. “People who would stop wasting your time with small commissions.”

“I have some affection for small commissions.”

“Surely not for the pay.”

“Sometimes for the person paying.”

He reached for a glass from a servant’s tray and held it out to her. The stem was warm where his fingers had rested.

“Then allow me to become one of them.”

On the last word, something moved beneath his voice.

Rosceline felt it as a note heard through a wall: too low to name, close enough to unsettle her teeth. Warmth rose at the back of her neck. For a moment the room seemed to have arranged itself sensibly around this man, and she wanted to stay where he could see her.

She set the untouched glass on the window ledge.

Sarem had made her listen to a kettle once. Nine kettles, eventually, until she could distinguish the boiling from the little working that kept one hot after the fire was dead. She had been furious with him by the sixth. Now she listened beneath Caspian’s easy breath and heard the tail of the same kind of intrusion, finer and directed at her.

“You make introductions sound intimate,” she said.

“They are.”

He did not push. That was well done. A foolish man would have reached for her again and given her a reason to withdraw. Caspian let the invitation lie between them, warm and apparently voluntary.

She wanted to cut his hand. Not badly. Just enough to spoil his confidence in the room. The desire was so sudden that she had to rest her own hand on the window ledge until it passed.

“And what do your friends do for you?”

“Remember who made the introduction.”

“For how long?”

“As long as it remains useful.”

“To which of you?”

He smiled. “Now we are discussing the same thing.”

A servant approached with a question about the supper wine. Caspian excused himself for a moment. Rosceline used it to look toward the musicians’ platform.

The porcelain mask was gone. The empty chair beside it was now occupied by an older woman eating a peach over her plate. Juice shone on her thumb. Nothing marked where the masked guest had sat.

“Someone you know?” Caspian asked on returning.

“I thought so.”

“There are always unexpected acquaintances at my parties.”

“Do you invite them?”

“Eventually.”

He offered his arm. She took it because the passage she needed was on the other side of the room and because refusing now would have told him something he had not earned. He led her beneath the portrait of the woman in red.

“Your wife?”

“Yes.”

“The flowers suit her.”

“She hated those flowers.”

Rosceline looked at him.

“She said they smelled of funerals. The household keeps replacing them. I have stopped explaining.”

There was no working under this. His voice had gone flat in a place charm could not cover.

“What did she like?”

“Things that were growing somewhere else. Preferably where they inconvenienced the gardener.”

He looked at the portrait long enough for the silence to become private. Then a man hailed him from beside the supper tables, and he turned with his public face already in place.

“You will save me a dance,” he said.

“Have one.”

She watched him go. The lingering warmth at her neck had become an unpleasant dampness. She rubbed it away with the inside of her wrist.

“He works on people,” she said quietly.

“His company, with a little magic to make sure I wanted it.”

“Then you should read this.”

Part III — The book comes out

Chapter 9 — The part she did not mention

The letter lay beneath an unfinished invitation on Caspian’s desk.

Ilaro had found it while looking for a sample of the host’s writing. He had expected a signature, perhaps an insulting correction on a bill. Instead he found the same hand that had written They came for you telling the Countess to stop interfering with his tenants.

Rosceline read without touching the paper.

“That line,” Ilaro said.

“I can see it.”

Livaea has expressed no objection to my arrangements. You write as though She had appointed you to supervise Her admirers.

Below that, Caspian had begun a sentence about the singer Letha, crossed out a phrase and tried again. Whatever courtesy he meant to put over the demand had not yet satisfied him.

“He hasn’t sent it,” Rosceline said.

“No. But she has sent him something. He’s answering it.”

“The Countess has a wide correspondence.”

“Don’t do that.”

She looked at him.

“I know how a letter works,” he said. “We asked about the protection. She described a portrait.”

Rosceline turned toward the door. The music sounded muffled here, a beat under the boards. A servant’s footsteps passed in the gallery and went away.

“She gave us the invitation.”

“For a claim she couldn’t identify.” He paused. “She—she knows more than she told us.”

He watched her read the page again. Her face had a way of staying pleasantly available while her attention moved somewhere else. He had once watched a banker talk into that expression for twenty minutes and emerge with an investment in a quarry that had no stone.

“Did he try it on you?” Ilaro asked.

“A little warmth. A pleasant interest in staying.”

“Are you still interested?”

“In taking his book.”

“That wasn’t what—” He stopped. The objection was becoming a habit. She waited, and he made himself finish differently. “I need to know whether you’re angry enough to make this harder.”

“Probably.”

It was a better answer than he had expected.

Pello appeared at the door carrying the empty wine tray.

“Orset’s looking for his keys.”

Ilaro took the ring from the desk and put it in Pello’s palm. “By the wine cooler. Where you took the tray.”

“He’s looking upstairs.”

“He’ll come down again. Leave them there.”

Pello glanced at the letter. “Are we in trouble?”

“We have a choice. Get Briska and Daska.”

“All of us in here?”

“One brief repair conference. She’ll know what to say.”

Briska arrived with her small bag and a strip of gilt molding. Daska followed carrying a basin of water. Pello kept the tray. They looked, Ilaro thought, like a group of people urgently failing to mend an expensive object. He moved the damaged glass bird to the desk’s edge to complete the appearance.

Briska read the letter and pressed her tongue against a back tooth.

“The invitation,” she said. “She had it ready.”

“Yes.”

“So she knew enough to bring a spare part.”

Daska looked toward Rosceline. “What does he do?”

“Makes his company seem like a better idea than it is.”

“Can he stop us leaving?”

“I don’t know.”

No one spoke for a moment. The orchestra struck a chord that vibrated through the basin Daska held.

“We can go,” Ilaro said. “We haven’t taken anything that matters yet. Hessel can meet us at the wicket. We give back the advance we haven’t spent.”

Pello’s fingers tightened on the tray.

“I’d have to pay my cousin.”

“Yes.”

Pello looked down at the tray. He said nothing more about his cousin.

Briska opened the side of her bag and checked the cream envelope. She did not take it out. “Or we find the book, see what actually holds it and decide there. Nobody offers anyone else a promise.”

“Nobody offers me one either,” Daska said.

Rosceline leaned against the desk. “We have the key. We have a likely phrase for the portrait. The man is downstairs making people grateful. I’d like him to have a disappointing evening.”

“I’d like the money,” Pello said. “I’m saying it in case we all start having better reasons.”

Daska set the basin down. Water trembled against its rim.

“We get Hessel out whether we take the book or not.”

“Yes,” Ilaro said.

Briska nodded. Pello looked offended that it needed repeating.

Rosceline said, “Of course.”

Ilaro studied her. Nothing in the answer invited an objection he could name.

He wanted the money too. There was an unpaid quarter on his rooms, and the woman who rented them to him had begun leaving his letters on the landing instead of sliding them under the door. Two hundred marks would let him stop apologizing for taking up space in a room he had once paid for without thought.

“We continue,” he said. “We stop if the thing on the book asks for more than the Countess has offered.”

“We ask what it means by more,” Rosceline said.

Ilaro kept his eyes on her. “Before anyone agrees to anything.”

That brought a smile, real enough to be irritating.

They separated by the routes that gave each an excuse to be seen. Pello took the key ring down toward the wine cooler. Daska returned the basin to the linen room. Briska asked Orset, loudly, whether the other contractor had been paid by the screw. The steward began explaining that he had inherited several unsatisfactory arrangements.

Rosceline stayed a moment. Ilaro returned the letter beneath the unfinished invitation and set the little glass bird exactly where its dusty outline showed it had stood.

“You could wait on the boat,” she said.

“And have you tell me afterward what happened?”

“I would make it bearable.”

“That’s what worries me.”

She left him with the smallest inclination of her head.

He stood alone in the office until the corridor cleared. His headache had become a narrow pressure behind the eye. He took a little bottle from his inside pocket, drank water that tasted of the leather stopper and put it away. He checked his notebook for the supper bell, the wine, the first boarding call. Three entries. He could keep three entries in order.

Downstairs the servants were removing the largest dishes. The smell of cooked fish had thickened in the heat. Pello stood near the service door, pretending to be denied another cup. As Ilaro passed, the younger man showed him an empty palm: keys returned.

Hessel waited inside the passage. He had turned his livery the proper way out and looked older in it.

“Five minutes,” he whispered. “The special wine’s behind the ordinary wine. I’ll have trouble.”

“Make it six,” Ilaro said.

Hessel looked at his face and, without asking anything else, nodded.

Chapter 10 — They came for you

The service stair held the day’s cold beneath the house.

Rosceline went down with her gloved hand on the rail. The noise of the gala diminished one turn at a time. Behind her, Briska’s tools gave a small metal click. Ilaro descended more slowly, testing the last edge of each tread with his shoe. Daska remained at the top to watch the passage. Pello had gone to check the boat.

At the foot, a corridor ran past shelves of empty wine baskets toward a single door. A lantern hung in a niche halfway along it. The flame shivered as though someone had breathed across the glass.

Rosceline stopped.

“Draft?” Briska whispered.

“Possibly.”

She listened. Somewhere behind the wall water moved through a pipe. Somewhere above, people laughed. Under those ordinary sounds she could hear the faint, repeated catch of a working sustaining itself. It had the irritating intimacy of a stranger humming on the other side of a thin partition.

The portrait occupied most of the door. A narrow iron band framed the painted woman, dressed here in blue, with one hand resting on the back of a chair. The wedding ring was high on her finger. Her other hand held nothing, palm half-open as though somebody had just left it.

“There she is,” Ilaro said.

Briska examined the lock below the frame. “Wait.”

She took the duplicate from her bag’s side pocket and wiped it on the cloth she carried. The teeth entered cleanly. At the turn there was resistance. Briska eased it back, changed the pressure and turned again.

A bolt withdrew inside the door with a satisfying, entirely human noise.

“Don’t touch it yet.”

Rosceline had not moved. She was watching the painted hand.

There was a small dark mark where a ring might once have been on the other finger. The painter had put it in. Caspian’s wife had possessed two rings, perhaps, or changed which one she wore. A pointless, particular detail. Rosceline felt the urge to ask what the woman had sounded like before remembering that nobody in the corridor could tell her.

“They came for you,” she said.

The lantern flame bent toward the door.

Nothing else happened.

Briska glanced at Ilaro. He kept his eyes on the portrait.

Rosceline stepped nearer. She had spoken as though giving a servant the correct instruction. It had sounded like that even to her.

She thought of the young portrait in the office, the line hidden behind it, and Caspian saying his wife had preferred flowers growing where they inconvenienced the gardener. She looked at the painted face.

“They came for you,” she said again, softly, with the smallest resentment at being reminded.

The woman’s gaze passed over her shoulder.

It could have been a change in the light. Rosceline did not turn to check. Under the paint came a click like a nail lifted from a table, and the door shifted against the seal of the frame.

Briska pulled it outward.

Cold air touched Rosceline’s mouth. It smelled of cedar, old perfume and metal that had been handled in a close room.

“Well,” Ilaro murmured.

Rosceline could not have said whether the working had needed her tone or had merely taken its time. She disliked that she wanted to know. There were more useful questions inside.

The vault was not large. Three cabinets stood against the walls, their narrow fronts inlaid with ivory flowers. Shelves held boxes, rolls of paper and bundles of letters tied in ribbons whose colors had faded unevenly. A blue silk slipper lay by itself beneath a glass dome. There was a child’s silver cup blackened inside, and a pair of jeweled combs joined by a chain fine enough to break between two fingers.

She had seen rooms like this before. Men called them strongrooms and filled them with things that made other people weak.

The book rested in an iron cradle on a waist-high table at the far wall. Brown leather, brass corners, red across the spine. Somebody had repaired the lower corner with a piece of darker hide. The clasp was shut.

Briska caught Rosceline’s wrist before she reached for it.

“Let me check for a trip under the cradle.”

Rosceline withdrew her hand. “Of course. I should hate to steal something improperly after all the trouble we’ve taken to get here.”

Briska knelt to examine the cradle. Ilaro stayed by the door, leaving it clear. Rosceline looked at the boxes. One had a false bottom so badly fitted that it annoyed her. Another had a surface of hammered gold showing tiny boats with too many oars. She could have put it beneath her coat. She resisted, with a feeling of being asked to exhibit moderation in a sweetshop.

“No wire,” Briska said. “No plate under it. The clasp may be something.”

Rosceline listened again. The humming had gone very thin. It seemed to come from the back of her teeth now, where no room should have been able to put it.

“We can leave it,” Ilaro said.

“We haven’t picked it up.”

“I am aware.”

Briska stood. “I’ll watch the cradle. Lift straight.”

Rosceline put one hand under each end of the book. The leather was cool. It had the weight of a thing written in every day, pages swollen slightly near the spine. She lifted.

The clasp burned the heel of her left hand.

She nearly dropped it. Briska’s hands came under hers; Rosceline twisted her wrist and kept the hot brass away from her palm. Pain ran through the glove into the flesh below her thumb.

“Hold it,” Briska said.

“I am holding it.”

A red line drew itself through the air from the clasp to the door. Thin as a hair, it stretched without sagging. Where it crossed the threshold, a bead of light gathered and swelled.

Ilaro moved aside.

From the corridor came a footstep.

The person in the porcelain mask stood beyond the open door. Cream glaze, black eye openings, a coat the color of dried wine. One gloved hand rested on the iron doorframe. The flame in the niche behind it grew steady.

“Whose hands are those?” the Collector asked.

The voice was close enough that Rosceline felt herself prepare to turn toward it, although the speaker stood several paces away.

She kept her grip on the book.

“Mine.”

“And who will miss them?”

The brass grew hotter.

Chapter 11 — One place at supper

Briska could smell the glove scorching.

She took the folded cloth from her bag, opened it between the hot clasp and Rosceline’s hand, and helped her change her grip. The red line moved with the book. It did not pass through the cloth. It passed through the space the cloth had occupied a moment before, as though it were always arriving slightly late.

“Can you put it down?” she asked.

Rosceline lowered it toward the cradle. The line tightened. Heat prickled Briska’s bare wrist.

“Apparently not.”

The Collector tilted its head.

“Would returning it undo your arrival?”

“We don’t need it to undo anything,” Briska said. “We need to know what holds it.”

“Did you think Lord Caspian paid for everything with silver?”

Ilaro drew a slow breath. Briska knew the sound by now. He was setting aside an answer that would satisfy him and make the situation worse.

“The book is pledged,” he said. “To whom?”

The Collector turned the mask toward him.

“Did you read his letter?”

“Yes.”

“Then do you require another name?”

There was a faint scratch inside the wall. Briska looked at the doorframe. The glove resting on it left a warm, dark print on cold iron.

“If we cross?” she asked.

“Would you like every door in this house to know you?”

Rosceline shifted the weight of the ledger. “I’d prefer a smaller acquaintance.”

Her voice had become too light. Briska looked at her glove, where a dark oval was spreading over the palm. This was not a good place for Rosceline to enjoy herself.

“Something taken can be replaced,” Ilaro said. “Is that what you’re offering?”

“What would you offer?”

Briska opened her bag with her free hand. The cream envelope lay inside its leather folder, exactly where she had left it after checking the seal upstairs. She took it out and held it by the corner.

The Collector’s mask turned before the envelope had cleared the bag.

“Whose hospitality?” it asked.

Rosceline answered with the Countess’s title. For an instant Briska smelled something strange and familiar: hot stone after a forge had been closed, with a particular bitter oil used on mountain tools. Her parent had smelled of it on visits. It made her want to stand straighter, an old bodily obedience she hated.

She lowered the envelope to the threshold instead of handing it over.

“Read it.”

“Would you read it to me?”

“The seal is intact. You can open it.”

“And you will stand by what it says?”

“She stands by what it says. We’re delivering it.”

The Collector’s hand left the doorframe. Briska could not see the warm print now, though she had watched it appear. The gloved fingers picked up the invitation.

The red wax broke without a sound.

Somewhere above, a drum gave three quick beats. A familiar tune began. Briska recognized it from Daska’s rehearsal and felt the loss of time in her stomach. The first boarding call would come during this dance.

The Collector unfolded the paper.

“One evening?”

“One,” Ilaro said. “A named guest. The Countess chooses the evening. Before the season ends.”

“And will the guest be welcome?”

“Received,” Briska said. “That’s what she offered.”

“Is there a difference?”

“There is in my house.”

The mask held still. The bitter oil scent faded. In its place came roses too long in water, sweet over rot.

Rosceline’s breathing changed. Briska saw her looking past the mask toward the dark corridor, her attention briefly caught by something nobody else had heard.

“Rosceline.”

The woman looked back.

“Keep it level.”

“I’m doing that.”

“Keep doing it.”

Ilaro took his notebook out. His hands were steady enough until he tried to find the pencil.

“The name,” he said. “We will need to report it.”

The Collector gave one. “Would Mistress Ossene find a place at that table?”

Ilaro wrote it down and repeated it. Briska did not know the name. Rosceline’s expression told her nothing useful.

“That is within the offer,” Ilaro said.

“Then what remains between us?”

Briska pointed at the red line.

“That.”

The Collector looked down at it, almost curiously. Then the gloved hand reached past the doorframe and touched the line with one finger.

The bead at the threshold opened like a wet mouth.

Briska’s stomach lurched. She had time to think of the invitation being consumed, of something much larger pulling at a very small opening. The line snapped back into the clasp. The heat vanished so completely that Rosceline made a sharp, involuntary sound.

Briska did not move immediately. She took the little strip of steel from her bag and passed it through the place where the red line had met the threshold. Nothing happened. She moved the ledger a hand’s breadth toward the door, supporting Rosceline’s burned hand.

No heat. No light.

“The clasp’s cold. It moves without pulling. That’s all I’ve checked.”

Ilaro opened the book before they wrapped it. The clasp, cold now, lifted under his nail. He found the date of a payment from the office papers and read the corresponding entry. Then another. His mouth tightened.

“That’s Deren,” he said.

Rosceline leaned over. “The sea expert?”

“Warehouse inspections. He pays to keep them predictable.”

“How sensible.”

“And the man with the gold sash paid to have a competitor’s boats held through a week of good weather.”

The Collector had gone.

Briska looked into the corridor. There was no place to have stepped aside except back toward the service stair. She had been watching the book. The ordinary explanation was available, and she took it for the length of the corridor.

At the door, she turned the duplicate key to withdraw it. The original bolt remained open. There was no useful reason to lock themselves out of a room they might have to return through. She put the key back in her bag and pulled the portrait door nearly closed.

Rosceline peeled off the damaged glove. A red patch shone below her thumb, with a pale center. She pressed it to the cool stone wall.

“You’ll want water,” Briska said.

“I’ll want a great many things.”

From above came the low, unmistakable sound of a horn.

One long note, a pause, then another.

Rosceline took her hand off the wall.

“Which call was that?”

Briska listened to the small sounds that followed: feet overhead, a door, a servant shouting for a basket to be moved.

“Second, I think.”

Rosceline moved past her toward the stair with the book held hard against her ribs.

Chapter 12 — The wrong man, at the right time

Pello met them at the turn of the stair.

He caught Ilaro’s sleeve and pulled him against the wall. The movement struck the sore place behind Ilaro’s eye with a burst of light. He swallowed his first answer.

“Tell me.”

“They’ve got Hessel.”

Rosceline stopped two steps above them.

“Who?” Briska asked.

“Thorn. Two of hers. He’s in the guardroom.”

“What happened?” she asked.

“You saw the note?” Ilaro said.

“I saw her open it. Red ribbon.”

Rosceline looked toward the door above.

Ilaro waited for the next fact. Instead, he found himself looking at her.

“What was in it?” he asked.

Pello shook his head. “I couldn’t—”

“Rosceline.”

She met his eyes. She had taken the mask off. A little color had run where it touched her cheek.

“His name. The keys. Money they could find.”

“You sent it?” Daska said.

“After we should have left.”

“We haven’t left.”

“Yes, Daska. I can see that.”

The monk moved one step down. Briska put her bag on the stair between them, as though the space had suddenly acquired work to do.

“Thorn told him fingers would be useful for writing it down. He didn’t like that. He’s back in the hall now.”

“I meant to leave one who would be beyond their reach.”

“You didn’t ask us.”

“You would have made it a discussion.”

There had been a room behind a wine shop, years ago, where another person had told him everything was arranged. He could remember the wet rim of a glass on the table. “The wicket. She’s checking it.”

“I didn’t promise a good man a boat.”

“All of them.”

Part IV — A departure worth noticing

Chapter 13 — The promised dance

The musicians had begun putting down their instruments.

Rosceline went to the platform before they could finish. The leader was loosening the horsehair on his bow. Sweat had flattened the curls beside his ears. He looked at her with the hostility of a working man offered an opportunity to work longer.

“We have played the last.”

“Then Lord Caspian should have begun earlier.”

She liked him. She put a gold piece on the edge of his music stand.

“Something slow enough for him.”

The leader looked at the coin, then at Caspian across the hall. He tightened his bow again.

“The summer air?”

“Perfect.”

She did not know it. That was a problem for the next minute.

The first violin rose above the conversations. A woman near the platform clapped her hands in delight. Her companions joined her. Rosceline turned toward Caspian with her arm extended, inviting the whole room to notice that she was waiting.

He crossed to her smiling.

“I feared you had escaped me.”

“Not yet.”

He took her right hand. She moved close enough that only he could see the corner of the oilcloth beneath her wrap, and drew it aside just far enough to show the red band on brown leather.

“Deren,” she said softly. “Predictable inspections. An admirable investment.”

The salt merchant was three paces away, settling himself beside a woman who had begun to admire his knowledge of the sea.

“You misunderstand what you are holding.”

“Then explain it while we dance.”

The room had made a space for them. Refusal would have required more words than he wished to provide. He set his hand at her waist. She put her burned left hand on his shoulder and let the first movement tell her how the air went.

He was a good dancer. She had expected him to be a confident one; the distinction troubled her. His lead was exact, and he adjusted to the weight she was carrying without breaking the rhythm. Around them the chandeliers divided into bright streaks as they turned.

“The Countess.”

She smiled.

“Has she?”

“Her people take risks she has ceased taking herself.”

“Most employers develop the habit.”

“The night air is unkind to the flowers.”

She looked at him properly.

“Marks,” he said. “Tonight. A room near the galleries, with your name above the door. Introductions to people who can afford your taste. Keep the Countess’s advance. Tell her you failed.”

The offer had size. She saw the room before she chose not to: good north light, a locked cabinet, enough money to refuse tiresome men before hearing the rest of what they wanted. She could have kept the purchasing identity for another year, perhaps two. She was fond of it.

Across the hall, Thorn sent two guards toward the kitchen and disappeared into the service passage herself.

“I have associates,” Rosceline said.

“You can pay them.”

“You haven’t seen their expenses.”

“Then leave them to the Countess. She will find a use.”

He said it kindly. As a practical reassurance. Rosceline thought of Daska’s earrings on the workshop bench, Ilaro lowering himself toward the boat with his eyes shut, Briska wiping at a kiss that had not touched her cheek.

She stepped harder than she needed to on Caspian’s foot.

He breathed through his nose. “A miscalculation?”

“A narrow passage.”

“This floor is twenty paces across.”

“You keep steering me toward the same place.”

The music lifted. She let him turn her beneath his arm. Pain caught in her left hand as the fingers opened; she completed the movement with the right and returned close enough for him to smell the smoke on her glove.

His eyes dropped to it.

“You met the keeper.”

“A person of few statements.”

“What did you offer?”

“Something that was accepted.”

For the first time his composure broke without permission. Anger drew the skin tight around his mouth. He had known the book was taken. He had not known it could be taken cleanly enough to carry beyond the protections of the house.

“She gave you an invitation.”

Rosceline said nothing.

“Who?”

“You would have to ask her.”

He laughed once, without pleasure. “You don’t even know whose company you have sold her.”

“She named the price.”

“Yes. She does that very well.”

They passed beneath his wife’s portrait. The flowers smelled suddenly unbearable, hot and thick as breath under a cover. Rosceline shifted the book against her side. She had thought it would be easier to carry once the magic let go of it.

“Give it to me,” Caspian said. “We walk to the study. You receive your money. The people at the doors continue believing you are a welcome guest.”

“And Hessel?”

The name changed the conversation again. She regretted it as soon as she heard herself say it.

“Ah.”

“He leaves.”

“So that was your arrangement.”

“It is now.”

“Thorn thinks he has been selling access. She will establish the truth.”

“She may already have enough of it.”

“Then he should have been more careful with his employers.”

Rosceline held his gaze. The violin repeated the first phrase. It was a long air, or the leader had understood what the gold piece was paying for.

She did not know how long the guardroom would take. She knew how many people Thorn had sent to the canal, and that there would be no returning to the old plan now. She had the book. She could still offer it, take the money, name the loading door and step away from the rest.

Caspian felt the change in her attention and smiled.

“There,” he murmured. “You see the practical course.”

She smiled back so convincingly that his shoulders eased.

“My lord, I’ve been looking at it all evening.”

The next turn carried them past the musicians. Rosceline caught the leader’s eye and moved one finger. Once more.

He looked at the dance floor, then at the gold still lying on his stand. His bow bit harder into the strings.

Chapter 14 — The man they promised

The guardroom door had been repaired by somebody who understood doors.

Briska could have hated him for it. A new strip of oak ran down the latch side, closely fitted and pegged from within. She slipped the jaws of the spreader into the gap, turned the screw a quarter revolution and felt the little housing begin to bow before the wood gave anything at all.

She stopped.

Daska stood over her, barefoot on the warm stone of the passage. She had taken her amber mask off. Her face without it looked larger and less cheerful.

“How long?”

“This won’t do it.”

“You said—”

“The jamb is pegged into the frame. It won’t spread. I need him to open it.”

Briska backed the screw out and took the damaged tool away. The steel jaws had left two shallow marks near the catch. She covered them with her hand while she considered the gap.

From inside came the scrape of a bench.

“Hands where I can see them,” a man said.

Daska leaned toward the barred opening above the door’s center. It was shuttered on the inside. She could hear better than Briska. Her jaw moved once.

Pello waited at the passage bend. Farther back, Ilaro was speaking to the patrolling guard in the low, abrasive voice he used with people who had omitted the important part of an account.

“Orset has found them. Now he’s saying someone in a guard’s coat took them upstairs. Mistress Thorn will want to know which coat.”

The guard swore. “They were under the tray.”

“Tell him that. He isn’t telling it that way.”

Boots moved away toward the hall.

Ilaro joined them. He had acquired a small tray with two mugs and a jug of something dark enough to be coffee. Steam rose from the spout.

“The kitchen,” he said. “They were meant for the men by the gate.”

Daska took the tray. “They’ll live.”

Briska removed the thin shim from her kit. She slid it between the latch and the jamb. The repaired wood allowed almost no space, but the metal passed. It would not lift the catch against a hand holding the door; it could keep the catch from falling fully home once the door had opened.

“Get him to look out,” she said.

Daska knocked with the tray’s edge.

“Coffee. Mistress Thorn says keep him awake.”

The shutter moved. An eye appeared in the opening, followed by a portion of a nose that had once been broken badly.

“We didn’t ask.”

“Neither did I. Hold the tray or I put it on the floor.”

The eye considered her. Briska crouched close to the wall, out of its angle, one hand on the shim.

The shutter closed. A bolt drew back.

The door opened the width of a man’s shoulder. He had a short blade in his right hand, held low. His left reached for the tray.

Daska let him take the weight. Then she pushed.

Coffee burst across his coat. The tray struck his chest and turned sideways. His blade came up; Daska stepped inside the arm, shoulder driving beneath his chin, and the edge opened a red line along the outside of her right upper arm.

Briska shoved the door. The second guard threw himself against it from within. The good oak hit her shoulder and nearly put her on her back.

She drove the shim down into the latch gap. The door caught on it with a harsh metallic note. Not enough space. Enough to stop it closing.

“Pello!”

He struck the door beside her, both of them pushing. Inside, something heavy scraped the floor. A man’s knee appeared in the opening and folded at the wrong angle beneath a sliding bench.

Hessel had kicked it.

The second guard went down. Pello and Briska fell through the door together.

The room smelled of sweat, leather and the coffee spreading across the stone. A narrow window faced a wall too close to admit useful light. Hessel sat at the far end with his wrists tied in front of him by his own belt. One eye was swollen. He drew his legs back from the bench and kicked again as the fallen guard tried to rise.

Daska had the first man’s wrist against the jamb. His shoulder stood high, his body twisted below it. He was very strong and very frightened. He tried to pull away. She moved with him and drove the side of her hand into his jaw.

The noise was soft. His knees loosened.

She took his blade as it fell, struck him once more with the heel of her palm and caught enough of his weight to keep his skull from hitting the threshold. Blood ran from her arm onto his coat.

Pello had a knee on the second guard’s back. The man reached backward for his ankle. Briska hit the reaching hand with the end of her rule. He shouted and stopped reaching.

“Don’t make me do that again,” she said.

He tried to turn his head. Pello put more weight down.

Ilaro closed the door as far as the bent shim allowed and stood listening beside it.

Daska looked at the first guard. His lips were parted around blood and broken tooth. Her hand remained above him for a moment after there was any need to strike.

“Daska,” Briska said.

The monk blinked, put the stolen blade on the table and tore a strip from the man’s scarf.

“Turn him over. He’ll choke.”

They tied him on his side. The other guard cursed them until Hessel asked whether he wanted his mouth tied too. The guard looked at him and became quiet.

Pello cut Hessel’s belt away with his rigging knife. Hessel flexed his fingers. Both hands worked. He stared at them for a moment, then tried to stand too quickly and sat down again.

“Can you walk?” Briska asked.

“Yes.”

“Then don’t run yet.”

He looked at Daska’s bleeding arm.

“They did that?”

“Hold this.”

She gave him a folded cloth from the coffee tray and put his hand against the cut. Briska pulled another strip from the guard’s scarf and tied the pad above the muscle, tight enough to stay, loose enough not to stop her hand. Daska lifted and lowered the arm. Her expression changed, then settled.

“The boat?” Hessel asked.

“Still real,” Pello said. “Different door.”

Ilaro lifted a finger. Voices passed outside, too close. The patrolling guard had returned with Orset. They were arguing about keys and whether an accusation had actually been made. Briska held her breath. On the floor, the conscious captive drew a little air into his nose as though preparing to call.

Hessel knelt beside him and put the short blade flat against his cheek.

Nobody spoke until the voices had gone.

Then Hessel lifted the blade, laid it on the table and stood. He looked at Daska, perhaps waiting to be told what that had made him. She opened the door a finger’s breadth and checked the passage.

“Stairs.”

Pello took Hessel first. Ilaro followed with one hand near the wall. Briska stayed beside Daska, gathering the tools she could recover. The spreader went into the bag bent. The shim would not come free of the jamb.

She left it.

At the first turn of the service stair they heard the ballroom applaud. The violin began its phrase again, louder. Pello looked back from the landing above.

“She’s still dancing?”

Daska’s mouth opened in an unwilling, astonished grin. “She’d better be doing it well.”

He took Hessel toward the linen room. Briska watched him go, then heard Thorn’s voice from the passage below.

“Who opened that door?”

Daska moved to the narrowest part of the stair. She rolled her good shoulder once and put her hand out behind her.

“Briska. Something I can hit with.”

Briska gave her the short iron bar from her bag.

Chapter 15 — The host’s sleeve

Caspian’s eyes went past Rosceline toward the eastern passage.

She did not turn. She had learned enough from his face. Whatever was happening there had stopped being a private inconvenience.

“You can still leave with me,” he said.

The offer had lost its room above the galleries. There was something smaller and harder beneath it now.

“Where would you take me?”

“Somewhere we can finish this.”

“I think we’re nearly finished.”

She stepped away at the end of the measure. His hand stayed at her waist. She put her burned left hand over it, smiling for the people who had begun to applaud, and lifted his fingers one at a time.

“A lovely air,” she said, loudly. “Though I understand good weather can be held for a week if one pays the right men.”

The gold-sashed guest stopped applauding.

Rosceline looked directly at him.

“Your competitor’s boats. Seven mornings at the inspection quay. You must have been pleased with the result.”

The man beside him lowered his glass. He was, by luck or Caspian’s sense of entertainment, connected to the injured competitor. Rosceline saw the recognition and leaned into it.

“My lord keeps excellent records,” she said.

“You are mistaken,” Caspian told her.

“About the number of mornings?”

“About where you are.”

He reached beneath her wrap for the book.

She turned against the hand, keeping the ledger pressed to her ribs. Her right hand went under her left arm. Thumb to the release. The corrected catch gave at once. Steel ran clear of silk.

She put the point through Caspian’s embroidered cuff and into the edge of the serving table behind him.

The nearest woman screamed. Caspian jerked his arm. Rosceline twisted the blade free with a tearing rasp and stepped past him while he was still trying to pull his sleeve away from the table.

Wine went over. A servant dropped a tray of little glass bowls. They shattered so brightly beneath the chandeliers that for one second people seemed unsure whether the noise was part of the entertainment.

“Keep back,” Rosceline said.

She meant it. The rapier shook a little in her grip, and her left side had become clumsy with the book and the pain in her hand. She would cut anyone who made her choose in a hurry.

Caspian’s guard came between two guests with a short cudgel in his right hand. He saw the blade and stopped just outside its reach. Sensible man. He did not want her dead in the middle of the room; he wanted her arm damaged enough to stop holding it.

Rosceline backed toward the western stair.

“That book is stolen,” Caspian called.

“So was the week at the quay.”

The gold-sashed man told her to shut her mouth. Another voice demanded that Caspian explain. Deren, the sea expert, had begun making his way toward the entrance with the discreet urgency of a man whose warehouse was on fire somewhere else.

The guard lunged when Rosceline’s heel touched the first stair.

She took the cudgel on the strong part of her blade and felt the shock run to her shoulder. His left hand caught the wrap. He pulled. She drove her point at his face, forcing him to let go, and gained two steps.

He came after her before she could turn.

The cudgel struck her ribs just below the right breast. Pain emptied her. She could not breathe. The room narrowed to the iron taste at the back of her mouth and the man’s boots on the stair below.

He reached for the sword arm.

She cut downward across his forearm. The blade opened cloth and flesh. Blood spattered the white flowers tied to the rail. He shouted and stumbled into a guest trying to come down.

Rosceline tried to breathe again. Air entered in a thin, savage thread.

She climbed.

At the turn of the stair a great green swag hung from a cord looped around the balustrade. She had admired the fabric on entering. She cut the cord now, twice because the first stroke caught only half the fibers. The silk fell between her and the pursuing guard, folding over his head and the shoulders of the man behind him.

There were more shouts. Something heavy struck the rail.

She reached the upper gallery on her knees, got one foot under her and stood. The covered shepherd stood beside Ilaro’s empty chair. She caught its flute in passing, steadied herself and broke the tip off.

“Sorry,” she told it.

The little piece of marble remained in her left palm. She dropped it. Her hand would not close properly around anything except the book.

Across the gallery, light spilled from the eastern service stair. Metal rang against metal. She went toward it with the sword low and the wrap gripped against her side.

Daska stood at the top of the narrow flight. Blood had soaked through the pad on her right arm. She held Briska’s iron bar in her left hand, turning it to meet Thorn’s sword. Thorn was one step below, knees bent, point steady between them. A guard behind her tried to find room to pass and could not.

Rosceline had seen Daska fight in alleys where people supplied their own mistakes. Thorn supplied none. She advanced by the width of a foot, made Daska move, then took the ground that opened. Her blade touched the monk’s sleeve and came away redder.

Briska was at a washstand near the linen-room door, pulling a wide ceramic basin out of its wooden cradle.

“Move back!” she shouted.

Daska gave a step. Thorn followed.

Briska hurled the basin at the stone between them.

It burst with a hollow crack. Water slapped the walls. White fragments skidded across the landing and down the stair. Thorn’s leading foot slid; she caught the rail before the movement became a fall. The guard behind her swore as a shard struck his shin.

Daska retreated across the wet landing. Her bare heel came down on a broken edge. She made a short, furious sound and did not look at it.

Rosceline reached her side. Thorn looked up from the stair and saw the book, the sword and the blood on the green dress.

“Madame.”

“Mistress.”

“The boat is watched.”

“You’ve been very thorough.”

“You haven’t seen thorough.”

Briska caught Rosceline by the back of her dress and hauled her into the linen room. Daska came after them, kicking the door shut. The repaired hinge carried it smoothly into place. A blade struck the wood from the other side before the latch had finished falling.

“Something to hold the door,” Daska said, laying the short iron bar on a shelf.

There was none. Pello and Hessel dragged the freestanding linen rack across the floor. It was heavy with folded cloth, but one foot had rotted short at the bottom. The rack rocked as they jammed it against the door.

Daska braced her good shoulder beneath its crosspiece and drove her bare left foot against the unsteady leg. The door shuddered. The rack held.

“Go.”

Pello looked at her bleeding heel.

“Go, Pello.”

He ran to the loading door. Below, his cousin’s scow lay against the wall. The man looked up, saw the first person at the opening and spread both hands in a gesture so expressive Rosceline almost laughed. The attempt hurt enough to stop her.

Ilaro stood beside the hoist with the sling in his hands. He looked pale and very present.

“The book,” he said.

Rosceline clutched it tighter.

“Pello can carry it down without using his hands.”

Pello opened the cargo satchel slung across his back. Wide strap, close-fitting cover, no loose ornaments to catch a rope. She had watched him bring a stolen marble head down a drainpipe in it.

She took the wrapped ledger from beneath her wrap. For an instant she could feel the count of recoveries on a page in another house, the book she wanted still waiting there. Then she pushed the ledger into the satchel and saw Pello buckle it shut.

“It’s the real one,” she said.

“I know.”

“Don’t drop it.”

Pello pulled hard on the closed buckle, testing it, and turned to Hessel.

The door struck the rack again. Daska’s injured arm hung beside her, blood running toward her fingertips. She changed her stance without taking the foot from the broken leg.

Pello put Hessel into the sling. The guard’s hands worked clumsily around the rope.

“Let it carry you,” Pello said. “Don’t try to climb the wall.”

Hessel sat on the sill, looked down and went rigid.

Below, the cousin called, “You wanted a boat. Here it is.”

Hessel pushed away from the sill. The line took his weight and began to run through Pello’s hands.

Chapter 16 — A boat of sufficient size

Ilaro watched Hessel descend until the guard’s boots touched the scow.

Then he made himself look at the knot instead of the water.

Pello brought the sling back up. The door behind them thudded. Something in the linen rack cracked, followed by Daska telling it, with intense feeling, to stay where it was.

“You next,” Pello said.

Ilaro sat in the strap. He had practiced this. The difference was the dark water below and the woman behind him bleeding against the door.

“Lowering,” Pello said.

“All right.”

The sill went away beneath him.

For a moment he could see everything: the pale wall moving past his face, the canal black between reflected lanterns, a guard at the wicket turning his head. Then the rope swung and the lights smeared together. Nausea rose so sharply that he could not separate it from terror.

He closed his eyes.

“Keep coming,” the cousin called. “I’ve got you.”

Hands took his shoulders. His feet struck wood. He was in the boat, bent forward, trying to remember that the ground now moving beneath him was meant to move.

Hessel pulled the sling free. Ilaro crawled beneath the raised edge of the gunwale and held on to a thwart until his sight settled.

“Don’t be sick on the tools,” the cousin said.

Ilaro lifted his face.

“If I can choose, I’ll be sick on you.”

The man grinned and looked up again.

Pello threw the long free end of the lowering line down to his cousin, who passed it around a mooring ring to take the strain. With the hoist controlled from the boat, Pello came down third, the satchel tight across his back. Halfway, his cap fell off. It landed in the canal and floated away with stately indifference. He watched it go until his cousin shouted at him.

“Bought you that cap.”

“I know.”

“Your mother’ll ask.”

Pello looked once more toward the cap. It had gone into the dark beneath a moored barge.

Pello landed lightly. He checked the satchel’s buckle before unhooking the sling, and Ilaro felt a small relief. The book had reached the boat. They had something to show for being hunted.

Briska followed, using both hands on the rope and complaining under her breath about the angle of the loading beam. When she reached the deck she took the lowering line from the cousin, freeing him to handle the boat. She glanced at the tool frame but did not try to replace the pieces she had lost.

Above, Rosceline appeared on the sill.

She had tied the sword’s small sheath back over the point and thrust the weapon into its diagonal harness. Her green dress was dark on one side. Ilaro could see the effort it took her to sit, the way she kept her right elbow close to her ribs.

The sling rose to her. She fitted it around herself and looked back into the room.

Something struck the door hard enough to sound like the end of the rack.

“Rosceline!” Pello shouted.

She came off the sill. The line jerked, settled, and lowered her. Hessel caught her before she hit the gunwale. She swore into his shoulder with a richness that made him blink, then folded onto the deck beside Ilaro.

“How bad?” he asked.

“I don’t want to discuss it.”

“Breathing?”

“I’m doing it.”

“Keep doing it.”

She looked at him with genuine hatred for a moment. That reassured him more than a brave answer would have.

The sling rose for the last time.

Daska appeared at the loading door, one hand on the side of the frame. Behind her the linen rack went over with a roar of falling shelves and scattered cloth. She did not put the sling on. She caught the rope below it and slid, her feet braced against the wall.

At the wicket, a man shouted. A second leaned out with a bow.

“Push!” Pello said.

His cousin drove the scow off the wall with the long sweep. Hessel and Pello reached for Daska as the boat began to move. Her feet hit the outer gunwale; she lost her grip on the rope and went backward toward the water.

Pello caught her wrist. Hessel caught the back of her coat. Briska let the now-unweighted lowering line run free of the mooring ring.

For one appalling instant she hung with her weight pulling both men over the side. Briska threw herself across Pello’s legs. The cousin put his shoulder against the sweep and held the boat from turning.

Daska caught the gunwale with her good hand.

They hauled her in over it, hard. She landed on the tool frame, rolled off and lay with her mouth open, making no sound.

Above them Thorn reached the loading door. She took in the rope and the boat, then shouted to the men below.

Briska unfolded the tool frame and heaved its canvas-covered side against the gunwale. It rose between them and the wicket just as the bowman loosed.

The arrow tore through the canvas. It struck the thwart beside Ilaro’s knee with a noise like a hammer driven into green wood.

He stared at the shaft.

“That’s why we keep down,” the cousin said, and pulled.

Pello took a second oar. The scow swung into the traffic left in the guest barge’s path. A small boat full of laughing people shouted at them for crossing its bow. A woman threw a half-eaten peach. It struck the canvas beside the arrow hole and slid down in a wet yellow smear.

Daska made a sound.

Rosceline bent toward her. “What?”

“I hate that song.”

The orchestra was still playing inside the villa.

Ilaro looked back. The loading door had become a bright square above the canal. Thorn stood in it, one hand on the frame, watching. She had stopped shouting. That was worse. She was remembering.

The scow went beneath a low bridge. Stone passed a hand’s breadth above the raised canvas. Briska pulled it down before it caught. For a few strokes they were in complete dark, water slapping the arch with a noise too large for the narrow space.

Ilaro put his fingers against the arrow shaft to keep himself still. The wood trembled with the boat.

“Not the straight cut,” he said when they emerged. “The watch can get there from the road before us. North, then under the dyers’ walk.”

Pello shook his head. “Closed.”

“When?”

“This morning. Dredger across it. I saw the chain.”

Ilaro had walked past that canal. He remembered a woman washing a red skirt on the steps. He did not remember the dredger.

He closed his eyes for one breath and placed the routes against each other.

“South, then. Behind the charcoal sheds. If they think we’re following the barge they’ll send the first boat to the outer landing.”

“We’ll go to my cousin’s,” Pello said.

The man at the sweep gave him a look.

“Your other place,” Pello added.

“You do like spending things that are mine.”

Pello kept the oar in the water, waiting for his cousin to turn.

The cousin turned the scow south.

They passed under hanging lines of wet canvas, through a drift of fish scales glittering on the surface, and into a reach where the lamps were mostly out. The stink of charcoal dust and stale bilge replaced the villa’s perfume. Ilaro found himself grateful for it. He could name what was making him feel ill now.

Rosceline knelt beside Daska with the basin cloth pressed to the upper-arm wound. Briska was inspecting the cut in the monk’s heel. Hessel held the shaded lamp for her. Its light showed a thin white splinter embedded in the flesh.

“Don’t pull it until we stop moving,” Ilaro said.

Briska looked up. “I wasn’t going to.”

He nodded and stopped offering advice he had not been asked for.

They reached a shuttered boat shed on a side canal. The cousin let the scow drift the last few yards, caught a ring in the stone with the boathook and drew them under the overhang. Pello climbed out first and lifted the shed’s loose lower shutter from inside. There was a bench, a pile of nets and the dry smell of tarred rope.

Hessel went in to hold the lamp. Briska followed with her bag. Together they helped Daska across the landing. Rosceline tried to stand without assistance and had to wait, gripping the gunwale, until the pain allowed it.

Pello came back for the satchel. He opened it in the lamplight, took out the oilcloth bundle and handed it directly to Rosceline.

“Still real,” he said.

She looked at the knot, then at him.

“Thank you.”

He did not answer. He went to help his cousin pull the boat deeper into the shadow.

Ilaro sat on the landing with his feet inside the scow. He had meant to be last out and now could not quite remember the movements needed to accomplish it. Hessel came back, offered an arm and waited without urging him.

He took it.

Inside the shed Daska was telling Briska to stop saying it would hurt and remove the bloody piece. Briska did. The monk’s fist struck the bench hard enough to shake the lamp in Hessel’s hand.

Part V — What they keep

Chapter 17 — Paid in full

Hessel’s hands shook while he counted the money.

He had said they were sound. They were sound enough to count, though he had to begin twice. Pello sat beside him on an overturned basket with the passage paper open across his knees. The ship’s name looked very ordinary in the yellow lamplight.

Rosceline waited until the guard had finished.

“The note to Thorn was mine.”

He looked up. One eye had narrowed almost shut. The other moved to Daska and then to Pello.

“They didn’t know,” Rosceline said.

Hessel put the counted coins back in their little bag.

“Why?”

“To make the search follow you after we left.”

He sat still long enough that she wanted to tell him again when it had been meant to arrive. She could give the delays, the inspection, the thing on the book. She knew how to make the sequence sound inevitable.

He held his hand out to Pello for the passage paper.

“Is this part real?”

“Yes,” Pello said. “So is the money.”

The guard folded the paper carefully. “And that one over there? The boatman?”

“My cousin. He wants paying. He doesn’t want you.”

Hessel nodded. He looked at Rosceline once more.

“Don’t write my name again.”

She opened her mouth, then closed it and nodded.

He stood to help Daska with her coat.

They stayed in the shed until the sky over the canal had lost its blackness. The cousin brought plain clothes in a sail bag, complaining that none of them was the right size for respectable people. Briska helped Rosceline out of the ruined dress. Every movement pulled at her ribs. The silk smelled of perfume, iron and another man’s sweat.

“I’ll keep it,” Rosceline said.

Briska held up the torn side. “For what?”

“To remind me to charge more.”

She wrapped the dress around the sheathed rapier and tied the bundle. She wore a borrowed brown coat over a shirt that closed too high on her throat. The book went inside the coat, wrapped as before. Her burned hand had been dressed with a clean fold of linen from the cousin’s stores, which he added aloud to the bill.

Ilaro had fallen asleep sitting against the wall. Without the tension around his mouth he looked younger and more damaged. A pale seam showed above his ear where the hair had never grown back properly. Rosceline shaded the lamp with an empty tin and left him until Pello said they had to move.

At the coastal landing the Little Sister lay below a tangle of spars and drying clothes. Its master had a bald head and a voice that could have loaded the cargo without assistance. He read Hessel’s paper, checked a mark in his own book and pointed toward the gangway.

“One chest.”

“I don’t have one,” Hessel said.

“Then you’ve no complaint about the allowance.”

Pello handed over the balance of the fare. Daska stood with one foot off the stone and her injured arm tucked inside the coat. Hessel turned to her.

“You came back.”

“You were in the wrong room.”

He smiled with the uninjured side of his mouth. It was brief, and it went away when he saw Rosceline behind her.

“Will you—” He stopped and tried another question. “The men in the guardroom?”

“Alive when we left,” Daska said.

He nodded. He had carried the stolen blade as far as the shed, then left it there. Rosceline could see the empty place at his belt when he turned.

The master called for passengers. Hessel crossed the gangway without looking back again. He found a place beneath an awning among three women, a sleeping boy and a man holding a covered cage. A crewman pulled the gangway in. Pello caught the tossed shore line and freed it from the bollard.

The Little Sister moved out slowly, sail beginning to fill above its crowded deck. Hessel put one hand over his swollen eye against the dawn. With the other he held the passage paper until a woman beside him said something and persuaded him to put it away.

Daska waited until the boat had passed the outer post.

Then she sat down on the bollard and said, “Someone find me a chair. I have been very athletic.”

They hired two. Pello walked beside the bearers, carrying the dress and the sword. Briska supported Ilaro by pretending she needed his opinion on the damaged spreader. Rosceline heard him tell her that he would like to examine its failure in a room where nothing was moving.

The Countess’s door opened at their approach.

Celise took one look and sent for hot water, a surgeon and additional linen, in that order. She relieved Pello of the bundle and placed it on a side table without opening it. She did not ask why it was wet.

“The book?”

Rosceline took it out of her coat.

“In person,” she said.

Celise looked at her face. “Very well. This way.”

The Countess received them in the same room. The flowers had been changed. Rosceline noticed it with an unreasonable feeling of injury. In this house the night had passed cleanly into morning.

She put the oilcloth bundle on the table and untied it with her right hand. The ledger lay between the teacups, brown and dull and slightly damp at one corner.

Serafine did not touch it immediately.

“All of you?”

“Yes,” Ilaro said. “And the guard has sailed.”

The Countess inclined her head to Daska, who was watching the servant put food on a tray.

Then she opened the book. Celise stood beside her while she checked the first page, the last, several places between. Rosceline saw recognition travel over the small visible portion of her face. At one entry the mouth behind the veil became still.

“This is it.”

Five purses were brought. The preparation expenses were settled separately. Briska put down the folded contractor’s bill and the cousin’s increasingly detailed account. The Countess read both without comment and paid them.

Ilaro took his notebook out.

“Mistress Ossene.”

The Countess looked up.

“One evening. Your choice before the season ends. The Collector kept the invitation.”

For a moment she looked past them toward an empty chair. Then she said to Celise, “Not the musicians’ evening.”

“Certainly not.”

“The small supper after the west-coast buyers leave.”

“I’ll see to it.”

Ilaro kept the notebook open.

“You knew Caspian served Livaea.”

“Yes.”

“We—we asked about the protections.”

“I gave you what I believed would release the claim.”

“That isn’t the same answer.”

The Countess looked at the bandage around Rosceline’s hand, then at Daska’s arm.

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

Ilaro waited.

“I wanted the book. I thought knowing the extent of the association might persuade you to leave it where it was.”

“It nearly did,” Briska said.

“You made your decision.”

“After we were in his house.”

The room went quiet. Rosceline was tired enough to enjoy the Countess having to occupy it.

At last Serafine said, “You are right about that.”

She did not improve the admission. Ilaro shut the notebook.

“And now?” Pello asked. “He knows our faces.”

“He would have to explain which book was taken if he made a formal complaint. Several of the people who would hear it are in these pages.”

“He has men who don’t need a complaint,” Daska said.

“Yes. Avoid his part of the canal. Let Celise know where messages can reach you. If Thorn approaches you, hear her out before doing anything theatrical.”

Rosceline looked down at her purse. She would have liked protection that sounded less like another set of useful instructions. She would also have liked her ribs back as they had been yesterday. Neither seemed available.

The Countess turned to the entry that had stopped her. A narrow knife came from the writing box. She slid it down the inside edge of a leaf, close to the stitching, and freed the page.

Letha’s name stood near the top.

Rosceline could read two dates and the words information received. The amounts below were small. Small enough to be believable, large enough to make the singer look paid.

“She refused,” the Countess said. “He kept the entry.”

“Useful if she ever complained,” Ilaro murmured.

Serafine folded the leaf and held it to the candle. Flame took the edge. She let it burn almost to her fingers before placing it in the empty hearth.

The rest of the book she gave to Celise.

“Lock it away. We will need it.”

Rosceline watched the two portions of Caspian’s work go to their different destinations. Then she took her private agreement from the inner pocket of her borrowed coat and laid it beside the purses.

The Countess read the addition Rosceline had made the day before. She wrote beneath it, signed, and turned the paper so Rosceline could see.

First recovery satisfied. Three remain.

The ink was still wet when Rosceline folded the paper along its old crease.

Chapter 18 — A very promising painter

The surgeon said Rosceline’s ribs might hurt for several weeks.

“Which ones?” she asked.

He touched her right side. She caught his wrist before he could press.

She had expected more sympathy from a man charging that much. He bound her burned hand, told her which changes in the pain would require him back, and moved on to Daska, who asked whether she could dance.

“Not on that heel.”

“I have another.”

He waited until she stopped smiling, then showed her how little weight to put on the injured foot.

Celise stood by until they had both listened to the instructions a second time. She made Ilaro lie down in a room with shutters and told the servant carrying his breakfast not to ask him any questions. He thanked her so directly that Rosceline looked away.

By noon the company had been washed, fed and made sufficiently presentable to argue about money.

They did it in Briska’s workshop the following day. Pello brought the scow’s final account and a new cap. Briska brought the broken latch-spreader and seemed to think the appearance of the object ought to settle the cost of replacing it. Daska occupied the chair that did not creak, with her left foot on a stool and a bowl of cherries within reach.

Rosceline had slept for eleven hours and woken angry at every joint. She wore a loose shirt and had given up pretending that sitting down was easy.

“A cap isn’t equipment,” Briska said.

Pello laid the new cap on his knee and left the charge on the paper.

“Lost during the job,” Rosceline said. “And I won’t have him carrying my things through Silkstrand with the sun cooking his head. Pay for the cap.”

Ilaro, who had arrived last, sat with his back to the window. He put his coins beside Pello’s without joining the argument.

They paid for the cap. The cousin’s charge for shelter remained a subject of dispute. He had added an amount for the arrow in the thwart and then, on the line below, an amount for keeping the arrow as a souvenir.

“He’s charging both?” Rosceline said.

“He says they’re different services.”

“I like your cousin.”

Pello slid the account toward her.

Pello took his share and counted it twice, once for accuracy and once for pleasure. The boat he wanted was still beyond it, but less far beyond it than it had been. He wrapped most of the money in a cloth and left a few coins loose for lunch.

Briska picked up the spreader. One jaw stood visibly higher than the other.

“I can remake the housing.”

“Will it open that door?” Daska asked.

“No. But it will stop before bending.”

“An improvement.”

“I think so.”

She put it aside instead of beginning work immediately. Rosceline noticed. It meant they had arrived at the part nobody wanted to purchase by the hour.

Daska took a cherry, split it with her thumb and put the stone into the empty bowl.

“If I tell someone I’ll get them out,” she said, “you don’t arrange to make that harder behind my back.”

Rosceline leaned back. Her ribs objected. She leaned forward again.

“I believed he would be clear.”

“I know what you believed. I heard you.”

Pello laid the tied money cloth on the bench. “My cousin would have been under that wicket when the search began if we’d been slower still. He wasn’t in your note. He was in your plan.”

Rosceline looked at him, then at the others. None offered her a helpful interruption.

“What do you want agreed?”

“Names,” Daska said. “If you use someone’s name to send trouble after them, we hear it before the note leaves. They hear it if they’re one of the people helping us.”

“Sometimes that will make it impossible.”

“Then you find something else,” Pello said.

Rosceline could have laughed at them. There were plenty of people in Silkstrand who would sell a man to his employer for less than the price of last night’s dress. Most of those people could not lower six others out of a watched house or hold a door with one damaged arm. She looked at the empty sleeve of Daska’s coat, folded over the chair.

“Agreed.”

“Say what you agreed,” Ilaro said.

She turned toward him, irritated.

He waited. It was one of his better talents.

She said it. Not pleasantly, and without embroidery. Daska nodded once and reached for another cherry. Pello untied his money cloth to take out a coin he had put away by mistake. Briska began examining the spreader again.

Nobody embraced her.

After a while she found that she was less angry than she had intended to remain.

The Countess let her visit Sarem’s book three days later.

Rosceline went alone. Her hand had a clean dressing; her ribs still caught when she climbed stairs too fast. Celise showed her into a small room overlooking a courtyard, where laundry stirred above pots of herbs. The book lay on the table. There was a cup of water beside it and no tea to spill on the pages.

“The Countess has given you half an hour,” Celise said.

“How generous.”

Celise looked at her, then at the chair.

“You may shut the door.”

She left.

Rosceline sat. The bad stitching on the spine was worse in daylight. She remembered Sarem attempting to mend a sleeve while reciting, the needle stopped in the air as he lost his place. She had corrected the line and been told that if the daughters of Ahakar were going to be so particular about meter they might at least learn to sew.

She had not learned then. Someone else always did it, in those houses. She had learned later, with a coat between herself and a cold wall, and discovered that he had been bad at it because he could afford to be.

Her finger found the little mark.

“Across the bronze—burnished hills.”

The same pause. The same annoyance. She let it end without supplying the word.

Eleven months had been a long time once. Long enough to stop listening for a particular step outside a door. Long enough to make a joke and expect him to hear it. The people who had sent her to him had waited for that. She had spent years wondering which laugh they had heard and understood as permission.

She moved her finger away from the mark.

Beyond the window, a woman shook a sheet out over the courtyard. Water fell onto the leaves below, making them nod. Someone called up a complaint. The woman called down something that made both of them laugh.

Rosceline opened the book to another page. A line of small, awkward writing lay beneath Sarem’s. Her own. She had forgotten how cramped her letters had been.

She read the correction she had made there and smiled despite herself. She had been right. He had drawn an irritated little stroke beside it that conceded nothing and changed the line above.

When the half hour ended, she had heard the voice only once more.

She closed the book. At the door she stopped with her hand on the latch, listening to the courtyard. Then she went to find Celise and ask for the name of the woman who had offered to repair her dress.

Lady Wren’s salon fell on the following afternoon.

Rosceline had considered sending an apology. She had a serviceable one, signed by the surgeon if she wanted to pay him for the additional use of his hand. Instead she wore the green dress, repaired along the old tear with a narrow panel of darker silk, and arrived with the others at a house that smelled of coffee and fresh plaster.

“My dear,” Wren said. “Caspian tells the most extraordinary account.”

“Does he include the sea?”

“Not so far.”

“Then it could use more work.”

Wren looked at the dress, the bandage and the four people behind her. There was a limit to how much visible injury could be passed off as fashion, even in Silkstrand.

“I hope you’re all well.”

“Improving,” Daska said.

She had her plum earrings on. Her arm remained in its dressing, and the cheap replacement shoe on her left foot had been cut away around the heel. She had argued for it at considerable length and could walk without wincing if nobody hurried her.

Wren gave Ilaro a chair away from the small group of musicians and took Rosceline toward the paintings.

The young artist waiting beside them wore a coat that had been pressed so recently it still smelled faintly damp. There was paint under one thumbnail. He had tried to clean the other with a knife and cut it.

“Tavio,” Wren said. “Madame Rosceline.”

He bowed too far. “I’ve heard about your judgment.”

“That’s unfortunate. Show me something.”

He showed her a view of the harbor first. It was skillful and dull, with water that Deren might have approved of. She said something about the light. He moved quickly to the next, a prosperous family arranged around a table whose perspective threatened to slide the youngest child onto the floor.

Then she saw the fishmonger.

The woman in the picture had turned halfway from her stall, one hand still holding a fish by its tail. The other rested on her hip. There was a red indentation where a basket strap had pressed into her wrist. Her fingers were broad, capable and slightly curled against the habit of cold. The smile had not been painted onto her mouth; it seemed to be arriving from something said beyond the edge of the canvas.

Rosceline stepped nearer.

“Who is she?”

“My aunt.”

“Did she buy it?”

“She says she can see herself for nothing in the good pan.”

Rosceline laughed. It hurt. She laughed again more carefully.

She called over a woman she knew who had spent two seasons asking painters to make her father look less tired. Rosceline introduced them without adding a false history of commissions or a claim that someone more important was about to buy the picture. She pointed out the fishmonger’s hand and let the woman look.

When they began discussing a sitting, Rosceline withdrew.

At the far side of the room Daska had found the wiry dancer from the gala. They were exchanging an account of the evening that made generous use of their good hands. The dancer said something and Daska laughed, caught herself at the pull in her arm, then laughed once more anyway.

Ilaro sat with coffee and the catalog. A woman beside him was asking about a missing necklace. He asked her when she had last worn the dress she thought it had been taken from. She stopped talking to consider it.

Pello and Briska had cornered Tavio when he escaped the prospective client. Pello was describing the side of a boat with both hands.

“There would be room for a name here. And a little work around it.”

“A serpent?” the painter suggested.

“Something people like to see approaching.”

“What boat?” Briska asked.

“When I buy it.”

“Then buy one with a sound stem before you pay to decorate it.”

Rosceline took a glass from a passing tray. Nobody was attempting to put a working into it. As far as she could hear.

For once she had no reason to cross the room before someone stopped looking, and no appointment elsewhere that became urgent if she stayed. She could see an unoccupied chair beside Ilaro. She went toward it slowly, giving her ribs the time they required.

Behind her, Pello said, “I was thinking of calling her the Fair Return.”

Briska looked up from the little sketch Tavio had begun.

“Leave room to repaint that.”
