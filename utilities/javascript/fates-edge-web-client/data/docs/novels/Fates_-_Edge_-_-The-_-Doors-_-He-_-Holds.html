The Lamplighter’s Echo

Chapter 1 — The man who came up counting

The first man Saikou met in Vent-Deep had come out of the mine without his boots.

He sat on a coal box beneath the winding shed, rubbing his bare feet against each other. A woman crouched beside him with a pair that belonged to somebody else. She tried one against his heel, found it too short, and looked at the other as though it might have more sense.

“Where did you leave them?” she asked.

“Thirty-seven.”

“Your boots, Orel.”

“Thirty-seven.”

Saikou put down his travelling bag. Snow had collected in the stitching and begun to thaw in the shed’s warmth. He had walked the last slope behind a mule carrying lamp oil; the mule had repeatedly stopped to consider whether it needed an exorcist, and he had assured its owner that his fees for animals were discouraging.

The man on the box looked up. His mouth continued moving.

“Were you at thirty-seven when you came out?” Saikou asked.

The woman turned towards him. “He was at thirty-seven when they brought him out.”

“Then we needn’t improve it.”

Saikou crouched, making the man meet his eyes before touching him. Orel was young enough that the dark beard along his jaw looked borrowed. His left calf had been wrapped in coarse linen. Blood had reached one edge of it and dried.

“What did you eat before you went down?”

“Thirty—”

“Was there bread?”

The young man swallowed. His eyes moved to the woman.

“Black bread,” he said.

“What was on it?”

“Nothing.”

“That will do. Tell me where you bought it.”

He had not bought it. His sister had brought it, and it was not black, only darker where the crust had burned, and if Saikou meant to complain he could make his own. The woman with the boots sat back on her heels.

“Good,” she said. “He’s improved enough to be unpleasant.”

Saikou smiled, but kept Orel answering until the movements of his mouth followed the words he spoke. Then he asked the woman to keep him above ground and warm. She said she had intended to do that before his advice, and he said that happened to him often.

A broad man approached through the shed. He wore an old iron badge over his coat, its red enamel chipped away at the edges. There was a grey line through one eyebrow where hair would not grow. Two labourers followed him carrying a narrow frame with a mattress on it. The broad man stopped them with a hand.

“Ira?”

“Yes.”

“Harken Veth.”

Saikou took the offered hand. Harken’s grip was warm, calloused, and brief. His attention went directly back to Orel.

“They found him beside the drain,” he said. “He’d taken the dry branch because he heard the count from the wet one. He went through a rail that should have been replaced. I’ll show you that too.”

“The count?”

“The bell. People put numbers on anything they have to bear.”

Harken signalled the carriers forward. Orel protested at being laid on the mattress, less because it hurt than because two younger men were watching. His sister found a cloth to put over his feet. Nobody mentioned his boots again.

There had been two falls in six nights, Harken said as they crossed the yard. One man had broken a wrist. The other, Orel, had been lucky enough to land on old spoil instead of water. Saikou asked whether either had been examined for bad air.

“Both. The clinic sister says his lungs are clear. She didn’t say they’re immortal.”

Wind came down the mountain between the sheds. A timber haulage frame stood against the white slope, with frozen ropes hanging still. Above it, half buried in the cliff, was a stone doorway large enough for carts. Four lamps burned inside the arch. Below, houses stepped down towards a square in which somebody was throwing hot ash onto ice.

Harken’s office was smaller than the stable. He had a chair for visitors and a stool for himself, and Saikou saw that the chair had been offered often enough to wear the near arm smooth. A loaf stood beside an open ledger. Harken cut two pieces with his work knife.

“I was told you’d want a mirror and somewhere quiet.”

“I also want this.”

“The bread?”

“The bread.”

Harken pushed it across. It was good, with caraway in it, and Saikou had not eaten since the mule decided against breakfast.

“Tell me when it began.”

“After we opened the old return. Three weeks. Not every night at first. Now every night below the third stack.”

“Who died there?”

Harken set the knife down.

“A lamplighter. Others with him. Gas went off in the old return. Long before I had charge.”

“His name?”

“We’re finding it.”

Saikou waited.

“The name on the pay sheet is ‘third lamp,’” Harken said. “That wasn’t his name. His wife is alive. She’s coming.”

On the wall hung a drawing of the workings, red lines laid over older black. Saikou followed the red to a hatch marked with crossed strokes.

“What is that?”

“The place I can close without taking the whole return with it.”

“Close how?”

“Charges. Placed, not lit.”

Saikou swallowed the last of the bread before answering. It was easier to be solemn on somebody else’s empty stomach.

“What do you expect that to do?”

“Fill a tunnel with rock. I understand that part of it.”

“And the warning?”

Harken rubbed his thumb against the iron badge. “You understand that part. That is why I sent.”

He had sent two letters. The first had found an exorcist who proposed to burn silver in every gallery for a week. The second had found Saikou, who had asked in reply how the galleries were ventilated and whether the proposed silver burner intended to work with a naked flame. Harken had kept that letter.

“I’m not going to blast while you’re looking,” he said. “I can give you two shifts stopped. After that the pumps have to be paid, the men have to eat, and the buyers stop believing what I promise them. The west face is sound. We could use it if men could pass the third stack without forgetting where their feet are.”

“Why count breaths?”

“Because that’s the allotment for the sealed inspection. Because people have done it since their fathers did it. Because a count is something you can carry when a lamp looks wrong.”

“Breaths do not tell you how much air is in a room.”

“No. Breaths tell you when to turn round while you have air enough to argue. Our warden understands the distinction. Not everyone below does.”

Saikou drew his mirror from its wrapped case. The silver showed Harken’s ceiling, a water mark, and the old fracture running up its left edge. He turned it towards the plan. Nothing moved in it except his hand.

Harken watched. “Does it tell you whether I’m lying?”

“It tells me whether I’ve put it where the light can reach it.”

“That’s less useful.”

“Usually people have been doing the lying for so long that they don’t need help. I ask them questions.”

Harken’s mouth twitched. Then a knock came and he stood before the door opened.

The woman who entered carried a basket covered with a clean towel. She was small, wrapped in a brown coat too large across the shoulders, with thin white hair braided back tightly enough to pull the skin at her temples. She looked at Saikou before she looked at Harken.

“You’re late,” she said.

“I’m sorry.”

“I meant him.” She pointed at the loaf. “He was meant to send for the fresh ones.”

Harken took the basket and cleared a place for it on the table. The woman inspected the abandoned crusts and seemed relieved somebody had eaten them.

“This is Sanna,” he said.

Saikou rose. “Your husband worked the third lamps.”

She nodded. Her hands had gone under the edge of the towel, gripping the basket though Harken now held its weight.

“Would you tell me his name?”

“Edran.”

She said it quickly, before he had finished asking, and then looked frightened by the speed of her own answer.

“Edran what?”

“Just Edran. That is enough for him.”

Saikou repeated it. He saw her listen to hear whether he would put the stress in the right place. When he did, her fingers opened.

“You won’t blow him up again,” she said.

“No charges will be fired while I’m below.”

“Not what I asked.”

Harken stood with the basket against his belly. He looked suddenly like a man carrying a child somebody had handed him in the middle of a quarrel.

Saikou put the mirror away.

“I don’t yet know what I’ll have to stop,” he said. “I intend to find out before anyone does anything they cannot take back.”

Sanna considered him. Then she took a loaf out of the basket, broke it with both hands, and gave him half.

“You can find out better with that,” she said.

He took it. Harken fetched another chair.

Chapter 2 — The loaf that would not rise

Sanna had forgotten to put salt in the morning’s first dough.

She discovered it by tasting a crumb from the loaf she had cut for Harken. The bread was pale inside and heavy, although the yeast had been alive when she set it beside the stove. Edran would have eaten it and complained afterwards. He had considered that a fair arrangement: first the thing, then the opinion, so nobody might mistake what he intended to do.

She carried the unsalted loaf home in her basket with the towel folded over it. At the door, Maela from the next house had set a jug of milk beneath the overhang. Sanna wished people would stop putting things where she had to bend, then remembered that she had asked them not to leave them on the latch because she might not see them.

There was snow on the sill inside. She closed the shutter. The wind had turned while she was at the office; she would have known that once by the smell from the chimney. Now she knew when something happened in front of her face.

Her daughter’s letter lay beside the breadboard. It had come with a carrier from the lower valley, along with a packet of needles and a request that she use the money enclosed for a journey, not for other people’s flour. Sanna had put the money in a cup. It was still there. She had written that she might come after the thaw. The thaw was a useful distance away. Everyone knew it would arrive and nobody could ask her to show it to them.

She sat down and looked at the loaf.

She had not told the stranger about the bell inside her chest. She had told two men before him. One had said it was grief. The other had said he knew, which was worse because he plainly didn’t. He had then asked whether she sometimes heard Edran’s voice at night. She did, sometimes, in the words he would have said about a door left open or the flour merchant’s prices. She did not mistake that for a man being in the room.

The bell had not been like either thing.

She had been at this board, with both hands in dough. A delivery had come early; there were forty loaves wanted instead of the twenty she had prepared for. She remembered being angry with Edran for not being home to carry water before she remembered he was below. Then something struck beneath her breastbone. She had held still, feeling the dough rise between her fingers. It struck again. Her ears heard the clock on the shelf and a boy outside calling for his mother. Her body heard metal.

The warning bell.

She had run as far as the square with flour to her elbows. A man from the mine stopped her because carts were coming down too quickly and a wheel had broken. She asked what had happened. He said nothing, and then asked her to keep back, and then said the old return had gone.

She had not believed him. Returns went slowly. They were walked and measured and marked. The old return had always been there. It had more time than Edran had, more time than the men who named it.

A piece of dough had dried across her wrist by the time they brought the first bodies up.

There were things she had not forgotten.

She cut the loaf into smaller pieces. The worst part of being alone was not the quiet. It was the quantities. A panful was too much and half a panful caught at the bottom; a loaf lasted until the crust became a struggle; milk arrived for someone whose appetite had gone on being a subject of ordinary expectation long after he had died. She put one piece beside the stove and the others in a covered bowl for a pudding.

Somebody knocked. Not Maela’s knock, which was a little hurried apology on wood. Three separate taps, carefully spaced.

When Sanna opened the door, the stranger had snow on his shoulders and a paper packet in his hand.

“Harken said you took honey in tea.”

“Harken takes honey in tea.”

“I have been misinformed. It’s happened before.”

She let him come in. He did not examine her room while she fastened the door. People often did, finding the empty place beside the stove, finding Edran in the coat peg and the shelf his hands had made, as though they were better at knowing where he wasn’t than she was.

Saikou put the packet on the table. She filled the kettle. He asked where it should go, took it to the stove, and came back without rearranging the wood.

“Would you like to tell me what happened at home?”

“I was making bread.”

He nodded.

“That isn’t the useful part,” she said.

“It may be.”

She told him about the forty loaves and the blow below her ribs. She showed him where it had struck, with two fingers over the cloth. He did not put his hand there or ask whether it had hurt. He asked how many blows.

“Two. Perhaps a third while I was running. I couldn’t be sure.”

“And what did you hear with your ears?”

She told him about the clock and the boy. The kettle began to murmur. He got up when she looked towards it, poured the water without filling the cups too high, and brought her one.

“Was the window open?”

“Yes.”

“Could sound have come up the shaft?”

“The shaft is over there.” She pointed through the wall. “The window looks down the slope.”

“It could still have come round.”

“Not like that.”

He nodded again, but this time she saw him keep something back.

“What?”

“I believe you experienced it. I need to distinguish what reached you from what could have reached somebody standing beside you. There may be more than one way for the warning to arrive.”

She took a drink. The tea needed honey. She decided not to tell him.

“I was the only one,” she said.

“The only one you know of.”

“The others had ears.”

“So do I. Mine are frequently inadequate.”

He had a pale mark at the side of his neck, vanishing under the collar. She wondered whether that had been a bad night’s work. He looked old enough to have had many, although she could not put an age on him. He did not carry oldness as the men here did, with caution about which knee they put down first. He carried it in the pauses before answers.

“Edran wasn’t a brave man,” she said.

Saikou set his cup down.

“He disliked walking the narrow staging above the pumps. He’d send a boy if he could, then quarrel with himself and go anyway. Once he stayed home because he’d dreamed the lamp door fell on his fingers. I was cross. We needed the money. He went in late and said I’d delayed him making breakfast.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

She laughed before she meant to. It caught in her throat and became a cough. He waited while she drank.

“I won’t have them make him into one,” she said. “A man without anything wrong with him. They’ll put him over the chapel door and nobody will know him.”

“I need the man who went to work. That should be sufficient.”

She looked at him for a long time.

“The one who liked salt,” she said, and offered him the failed bread.

He ate it without an opinion. She liked him better and distrusted liking him so quickly. At the office he had given an answer to Harken when she was asking a question about her husband. Here he was giving her enough room to make a house around that husband again. Neither thing told her whether he could stop the mine being filled with stone.

When he opened his notebook, she asked what he intended to write.

“Your account of the warning. That your name for him is Edran. Where you were. What remains uncertain.”

“Will they see it?”

“Who?”

“The people who say he had another name.”

Saikou’s pen stopped above the page.

“Who says that?”

“The clerk in the upper archive. Years ago. He said there was no Edran in that crew. He showed me a column full of figures. I could not argue with figures. I went home.”

“And you didn’t ask again?”

“My daughter was sick. There were loaves to make. He said it very firmly.”

Outside, a child went down the street dragging something that knocked at each step. Saikou watched the unfilled line in his notebook, then wrote her husband’s name at the top in a hand clear enough for her to read upside down.

“Was that the name he used with you?”

“It was the name he used when he was scolding the chimney. I heard him give it to a magistrate. It was his name.”

“Then I’ll begin with it.”

“You should have begun with it at the office.”

“I did.”

“You began with asking me what else went after it.”

He looked up. She thought he would explain why that had been needed. Instead he drew a small line through the space he had left after Edran, and closed the book.

“Tomorrow I’m going below,” he said. “I’d like to return afterwards.”

“If you come up.”

“Yes.”

She put the honey into her own tea after he left. It was good. The unsalted bread would take longer to turn into something worth eating, but she had a way of doing it and a little milk nobody else needed.

Chapter 3 — What the mountain counted

Saikou was given a helmet with somebody else’s initials inside it, a lamp that had been tested twice, and a piece of bread so dense he thought of keeping it as a doorstop. He ate the bread. Doors had other means of staying open.

At the descent gate a woman in a leather apron took his name without asking what it meant. She wrote it on a slate, then wrote Harken’s underneath. Beside each name she made a row of small white marks.

“Derren,” she said, catching his glance at the apron’s stitched name. “Warden of this gate. You come back through here. You don’t hand your marks to anyone else. If you find another way out, stay out and send somebody to tell me.”

“How many marks for getting lost?”

“The same as for finding your way.”

She held out two rods of black wood capped with dull silver. Saikou took one. It was heavier than it looked.

“Put that across a shut gate and the gate stays shut while your undertaking holds,” she said. “This undertaking is that you will not leave an unwatched man below. Don’t use it to prop a roof. People do, because people are inventive.”

“And then?”

“The roof falls with a particularly expensive stick in it.”

He liked her immediately, which was no reason to trust her. He asked her to explain the marks.

“They’re not breaths,” she said. “They’re the allowance. Some men call them breaths. Then they count their breathing, and soon they’re managing themselves instead of looking where they put their boots.”

“Who made the allowance?”

“The under-altars. It covers the old return when we open it for inspection. Air travels badly there. The measure gives us a limit the gate can recognize. It does not give us air.”

Harken had been listening with the expression of a man hearing his own warning repeated more expensively.

“We test air,” he said. “We test supports. And then we have this as well.”

Derren made him say the gate undertaking aloud. His voice sounded foolish to his own ears, as spoken undertakings often did when there was nobody he wanted to impress. She made Harken say it too. The silver caps warmed briefly against Saikou’s fingers.

Their guide was waiting past the gate, a thin old man with a stone hammer tucked beneath one arm. Saikou had first mistaken him for a stack of coats. His name was Neth. He spoke so quietly that Saikou leaned closer, and Neth shook his head.

“Don’t chase it. You’ll hear.”

They descended a stair worn into shallow bowls. Water moved somewhere beyond the wall. The air smelt of wet leather, lime, and a bitter mineral scent Saikou could not place. At the first landing Harken put a sampling tube into a wall recess and waited. At the second he tested again. Neth stood with two fingers touching the stone.

Saikou kept his censer packed. He had met invokers who called every difficulty an invitation to burn something. He had recovered two of them from a room that had ceased to contain enough air for that opinion.

The west passage branched from a broad haulage way. Its rails were bright. Along the wall stood the men who would have worked this shift: nine in all, carrying food tins, picks, and the peculiar reserve of people told to wait while a stranger evaluated the risk they ordinarily accepted. A young woman with red wool wound round one wrist stared at the mirror’s case.

“Does it show him?” she asked.

“If there is something to show.”

“Will he see us?”

“No.”

Saikou waited. The distinction mattered enough to let it inconvenience him.

“He won’t know we’re here,” he said. “What you’ve heard is an event continuing. It is not a man choosing to frighten you.”

She looked at Harken. “Then why does it get worse when we come?”

“That,” said Saikou, “is a question I haven’t earned an answer to.”

Her name was Isset. She had worked this level for four years. Orel was her cousin. She asked to come as far as the third stack, because she had been beside him when he fell and did not want other people making a story of it. Harken started to refuse. Saikou asked whether she was qualified for the inspection route. Harken said she was.

“Then I’d like her account from where it happened.”

Isset left her food tin with a man whose beard was caught in his collar and joined them. Harken took another rod from Derren’s rack. Neth watched the silver warm and said nothing.

The third stack was an old stone partition: fitted slabs rising to the roof. A gap had been made at its foot. Timber braces framed the opening. Beyond it the old return turned sharply downhill.

“That brace is new,” Isset said. “The rail Orel took for a step wasn’t.”

Saikou looked through the gap. There was darkness, his own lamp’s reflection on a wet nail, and a small drift of white dust beneath the roof.

Neth put his hand out. They stopped.

A grain struck Saikou’s helmet. Then another. Somewhere overhead the stone made a sound like a man clearing his throat in a house where everybody was asleep.

Nobody moved.

Neth looked sharply at Harken. Harken lifted one foot and set it sideways on an old sleeper. Isset followed him. Saikou started to place his boot where hers had been. Neth caught his sleeve and pointed to the sleeper itself.

They crossed the mouth one at a time. Behind them the pale drift spread across the gap. A strip of stone peeled from the roof and hit the floor with a flat, enormous slap.

Saikou’s shoulders had locked. He made himself look at the place where his boot had almost gone. The slab lay across it.

“Was that him?” Isset whispered.

“No,” Neth said.

His voice carried perfectly.

They retreated to the last sound brace. Harken examined the timber without touching it. Neth knelt, laid his hammer against a seam, and listened. His hammer had a head of dark stone with a pale stripe through it. He slid a wooden wedge from his pocket and tapped it into a narrow crack. Each blow was so small Saikou could feel it better through his soles than hear it.

“You can bind that?” Saikou asked.

“For walking back. Not for work.”

“How do you tell?”

“By staying to find out whether I’m wrong.”

Saikou considered that, then unpacked the mirror.

Harken held the lamp. The silver showed an empty passage. Its old crack divided the reflection just left of centre. Water shone along a rail that had not been present when they arrived.

A man came into view.

He carried a lamp high in one hand. His mouth opened. He turned, struck something at shoulder height with his free hand, and bent under a weight nobody else could see. The lamp fell. He reached upwards again.

Saikou lowered the mirror.

The passage was empty. Isset was clutching Harken’s forearm.

“How many?” she said.

He felt the question arrive before he understood it. Thirty-seven. The number fitted against his ribs with an appalling completeness, as if he had been looking for it all morning. He wanted to take another breath and put it in order.

Instead he pressed his tongue against his front teeth and exhaled badly.

“Don’t count,” he said.

Isset stared at him.

“Tell me about your wrist.”

“What?”

“The wool. Who put it there?”

“My little girl.”

“What colour did she call it?”

“Red.”

“Was she right?”

Isset made an angry, astonished sound. “Of course she was right.”

Harken was looking down at his own marks. His lips moved. Saikou slapped the slate out of his hand.

It broke on the rail.

The sound freed Harken’s mouth. He swore at Saikou with a thoroughness Saikou found encouraging.

“Good,” Saikou said. “Keep doing that.”

Neth had already moved back towards the new brace. He raised a hand to the seam. His fingers trembled. The dust resumed falling.

They left without taking another image. Derren met them at the gate and counted the returned rods, the returned names, and the chalk marks Harken had put on a piece of timber after Saikou broke his slate. She did not accept Saikou’s assurance that they were all there until she had looked at each face.

Aboveground the day had become bright and painfully ordinary. Isset sat on a step. Harken stood beside her, staring at the broken slate he had brought up for no useful reason.

“The warning,” Saikou said, “was being struck on something beside him. A lever, perhaps. He wasn’t ringing a bell in his hand.”

“There was a bell circuit,” Harken said. “Before my time.”

“Where did it go?”

Harken looked towards the upper buildings, where a barred window shone above a narrow street.

“The archive may have a plan.”

“Will they give it to you?”

“They’ll give me the plan I’m entitled to.”

Saikou wrapped the mirror. On the inside of his cheek he had bitten a small piece of skin without noticing. It stung when he swallowed.

Derren took the rods from him.

“You have a different number in you,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Does it mean anything?”

“It meant something to him.”

She set the rods back on their rack, leaving a small space between them and the unused ones.

“Then don’t make it our allowance,” she said.

Chapter 4 — The names that were kept

The archive clerk offered Sanna a chair, which was an improvement on the last time. She declined it. Sitting down would make getting up into part of the argument.

Harken had sent them with a letter. Saikou carried it folded, having broken its seal at Harken’s request to make sure it said what Harken thought it said. The clerk read it twice. Her name was Pella, and she had one hand tucked inside a wool sleeve because the room was cold.

“Working diagrams are downstairs,” she said. “Historical pay records are behind the grille.”

“We need both,” Saikou said.

“Your authority is for working diagrams.”

Pella laid the letter on the counter between them. Sanna saw the words for the third stack, inspection, necessary expense. She had watched Harken write them. He had not written Edran.

“Then bring us the diagram,” Sanna said. “We’ll begin with what you can do.”

Pella’s shoulders relaxed a little. She went down a stair so narrow that her sleeve brushed both walls.

Saikou looked at Sanna. “You’ve been here before.”

“Yes.”

“Was it her?”

“No. A man. There have been several men.”

She had imagined a confrontation on the way uphill, and now the clerk was a woman with a cold hand and a rule that somebody else had given her. The absence of the proper enemy left Sanna uncomfortably aware of the effort she had spent preparing for him.

Pella returned with a plan rolled round a wooden core. They spread it on the counter. The third stack was marked. So was the old return, shown as a short line ending at a crosshatched patch. Nothing led to a bell.

“This is the replacement survey,” Saikou said.

“The current survey.”

“What did it replace?”

“The earlier one.”

Sanna heard herself laugh. Both of them looked at her.

“Bring that, too,” she said.

Pella rubbed her cold fingers. “There’s a release procedure.”

“For a drawing of a tunnel that isn’t there?”

“For a drawing of what is under the hold.”

Saikou put a coin on the counter. Pella did not touch it.

“The copying fee,” he said. “Not a suggestion.”

“It’s three.”

He added two. Sanna disliked him for being able to do that, then disliked herself for treating a man’s available money as an accusation. Her daughter’s money was still under the cup at home. She could have brought it.

Pella gave them a receipt and opened the grille.

The stacks beyond were darker than the public room. They smelt of ink, wool, and the dry dust that rose from things nobody had wanted to throw away. Names had been written along the shelves in hands that changed every few feet. Sanna saw floods, wages, quarrels, water allotments, three marriages under dispute, and a winter grain account. There was no shelf marked men who did not return.

Pella brought out a flat box tied with grey tape. Inside were plans, several with burned corners. Saikou lifted each with both hands. Sanna stood beside him, unable to keep herself from searching for a drawing of Edran among the lines.

The old return had once continued beyond the present crosshatching. It entered a narrow chamber labelled Second Reserve, then climbed into a passage leading towards the square. Beside the reserve chamber a tiny outline held two parallel strokes.

Saikou touched the air over it.

“Bell pull?”

Pella fetched a list of conventions. She turned its leaves against her knee.

“Warning station.”

A report had been folded beneath the plan. Water entering the lower return had displaced foul air into the lamp route; the gas ignited before the shift cleared. A later hand had underlined the sentence about water and written warning not passed in the margin. Sanna read the first sentence twice. She had known about the explosion. Nobody had told her that water came before it.

Saikou left the report beside the drawing.

“Where was the bell?”

“The line goes up.”

It went up through three sheets. On the last it emerged into a building Sanna knew. The chapel had once been a washhouse. She had scrubbed Edran’s collars there when their pump froze.

“They put it over the door,” she said.

Pella looked up.

“The old bell. After the washhouse closed. In the chapel. It was a gift.”

Saikou was still following the line backwards.

“It wasn’t the same bell that warned the square.”

“Yes, it was.”

“I mean it didn’t warn directly from the third stack. There was a receiving station here.”

He showed her a little box by the reserve chamber. A second line left it, ending at a board outside the washhouse. She remembered that board. Numbers painted beside hooks. Children had played at hanging rags on the hooks after the workers went home.

“Somebody had to pass it on,” she said.

“It looks that way.”

Pella opened another box. This one held narrow books and loose returns. On its cover a clerk had written final settlement. Sanna knew before it was opened that her husband would not be inside as Edran.

There were twenty-four entries. Some were names. Some were initials. Four were duties. Third lamp. Upper haul. Reserve watch. Assistant at haul.

“Third lamp,” Saikou said gently.

Sanna looked at the page. Alongside the duty were a sum, a receipt mark, and the word issued. No woman’s name. No child’s. The sum was smaller than she remembered, though she had no reason to expect remembering it correctly after all this time.

“That was mine,” she said.

Pella slid a finger down a second column. “The receipt is elsewhere.”

“Of course it is.”

“I can find it.”

Sanna heard the hurt in her voice. She took her hand off the book.

“I know. I wasn’t speaking to you.”

Saikou said nothing. She was grateful. Gratitude made her eyes sting more than the page had.

The receipt, when Pella found it, bore Sanna’s mark. There was a crooked stroke where Edran had taught her to make the beginning of her name. She had made it for money already spent, bread already eaten, a burial she had attended without a body. Beside it the office had written widow of third lamp.

“You didn’t even need his name,” she said.

Pella’s cold hand lay on the edge of the paper. “For this account, no.”

They wrote the entries onto a clean sheet. Sanna supplied Edran. She knew the man on the upper haul had been called Meret because his wife made candles. She did not know the assistant. Pella found a lodging register, but it contained two men who could have held the duty, and neither could be made certain. Saikou left a space beside it.

“Do you have to know them all?” Sanna asked.

“We have to know what we know.”

“And what we don’t.”

“Yes.”

She thought of telling him the assistant had been a young fellow with uneven teeth. There had been such a boy. She could remember the teeth but not whether he had died in that winter or gone away before it. She kept the memory to herself until she could say it without placing him underground by mistake.

By the time they left, Pella had ink on her sleeve and Sanna had the clean sheet folded in a borrowed envelope. The archive retained a copy. Pella wrote on that copy that Edran was attested by his wife, and Sanna made her mark beneath it. Saikou made his beneath hers. His signature was unexpectedly difficult to read.

“They have your name now,” she said on the steps.

“They had it before.”

“Your proper name.”

He looked at the ink on his fingers. “If anybody can make it out.”

The chapel stood across the square, its door open to the afternoon. A young man was sweeping the threshold. Above him hung a bell green with age, its lower rim polished in one small place by hands that had touched it while passing.

Sanna stopped.

She had entered that door in every sort of weather. She had listened to prayers beneath the bell, waited beneath it for bread to be blessed, passed under it with a coffin that contained her neighbour rather than her husband. She could not remember ever seeing it ring.

The young man saw her looking.

“Tomorrow night,” he said.

“What happens tomorrow night?” Saikou asked.

The young man put his broom against the wall. “It comes to the door.”

“The Hollow?”

He nodded.

“Every ninth night,” Sanna said. “We stay inside. We’ve always stayed inside.”

Saikou looked back across the square, towards the mine stair and the boarded route beneath it.

“You never asked why?” he said.

Sanna almost answered sharply. Then she remembered the washhouse, the painted hooks, and the little children putting rags where warnings had once been hung.

“There were other things to ask,” she said.

Chapter 5 — The men who answered

The borrowed bell had come from the lower yard, where it ordinarily called carters to a meal. It made a good sound. Saikou tested it once with his hand over the rim, and a dog in the next street replied. Harken stood beside the cart on which they had brought it, looking at the holes left in its timber frame.

“That has to go back,” he said.

“It will.”

“Before the carters decide they haven’t had dinner.”

They had assembled beneath the open mouth of the main stair. The light was fading. Men who should have been below were drinking weak tea from cups somebody had collected out of three households. Sanna stood with the envelope from the archive. Isset had brought her daughter, a grave child who was allowed to tie more wool round her mother’s wrist but not allowed to climb the cart.

Saikou had chosen this threshold because the current crew crossed it together. It was above the bad air, outside the falling roof, and still part of the route that had carried the old shift into the hold. Witness did not offer approval. In the mirror he could see the stair, the lamp brackets, and a movement far below that might have been his own hand reflected through the crack.

He asked the crew what a warning meant to them.

“Stop,” said Harken.

“Not always,” Isset said. “Sometimes clear the rail. Sometimes change your lamp.”

“What did this one mean?”

“Get out.”

Saikou asked her to say it again when they rang the bell. Harken would read the record, including its gaps. Sanna would name the third lamp. Then the living crew would acknowledge that the warning had been given. Nobody would announce that all debts were settled. He had struck that phrase out of the chapel attendant’s proposed prayer before the man could finish apologizing for it.

The attendant’s name was Corren. He had spent the afternoon getting signatures on a permit for the bell’s movement through the square. He looked too young for the exhaustion in his face.

“The under-altars will send somebody,” he told Saikou. “Lumenor herself may come.”

“Because of the Echo?”

“Because of the bell over our door.”

“Why now?”

Corren looked at the cart, then at the people waiting. “I asked now.”

Saikou put away the mirror. He had not expected the distinction to please him as much as it did.

They rang at the beginning of the shift that was not going down. Harken read slowly. Meret of the upper haul. Third lamp, attested as Edran. Two men whose names Pella had recovered from the lodging register and whose duties could be joined to the account without doubt. Reserve watch, personal name unconfirmed. Assistant at haul, personal name unconfirmed. The record became less tidy each time he admitted what it did not contain.

Sanna spoke without lifting her voice.

“Edran. You warned them.”

The crew answered.

“We have heard.”

The dog barked again. A child laughed and was hushed. Saikou had not asked for perfect silence. He let both sounds belong to the square.

The bell rang a second time. Isset said, “Get out.”

Something moved in the mirror before he raised it properly. He saw the man’s lamp, the reach of his free hand. Beyond him another figure stood at a board. Then the silver clouded at its cracked edge and showed Saikou’s own face.

He lowered it.

Harken was looking at him. So were the others. Their faces held a hope he had deliberately tried not to promise and had nevertheless helped to make.

“Wait,” Saikou said.

For several breaths there was nothing. He heard a cart axle, a woman closing a shutter, the scrape of a cup against the stone on which it had been put down. His own ribs felt unclaimed.

Then a man on the stair coughed.

It was an ordinary cough. Saikou knew it was ordinary. He also knew, with the certainty of a word learned in childhood, that it was the thirty-seventh sound.

The bell’s frame shuddered.

Isset caught her daughter by the shoulder. Harken went towards the stair. Saikou reached for his sleeve, missed, and followed him down three steps.

Below, somewhere beyond the reach of daylight, metal struck metal. Once. Again. In the square behind them the borrowed bell trembled without sounding. Its clapper hung still.

“Everybody away from the stair,” Saikou said.

Corren repeated it. His voice was louder and more useful. Saikou put his bronze bell without a clapper on the top step. He touched it with two fingers and asked the Sentinel to witness the boundary: those above had not undertaken to descend; those below were to be brought out.

The little bell gave one dry note.

A man stumbled into view carrying a bucket. Another pushed him from behind. They were the pump watch. Harken seized the bucket man’s elbow. Water came after them in a shallow brown rush.

“The chain’s gone,” the second man said. “It jumped the wheel.”

Harken asked which chain. The man was trying to answer, but he kept taking short breaths between the words. Saikou took the bucket from him and told him to stand with Isset.

The boundary held people. It did not hold water. Saikou stepped aside as the brown stream reached the square and spread around his boots.

Harken was already turning towards the yard.

“Hand pump. Two teams. Now.”

The crew moved. Isset put her daughter into Sanna’s care and ran after him. Corren went to fetch ropes. Saikou stayed at the stair until the last pump watchman came up. There had been three, not two. The third had stopped to collect a toolbox.

“Leave it next time,” Saikou told him.

“You’ll buy another?”

“Yes.”

The man looked him over, decided he meant it, and sat down hard on the top step.

By midnight the lower pump had been shut off. The broken chain lay in the yard. Half its links had parted at old wear marks; one had snapped fresh. Harken did not ask the Ghost to pay for it. He put a finger through the clean break and asked Saikou whether another bell would have done better.

“I don’t know.”

“Did you make it happen?”

“I changed the conditions around a thing I hadn’t understood. Yes.”

“Was the chain going to break?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“I don’t know.”

Harken let the chain drop. Saikou waited for the anger. It came, but it was not as helpful as he had hoped.

“I can’t charge a buyer for those answers.”

“No.”

“I can’t pay the men with them.”

“No.”

Harken looked past him at the hand pump. Two workers were pulling while two rested. Their boots were sunk ankle-deep in mud. The west face could not reopen while water rose through the haulage way. An inspector would require a new chain and a survey of the bearings. The reserve in the pay chest would cover neither those things nor a second idle shift in full.

Saikou asked how much he could advance.

Harken named a sum larger than Saikou carried. Saikou gave him what he had and wrote an undertaking for the rest. It would take work elsewhere to honour it. Harken read the paper before accepting it.

“This doesn’t buy you another trial.”

“I know.”

Sanna was sitting on the cart beside the borrowed bell. Isset’s daughter slept against her coat. The envelope from the archive lay under Sanna’s hand, spotted with rain that had begun after dark.

Saikou sat on the other end of the cart.

“He didn’t stop,” she said.

“No.”

“Did he hear us?”

“No.”

She stroked the child’s hair.

“I keep doing that,” he said. “Asking a record to accept an answer.”

“What should you ask?”

He watched the pump teams exchange places. In the mirror there had been a second figure, a board, a movement that ended before the warning crossed it.

“Where the answer was supposed to go.”

Sanna shut her eyes. He did not know whether she was tired or trying to see something.

“The hooks,” she said. “I remember the hooks.”

Chapter 6 — A door with two sides

Lumenor arrived without a procession. Sanna knew her by the small bell hanging at her throat and the young woman who walked half a pace behind her, carrying a narrow case. They came across the square while the morning pump crew was changing. The sound of water had been in Sanna’s dreams. It continued when she woke.

Corren met them at the chapel. His sweeping had pushed mud into a dark ridge beside the threshold.

Sanna was already inside, with the old diagram spread on a pew. Saikou had gone below with Harken to examine the flooded route from its upper end. He had told her he would not touch the Echo. She wondered how a person knew whether he had touched a thing nobody could see without a piece of silver.

Lumenor laid a hand on the diagram. She was smaller than Sanna had imagined. Her hands were broad, and the nail of her left thumb had been crushed and grown back crooked.

A single bell tone left her mouth.

The young woman beside her said, “She asks permission to sit.”

Sanna moved her basket off the pew. Lumenor sat.

Sanna had heard that the Keeper of the Under-Altars never spoke in a human voice. She had imagined an absence, something kept out of reach. The tone was not an absence. It had carried an ordinary request, and somebody had to know how to hear it.

“Does she understand me?” Sanna asked.

“Yes.”

“Then I’ll speak to her.”

The young woman inclined her head and stood beside the door. Her name was Aven. Sanna kept it.

“You have a Hollow,” Sanna said. “And we have my husband. I’d like to know why both of them have business with that bell.”

Lumenor looked up at it through the open inner door. Her answer was three notes, with a pause after the second. Aven listened, then came back to the pew.

“The bell was taken from a closed way without the way’s charge being discharged. It stands on a threshold where the dead are admitted by name. Every ninth crossing the Hollow finds that uncompleted admission.”

“Who put it there?”

“People who thought they were preserving it.”

“Are they wrong?”

Lumenor made one low note.

“No,” Aven said. “And they did not preserve all of it.”

Sanna traced the warning route with her nail. The line entered the reserve chamber. It left towards the old washhouse. Between those places was the little receiving box Saikou had noticed.

“Was there a man here?”

Corren brought a book from the vestry. He opened it to a page with two paper slips tucked under a string. Sanna expected a name. What she saw was an account of the bell’s receipt, its weight, and the prayer used to dedicate it.

“The ironwork was separated,” Corren said. “The bell came here. The board was used for shutter repairs. The chain was sold.”

“Sold to whom?”

“A smith. It doesn’t say which.”

Sanna folded her hands to keep from tearing the brittle paper out of the book.

Lumenor touched her wrist. Not to restrain her; the paper was still beneath Corren’s hands.

Aven interpreted the next tones slowly.

“Keeping a bell does not keep its obligation. The obligation may be completed in the place where it was interrupted. This bell can return there. The Hollow need not follow it, if its crossing is held correctly.”

“Can you hold it?” Sanna asked.

Lumenor’s eyes moved to the narrow case.

“Once,” Aven said.

“And then?”

“Then that instrument will have been rung.”

Sanna waited for the part of the answer that made this sound less ridiculous. Neither woman supplied it. Corren took his hand off the old account and went to the door to tell a boy to stop swinging from the bell rope.

Outside, Harken was arguing with a carter. The carter needed his meal bell returned. Harken pointed towards the pump, then to the cart. The argument changed shape. Eventually both men lifted the borrowed frame and carried it towards the yard.

“Everybody gets something back,” Sanna said.

Lumenor did not answer.

“I’m not asking you to make a promise.”

The keeper laid both hands flat on her knees.

Aven sat at the opposite end of the pew. “The measure chamber has a cost if you ask it to hold more than inspection. It was made for a person to enter, perform a duty, and leave before the allowance was spent. To finish a duty that has remained there after death, somebody must bring a living remembrance into it.”

“Of the man?”

“Of the person whose act is unfinished.”

“And leave it.”

“Yes.”

Sanna looked at the diagram. At the mine stair, beyond the chapel door, men were still pulling water up out of a hole.

“His name?”

“No.”

“What, then?”

“The way his face is held by you.”

Sanna’s first feeling was anger at the awkwardness of the phrase. Held by you. As if her mind were another archive in need of a clerk’s permission. As if his face were a book she had failed to return.

She stood up. Her knees hurt.

“I know his face.”

Lumenor lifted her gaze. The bell at her throat did not move.

“I know where he broke that tooth. I know how he looked when he lied about the price of fish. I know the line beside his eye that deepened when he thought he was keeping a smile from me.”

Aven said nothing.

“You don’t get all that by taking one face.”

The keeper sounded two notes. Aven took a moment before answering.

“No. We do not know precisely how the absence will gather around it. We know what was paid by the last person who used such an allotment. She kept her brother’s words. She knew his clothes. When she tried to call his face, she found no person there.”

“Did it come back?”

“No.”

Sanna picked up her basket. There were two loaves inside and a small jar of honey she had meant to return to Harken. She had forgotten why she had brought bread to a chapel.

“Then find another way.”

Lumenor’s answer was a clear ascending note.

“Yes,” Aven said.

That answer made Sanna hesitate longer than an argument would have done.

“Not a cheaper version of the same thing,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Tell the man with the mirror.”

“He will be told.”

Sanna walked home by the street that did not pass the stair. At her door Maela was waiting with a bowl under a cloth.

“You didn’t come for your milk.”

“I didn’t want it.”

“You put your money on my table yesterday.”

Sanna took the bowl. Maela kept hold of it until she had both hands around its sides.

“Are you ill?”

“No.”

“Have you eaten?”

She thought of the bread in her basket. “Not yet.”

Maela followed her into the kitchen and cut one of the loaves without waiting to be offered a knife. Sanna sat in Edran’s chair. It was the nearest chair, which had always been the reason she sat there, despite the other meanings people liked to give it.

Maela buttered two slices.

Sanna closed her eyes and found his face at once, looking past her towards some annoyance beyond the window. She opened her eyes before the annoyance could become hers.

“What do you want me to do?” Maela asked.

“Eat with me.”

Maela pushed a slice across the table. They ate while the pump continued, far enough away that neither of them could hear it from the kitchen.

Chapter 7 — The shorter way

By the time Saikou reached the gate again, Neth had made a chair out of two boxes and was sitting on it as if it had been built for him. His stone hammer rested across his knees. A cup steamed beside his boot.

“Did you sleep?” Saikou asked.

“Yes.”

“Enough?”

“No.”

“I’ve noticed people here prefer short answers.”

Neth lifted the cup. “Spend a day without yours. You’ll hear more.”

Saikou accepted the rebuke provisionally. He was not sure Neth had intended one. He sat on the lower box and unfolded the old diagram.

The under-mason studied it without touching the paper. Then he pointed to the reserve chamber.

“That wall isn’t there.”

“Gone?”

“Moved.”

“By how much?”

Neth held his fingers apart. The distance was small enough to be less reassuring than a larger one.

They had three possible routes. The original return was blocked beneath the settling seam. The warning shaft could be opened from above, but its footing supported a live haulage gallery. The reserve chamber could be reached through an old air branch if they passed a narrow flooded sill. Harken had marked that last route in blue. He preferred it because it did not require taking down the gallery above.

“Can we bring a bell through there?” Saikou asked.

“No.”

“Can we bring it through in pieces?”

“Not a bell.”

Saikou folded the diagram to show only the shaft. “Then this.”

Neth nodded.

Harken came through the gate with Derren and a rolled permit. His expression was too carefully controlled.

“How many men does that gallery employ?” Saikou asked.

“Eleven. Thirteen if we count haulage.”

“Can they work elsewhere?”

“Some. Not all at once.”

Derren put the permit on the box beside Saikou. Its seal showed an open hand under a bell.

“Inspection of the reserve chamber. One use. Not yet permission for a discharge.”

“Who decides that?”

“Lumenor. You. The person who pays. Me, if somebody lies about what will be done behind my gate.”

“How will you know?”

“I’ll ask before I open it.”

He looked at the silver-capped rods. “You don’t hear every lie?”

“No. I hear enough of them to dislike the suggestion.”

Saikou apologized. She accepted with a nod that gave him no further entertainment.

Harken’s alternative was ready. He could close the lower workings permanently. The charges would bring the old return down into the reserve chamber, then the outer wall would be filled from above. The west face would have to be abandoned because its pumps shared the flooded route. At least the upper gallery could reopen after inspection. He had obtained terms from a buyer willing to advance half the new chain’s price against the next season’s ore.

“Could it worsen the Echo?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Could it stop it?”

“Yes.”

“What is the difference?”

“I haven’t seen the whole recording. I don’t know which part of the event the collapse would preserve.”

Harken rubbed his forehead. “Can you find out without asking Sanna to lose anything?”

“That is what we’re doing.”

They went down with four helpers and no bell. Neth took the first position. One helper carried supports cut to the sizes he had requested. Another carried a sampling bag. Isset came last, marking each turn with chalk and measuring the distance back to the open route. Saikou asked her whether she wanted someone else to do that. She said no.

At the junction Harken stopped the party and read the pressure gauge. He made each person look at it. Nobody was to treat the permit as proof that a chamber could be breathed in. The gauge mattered. So did the lamps. So did the strip of paper that moved in the draught over the route they had entered.

Neth listened to the shifted wall. After a long interval he began taking stones out near its base. He did not strike the wall. His hammer moved against wedges, and each wedge received a blow small enough to disappoint a person who thought a hammer’s merit lay in noise.

Saikou helped carry the loosened stones. On the fourth he felt a sharp drag across his palm. He put it down and found blood gathering in a crease below his thumb.

“Sharp edge,” Harken said.

“Yes.”

“Not a curse?”

“Probably an insult from the mountain.”

Harken tore a strip from a clean wrapping and bound the cut. Saikou held still. The wound was minor; it also made lifting the next stone harder. By the time they had enough space to see through, his wrist ached from using the wrong grip.

Beyond the opening lay a little room full of shelves made of iron bars. A broken stool stood beneath them. Saikou had expected a larger place. He could reach its far wall almost by leaning through the gap.

There was the warning board.

Its hooks had been removed, but their shadows remained on the wood. Beneath each shadow was a painted sign. One for water. One for smoke. One for a failed lamp circuit. Beside the board a lever hung sideways from its spindle.

Neth moved back. Isset said the allowance was half spent. Derren was counting the party’s marks at the junction, not their breaths.

Saikou lifted the mirror.

The man with the lamp appeared as soon as the silver faced the board. He was smaller here, compressed by the angle through which Saikou could watch. He raised his arm. The lever moved in the reflection. On the room’s actual wall it did not.

Then the second figure came.

He wore a short coat and had one boot unlaced. He looked towards the board without turning his head fully. His fingers touched a hook. He paused.

Behind him somebody walked through the room carrying a tin cup. The figure at the board took the cup. His arm blocked the painted sign. He lifted it towards his mouth.

The lamplighter struck the lever again.

The second man looked down at the cup.

The silver showed black water. Not liquid arriving from any visible direction: darkness filling the room from its lowest corner. The short-coated man put the cup on the stool. He opened his mouth. The lamplighter reached upward.

Saikou lowered the mirror. His heart had begun to beat against something that did not belong to it.

He said the first line of an old marching complaint aloud. Harken answered the second, though he could not possibly have known it. He had supplied a different curse with the same number of beats. Saikou accepted the improvement.

In the opening, the hooks appeared again.

He had put the mirror down. He could still see the hooks on the wall. A hook slid sideways into the crack between two stones. The lever stood upright. For one instant the wall had the depth of a room Saikou was standing inside rather than looking into.

“Back,” Neth said.

Saikou took his foot off the sill. The wall returned. His lamp guttered and recovered. Harken pulled him by the back of his coat until he was clear.

Water was rising under the iron shelves.

The pressure gauge had changed. Harken ordered the helpers out. Neth set a wedge against the new seam and held it with both hands. Isset gathered the supports they had not used. Saikou reached for the mirror’s case and found it already in her hand.

“We stop,” Harken said.

The sentence had no room for argument. They left.

At the upper junction, Derren counted eight people. She counted again. Saikou leaned against the wall and let her. The number in his ribs had lost its certainty, but the hooks remained in his vision whenever he blinked.

Neth came last, without the longest brace.

“The room will not stay open,” he said. “That gallery has to be unweighted. Or the whole corner goes.”

Harken took the diagram. His thumb rubbed the blue route until the colour spread.

Saikou described what he had seen. He did not call the man at the board negligent. He did not know whether the cup contained medicine, whether the man had already been ill, whether he understood the signal, whether his pause lasted a heartbeat or the whole warning. He knew only that the relay had not been completed in the recording.

Harken asked if they could complete it from here.

“Maybe. With the original bell returned to the route, and a living witness inside the measure.”

“And without?”

Saikou looked at the timber Derren used for her tally. There was a simple pleasure in a number which could be checked by looking at the people it described.

“I can try a second public account,” he said. “I think it would be another experiment with your men beneath it.”

Harken rolled the diagram.

“Then don’t.”

Chapter 8 — What could be spared

Her daughter’s letter contained no portrait. Sanna knew that, but she unfolded it anyway. There was news of a neighbour’s troublesome daughter, the price of wool, a roof leak, and an invitation written so carefully that Sanna had disliked it the first time she read it. Her daughter had made room for her without saying there was now enough room because someone else had gone.

Maela stood in the doorway holding a basket of Sanna’s loaves.

“Which houses?”

Sanna gave her the list. Maela repeated it incorrectly on purpose, putting the chapel twice and forgetting Harken. Sanna corrected her before she understood the kindness. Then she was angry, briefly, because kindness could be another way of arranging her.

“I can still deliver them.”

“I know. I can still offer.”

Sanna folded the letter and put it away.

Saikou came at noon with Harken, and neither of them brought a priest. Harken removed his boots at the door because they were wet. His socks had holes at the heels. He carried the terms he had obtained from the ore buyer.

“If we close the west face,” he said, “I can retain nineteen of the thirty-two. The others can draw six days’ pay before the advance comes due. I’ve asked the outer quarries to take them. Five may get places.”

Sanna poured tea. Saikou accepted a cup. Harken forgot his until she pushed it towards him.

“And the warning?”

Saikou gave her the answer he had given Harken. Collapse might end the recording. It might lodge it through a wider part of the hold. There were safer ways to close the route than detonating the charged return, but they would take men, material, and time. The charge line could be removed. Stone could be filled gradually from the upper shaft. During that work the remaining galleries would stay closed. More workers would leave. Water might make the west face unrecoverable regardless.

“You could keep everybody aboveground,” she said.

“Yes.”

“For as long as it took.”

“If somebody paid them. Or if they could afford to remain without pay.”

Sanna looked at Harken’s socks. “And you?”

“The same as they do. With a buyer owning the next ore before it comes out.”

She did not ask whether he would be ruined. People said ruined as though it were a clean condition, reached at once. Harken would still have his hands, his training, his boots. Other people would have claims on most of what he could do with them.

“Then it isn’t my face against thirty-two lives.”

“No,” Saikou said.

“Then don’t let them say I kept those men down there.”

Harken’s head lifted. “They won’t hear it from me.”

She told them to eat before they went on. There was yesterday’s pudding and enough fresh bread. Harken took off his coat. Saikou put his injured hand on the table where she could see it. She asked what had happened.

“A stone.”

“Did you move it?”

“Yes.”

“There’s your mistake.”

He smiled and reached for the pudding with his other hand.

After they ate, Saikou explained what they would ask of her. Her remembrance of Edran’s face would be the deposit that kept the chamber open until the warning was completed. Lumenor believed it would answer because Sanna had known the man whose name she would speak.

“You don’t know?” she asked.

“No. She has records of three discharges, not of every use of such a chamber. She can tell us what happened in those three.”

“And if it takes the memory and fails?”

“Then you will have paid and we will have failed.”

Harken stared at him. Sanna was glad Saikou had said it before Harken could stop him.

“Can you pay something?”

“Yes. But I haven’t carried this man in my life. I could undertake a boundary, or offer a memory for another use. I cannot make my remembrance of a stranger equal to yours because I’m willing to lose it.”

She looked at his face. It was tired, ordinary in its tiredness. The silver case on his belt made him no less capable of wanting a problem to end.

“Can I take paper in?”

“The written name can come out with you.”

“I meant a drawing.”

He paused. “If you have one.”

She did not. They had once paid a young artist for a charcoal likeness at the summer stalls. It had looked like Edran if he had spent his life being disappointed in horses. He laughed at it until she had laughed too. Their daughter had taken it to school for some lesson and lost it. There had been a quarrel. After supper Edran had gone round the neighbours asking whether they had seen a picture of a man who owed money to a horse.

She told the story. Harken laughed first. Saikou laughed after him, carefully, as though he had not yet earned the right.

“I thought of drawing it myself,” she said. “Last night.”

“Did you?”

“I made a mouth. I couldn’t make the eyes. Then the mouth looked wrong.”

Saikou did not tell her that imperfect drawings were often the best ones. She liked him more for it.

After they left, she took the paper from the drawer. The mouth was not his, but she could see the mouth she had meant. She turned the page over. There she wrote what she knew about the face: the tooth, the scar by his chin, the left eyelid lower when he was tired. She spelled scar incorrectly and crossed nothing out.

Maela returned with the basket. Two loaves had been exchanged for eggs. One house had paid late. Harken had already been given bread and had paid twice. Maela placed the extra money beside the daughter’s letter.

“Keep that,” Sanna said.

“What for?”

“The deliveries.”

“We haven’t arranged that.”

“Then let’s arrange it.”

They made an agreement which neither called help. Maela would bake on the mornings when Sanna did not want to. Sanna would teach her the measure for the dense carters’ loaf, which Maela kept making too light. They would share the payment from the mine according to whose hands had kneaded, baked, and carried. If the mine stopped buying, they would have to find other houses or make less.

Sanna heard herself say if. It was an ordinary word. She had let a great deal of work collect behind it.

At dusk she went to the chapel. Lumenor was still there. Aven sat on the floor repairing the strap of the narrow case. Corren had put a bucket under a roof leak and was trying to get a ladder through the vestry door.

Sanna waited until he succeeded.

“I’ll do it,” she said.

Lumenor made no sound.

“Not because all the men will die if I don’t. Not because you know better what a memory is worth.”

The keeper inclined her head.

“I want him finished with this. I want the men able to come home without my kitchen pulling them underground. And I want to go and stay with my daughter before I make another winter into an excuse.”

Aven stood up. “You may still withdraw.”

“I know. That’s part of doing it.”

Sanna set her hand on the case. The bell inside was smaller than she had expected. Its surface had no green on it, no mark where a finger had rubbed. Nobody had rung it.

“Tell me all the places I can stop,” she said.

Aven put down the repaired strap and began.

Chapter 9 — The bell that had not been rung

Corren wanted to know whether he should remove the chapel flowers. Saikou asked why.

“They might offend it.”

“Have they before?”

“No.”

“Leave them.”

“They might attract it.”

“It comes every ninth night with or without flowers.”

Corren looked at the little bunch of winter stems by the altar. He moved them to a shelf where nobody was likely to knock them over. Saikou did not object to an improvement that had nothing to do with the Hollow.

The square had emptied. Those whose windows faced the chapel had shuttered them. Harken had posted two watchers at the end of each approaching street, outside the planned boundary, with instructions to turn people away without explaining enough to draw a crowd. The watchers had been paid from Saikou’s advance. One had asked whether danger entitled him to more. Harken had said yes and amended the amount.

Inside the chapel Lumenor stood beneath the old bell. Neth had examined its fastenings. They were sound for hanging, doubtful for ringing, and inconvenient for lowering. He had replaced one peg and fitted a sling. The sling led through the inner doorway to the place where they would move the bell after the crossing was contained.

The old bell had no clapper. Corren had shown Saikou the empty interior. The clapper had been taken out when the bell entered the chapel, because it belonged to the warning circuit, not to a free-swinging rope. They could replace it with iron of the right weight. They could not return a missing chain to the old system by declaring that every piece of iron was spiritually equivalent.

Aven held the new clapper wrapped in linen. Harken’s smith had made it while the pump crew worked. It had cost more than an ordinary repair, because the smith had closed his shop to other customers for the afternoon.

Saikou checked the boundary with Corren. The Hollow approached the outer threshold. They would remain inside the inner one, which stood a few steps farther back. If it crossed the outer threshold, Lumenor would hold its admission to the bell’s old charge. Saikou would set the Sentinel at the inner line. No one was to step into the space between for any dropped object, unanswered question, or voice they happened to recognize.

“What does it sound like?” Corren asked.

“Here?”

“Yes.”

“You’ve heard it more often than I have.”

“I’ve never let myself listen.”

Saikou looked at the young man’s clenched hands.

“Then tonight you can watch my hand. When I close it, stand behind Aven. You don’t need to identify it by ear.”

Corren practised moving once. He looked embarrassed. Saikou asked him to practise again. On the second try he did not catch his coat on a pew.

Sanna sat beside the wall. She had elected to witness the crossing, though she was not required to be there until the descent. Saikou had shown her the same route and the same hand signal. She said she did not intend to drop anything. Her basket lay under the pew, its handle resting against her ankle.

The case stood open on the altar. Inside it was a small black bell. Its rim was as thin as the edge of a cup. Saikou could see no clapper. It did not resemble his own bronze instrument, which had acquired dents from travel and one from a stubborn mule.

“What does it admit?” he asked Aven.

“One witness from the dead, where an account has been carried across a threshold but left incomplete. Not the Hollow. We cannot command that.”

“The lamplighter?”

“No. His event is here already.”

“The relay keeper.”

“If the account was his.”

Saikou looked towards Lumenor. “And if it wasn’t?”

She sounded two notes.

“We will not ring twice to correct ourselves,” Aven said.

He thought of every explanation he had made that day, and of the amount of confidence people could mistakenly extract from a man who knew the names of his tools. He took out his journal and read his account of the mirror aloud. The man with the short coat. The unlaced boot. The cup. The board. The pause. No name. No assertion of guilt.

Lumenor listened. She touched one sentence on the page: he received the warning.

Saikou crossed out received and wrote stood beside.

The keeper removed her finger.

A sound came from the square.

Not a bell. A foot dragged through grit. Saikou could not see anything beyond the outer door, but the grit moved into a narrow dark line, as though a wet hem had passed over it. The line stopped at the threshold.

He closed his hand. Corren moved behind Aven. Sanna stayed in her place, looking at Saikou rather than the door.

The old bell gave a small movement. Its sling tightened.

Something knocked on the doorframe at the height of a man’s mouth.

Lumenor answered with a tone Saikou felt in his uninjured palm. The knock repeated, lower this time. The light over the outer threshold shortened without becoming dim. Saikou set his bronze bell on the inner line and spoke his undertaking to the Sentinel.

“No living guest beyond this line has offered entrance to the space before it. I will keep them within the witness I have taken.”

His bell sounded once.

The shadow in the doorway gathered itself into a tall absence. Saikou could see the opposite jamb through it and could not make his eyes agree that this was possible. It turned towards the hanging bell.

Lumenor lifted the black instrument from its case.

Aven spoke the account they had agreed. The old warning station. The unrepeated signal. The man at the receiving board, whose name they did not know. She invited him to testify to his own act, not to excuse another person’s interpretation of it.

The black bell rang.

Saikou had heard great bells crack in a burning tower. This was quieter than a teaspoon put down on stone. He knew at once that it would never make the sound again.

There was a man between the thresholds.

He had the short coat. His boot was unlaced. In one hand he held a cup that showed no reflection in Saikou’s mirror, although the hand itself appeared.

The Hollow turned towards him.

Lumenor held the black bell at her breast. The man looked at her, then at the old bell above the door.

“I put it under smoke,” he said.

His voice sounded as if he had begun speaking a long way away and come nearer midway through the words.

Saikou waited.

“It was the wrong hook.”

“Why?” Aven asked.

The man’s face tightened. Saikou did not know whether it was anger, pain, or the effort of remaining a face.

“The water sign was down. Somebody had taken it for the cart. I thought he meant change the lamps.”

“Did you send that?”

“No. I went to ask.”

Saikou saw him glance towards the room that no longer stood behind the washhouse. The cup moved in his fingers.

“By then the return was gone. I didn’t get back to the board.”

The Hollow leaned. The man became thinner. Corren drew a breath sharp enough to make Saikou turn. The young man had moved one boot towards the line.

Saikou held out his hand. Corren stopped.

“What was your name?” Sanna asked from the wall.

The man looked at her. His mouth opened without a sound. The cup vanished first. His hand followed. Lumenor sounded one clear note, and he closed his eyes. Then he was no longer between the thresholds.

Nobody had learned his name.

Saikou hated that with a suddenness that almost made him step forward. His bronze bell gave a faint vibration beneath his boot. He stayed where he had undertaken to stay.

Aven turned towards the Hollow.

“The warning remains unpassed,” she said. “Its instrument is being returned to the place of its charge. This door receives no further duty from it.”

The shadow did not withdraw at once. The old bell moved again, harder. Its fastening creaked. Neth put a hand against the sling from behind the inner line. Lumenor’s tone descended until Saikou could no longer hear it, though the skin around his injured hand prickled.

The shadow passed across the bell rather than into the chapel. For a moment the green metal seemed to contain a depth larger than its hollow interior. Then the line of wet grit outside the door broke apart.

Saikou waited until the nearest watcher called from the street.

“Nothing here.”

He asked the Sentinel to witness the released boundary. The bronze bell was silent when he picked it up.

Lumenor laid the black instrument in its case. Aven wrapped it without touching its rim.

“The hook was wrong,” Corren said.

Saikou looked at the young man. “Yes.”

“He wasn’t ignoring him.”

“We saw a pause. We didn’t see its meaning.”

Neth began lowering the old bell. Harken came in with two helpers and took the weight when the sling reached his shoulder. Sanna stood aside for them. As the bell passed her, she touched the polished place on its rim, then took her hand away.

“Now we know which warning to give,” she said.

Saikou put away the mirror. His own certainty about the cup lay on the page in crossed-out ink. It would remain there.

Chapter 10 — The place to stop

The bell lay in a cradle made from a cart’s spare runners. It looked larger on the ground. Sanna had passed under it for years without thinking about its weight, as she had passed under roofs and beneath hills.

Harken and Isset tested the cradle by lifting one end, setting it down, then lifting the other. Neth checked each binding. Saikou watched the test with his bandaged hand inside his coat. His left shoulder looked stiff. She wondered what he was carrying that did not require a pack.

They would take the bell down at the shift change, after the workers still engaged with the pump had come aboveground. Nobody would be asked to share the old warning chamber’s allowance by happening to be below it. The upper gallery had been emptied. Its tools and timber were being carried to another level, except for the pieces Neth wanted for support.

Sanna heard the men moving them, one beam at a time. A decision written on Harken’s plan had become men carrying things they had expected to use tomorrow.

Aven came to the gate with the written terms. Sanna had asked for them because she might not remember everything that had been said. There was no shame in that, though asking had felt shameful until Aven brought a page with large, separate lines.

She could withdraw before crossing the chamber sill. She could withdraw inside, before naming the man and offering the remembrance. After the offer had been accepted, they could stop the work but could not require the remembrance back. The chamber would hold for one completed warning and the return of the people admitted under its measure. It would not hold the roof forever. It would not make the old air safe. It would not give authority to send anyone else into the work afterwards.

Sanna read each line. She could read much more slowly than Saikou wrote, but this was a different hand.

“Who says it has been accepted?”

“The measure will show it,” Aven said. “The return marks will be complete before the act is complete. You will feel the absence.”

“What if I can still see him?”

“Then do not ring. Tell us.”

Sanna handed the page to Maela, who had come carrying a bag of bread. Maela read it too. They stood beside the gate while the men tied the last rope.

“Do you want me here?” Maela asked.

“Yes. I want you to keep that page. If they bring me up saying something else happened, I want somebody who knows what they asked.”

Saikou heard her. He came over and signed beneath the terms. Aven signed after him. Derren added her name and said the gate account would retain a copy.

Maela folded the original into her apron.

“Your daughter?” she asked.

“I wrote.”

“What did you say?”

“That I would come after the work was finished. That something might be changed when I did.”

“Will she understand?”

“No. She’ll come here if she doesn’t, which is her way of telling me I’ve been an idiot.”

Maela smiled without agreeing.

Sanna had not put the price into the letter. She had started, then found herself writing as though she were explaining why a picture had been lost. The paper could not hold the difference between an old face becoming hard to remember and a face removed while every other part of knowing remained. She had torn that draft into strips and used them to light the stove. The finished letter said enough to make her daughter worry, not enough to let her worry accurately. She would have to answer for that.

Derren opened the gate. The bell went first.

The descent took longer than Sanna expected. She had been down shallow stairs to fetch men’s lost mittens, deliver bread, tell Edran their daughter was feverish. Those stairs belonged to rooms where daylight had an ordinary claim. This one continued after she felt it should have stopped.

Isset walked beside her. At the first turn she offered an arm. Sanna refused. At the second she accepted it without explanation. Isset adjusted her step to Sanna’s and looked ahead.

They passed a wall marked with fresh chalk. At its foot stood three empty food tins. One had a child’s scratched flower on its lid. Sanna thought of the sleeping girl against her coat, the little red wool. She had let herself think of the crew as an answer to Edran’s warning. Each one had come with another answer attached, someone waiting elsewhere.

Harken stopped them at the upper junction. He tested the air. Saikou asked everyone to look at the gauge, as Harken had taught him. Sanna could not read its divisions. Harken showed her the safe band and the line at which they would leave.

“Even if I’ve already paid?”

“Yes.”

“Even if the bell hasn’t rung?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the band again until she could point to the line without his help.

Beyond the junction the way narrowed. The empty gallery had left a long space above the new supports. She could see places where tools had hung because the stone behind them was clean. Neth had driven his wedges beneath a great diagonal seam. He stood on a low plank, resting his forehead against the wall.

“Does it hurt?” she asked.

He turned. “Sometimes.”

“What are you doing?”

“Listening.”

“To the warning?”

“To what comes after it.”

She did not understand. He stepped down and offered her the place where his forehead had rested. The stone was cold. Through it she felt something move, not against her skin but beneath it, a tiny travelling strain. She pulled away.

“That isn’t Edran,” Neth said.

“No.”

He nodded as though she had learned something difficult. It was not difficult to distinguish her husband from a hill trying to settle. It was difficult to accept that both might need attention at once.

The bell reached the reserve chamber with a scrape and a muffled curse. Harken apologized. Sanna told him Edran would have said worse.

Saikou let her sit while the others fitted the clapper. He crouched far enough away that she did not have to look down to see his face.

“We can go back,” he said.

“You’ve told me.”

“I know.”

“You can stop telling me until the last place.”

He took the rebuke and stood.

She opened the little packet in her coat. Inside was the paper with the bad mouth, the notes on the reverse, and her daughter’s last letter. She had not brought them to cheat the price. She had brought them because they were hers and she was going somewhere unfamiliar.

Edran’s face came when she called it. He was looking at the loaf he thought she had cut unevenly. He opened his mouth to say something useful and infuriating. She could almost hear him.

She let the face stay until the work was ready.

At the sill Derren stood with the tally. Harken held the lamp. Isset waited beside the return route, one hand on the rope that would guide them back. Saikou stood in the chamber with the mirror covered. He had told her he would not show her an image of the man after she had paid and call that restitution.

Aven read the terms once more, this time in a voice almost lost beneath the pump’s distant rhythm.

“This is the last place before the offer,” she said.

Sanna looked back up the route. Maela was not visible. The daylight was not visible. The rope went on through the dark to hands that would know what to do with it.

She put away her papers and stepped across.

Chapter 11 — Water, not smoke

Saikou had placed the bell low enough for Sanna to reach the clapper without lifting her arm above her shoulder. Harken’s men had built its stand from the dismantled gallery. A large knot showed through the crosspiece, pale and round as an eye. Saikou looked at it whenever he needed to remember where he actually stood.

They had fixed a new board beside the old one. There were only two signs on it. One was the old water sign, copied from the diagram. The other showed the evacuation mark, with clear the workings written beneath it. Isset had written the words. Pella had checked the old mark before the board was brought down. Neither correction depended on a miner being able to read while running.

The chamber held Sanna, Saikou, Harken, and Aven. Derren stood at the sill. Neth waited outside with the rope. The remainder of the living crew was aboveground. They would receive the warning through the temporary mechanical line, which led from the chamber to a small signal bell at the main gate. The line had been tested four times. On the last test the gate watcher sent a piece of white cloth down the rope as acknowledgment.

That cloth lay on the new board now.

Saikou uncovered the mirror and set it where Sanna would not have to face it. Its angle held the lever, the old board, and part of the bell. The light came from two inspected lamps. Aven’s third lamp waited at the exit in case either failed.

At the edge of the silver, the lamplighter lifted his arm.

The number arrived beneath Saikou’s ribs. He let his breath escape through his nose. He had not told himself not to think of thirty-seven. That was a fine way to put a thought at the head of a table and serve it dinner. He watched the knot in the crosspiece and named the things around it: wood, iron, rope, hand.

Sanna touched the old bell’s polished rim.

“Edran,” she said.

In the reflection the man struck the lever. The bell’s new clapper shifted, though nobody had touched it.

Sanna closed her eyes.

“The face I have kept of you,” she said. “For the warning you could not finish.”

The lamp beside the board burned taller. Saikou saw Derren look at her tally. Every return mark had filled with white. The chalk she had used lay dark against them.

Sanna opened her eyes. Her hand dropped from the bell.

Saikou waited. Aven had moved towards her, but stopped before touching her arm.

“I can’t,” Sanna said.

The word was small. It went through the chamber more clearly than a shout.

Saikou looked at her hands. They were empty. She turned them as though something ought to have fallen between them.

“Not even the mouth,” she said.

Aven lifted her lamp towards Derren. Derren nodded.

The offer had been taken.

A sound came from above the chamber roof. Neth put one hand against the seam outside. His other hand closed on the rope. Harken checked the gauge, then pointed to the board. The air remained in the safe band. The roof had its own account.

Saikou turned the new water sign into place.

In the mirror the relay keeper stood beside the old board. He took the cup. The lamplighter reached again. The darkness began filling the lowest corner of the reflection.

“Now,” Saikou said.

Sanna grasped the clapper’s short cord and drew it towards the bell. She had been told not to swing freely. A warning circuit needed a clean strike. Her arm trembled. Aven laid a hand beneath the cord, supporting its weight without taking it from her.

The bell rang.

Its note was deep enough to make the new crosspiece vibrate. Saikou could hear an imperfection in it, a narrow buzzing beside the tone. He had feared the sound would be unlike itself because of the replacement iron. It was unlike every bell he had heard aboveground, and that was as much as he could know.

Harken pulled the mechanical signal.

At the far end of the line a smaller bell answered. Too faint to hear from this room, but the cord moved against his hand. He pulled again.

“Water,” he said. “Clear the workings.”

The darkness in the silver reached the relay keeper’s ankle. The man’s mouth opened. He put the cup on the stool. Nothing had changed.

Saikou felt cold along his spine.

They had paid. They had rung. They had not yet completed anything.

The roof spoke above them.

Neth said, “Out of the middle.”

Harken pushed Aven towards the wall. Saikou reached for Sanna. She was still holding the cord, looking at the bell with a bewilderment that did not belong only to grief.

A lump of stone fell between them. It hit the stand and sheared a corner from the crosspiece. The bell rocked. Saikou caught Sanna around the shoulders and pulled her beneath the supported edge.

His left forearm struck the stand. Something in it gave with a hot, clean pain.

He did not let go. Harken caught the bell’s sling. Aven lifted her lamp clear of the floor. Neth forced a wedge against the seam with three hard blows of the stone hammer.

Saikou heard himself curse. He recognized the marching complaint and was furious to have returned to it.

The signal line went slack.

Harken looked towards the exit. Isset shouted from beyond Derren’s shoulder.

“They’ve received. Cloth coming.”

“Bring it,” Harken said.

Derren was holding the gate rod across the narrow opening. The opening would remain passable while her undertaking held: no admitted person left unwatched. It did not protect the chamber from falling fragments. Aven moved Sanna’s hand off the cord and wrapped it round her own wrist. She kept the lamp in her other hand.

Saikou tried to lift his mirror. His injured arm refused. He changed hands and saw the black water rise in the silver until the relay keeper stood waist-deep.

The white cloth emerged on the rope. Neth caught it, passed it to Derren, and Derren held it through the opening.

Harken took it. He hung it from the new board.

“The living crew is clear,” he said. “The warning has been received.”

The relay keeper in the silver did not move.

Saikou looked at the old hooks. The recorded man had gone to ask. His warning had not been carried on the right hook. They had brought the sign back. They had carried the signal out. They had named its sender. There was still the receiving station’s own act, the return acknowledgment which told the sender that he did not have to strike again.

“Back down the line,” Saikou said.

Harken turned to him.

“The answer. Give it back.”

Harken pulled the old lever.

It resisted. He put his shoulder beneath it, braced a boot against the lower wall, and lifted. A rusted joint broke with a report like a cracked tooth. The lever moved into the position Saikou had seen only in reflection.

“Received,” Harken said.

The bell gave one small answering note as the line tightened against its repaired clapper.

In the mirror the lamplighter’s arm stopped.

Saikou watched it stop. The hand remained raised for one heartbeat, two. Then the man lowered it to his side. His lamp was still on the floor. He looked towards the place where the return acknowledgment had arrived.

The silver emptied.

There was a room, a board, a crooked lever. Saikou’s face showed in the far edge, distorted by the crack. No other face stood beside it.

Neth said, “Now.”

They left.

Saikou could not help carry the bell. Harken did not try to bring it. He released the sling from its temporary hook and left the instrument resting on the broken stand. Its duty had finished where it had begun. If the roof took it now, the roof would take bronze and iron.

Aven led Sanna through the opening. Derren followed them once Harken came out. Neth removed the gate rod and handed it to Saikou, who could carry that much with his right hand.

At the first turn the chamber roof settled. The sound travelled up the wall beside them. Saikou looked back and saw dust spread across the opening like flour dropped through a sieve. Neth’s last wedge disappeared into it.

The rope remained taut.

At the upper junction Harken tested the air again. Derren checked each person. Saikou wanted to sit down. He did not, because sitting would make the arm’s pain larger and he still had stairs to climb. Isset came to his right side without asking for an account of his injury.

Aboveground the crew was waiting with the small signal bell. Its keeper held a second piece of white cloth. They had practised the acknowledgment with scraps cut from an old sheet. One corner still carried an embroidered flower.

Harken looked at the cloth. Then he went to the chalkboard where the shifts were listed and wrote west face closed for survey. He added no reopening day.

Orel sat on a bench with his leg propped up. He had come to hear the warning. He looked at Saikou’s arm, which Isset was now supporting.

“Did it work?”

Saikou took a breath. He did not count it.

“The warning finished.”

Orel looked past him at Sanna.

She stood beside Aven with both hands against her coat. Her eyes were dry. Saikou had seen people stare like that after coming up from water, when everyone wanted their survival to look like gratitude.

Maela crossed the square and took the basket from beneath the chapel pew. She brought it to Sanna, held out its handle, and waited until Sanna took it.

Chapter 12 — What was still there

The physician gave Sanna a cup of tea before she asked for one. It was weak, and the cup smelt of the cupboard where it had been kept. Sanna drank because her hands knew how to hold it.

Saikou sat on the other side of the room, his arm wrapped against a thin board. The physician was telling him what he could not do. He looked increasingly injured as her list continued.

“How long?” he asked.

“Longer if you keep asking whether a particular foolishness counts.”

Harken stood at the door. Mud had dried on his socks. He had brought the mine’s medical account book, which the physician ignored until he put it on the table and asked whether she needed a signature.

“I know where you live,” she said.

“That isn’t a signature.”

She looked at him, then opened the book.

Sanna watched them write. Saikou’s injury had an outline. Bone, board, binding, time. She did not begrudge him the outline. She wanted one.

Maela took her home. They walked slowly. At the bakery window a woman had put a pair of lamps close together and hung their repaired shades above them. Sanna noticed that one shade had a new patch darker than the other cloth. She could still see things exactly.

Inside her kitchen the failed drawing lay on the table where she had left it. Maela reached to put it away.

“Leave it.”

Sanna sat and turned the paper over. Broken tooth. Scar by chin. Left eyelid low when tired. She read the words. A man could be assembled from those words, as a man could be assembled from a short coat and an unlaced boot. She could imagine such a man. He was not Edran.

She turned the page back to the mouth.

“Was it like that?” she asked.

“I didn’t know him then.”

Sanna knew. Maela had come to the street years later, with two children and a husband who had subsequently left. Sanna had told her stories of Edran until Maela could speak of him as though he had once sat at their table. There was no reason that gift should now make Maela an eyewitness.

“No,” Sanna said. “Of course.”

“I can ask someone older.”

“Not today.”

Maela filled the kettle. Sanna looked at Edran’s chair. She knew the wear on its left arm came from his thumb rubbing the wood. She knew he had shaved outside when the weather allowed, and indoors when the weather did not, leaving soap where she would find it with a sleeve. She knew he disliked being called brave. She knew he had been afraid of a tooth extraction and angry with himself for telling her so.

She could remember being angry with him. That came whole, embarrassingly whole. He had put a wet coat on top of the sheets she had folded. She could see the coat. The sheets. Her own hands lifting them. Above the coat’s collar there was a place where seeing ceased.

She made a noise she had not intended.

Maela came back from the kettle and sat beside her. She did not touch her at once. Sanna took her hand, which was warm and had flour beneath one nail.

“I thought it would be a blur.”

Maela waited.

“It’s not. I know exactly where he is.”

The kettle began to hiss. Neither woman moved until the sound rose into a thin shriek. Then Maela stood, took it off the heat, and made fresh tea.

Sanna drank this cup more slowly.

Aven called in the afternoon. She brought the chamber record and a small stoppered jar of ointment for a scrape on Sanna’s wrist. Sanna had not noticed the scrape. It lay beneath her coat’s cuff, where the cord had pulled against her skin.

“The keeper would like to come,” Aven said. “If you wish.”

“Not yet.”

Aven nodded and put the ointment down.

“Did her bell cost her anything?”

“It cannot be used again. She had carried it twelve years.”

“Was she saving it?”

“Yes.”

“For what?”

“She hadn’t told me.”

Sanna thought of the small case, the repaired strap, the untouched rim. There was comfort in learning that something had been spared for her which had not first been called insignificant. There was also a temptation to weigh it against her own payment. She left that alone.

“What will they do with it?”

“Keep it among the instruments that have been used.”

“Will it have my name?”

“If you permit it.”

“Edran’s,” Sanna said. “And the station keeper whose name we don’t know. Say that we don’t know it.”

Aven wrote the request on a separate slip.

The room darkened. Outside, men carried a length of pipe along the street, calling to one another at the corners. Their voices were ordinary and irritating. Sanna realized she had been waiting for the blow inside her chest. She had waited for it while reading the paper, while talking to Maela, while listening to Aven. It had not come.

She touched her breastbone through her dress.

Aven saw the movement and did not ask.

After the visitor left, Sanna made dough. Maela stood at the table with her. They measured together, flour dusting the clean page on which Aven had left the account. Sanna moved the page away before the water could spill.

“Salt,” Maela said.

“I know.”

“You said you were teaching me.”

Sanna gave her the bowl. “Then you put it in.”

Maela did. The dough came together under their hands. Sanna could still tell when it needed more water. She could still distinguish the right resistance from a dough made too stiff by somebody impatient to be finished. She could still be impatient herself.

When it rested, she took out her daughter’s letter and wrote another.

This time she put down the price. She described the chamber, the warning, the face. She stopped twice to correct sentences that made the thing sound smaller than it was. She did not write that she was all right. She wrote that she had eaten, that Maela was baking with her, that she meant to come when the mine could settle its account for the week. Then she crossed out the last condition.

She meant to come.

At supper Maela’s younger son knocked and asked whether the bread was ready. He looked at Sanna with the plain alarm of a child who had heard adults saying something had happened and been given no adequate account.

“Are you still you?” he asked.

Maela closed her eyes.

“Yes,” Sanna said.

“What did you lose?”

She looked at the loaf cooling on the board.

“Something I wanted to keep.”

He considered that. Then he asked whether she wanted him to leave.

“No. Wash your hands.”

He did not wash them well enough. Maela sent him back. Sanna cut the loaf and put the first slice on the plate where he would sit.

Chapter 13 — Above the count

Saikou spent his first day of silence listening to other people explain why he had stopped speaking. Neth had asked for a day, as payment for what the under-mason had taught him. The request had sounded easy while Saikou still had a usable arm and work belowground to distract him.

At breakfast Corren asked whether silence was required after an invocation. Saikou shook his head.

“Is it mourning?”

Another shake.

“Have you lost your voice?”

Saikou pointed at the slate beside his cup. On it he had written listening to stone.

Corren read it and looked towards the wall.

“Here?”

Saikou closed his eyes. The young man took the hint eventually, though not the intended one, and brought him a pillow.

Neth came after breakfast. He sat in the chair beside the window and put his hammer on the sill. A piece was missing from its head. The pale stripe ended in a fresh fracture.

Saikou pointed to it.

“The last wedge,” Neth said. “Stone in stone. It took the wrong edge.”

Saikou wrote how much.

Neth shook his head. “Not money. I’ll need a good piece of that seam. Takes choosing.”

Saikou added I can help.

The under-mason looked at the board on his arm. “Later.”

They sat through the hour in which the square filled with people who could not work in the mine and were therefore doing work elsewhere. A cart arrived with the new pump chain. Harken counted its links in the yard. Two workers made a temporary gutter along the chapel wall. Maela’s younger son carried a loaf to the gate, walking carefully until he thought no one could see him, then running.

Neth did not ask Saikou what he heard. Once, when a wagon crossed a loose paving stone, he tapped the sill. Saikou felt the answering vibration through the heel of his hand.

That evening, after the day was done, Saikou went to the archive.

Pella had prepared a new cover for the disaster account. It included a note of Sanna’s testimony, the chapel record, and Saikou’s corrected description of the relay keeper. He asked to read the copy before she stitched it in. She had retained the crossed-out word received in the account of the first inspection, alongside a clear reference to the later testimony.

“Will people understand that?” he asked.

“They might if they read both.”

“Can we make them?”

“No.”

He paid for another copy. She wrote his name on the receipt, slowly enough that he could recognize it. It appeared beside the copying fee and the amount outstanding on the first account. He had been remembered as a customer, a witness, and a man who still owed two coins.

Outside the archive Harken waited with the survey report.

The chamber had collapsed. So had the end of the emptied upper gallery. The west face remained recoverable if they installed a separate pump circuit and cut a new return. Harken could employ twenty-three for the first part of that work, though only sixteen would draw full wages. The buyer had refused to improve his terms. The outer quarries had accepted four of the men Harken had asked them to take, not five.

“No falls since,” Harken said.

“How many shifts below?”

“Six.”

Saikou looked at him.

“I know,” Harken said. “Six is six. I haven’t written forever.”

He unfolded the report to show the new route. It avoided the old reserve chamber. At every warning station the signals would have a fixed home on the board. A missing sign would mean clear the workings until it was replaced. The rule had annoyed two experienced miners, who said nobody could work that way if children stole a marker for a cart. Harken had told them to stop letting children use the markers.

“The water sign was taken by a grown man,” Saikou said.

“Then I’ll tell him too.”

Harken smiled for the first time since the bell’s descent. The smile did not make him younger. It made Saikou aware that he had been holding his own face in preparation for Harken’s arrival.

They walked to the gate. Derren had made new slates. She no longer called the marks breaths, and corrected anyone who did. Orel sat outside sorting damaged links. His leg was healing. The physician had refused to certify him for descent, so Harken paid him for salvage work until he could walk the stair without relying on a rail.

Isset’s daughter had painted the small signal bell’s frame red. Harken had objected, then asked her to paint the other side as well. The paint was uneven. Nobody had yet suggested it made the signal less reliable.

Saikou stood above the stair and listened. Below, men moved pipes and called measurements to one another. Their warning bell sounded during a test. The gate keeper acknowledged it through the line. Saikou felt the sound in his chest and waited for it to seize a number.

It remained a sound.

He left his final report with Derren. She read the conclusion twice.

“Ceased following completed warning,” she said. “Not dispersed?”

“I saw the recording end. Dispersed would suggest I knew where it went.”

“And the bell?”

“In the chamber.”

“Worth a great deal of bronze.”

“Yes.”

She closed the report. “We won’t send somebody down to price it.”

At the chapel, Corren showed him the threshold after the next expected crossing. The wet line had not returned. He had swept the stone before dark and left a thread of flour along the outside edge to show a passing foot. Wind had disturbed it. So had a mouse. Corren had spent much of the night deciding which mattered.

“Did you stay behind the inner door?” Saikou asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Do I keep staying there every ninth night?”

“For another crossing. Tell Lumenor what you observe.”

Corren looked disappointed. Saikou knew he had hoped for an ending which released him from paying attention.

Lumenor was at the under-altars. Aven brought Saikou a copy of the used instrument’s account. It named Edran of the third lamp and an unnamed keeper of the reserve relay. It named Sanna as the living witness. The price was set down in the same hand as the service, on the same page. Saikou read it and found nothing he wanted to correct.

When he uncovered the mirror to pack it that night, the silver showed two lamp flames where there was only one lamp in the room. He shifted the angle. One flame remained. He waited without drawing a conclusion, then wrote the observation in his journal beneath the case account. A small disturbance did not have to be a summons. It still belonged in the record.

Sanna left three days after that.

Her daughter had come to fetch her with a hired cart and a manner Saikou thought he recognized from a woman he had met in another kitchen. She checked the basket, the blanket, the medicine jar, and the letter she had brought to say that she had come. Sanna told her to stop fussing. Her daughter said she would when somebody else began.

Maela had baked the travelling loaf. It was too light for the carters and right for people who would eat it cold on the road. Sanna examined the crust, then wrapped it without criticism.

Saikou held the cart steady while she climbed. His good hand was on the shaft. Her daughter took the basket. When Sanna sat down, she looked towards her house, where Maela stood with the key.

“Don’t move the chair,” she said.

Maela nodded.

Sanna turned to Saikou. “You owe Harken money.”

“Yes.”

“He says you’ll earn it.”

“I intend to.”

“He says intending doesn’t mend a pump.”

“An accurate man.”

Sanna smiled. Then she looked at him carefully.

“I still can’t see him.”

“I know.”

“Don’t write that I’ve learned to do without.”

“I won’t.”

She took the copy of the mine account from her basket. Edran’s name showed on the outer fold, where Pella had written it for her. She put it back beside the loaf.

The cart began to move. Her daughter told the driver to take the lower road because the higher one had a loose bridge. Sanna said she knew about the bridge. Her daughter said that was excellent and they would take the lower road.

Saikou stood until the cart turned out of the square.

He went to Harken’s office to collect the letter of undertaking he would carry to the next hold. Harken had added the mine seal, which would make the debt harder to deny and the work easier to obtain. Saikou folded it inside his journal.

On the way out he bought bread from Maela. She had put enough salt in it. He ate a piece before the first climb, saved the rest for the road, and kept his injured arm close while he walked.

The Ghoul of the Sewers

Chapter 1 — The things that stayed

The button had four holes and a little bridge of thread across two of them. Zel kept finding it with her tongue.

She had not swallowed it. She knew she had not swallowed it because it lay in her palm, where she could see the green enamel worn away at the rim. Nevertheless, when she closed her eyes, something hard touched the back of her teeth. She could feel the coat giving way. The man’s shoulder had come out of it with the noise a cabbage leaf made when it was pulled from the head.

He had screamed after she told him not to.

She did not blame him for screaming. She had told him because she could not bear the noise, and now she could not bear the quiet either. Beyond the culvert, Port Shed was waking. Water slapped the steps below the spice warehouses. A pulley squealed. Somebody on the quay swore at somebody else, using a name Zel knew, and for a moment she was so pleased to recognize it that she forgot the button.

Then the taste returned.

She pushed the button into a crack beside the child’s shoe. The shoe was brown, with a strap much too long for it. It had been there when she came to the chamber. There was no child. She had looked. At first she had thought the shoe a reason to leave; later it had become a reason to come back. Anyone returning for it would need to find it in the same place.

The chamber had once been a settling tank. Its wall was a circle of pale brick, narrowed at the top by a ledge where old lime had dried in ridges. At low tide Zel could lie along the ledge without getting wet. At high tide she sat with her knees beneath her chin and watched bubbles come out of the lower passage. The great sluice farther down had a broken tooth. Each time it lifted, iron knocked stone twice, paused, and knocked again. She had begun to count the knocks by the things she needed to do before the next one.

Wash her hands. Move the letters. Stop looking at her hands.

Her mother had sent seven letters. The first three asked her to come home. The fourth told her that the upstairs room had been let to a sailmaker and that she would have to use the smaller bed, which her mother hoped would make coming home sound less like an event. The fifth was about a neighbour’s hens. The sixth said she was sorry about the hens, she had not known what else to put. The seventh had no greeting.

I will be at the north mouth after the second shift. Do not make me go down the steps.

Zel had gone to the north mouth. That was six nights ago. Her mother had brought blood in a covered bowl, still warm through the cloth, and a comb which Zel had left behind when she went away. The bowl had helped. It always helped before it stopped helping.

The comb had twelve teeth, all of them sound. Zel had broken one trying to get the dried knot out of her hair. She had not gone back to tell her mother.

A rat crossed the ledge. It saw her, stopped, and turned round so quickly that its tail went over its own head.

For nearly two years she had caught animals instead of asking anyone why ordinary meals had begun tasting like wet rags. She was quick enough to catch a rat beneath a sack. Afterwards she hid the little bones, washed, and went back to work. At first that had kept her ordinary for days. Later it bought an evening. She came below when an evening stopped being enough, and Anja began bringing bowls when Zel could no longer come home to steal them herself.

“Good,” Zel said.

Her voice sounded young in the round chamber. That offended her. She was twenty-six. She had earned wages, rented a room, kissed someone she did not like enough to kiss a second time, and once stayed awake all night deciding whether to leave Port Shed. It was unfair that fear had brought her back to the age at which people asked where her mother was.

She took the fourth letter out of its oilcloth. The corner had gone soft. Her mother had put the sailmaker’s name in full, as if a stranger’s presence might assure her that ordinary life continued upstairs. Zel pictured the man spreading his work over her old bed. He would find the place where she had carved a notch into the frame to mark the height of a kitten. The kitten had never stood still long enough to be measured properly. There were six notches and no useful information.

She laughed. The sound caught. For a while she held the page without reading it.

Footsteps came from the north passage.

Not her mother’s. The soles were hard, and there were two people, one keeping farther back. Zel put the letter away. She rose before she meant to, the movement so quick that her head struck the overhang. It did not hurt enough.

“Girl?” a man called.

She knew that voice. Rowan spoke to everyone as if he had just found them doing something amusing. He had once taken her mother’s washing across the quay on his cart because rain was coming. He had asked no money for it. Afterwards, whenever Anja said he was a rogue, she added that he had helped with the washing, and Zel had grown tired of hearing the qualification.

“There’s food,” he said. “We’ve brought food.”

The second person breathed through his mouth. Zel could hear a little scrape in his throat, and below it, more quietly, the blood moving at his jaw. She shut her eyes.

“Leave it,” she said.

“We can’t leave this kind.”

Something grated on the brick. A cage. Inside it an animal threw itself against wire, stopped, and began throwing itself again.

“A piglet,” Rowan said. “Small one. You’re not expected to eat the crate.”

Zel opened her eyes. The hunger had become direction. It had been everywhere inside her; now it ran out through the passage and pulled. She put a palm against the wall.

“Take it away.”

Rowan said something to his companion, too low for an ordinary person to hear. Zel heard it distinctly.

She can still talk. Good.

The words made a little space between herself and the pulling. She used it to sit down.

“I don’t want a live one.”

“What do you want, then?”

“My mother knows.”

“Your mother doesn’t know what to do with you. That’s why you’re down here.”

She could have crossed the passage before either man understood she had moved. The knowledge sat pleasantly inside the hunger. Their arms were soft. The cage had a weak hinge. She could take the pig, go back along the wall, and let them tell each other they had seen a shadow.

She stayed seated. She looked at the child’s shoe.

Rowan’s companion shifted his feet. He was frightened now; she could hear the change. It made Rowan sound brave when he spoke again.

“Listen. I know a man who understands these things. A learned man. He’s seen what happens to people who drink from the wrong place. He doesn’t think hiding is a treatment.”

Zel had drunk from the well because the yard pump was broken. The afternoon had been hot. Everyone had drunk. She had been the last, after the man with a red sash took something out of the curb and dropped it into his sack. She remembered the shape: a white oval, broken through the middle, with a dark line down one side. She had thought it a piece of pot. He had told her the water was sound.

She had believed him because he was paid to tell people such things.

“Is your learned man here?” she asked.

“No. There’s a ship. Good cabins. Your mother could come as far as the outer road.”

The piglet gave one thin squeal. Zel put both hands over her ears. That made no difference at all.

“I can’t go on a ship.”

“Not every ship’s a coffin, girl.”

She looked up sharply. Rowan could not see her face, but he heard her breath catch, and mistook it for agreement.

“We’ll come again at dark. Think about what I’ve said. It needn’t go through Kes. This can be yours.”

He took the cage away. His companion waited until Rowan had gone some distance before following him. Zel could still hear the piglet after their feet disappeared.

She pressed her knuckles between her teeth. Once she had bitten through the skin. She had been ashamed of that until she discovered her own blood brought her no relief whatsoever. Then shame had been replaced by a colder disappointment which she could not explain to anyone, even in a letter.

She took out a sheet. There was half a pencil wrapped in the fold, stolen from a clerk’s open window. She sharpened it against the brick and wrote her mother’s name.

Rowan came.

She stared at that. Her mother would go to Rowan. Her mother would think a ship meant a place away from people who could hurt her daughter. Zel could see her asking about cabins, counting the coins in her bag, making herself pleasant while afraid. She drew a line through the sentence so hard the paper tore.

Above, the pulley stopped. A man began singing the yard song. Eight blows, then the empty place. Zel knew how to work through the missing beat. On that beat you changed hands, or lifted the wedge, or put down what you could not lift any longer. As a girl she had wondered whether the dead apprentice had made a useful silence on purpose. Her father said dead apprentices ought not to be asked to help with production.

He was dead himself now. Nobody had made a song for him. He had died in bed after a winter fever, with his wife angry because he would not drink, and Zel in the doorway holding a bowl which had already gone cold.

She could remember his voice. It did not bring his face with it every time. That was beginning to happen to everyone.

She put down the pencil. She took the button out of the crack and held it while the workers sang above her. When the missing beat came, she rubbed the bridge of thread with her thumb.

The man whose coat it had been was alive. She knew that much. He had run from her, dropping a shoe on the steps, and she had carried the shoe out after him because it was his. He had screamed again when he saw her coming.

She had left it on the quay.

For the rest of the morning she tried to remember whether he had taken it.

Chapter 2 — A quiet commission

Old Kes offered Saikou breakfast before he offered him money. Saikou distrusted commissions which began with breakfast, but the bread was fresh, and he had come straight from a boat whose cook believed anything pickled was a vegetable.

Kes watched him eat. He was a Sidhi with a broad face and a beard divided by a scar beneath the lip. Saikou remembered him younger, shouting measurements across a wet deck while a customs officer asked whether the vessel would be ready to sail. Kes had answered that it would be ready when he could walk on it without falling into the cargo. The customs officer had not enjoyed the answer.

“You’ve heard about the woman,” Kes said.

“I’ve heard about three women. One of them married the magistrate.”

“Under the warehouses.”

“Yes.”

“And you’ve heard nobody’s dead.”

Saikou broke the bread. “Are you certain?”

“All the missing ones have come back.”

“How many missing ones?”

“Three.”

“How many found?”

Kes looked towards the door. A young clerk stood there with a tablet, waiting to be noticed or dismissed. Kes dismissed him.

“Three,” he said. “One with a wound. Two frightened. The watch wants to send men below. I said the spring dredging was underway and they ought to wait for the channels to clear.”

“Is it underway?”

“It will be.”

The room overlooked the cutter yard. Narrow hulls stood above channels of mud, braced on props which looked inadequate until one understood how the weight had been distributed. Apprentices carried timber between them. A woman with a shaved head was painting a line on a bow while the owner instructed her from below. She dipped the brush, ignored him, and continued at the same pace.

Saikou liked the yard better than the room.

“What are you asking?” he said.

“Find out why she’s still hungry.”

That was an interesting question. Saikou put down the bread.

Kes reached beneath the table and brought out a small cloth purse. He did not push it across. “Her mother has been providing food. Three months. I’ve paid for it. A month ago we thought she was better. Then a lighterman came out of the north drain with his arm torn open.”

“Why did you think she was better?”

“She stopped coming up.”

Saikou looked at him. Kes gave a slight, impatient movement of the shoulder.

“What would you have called it?”

“I’d have called it being out of sight.”

Kes took that badly, which was not Saikou’s purpose, although it was not wholly unwelcome. There were mornings when he resented being asked to turn another man’s useful ignorance into a professional judgement.

“Her name?”

“Zel.”

“Her mother’s?”

“Anja. Lives above the net shop on Basket Street.”

“May I speak to her?”

“I won’t stop you.”

“Would she expect you to?”

Kes tightened the purse cord. “I don’t want this going before the Admiralty. They hear ghoul, they send fire. Then everybody has questions about which store wall came down with the drain, and nobody remembers why they started burning.”

Saikou glanced towards the hulls. The yard song began outside, mallets keeping time. He knew a version from his childhood and found himself quietly supplying the last words. On the next phrase he came in at the missing beat.

The mallets continued. A boy beneath the window looked up.

Kes smiled for the first time. “You haven’t worked here.”

“Apparently not.”

“Don’t do that down there.”

“Why?”

“Because they won’t like you.”

Saikou looked at the boy, who was still looking back. He had investigated things which ate the names of towns and once persuaded a dead admiral to acknowledge a mistake. He could nevertheless be defeated socially by a sixteen-year-old holding a mallet.

“How much?” he asked.

Kes loosened the purse and laid out coins. A sufficient sum for investigation, with the promise of more when the matter was concluded. Saikou asked for the first sum now and accepted no definition of conclusion beyond a written report and the return of living persons where possible. Kes hesitated over the word persons. Saikou waited until he wrote it.

It was not protection for Zel. It was a nuisance Kes would have to undo if he wished to say she had never been included.

The wounded man was named Tevi. Saikou found him at his sister’s house, sitting beside a window with his left arm bound to a board. The sister, Sen, had put all her sewing within reach of his good hand so he could hand her things and stop pretending he was not in pain.

“You’re the ghoul man?” she said.

“I have been called worse.”

“I mean the man who knows.”

“About some of them.”

She let him in reluctantly. Tevi would not look at him until he laid his satchel on the floor instead of keeping it between them.

The arm smelled clean. Saikou asked who had dressed it. A surgeon at the dock clinic; he had been twice, and Tevi had missed the third visit because he could not bear walking past the north mouth. Sen said she would take him by another street. Tevi said he could go himself. They had evidently had the argument before.

Saikou examined the wound with permission. Four narrow tracks ran into the forearm. The skin around them had gone pale, but not swollen. Tevi could move two fingers well. A third moved late, after he had stopped trying, and the fourth did not move at all.

“She said she was sorry,” he told Saikou.

“While she was hurting you?”

“Before. After. I don’t know.”

Sen began threading a needle, missed its eye, and began again. Saikou watched Tevi’s face rather than the arm.

“I thought she was caught,” Tevi said. “Someone crying down there. I had a hook. I went to see if I could reach her.”

He stopped.

“You needn’t give me the rest today.”

“I can give it.”

Saikou waited. Outside, a cart passed with something loose knocking against its side. Tevi put his good hand over the bandage.

“Her mouth was wrong. I don’t mean the teeth. I mean she looked as if she was trying to shut it with her whole face.”

Sen set down the needle.

“She didn’t let me go when I asked,” Tevi said. “I want that put somewhere. People keep telling me she didn’t mean it.”

Saikou took out his notebook.

He wrote Tevi’s words as spoken. Then he asked about the shoe. Tevi looked startled.

“What shoe?”

“One was found outside the drain.”

“Mine. I went back for it next morning. I didn’t have another pair.”

Saikou closed the book. Tevi had not been willing to return to the clinic, but he had gone back to the drain for a shoe. He had not felt that contradiction needed explaining. Neither did Saikou.

There were other changes. Hot food tasted of burned rope. At night Tevi woke with the impression that somebody was standing just outside the room. Sen had slept on the floor the first two nights, until he shouted at her for breathing too quietly. Saikou had seen such injuries fade. He had also seen them persist long after flesh healed. He said both things.

“Can you stop her?” Sen asked.

“Yes.”

“Will you?”

“I will stop her hurting people.”

Tevi turned his head towards the window. Saikou could not tell whether that was agreement or exhaustion. He left money for the next clinic visit, which Sen accepted after asking whether he intended it as a fee for their silence.

“No,” he said.

“Then don’t come back asking for that.”

He promised he would not.

At the yard the mallets had stopped. A dredge crew were quarrelling beside a chain drum. Their foreman had one trouser leg rolled above the knee and stood on a plank as if he meant to address a congregation.

“We can take the north mouth,” he said. “We cannot take it on the old rate.”

Old Kes stood below him. “You haven’t taken it on any rate.”

“Because there’s a woman with teeth in it.”

Saikou came closer. The foreman looked down at him.

“Are you here to remove the teeth?”

“Not yours, I hope.”

The crew laughed. Kes did not. The foreman stepped off the plank and introduced himself as Hadrik, then asked whether Saikou had worked a dredge.

“A marine’s answer would be yes. A dredger’s would be no.”

“Good. Stay off the drum.”

Saikou inspected the map they had pinned to a crate. The north drain emptied into a channel crowded with stakes and abandoned foundations. Cleaning it would open a quicker route for the shallow cutters. It would also empty the chamber where Zel had been seen. Hadrik marked the proposed cut with a dirty thumb.

“By the next high tide she has nowhere to sit.”

Kes said, “We cannot hold the whole harbour for her.”

“No,” Saikou replied. “But we can refrain from flushing her into it before I’ve spoken to her.”

Hadrik nodded at his men. “That’s another day’s wages.”

Kes looked at Saikou. It was going to be an expensive investigation before anybody opened a sewer gate.

Chapter 3 — Her mother’s bowl

Anja had put too much salt in the blood again.

Zel knew before she lifted the cover. The smell came through the chipped edge of the bowl, underneath the sharper smell of her mother’s coat. Anja had been at the fish yard. She had washed, but the cuffs retained it, and Zel could hear the little catch in her breathing which meant her back hurt.

“Don’t stand there,” Zel said.

“I haven’t come to stand.”

Anja set the bowl on the second step. She had brought a stool. It was absurd: the legs were too wide for the stair, and once she had turned it sideways there was nowhere to put her feet. Zel found herself watching it instead of the bowl.

“Take that home.”

“Your letter said you wanted to sit with me.”

“I didn’t say bring furniture.”

“I was supposed to sit in the slime?”

Zel almost smiled. Anja saw the movement and pushed too hard.

“There. You’re still in there.”

The smile went. Her mother looked down and pretended to examine the stool.

Above them, the mouth was a rectangle of daylight so pale that Zel could bear it if she did not look directly at the water beyond. The last time she had gone into full sun, her hands had blistered without heat. A dock child had asked why she was wearing gloves in warm weather. Zel had said she had a rash. The child had asked to see it. His mother pulled him away by the shoulder, and the movement had made Zel’s mouth fill with saliva.

She sat below Anja. The bowl remained between them.

“Kes sent a man,” her mother said.

“I know.”

“Not to kill you.”

“You don’t know.”

“He said he wasn’t.”

“People say things.”

Anja shifted the stool, found a less precarious place for its front leg, and sat more firmly. “So do you. You said you would come last night.”

“I couldn’t.”

“You always say couldn’t. You used to say wouldn’t. I preferred that.”

Zel wanted to cry. It took effort now. Tears came slowly, and afterwards her eyes hurt as if she had rubbed them with grit. She could not bear being accused of something ordinary, then hated herself for wanting the illness to protect her from an accusation she deserved.

“I heard somebody at the mouth.”

“There’s always somebody.”

“He was hurt.”

Anja looked at the bowl.

“Was it the man?”

“What man?”

“The lighterman.”

Zel did not answer. Her mother leaned forward, and the stool tipped. Zel caught its leg without rising. The ease of that frightened them both. Anja sat back.

“He’s alive,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then we can make it right.”

Zel picked up the bowl and drank before she could answer cruelly.

The blood was thickening at the edges. Salt had kept it from smelling as quickly, her mother said; she knew how to keep meat. Zel had no words for what the salt did to the other thing inside it, the part which had helped when Anja first brought a basin straight from the slaughterman. This blood filled her mouth and slid down. Some stayed inside her. More came back, with a taste of iron turned bitter.

Anja took a cloth out of her sleeve. Zel pushed her hand away.

“Let me.”

“You’ve got it on your chin.”

“I know where my chin is.”

She wiped it herself. Anja folded her hands over the useless cloth.

There had been better days. Once the bowls had been large and warm, and Zel had lain on the ledge while her mother described somebody’s disastrous attempt to dye a wedding shirt. For nearly an hour she had thought about the shirt instead of Anja’s throat. She had begun to imagine a room with a window facing north, a door her mother could open, a job nobody would have to explain to the whole port. Then the bowls grew smaller because the butcher complained, and came later because Anja had work. Zel had said they were enough. She had needed them to be enough.

“There’s more tomorrow,” Anja said.

“How much?”

Her mother hesitated. Zel looked up.

“How much, Mother?”

“Like that.”

“This isn’t enough.”

Anja’s face tightened, with fear first and then the anger she used when fear could get nothing done.

“I can’t bring a whole pig every night.”

“I didn’t ask for a pig.”

“Tell me what you are asking for, then.”

Zel put down the bowl. Something moved at the back of the passage. She listened. Water, a loose stone, nothing living. When she turned back her mother had put out a hand.

She had meant to touch Zel’s cheek.

The small scrape on her wrist was new. A fish spine, perhaps, or a splinter from the rack. One bead had come through where she bent the skin. Zel could hear her pulse under it.

Her mother’s hand was in her mouth before she understood that she had stood.

Anja did not scream. That was worse. She made one short, astonished noise, and Zel felt the bones of the wrist beneath her teeth. The blood was warm, absurdly warm. There was so little, and it reached everywhere.

For a moment the whole world had a single good thing in it.

Then Anja said her name.

She said it angrily, the way she had when Zel broke a jug by trying to catch it with another jug. The sound struck through pleasure. Zel opened her mouth. Her mother fell against the stair.

Zel backed down three steps and hit the wall. She could not remember crossing them. Anja was holding her wrist with her other hand, the cloth already pressed against it. Her face had gone grey.

“Go up,” Zel said.

“Let me—”

“Go up.”

“It’s a scratch.”

Zel could see that it was not a scratch. Two holes darkened the cloth. A strip of skin hung where it should not hang. Her mother was looking at it now, trying to decide which lie would let her stay.

“If you don’t go, I’ll do it again.”

Anja looked at her daughter. For the first time in three months she did not answer at once.

“Will you be here?” she asked.

“No.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know.”

Zel wanted to promise. She wanted to follow her mother upstairs, wash the wrist, listen while she complained about the stool and the bowl and the price of fish. She held herself against the wall instead. Anja climbed, taking one step at a time, pausing to breathe where the light began. She left the stool.

Zel waited until she could no longer hear her. Then she brought up what remained of the animal blood. Her mother’s blood stayed. She wished it would not.

At dusk Rowan’s men came without Rowan.

She heard four pairs of feet, and after them the cage dragged sideways to keep it from striking the wall. There was no pig in it now. The sound came with oil, rope, and a sharp smell she did not recognize until a man passed a lantern from hand to hand.

Silver. Not a blade: a mesh tied inside the cage door. Zel’s skin began prickling before she could see it.

She went into the lower tunnel.

One man called pleasantly that they had brought supper. Another swore because his boots had filled with water. Zel knew his voice. He was called Merrit, and she had once danced with him at a wedding. He had not stepped on her feet. She had enjoyed that much more than the dancing.

“Merrit,” she said.

Silence.

“Don’t come farther.”

“She’s that close?” a man whispered.

Merrit said nothing. The lantern moved. Zel could see their shadows stretched along the curve of the wall, great shoulders, small heads. One man had a hooked pole. He kept its end pointed at the ground, as if good manners altered what it was.

“Rowan says there’s a doctor,” Merrit said.

“Then bring him.”

“He can’t come down here.”

“He can’t come to Port Shed either?”

Merrit looked back at somebody. Zel could hear him turn. The hooked pole came up.

She ran.

The passage she chose had been too narrow for a person. She went sideways, one arm above her head, scraping both shoulders until skin peeled. Nothing followed except the smell of lantern oil. The hunger grew with movement; it burned through the brief warmth from Anja’s blood and began asking again. At the far end she broke through rotten slats into a drain she did not know.

There was a ladder. Above it, mallets and the yard song, the empty beat familiar enough to make her dizzy. She climbed halfway and stopped. A girl on the platform was working alone beneath a hull, holding a lamp between her feet while she drove a peg.

Zel had held a lamp like that. She had been nineteen, sweating inside her shirt, learning to fit a rib against a curve which refused to be the same curve twice.

The girl began the next verse. She waited through the silence and struck on the beat after it.

Zel put her head against the ladder rung.

Below her somebody kicked open the broken slats. Merrit called her name. Above, the girl shifted the lamp, and the light touched the ladder.

Zel looked from one to the other. She could go up. She could put a hand on the girl’s mouth before she shouted. She knew exactly how to do it.

Instead she dropped back into the water and turned towards the men.

“You want me,” she said. “Then keep it down here.”

The man with the hook saw her first. His face changed as he raised the pole. Zel caught its wooden shaft and pulled it from him. She could feel the thin weakness under his arm, the pulse frantic, his fear making everything taste stronger.

She broke the pole across her knee.

Merrit bolted past the cage. Somebody fell. Zel stood holding two pieces of wood while the lantern rocked and the men shouted different instructions. She threw the pieces at the silver mesh, not at their faces, and ran through the opening before they could agree on who would stop her.

She did not know which way she went. She followed water. When the water deepened she went along a pipe, and when that ended she hung by her hands until she found a ledge with her toes. At last she reached a little room whose floor was dry except for a stain beneath the door.

It was a place for storing lime. Empty sacks lay against the wall. She lay on them with her eyes open.

She had left her mother’s letters in the settling chamber.

For a while that mattered more than anything else.

Chapter 4 — A light upon the water

Anja opened the door with her left hand. Saikou saw the bandage before he saw her face, and disliked Kes a little more for having sent him to a house where somebody had already begun bleeding.

“She did that?” he asked.

Anja tried to put her wrist behind the door. “I caught it.”

“On what?”

“You don’t have to come in.”

“No. I would like to.”

She let him pass after a moment. The room smelled of fish soap and onions. An unfinished sleeve lay on the table beneath a spoon placed there to keep it flat. There were three cups on a shelf, two facing out and one turned round so that its broken handle could not be seen.

Saikou asked whether someone had dressed the wrist. She said a neighbour. He offered to look at it; she told him to find her daughter instead. He could respect that answer without enjoying it.

“Where was she when you last saw her?”

“The north stair.”

“Was she fed?”

“Yes.”

“Enough?”

Anja began moving things on the table. Saikou waited until she had arranged the spoon on three different parts of the sleeve.

“She said it wasn’t,” she told him.

He asked what she brought. Blood from a slaughterman near the south market, when he could spare it without asking questions. Salt to keep it, water when it thickened, a little broth once because her daughter looked so cold. He wrote down the amounts. Anja watched him, becoming angry in anticipation of something he had not yet said.

“I didn’t know,” she said.

“I don’t know enough yet either.”

“You look as if you do.”

“That is useful in my profession until it isn’t.”

She almost smiled. He asked for a bowl like the one she had used, and a piece of cloth Zel had worn recently. Anja brought a shirt from a chest. She held it for a long time before giving it to him.

“Will that bring her?”

“It may help me recognize a trace. I won’t call her through it.”

“Other people say they can.”

“They can sometimes call what recognizes the shirt.”

Anja gripped its sleeve. He let her take it back.

She found an old apron instead, with a burn near the hem. Zel had put it too close to a brazier. Her mother recounted the accident indignantly, and Saikou understood that the indignation was being offered to him as evidence. There had been a young woman careless with heat. She had not always lived beneath a quay.

He put the apron in his satchel. At the door Anja caught his sleeve with her good hand.

“Kes said you would keep this quiet.”

“I agreed to investigate.”

“He pays.”

“He does.”

“For the blood. For me to miss work.”

Saikou looked at her. Her fingers remained on his sleeve.

“He said the watch would kill her. I thought if he was helping, I should do what he asked.”

“What did he ask?”

“Not to make trouble.”

There were so many things a person could buy under that name. Saikou asked her to see the surgeon that afternoon, and told her he would return by dusk if he could. She insisted on keeping the apron until he explained that it would get wet whether he wore it or merely carried it. Then she said he could wear it if he wanted, but Zel would laugh at him.

He hoped so.

At the north mouth the cage lay on its side, abandoned with one hinge torn out. The rat-catchers had left a hook, a boot, and evidence of having frightened one another more thoroughly than intended. Saikou examined the silver mesh without touching its edges. Too little to stop a determined ghoul; enough to burn one into doing what a captor wanted. It had been purchased with an understanding of the difference.

He found Zel’s chamber empty. The letters were on the ledge, wrapped beneath a broken comb. He left them there and took out his mirror.

Its left edge had been cracked since the beach at Khemesh. In some lights the crack appeared to end before it reached the rim; in others it seemed to continue into the hand holding it. Saikou had stopped examining that after the first year. He set the mirror on the ledge where a smear of fresh skin marked the brick, and turned it until the lower passage filled the silver.

The Witness showed him a woman putting down a bowl.

He saw her hands, the scrape on her wrist, the younger woman’s face rising. Then water entered the reflection and broke it into bands. He had seen what the brick retained: proximity, contact, the last strong act laid over the older ones. He had not seen a reason. He did not need another witness to tell him whose bandage he had examined upstairs.

The mirror went dark.

Saikou folded it away. He could follow the torn slats without help from a patron. Beyond them he found a strip of cloth on a nail, then a wet handprint too high for an ordinary person travelling at that angle. At the junction the marks ended.

He took a flat, salt-worn stone from his pocket. It had been picked up at a waystation far inland, where every traveler insisted there was no beach. He held it loosely, letting his palm remain open.

The Traveler gave him no road. It gave him a draft against his knuckles, cooler than the water-smelling air of the passage. He followed it into a low pipe, removed his satchel to fit through, and found himself beneath a little iron hatch. The draft came through its broken rim.

He pushed. Nothing moved.

“Of course,” he said.

There was a bolt on the other side. He could hear somebody breathing above it, very slowly.

“Zel. My name is Saikou. Your mother asked me to look for you.”

Silence.

“She’s seen a surgeon.”

“Is she hurt?”

“Yes.”

The breath changed. Saikou wished he had a gentler answer which was equally useful.

“Will she lose her hand?”

“The surgeon hasn’t said that. There are things he can do if she lets him.”

“I told her to go.”

“I believe you.”

“I should have told her before.”

He stayed crouched beneath the hatch until his right thigh began shaking. The lime house had been placed above a drain deliberately; there would be another entrance. He did not go looking for it.

“I have food,” he said.

“Everyone does.”

“Mine is in a bottle. There is no cage.”

He had bought blood from the slaughterhouse on his way, unsalted and still warm when collected. It had cooled in his satchel. He did not know if that mattered. He set the bottle upright beneath the hatch.

“You can lift it without lifting me.”

After a long while the bolt moved. The hatch opened a little. He saw fingers stained with something which was not all mud. Zel looked down at him through the gap.

“That’s my apron.”

“Your mother said you wouldn’t mind.”

“She said I’d laugh.”

“She did.”

“You look stupid.”

“I suspected it.”

She took the bottle. He heard her drink, then stop. Her hand came back with the bottle empty.

“More?” she asked.

“Not here.”

She closed the hatch so quickly that the rim struck his knuckles. He bit back the word he wanted to say. It would have sounded enough like pain to interest her.

“Then go,” she said.

He did not. He waited until the pain in his hand settled, then told her about Tevi.

She made him stop after the first sentence. He stopped.

It took until the tide was coming in for her to open the hatch again. By then Saikou had convinced himself that he was too old for this work, that he had always been too old, and that a person with four patrons ought occasionally to be permitted a chair.

“If I come,” Zel said, “I want my letters.”

“We can get them.”

“And I don’t want my mother downstairs with me.”

“All right.”

“Don’t say all right as if I asked for another blanket.”

He looked up. Her face was in the opening now. It was gaunt and grey at the jaw, but the eyes belonged to the voice. She was angry. He was glad of that without wishing to say so.

“I will make a place where you can see her without being within reach,” he said. “If you want to see her.”

Zel stayed above him. Then she drew back, and he heard a door open elsewhere in the lime house.

He came out through the pipe with his satchel in front of him. When he found her at the side entrance, her hands were tucked into the apron she had taken back. She stood beneath the eave while the rain began. He offered his coat. She refused it because he was already wet, then took it when he admitted that he did not intend to become drier by arguing.

There was no safe room ready.

Old Kes had assumed Saikou would conclude his investigation underground. The yard had a disused tally office above the outer sluice, but its door faced a passage used by thirty workers and its window opened directly over water. Hadrik agreed to block the window, then refused to ask his men to work beside a hungry woman without being paid for the danger.

Saikou spent the last of the first purse before Zel had anywhere to sit.

The dock-syndic arrived while two men were lifting a shutter. Neral wore a plain brown coat and a whistle on a cord beneath it. Saikou had seen him make five boats give way to a sixth by asking its owner whether his mother was well. He asked no such question now.

“Kes hasn’t authorized a detention,” he said.

“She isn’t detained.”

“What is she?”

“Hungry.”

Neral looked past him at the closed office door. “That won’t answer the watch.”

“It answers me.”

“And who answers if somebody is hurt?”

Saikou had been expecting obstruction. The sensible question irritated him more. Before he could reply, a woman carrying a blue lantern came up the steps and told Neral to move because he was standing where the railing had given way.

Dalia was a lantern advocate. Saikou knew the title and had once mistaken it for a ceremonial office until a member of the profession kept him out of prison by noticing the tide had turned between his arrest and the filing of the charge. This one had her sleeves rolled above her wrists and a little glass water-timer tucked into the fastening of her coat.

“She can stay here until the morning hearing,” Dalia said. “If she agrees. I’ve brought the keeper’s warrant.”

Neral took the folded sheet.

“It authorizes a beacon.”

“And makes the sluice passage a service channel under the harbour court. The office becomes a refuge while the petition is heard. You may object after you’ve finished not falling through that railing.”

He stepped aside.

Dalia gave Saikou the lantern. Its blue panes would be visible from the water. The mirror-keeper’s seal was impressed below the signature, a web of fine lines around a plain circle.

“Who paid for this?” he asked.

“Isha waived the seal fee. She has interests in the new channel.”

“I’m sure she has.”

Dalia looked at him directly. “And I have an interest in not letting Rowan claim the woman as cargo before anyone has heard her speak.”

Saikou set the lantern in the high opening. Below, the water carried its blue light down the sluice towards the cutters.

He placed a line of salt inside the office threshold. Hadrik watched from one side; Dalia from the other. Zel sat on the floor far from all three, gripping the hem of her apron.

Saikou called on the Sentinel. The line brightened and became difficult to look across, as if the room beyond stood a little farther away than its wall. Zel rose involuntarily. When she touched the line, the barrier stopped her hand.

“Is it stopping the hunger?” Dalia asked.

Saikou looked at Zel. Her face had changed. She knew before he spoke.

“No,” he said. “It’s stopping her.”

“Then open it,” Zel said.

He lowered the ward. She put her palm on the empty threshold and remained there, breathing through her mouth.

Later, when another bowl had been brought and she had drunk it, she asked him to raise the barrier for the night.

Only then did he do it again.

Chapter 5 — The room above the sluice

The tally office had a smell of its own, older than Zel. Someone had spilled ink beneath the wall, and in wet weather the black stain shone through the limewash like a hole. There were scratches on the doorframe where clerks had measured one another’s height without admitting that they were bored. A shelf remained above the place where the desk had stood. Zel could reach it without standing on anything, which pleased her until she remembered why.

She put the letters there.

Saikou had fetched all seven, wrapped in a new piece of oilcloth which cost money he did not mention. He had also brought the comb, the child’s shoe, a tarnished ring, and three buttons. Zel told him those were hers. He looked at the ring, then at her.

“They’re here,” he said.

“That isn’t what I said.”

“I know.”

He left them on the floor beside the letters. After he went she put the ring in a crack where nobody would see it on entering. It had belonged to Iven, a ferryman whose wife died before Zel came to the sewers. He kept it on a cord round his neck. She had taken the cord because he was trying to pull away and she needed him close. Later she had found the ring caught between two bricks. He had not come back for it.

The blue lantern was in the opening above the shutter. Its light made her fingers look healthier. She wanted to smash it for that, then realized she had not noticed her mother’s blood on her thumb because everything looked blue.

She cleaned her hands with the water left inside the door.

The ward remained. Saikou had shown her how to call the watcher without crossing it. There was a little iron spoon and a pan hanging on the wall. Three blows meant she needed somebody. A separate hammering meant that they should fetch him before opening. The first time she struck the pan, a boy came so quickly that she apologized for waking him. He said he had not been asleep; he had been trying to remember how much he would be paid.

“You should write it down,” she told him.

“That’s what you people always say.”

“What people?”

“People who have pencils.”

She looked at the half pencil beside her letters. “You can borrow mine.”

He grinned. His name was Leto. He was seventeen and had come to the yard to learn work which would keep him off his uncle’s boat. His uncle took him aboard whenever there was nobody else. He said he preferred the smell of wood shavings to fish, then admitted that pitch was worse than either.

“What did you want?” he asked.

“I can’t sleep.”

“Neither can I, then.”

“I mean I don’t sleep.”

He looked at her. She could see him deciding whether to say something cheerful. He chose not to, which made her like him more.

“Want a game?”

She had not played tiles in three months. Leto brought a board and set it outside the threshold, then found that the ward distorted the lines if he put it too close. He moved it back. Zel could see well enough. She told him her moves; he played them, occasionally forgetting which piece she had named, and won because she became absorbed in hearing him complain about his uncle.

Her hunger did not go away. It sat behind the game, giving every pause a second purpose. Leto scratched his neck. Zel looked at the board.

“Your turn,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

She chose a piece. It was the wrong move. He pointed that out before taking it, then asked whether she wanted to choose again.

“No.”

He took it. The pleasure of being treated badly in an ordinary manner was so sudden she laughed. Leto looked relieved and began treating her considerably worse.

At dawn he was replaced by a woman named Omi, who brought a stool and no game. Omi had worked nights for fourteen years and regarded conversation before sunrise as a trick played by people who had not yet begun to ache. Zel let her sit quietly. Beyond the door she could hear the yard waking, bodies shifting under blankets, chains being lifted, a kettle lid struck against a post to call somebody who could not be bothered to answer his name.

She could hear every pulse.

When her mother came, the sun was above the warehouse roofs. Anja wore the coat with the sound cuffs and had put her bandaged arm inside it instead of using the sleeve. She looked well from a distance. Zel had learned that looking well depended enormously on distance.

“Are they feeding you?”

“Yes.”

“Properly?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Anja looked at the salt line. “What’s that for?”

“To keep me here.”

“They said you weren’t a prisoner.”

“I asked for it.”

Her mother stayed outside. Zel could have sat nearer, but the hunger had begun making suggestions, little movements so vividly imagined that she sometimes found her hands lifting before she had chosen them. She pulled the apron tight over her lap.

Anja brought a shirt. It was clean and mended. She had made the collar wider because Zel complained that things touched her throat all the time. There were small stitches where her mother usually used large ones; the injured wrist had changed the way she held the needle.

“You shouldn’t have done that.”

“It needed doing.”

“Someone else could.”

“Someone else could do many things. Do you want it?”

Zel said yes. Omi took the shirt through after Saikou returned and lowered the ward. He turned away while Zel changed. Omi did not turn away but examined the stitching approvingly and asked Anja what thread she had used.

For a few minutes the room contained two women discussing thread. Zel pulled the shirt over her head. It smelled of soap, and of the shelf beside the hearth. She put both hands flat against its front.

The next bowl arrived before noon. A butcher’s boy carried it under a cloth, with a note which Saikou read twice. He looked into the bowl, smelled it, and asked how long it had stood. The boy said since first killing. Saikou sent him back for another which had not been mixed with brine from the curing tubs.

“Blood is blood,” the boy said.

“No,” Zel told him.

He looked at her and left quickly.

The fresh bowl helped. The first swallowing went through her with such warmth that she almost dropped it. For several breaths she could not hear the boy’s heart outside. Then she could, but it was only a sound. She leaned against the wall and drank the rest slowly.

Saikou was watching. She wished he would look away, then thought of Tevi and held the bowl where he could see how much remained.

“What changes?” he asked.

She could not tell him in words which sounded reasonable. It was as if a hook behind her breastbone had loosened, and the rest of her could return to its own size. Her hands belonged to her again. Before, they had been things at the ends of hunger.

“I can hear you without wanting to come closer,” she said.

He wrote that down. He asked whether it lasted until evening. She said she hoped so. He crossed out whatever he had begun writing and waited beside her while Anja complained about a sailmaker who cut cloth across the wrong grain.

Zel had missed gossip. She had missed wanting to hear the next sentence without needing anything from the speaker’s body. She interrupted her mother twice to ask questions. Anja answered too loudly, delighted, and Zel told her to stop shouting.

Then they both laughed. Saikou took his notebook outside.

It did not last until evening.

By the time the yard shadows reached the door, the hook had tightened. Zel struck the pan. Omi came, looked at her, and went for Saikou without asking for a second signal. He brought what remained of the morning’s collection, now clotted beneath its lid. It helped less, but it helped.

“You’re getting better,” Anja said when she came back.

“No,” Zel answered.

Her mother looked wounded. Zel put the bowl down so that she would not grip it until it broke.

“I had a better hour. You keep doing that.”

“What?”

“Making an hour into the rest of my life.”

Anja sat on the stool outside the room. She looked at her bad wrist. Omi had gone to fetch water, and Saikou was speaking with somebody in the yard. Zel could hear the distant conversation: numbers, a slaughter time, somebody objecting to the length of a journey with a warm bowl.

“Kes paid you,” Zel said.

“Yes.”

“To keep me down there.”

“He said you’d be killed if anybody knew.”

“You knew people were getting hurt.”

“I knew you’d never wanted—”

“Mother.”

Anja stopped. Zel watched her, wanting her to become angry because anger was easier to bear than this face.

“I was frightened,” Anja said.

“So was I.”

“Yes.”

Zel looked at the shirt. The small stitches held. She could have pulled a seam apart with two fingers. She did not touch it.

“Did he pay enough?” she asked.

Her mother gave a small, astonished laugh. “Never.”

They sat for a while. At the door the blue lantern had begun to look brighter as the yard darkened. Somebody far below whistled, waited, then whistled again. Anja asked whether it was for a boat. Zel said she did not know.

She could hear oars. Four strokes, a pause, three more. A heavy object moved against wood.

She went to the blocked window. Through a narrow gap she saw a boat passing beneath the lantern. Its pilot looked up and raised a hand, though nobody had called to him. On his wrist was a strip of red cloth, tied to keep the sleeve from trailing in the water.

Zel backed away from the gap.

Her mother asked what she had seen. Zel said a boat. It was true, and much less than the truth, and for the first time she understood how an answer could be made small without a single word becoming false.

Chapter 6 — What the well had kept

The slaughterman complained that Saikou had made his apprentice afraid to carry a bowl.

“He was afraid before,” Saikou said. “He disguised it with an opinion.”

“I employ him to carry things, not develop character.”

The slaughterman’s name was Varo. He stood over a trough with blood up to his wrists and a knife resting on a ledge deliberately out of anyone else’s reach. His left eye had a white scar across its lower half. Saikou had been looking at it for several minutes before realizing the man turned his head differently to keep customers within the sound eye’s view.

“Your boy brought curing brine,” he said.

“He brought what was in the bucket.”

“What else goes in that bucket?”

Varo named several things. One was hot water from cleaning a bench. Saikou took his own bowl out of his satchel. The slaughterman looked at it as if it had accused his entire household of dishonesty.

“Separate,” Saikou said. “Unmixed. Collected before it reaches the floor.”

“Then you pay for somebody to stand with it.”

“I expected to.”

“And the bowl.”

“This is my bowl.”

“For being responsible for your bowl.”

Saikou smiled despite himself. They agreed a price. Varo had three killing periods most days, sometimes only two when boats brought no stock. He could reserve part of the blood, but he could not promise the same animal, and he did not propose to kill a goat nobody could sell merely to keep a woman comfortable.

Saikou said he did not require him to.

“You will,” Varo replied. “The day there isn’t any.”

They looked at one another. Saikou wrote that possibility beneath the figures instead of treating it as a reason to end the conversation. He had money for four days at the present rate. Four days was enough to learn something and very little else.

He took the first collection back himself. By the time he reached the office, the surface had begun thickening. Zel drank it slowly while he watched the movements in her throat. There was no difficulty swallowing, no violent sickness. She asked whether he intended to watch every mouthful she took for the rest of her life.

“Only until I know something useful.”

“You’ll need another stool.”

He grinned. She did not, but the line at her jaw had softened.

This time he had brought more. After the first bowl she said there was still something pulling. After the second she sat back and looked at Omi without moving towards her. After the third she refused another.

“That might be enough,” Saikou said.

Zel shut her eyes. “For what?”

He let the question remain with him while he marked the time.

At the morning hearing Dalia had obtained another two days, chiefly by forcing Rowan’s clerk to say aloud what species of property his employer believed he owned. The clerk began with payment for treatment and finished with damages to a cage. Between them he could produce no purchase, pledge or authority to restrain Zel. He became flustered when Dalia asked why a cage would be damaged during treatment.

The harbour magistrate was less interested in the cage than in the hungry woman’s location. He required two watchers when the door was opened, forbade work in occupied sheds until Saikou could give an account of the risk, and told Dalia that a refuge was not a permanent settlement.

She came out pleased and tired.

“Your precedent?” Saikou asked.

“The woman who survived drowning and was claimed by her own insurer. She was still a person even if inconveniently returned.”

“A ghoul isn’t drowned.”

“No. Rowan’s clerk spent an agreeable quarter-hour pointing that out.”

“And the magistrate?”

“Would rather hear another case. Which is why you must find something better before the two days expire.”

He asked about Isha. Dalia made a face, then took him to the mirror house.

It stood above a channel which had once been a street. The keeper had lined its upper room with small mirrors pointed through openings towards the water. Pilots watched those reflections at night, when a lamp on a distant stake might be hidden by the nearest hull. The mirrors required cleaning so frequently that Isha had an apprentice doing nothing else.

She was Lethai-ar, spare and dark-eyed, with a thin silver chain wound twice round her wrist. She listened while Saikou explained that a boat had passed beneath the new beacon carrying a man Zel recognized from the well.

“Recognition is not an offence,” Isha said.

“No.”

“Do you want that channel closed?”

“I want to know who uses it.”

“So does everybody who pays for a channel.”

She opened a little drawer and took out a list. The authorized passage served four warehouses, a private lighter station, and the cutter yard. Rowan rented space at the lighter station. His customers were not on the list.

“You must have known why the advocate wanted a refuge,” Saikou said.

“I knew she wanted a legal light. I gave her one.”

“And you gained a better route.”

Isha looked at him without embarrassment. “Yes.”

There was a tiny spider above the nearest window. It lowered itself on a thread, hung briefly in the passage of reflected light, and climbed again. Saikou watched it. Isha watched him watching.

“Do you have a charge?” she asked.

“Not against you.”

“Then don’t practise looking as if you do.”

He had deserved that. He asked for the earlier well inspection instead. Isha sent her apprentice to fetch it from a room where all the documents were wrapped against damp. The entry bore Kes’s authorization to clear an obstruction from the old curb. A separate line acknowledged the recovery of ceramic waste. There was no examination of the Veiled Sisters’ marking.

Saikou copied the names. He had no difficulty finding the worker with the red wrist strip. The man was mending a winch in Rowan’s rented shed. His name was Burl, and he had been paid for clearing a well, then paid again for carrying away the fragments.

“Who paid twice?”

“Kes for clearing. Rowan for the rubbish.”

“Did you know what it was?”

Burl spat into a crack between the boards. “I knew it came out in pieces.”

“Did you tell people they could drink?”

“The water was clear.”

“Did you test it?”

“With what?”

The man had a point. Saikou did not like it. He asked where Rowan had put the fragments. Burl said he had seen a white piece laid on red felt, as carefully as a jewel. When Saikou asked to be taken there, he laughed.

“You can look under a ghoul’s tongue if you want. I’m not opening Rowan’s desk for you.”

Saikou returned to the well. It was covered now. Someone had nailed a board across it after Zel went missing, not before. He removed two nails and held the mirror above the curb.

The silver showed water. Then the water became black, and something pale turned slowly underneath it. Two broken halves. Between them there should have been a narrow joining piece, but the gap was filled with a depth the well did not possess. Saikou’s hand grew cold. In the reflection a human shadow leaned over the curb without a body to cast it.

He lowered the mirror before his fingers lost their grip.

The Witness had shown a damaged sealing place. He could compare the mark with others he knew. That was the beginning of an answer, not its end. A well opened to something hungry had not made every drinker a ghoul. Zel’s particular contact, the last water after the seal came loose, the nature of the broken join: he could name uncertainties until nightfall.

Kes was waiting at the office when he returned.

“I paid to have a well cleared,” the dockmaster said. “I didn’t pay to have a girl ruined.”

“Did you know the fragments were sealed?”

“I knew the Sisters had left something there.”

“And after she became ill?”

Kes looked towards the ward. Zel was inside with Leto. Saikou could hear pieces being moved on a board.

“Then I paid her mother.”

“You could have sent for help.”

“I have sent for help.”

Saikou was tired enough to want to strike him. Kes must have seen it. He stepped back, and the small movement shamed Saikou more effectively than an accusation would have.

“Yes,” Saikou said. “You have. Now you will pay for the help you actually need.”

Kes said they would discuss it tomorrow. Saikou knew the sentence too well to mistake it for an undertaking.

Inside, Zel had eaten again. She was calmer. That made it harder to begin the conversation about the Well of Ichor, and therefore more important to begin it while she could listen.

He told her what he knew. Far away, in Zahk’Kozahn, there was a place where some of the cursed had found release. Those who returned did not remember everything they had taken with them. Hunger might disappear with the memories in which it had rooted. The face which mattered most could go as well. He could not promise a particular loss, a safe journey, or that the well would answer her as it had answered another.

Zel looked at the letters on the shelf.

“Mother would say no.”

“Would you?”

“I don’t know.”

“That is yours to decide.”

She was quiet for a long time. Then she asked whether anybody had gone who was glad afterwards.

“Yes,” he said. “A man in Ashaan told me he’d lost his brothers’ faces and could walk through a market without wanting to hurt anybody. He thought he’d been given more than he’d paid.”

“Did he still love them?”

“He said he did.”

Zel pulled at the seam of her apron. Saikou could offer her no better certainty.

“First I want to see whether this works,” she said, touching the empty bowl. “Not whether Mother thinks it works. Whether it does.”

He agreed. Outside the blue light widened on the incoming tide. It made the channel look clear all the way to the darker water where no beacon had been posted.

Chapter 7 — A useful woman

For most of the next day Zel was able to think about wood.

Hadrik brought a broken pulley block. Its cheek had split at the grain, and somebody had attempted to bind it with wire which cut deeper each time the wheel turned. Zel told him the wire would finish breaking what remained. He asked whether she could mend it.

“Not with that.”

“With what?”

She named a length of sound wood and a boring tool. He brought both, along with a wedge which was the wrong size. She sent him back. The second time he came, he called her a demanding customer, and she realized he was treating her as somebody from whom he expected work.

It felt good. It also felt like a door he might close.

“Are you paying me?” she asked.

“If it turns.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then you’ll have made it like the old one.”

She laughed. He left the pieces outside the ward. Saikou lowered it to let her take them, then stayed while she fitted the grain and showed Leto where to hold the block. His presence irritated her. She kept telling herself he would be there if a saw was being used by a child, and then became angry at having compared herself to a child.

The new block turned. She could feel the resistance through her fingertips, the way she had felt a rib finding its place in a hull. It belonged to a world in which a wrong thing could be cut away without taking the whole person with it.

Hadrik paid her. Not much, but enough that she held the coins for several minutes before putting them on the shelf beside her pencil. He asked whether she could do another tomorrow. She said perhaps, and was pleased when nobody changed it into yes.

After the afternoon feeding Saikou allowed her down to the empty outer shed. There were two watchers, a marked place where she would work, and no other crew nearby. She objected to the mark because it looked like a child’s play space. Saikou erased it and pointed out the same limits using the door and a stack of timber. She found that less humiliating and suspected he knew it was no different.

The shed held a small cutter awaiting repair. Zel put her palm against its flank. Her hand made the sound wood should make under a hand. She had thought, absurdly, that it would not.

Saikou let her sit beneath the hull without giving her a task. He was speaking with Hadrik outside. Omi remained at the door, taking something out of her stocking. A needle. She had brought a torn cuff to mend.

“You didn’t say you’d be sewing,” Zel said.

“I didn’t know it would tear.”

“Can you watch while you do that?”

Omi looked up. “Are you planning to slip past while I count stitches?”

“No.”

“Then we’ll try.”

The afternoon stayed ordinary long enough that Zel stopped waiting for it to end.

A man arrived with the second pulley. He was called Dren. He had worked in the yard while she was there, and remembered her father with an inaccuracy Zel corrected before thinking. Dren had confused him with a man who fell from the stern of a cutter while trying to urinate without stopping an argument. Her father had never been on that boat.

Dren laughed. “You’d rather I kept him respectable?”

“I’d rather you kept him the right man.”

“All right. The other one was much louder.”

He put the block on the bench. Its edge caught his thumb. There was a splinter, a little blood, and the world became narrow.

Zel had fed less than two hours before.

She remembered that afterwards. At the moment she remembered nothing except the sound underneath Dren’s laughter. She rose, crossed the space, and caught his wrist. Omi called her name. Dren tried to pull away. That made the pulse louder.

She put her mouth against the torn skin.

Someone struck her shoulder with a wooden staff. She turned on him before she could see his face. It was Hadrik. He had no weapon except the staff, and his eyes were wide enough to show white all round.

Saikou came between them.

He moved with a speed she had not expected. One hand turned her elbow; the other closed against her jaw, pushing her face away from Dren’s arm. She could have broken that hand. She began to. Then he put his knee behind hers and took her to the ground as if he had been doing it all his life.

He smelled of rain and old metal. Beneath that was blood. Not spilled blood. The blood inside him, the thing he had kept between her and the injured man.

She fought harder.

“Zel,” he said, and when she could not stop, he said it again differently, as if he expected to be heard by somebody stronger than hunger. He drew a line on the floor with salt from a little bag torn between his teeth. Omi watched it. Hadrik watched it. The air tightened between them.

Zel struck the barrier and fell back.

She lay beneath the hull, listening to everybody breathe. Dren’s breath was loudest. He had put his injured hand inside his coat. Blood ran down the cloth at his wrist.

“I didn’t think,” Zel said.

Nobody answered.

Saikou lifted his hand. Two fingers had gone pale where she had squeezed them. He flexed them once and stopped. Hadrik stood beside Dren, no longer holding the staff as if it might help.

“Take him to the surgeon,” Saikou said.

Dren looked at Zel. He had laughed at the wrong memory of her father. Now there was nothing to laugh about, and nothing she could offer which would restore that minute.

“I was bringing a block,” he said.

“I know.”

He left with Hadrik. Omi stayed, her sewing abandoned on the floor.

Zel wanted to break the bowl. She wanted to break the repaired pulley, the coins, the table on which she might work tomorrow. She wanted all of them to stop pretending a useful hand made the rest of her safe.

Saikou crouched outside the barrier.

“The feeding hasn’t held through effort and an injury,” he said.

“You should have known.”

“Yes.”

“I should have known.”

He did not deny it. She rolled onto her side so she would not have to see his face.

Back in the office she drank the evening blood without speaking. The hunger quietened; the memory did not. Omi collected the bowl. Zel asked whether she intended to stay.

“Until someone relieves me.”

“Would you rather go?”

“Yes.”

Zel looked at her.

“My daughter is visiting. I would have rather gone yesterday too.”

Omi shut the door on the outside, leaving the ward raised within it. Zel could hear her settling onto the stool. There was a squeak when she drew the thread through her cuff.

The fog arrived after dark. It came beneath the yard gates before it crossed the roofs. Zel had known fog all her life, but never from so many angles at once: drops catching on the shutter, moisture filling the throat of the drain, the little change in voices when people stopped trusting where their companions stood.

The blue lantern glowed above her like an eye behind cloth.

Omi got up. Someone called from below that a worker had fallen near the chain drum. Omi answered that she could not leave. The voice named her daughter.

Zel heard the lie before she knew why. The speaker had no breath in his voice. He was far below the office, projecting a call through a tube which made it appear to rise directly beneath the stair.

“Omi,” she said.

The stool scraped. The door opened.

“Omi, don’t.”

There were footsteps, too many. A grunt. Something fell against the door. Zel came to the salt line and struck the pan. She struck it again, hard enough to dent it.

A voice outside said, “The magistrate has ordered removal.”

She knew Rowan’s clerk.

“Then bring Saikou.”

“He isn’t required.”

Something was pushed beneath the door. Water. It ran along the worn boards, taking the salt into the gaps. The ward flickered but did not fail. Zel felt the room pull away from itself, as if a reflected space stood beside it. The blue lantern cast another door across the wet floor. For a moment she thought it was open.

She went towards it and struck the wall.

Outside somebody swore. There was a flash of silver at the threshold, a rod slid slowly across the thinning line. The rod was hooked to a narrow white fragment. Zel felt it enter before she saw it. Something beneath her breastbone lifted towards it, eager as a hand reaching for a letter.

Rowan had kept a piece of the well’s seal.

She could not turn her face away. The hunger became quiet, not gone but listening. The door opened. The cage filled it.

“No,” she said.

Merrit stood behind the cage. He would not look at her. She reached for him as the men pushed the silver door inward, and the mesh burned her hands until she let go.

They put a hood over her head.

She could hear Omi breathing on the landing. Still alive. The knowledge helped her remain a person for another few moments. Rowan’s men lifted the cage. The broken fragment knocked softly against its bars.

Down the steps. Across the yard. A turn which should have led inland, then another which led towards water.

The smell of a boat came up beneath her. She had thought she could not go on a ship. Now she understood how little that mattered to someone willing to carry her aboard.

Chapter 8 — Oars in the fog

Saikou found Omi beneath the stair with blood in her hair and the pan beside her. The man who had struck her had taken the spoon, perhaps because he believed that left her nothing with which to call for help. She had hit the pan with a loose stone until Saikou heard it from the slaughterhouse road.

“Boat,” she said.

He knelt. Her eyes followed him evenly enough to reassure him about some things, not enough to let him leave her alone.

“Who else?”

“Leto went after them.”

Of course the boy had gone after them. Saikou stood, shouting for the nearest worker. The fog swallowed the answer. He shouted again with the voice he had used aboard ship, and this time three men came out of the nearest shed.

“Stay with her. Fetch the surgeon. Nobody follows me without a light.”

They understood urgency better than the rest. He was already moving when Hadrik caught his sleeve.

“The water?”

“Probably.”

“Everything is probably the water tonight.”

Saikou heard chain turning. Hadrik’s men were still working at the dredge. He told the foreman what had happened, then asked which boats could have passed beneath the beacon without taking the outer channel.

“Rowan’s lighter. Anything with that draft. And mine.”

“Where do they meet?”

Hadrik pointed into the fog, then swore at himself and named the old customs post. Saikou knew it. Two moorings above a half-submerged foundation, close enough to the yard that cargo moved unnoticed beneath an evening’s ordinary noise.

Neral appeared out of the whiteness, Dalia beside him with her blue lantern. The syndic listened to Hadrik, then blew his whistle once, softly. He waited. Somewhere a man answered with another whistle.

“They won’t be here quickly,” he said.

“Nor will she,” Saikou replied, and ran towards the lower stage.

Leto had left the shore in a skiff which was never meant to carry an adult. He had taken one oar, hoping to paddle quietly, and Saikou found him trying to get round the edge of a hull while its wash pushed him back. The boy was furious rather than frightened, which did not improve his balance.

Saikou stepped into the skiff. It nearly sank.

“Give me that.”

“I know how to use it.”

“Then find me its other one.”

There was no other one. Saikou took the paddle and worked against the nearest pile, using it as a lever to turn them. Hadrik came down the stage behind them with his own boat and two men. Its blunt bow struck the skiff gently enough to feel like a deliberate insult.

“Get in,” the foreman said.

Saikou passed Leto across first. The boy objected to being held under both arms, then stopped objecting when he saw how far the water moved beneath them. Saikou followed with his satchel held high. Something in it had already become wet; he could smell salt and old ink.

They rowed by sounds. Hadrik knew which echo came from the warehouse face and which from the stone foundations. He also knew when he did not know, and stopped the rowers rather than letting confidence choose a collision. Every pause cost them distance. Saikou could hear another set of oars farther out, fading and returning as if the fog folded them between its layers.

The little waystone stayed cold in his hand.

He asked the Traveler for passage. For several strokes he felt nothing. Then the boat touched timber on its left side, a floating beam which should have carried them towards the outer tide. Instead it turned slowly against their bow, making a gap beside the old customs wall.

Saikou pointed. Hadrik took them through. A boat’s side rose out of the fog a few paces away.

It was Rowan’s lighter. The cage stood amidships beneath a canvas, lashed between grain bins which were empty except for stones to keep them from moving. A man near the stern was holding a white fragment on a silver rod. At his feet lay a coil of red felt. Zel’s hand came through the bars, reaching towards the fragment and retreating from the silver each time.

Rowan stood beside him.

“Go back,” he told Saikou. “You don’t want what you’ll make loose.”

Saikou put away the stone. He drew his Moonsteel cutlass. Its pale curve showed clearly against the fog. Rowan’s face changed.

“We have authority,” the smuggler said.

“Then you can be arrested with it.”

The first man came at him from the bow. Saikou caught his wrist against the gunwale and struck his throat with the heel of his hand. The man fell backwards without drawing a full breath. Hadrik’s boat drifted apart from the lighter. Saikou secured the mirror to his belt and left his satchel with Hadrik. He jumped before the gap widened, landed beside the fallen man, and felt a second blade pass through his coat near the ribs.

Merrit.

Saikou turned hard enough to tear the cloth from the blade. He caught Merrit’s elbow, struck once beneath it, and drove him back against the cage. Merrit tried to bring the knife round again. Saikou’s blade entered below his breastbone.

The man’s face opened in astonishment. He remained on his feet a moment longer than Saikou expected, then folded beside the grain bin.

There was no time to take the blade out carefully.

The man with the fragment spoke a word Saikou did not know. Zel struck the cage. Its lashings tightened. The hunger in her face had become something which watched the rod instead of the living people beside her.

The Ashaani buyer wore a travelling coat of good pale wool and kept himself behind the silver. He had expected handlers, not a fight. His free hand shook as he raised the fragment.

“Hold there,” he said to Zel.

She held. Blood ran from her burned palm.

Saikou heard the low noise she made. It was not obedience. Something had taken hold of the opening the well made in her, and she was hanging from it.

Rowan pulled a little jar from his coat. Saikou saw oil, then flame as the smuggler knocked a lantern into the canvas. Fire climbed across the cage roof. Zel lifted her face towards it.

Hadrik shouted from the other boat. A grapnel struck the lighter’s rail, slipped, and caught again. His men pulled. The lighter slewed sideways, throwing the buyer against the stern. The silver rod struck wood.

Saikou went for it. Rowan came between them with a long knife. The blade was meant for cutting cargo straps; it opened Saikou’s sleeve and the skin beneath it. Saikou struck Rowan’s jaw with the hilt of his weapon. The smuggler fell against the burning canvas, scrambling to get away from the heat.

Fire made a good light in fog. It also made an audience. Voices began reaching them from the posts. Neral’s men had found the boat, but the boats around it kept them from coming closer.

Saikou put his mirror flat on a grain bin and turned its face towards the buyer’s raised hand. He called on the Witness. The silver darkened, then showed the underside of the rod: a little wire loop holding the fragment in place.

He could see the loop in the reflection when smoke hid it from his eyes.

His cutlass did not appear in the Witness’s silver. He aimed with the hand he could see and the curve he knew. The wire snapped beneath the blade.

The fragment fell. The buyer tried to catch it and burned his fingers on the hot gunwale. He made a furious, childish noise, then stepped into a small boat tethered beneath the stern. Somebody unseen was already rowing it away.

Saikou let him go. Zel had both hands on the cage door now. Her face was changing. Free from the rod, she could smell Merrit’s blood.

“Zel,” Saikou said.

The hinge broke.

She came out as the canvas fell behind her. He stepped back, keeping his bleeding arm against his side. For one instant she looked at him, and he saw the same narrowing attention he had seen beneath the hull.

Then she looked down at Merrit.

He was not yet dead. His breathing made the blood bubble at the wound. Zel knelt beside him. Saikou reached for the salt, knowing there was not enough for a large line, knowing also that nobody outside the lighter could see clearly enough to witness it.

Zel gripped Merrit’s collar. She lifted him until his face was above the smoke.

“He’ll drown,” she said.

She was right. Hadrik’s men were dragging the lighter away from the burning canvas, and water had begun coming over the lower side. Saikou caught Merrit’s arm. Together they passed him across the narrowing gap to the dredge boat.

Zel would not let go until Hadrik had him.

Then she turned on Saikou.

He could not have fought her fairly and survived. He hit her with the grain-bin lid, hard enough to knock her against the stern, and drew the small salt line where the lid fell. Hadrik was looking now. Leto was looking. Saikou spoke the Sentinel’s name through smoke.

The line held between Zel and his blood.

She struck it once. Saikou’s arm shook. He had less strength left than the ward needed, and the boat beneath the line was filling. He had made a barrier on wood which would not remain above water much longer.

“Food,” he shouted.

Leto looked at him blankly. Hadrik understood first. He took a covered tin out of Saikou’s satchel, passed it across the rail, and swore when the lid stuck.

Saikou opened it with his blade. Blood slopped over the side. Zel looked at it, then at him, then down at her own hands.

“Take it,” he said.

She took it as he lowered the line. She drank, coughing smoke through the blood, while he pulled Rowan away from the fire by the back of his coat.

They brought everyone they could across. Merrit died in Hadrik’s boat with the foreman holding a cloth against him. His last words were about somebody taking his wages. Hadrik told him they were still his. Saikou did not know whether he heard.

The lighter burned until its cargo bins broke apart. Then it settled against the customs foundations and blocked the new channel Isha had been so pleased to open.

Zel sat beneath a wet blanket. She had emptied the tin. Leto watched her hands, unable to stop watching, while two harbour men bound Rowan’s wrists.

Saikou pressed his own wound closed. The buyer was gone. The well fragment was at the bottom of the channel or somewhere within the burned hull. Rowan could no longer take Zel anywhere tonight. That was what they had won.

Through the fog came a faint, familiar sound. The yard song, one worker keeping himself company while he held a light above the damaged stage. Eight beats. Silence. Then the next.

Saikou did not sing along.

Chapter 9 — What she would agree to

The new room was not above the sluice.

Zel lifted her head to check again. She had spent the night with her head beneath a blanket, drinking whenever somebody brought more blood, waiting for the smell of Merrit to leave her hands. Water had not taken it away. Soap had not. She knew it might not be there at all, but knowledge was a small thing beside a smell.

The room belonged to a quarantine house inland from the cutter yard. Its window faced a wall covered in climbing beans, dead for the season. The door opened into an enclosed courtyard. Someone had kept birds there; little brackets remained where the cages had hung. Saikou had drawn the ward inside the door after she asked him to, and sat outside until his arm stiffened so badly that the surgeon ordered him to lie down elsewhere.

Zel heard the order. She was glad somebody could make him move.

In the morning Dalia came. She brought no lantern, only a roll of paper and a cup of hot water she drank herself. Anja sat beside her, the bandage on her wrist smaller now. Neither woman entered until Zel said they could.

There were two watchers at the door. The ward was lowered. Zel had eaten enough that their heartbeats sounded irritating rather than wonderful. She hoped irritation was a good sign.

“Rowan is in custody,” Dalia said. “Three men have given accounts of the cage. The man who struck Omi has been found.”

“Merrit?”

Dalia looked at Anja, then back at Zel.

“Dead.”

“I saw.”

“His sister has been told.”

Zel pulled the blanket around her shoulders. Merrit had helped carry her towards a buyer. Merrit had been a young man at a wedding who knew how to turn without crushing a woman’s feet. The facts would not stop standing beside one another.

Dalia unrolled the paper.

“This house can hold you while another arrangement is made. The magistrate will not let you return to the public drains. He has heard about Dren.”

Anja opened her mouth. Zel spoke before she could.

“Dren didn’t come near me to hurt me.”

“No,” Dalia said.

“He came because we thought I was better.”

Dalia nodded. Zel disliked being nodded at more than she disliked the truth in what she had said.

“What does another arrangement mean?”

“Some want you destroyed. Some want you put aboard a ship. Some want to forget you as soon as they know you’re behind a door.”

“And you?”

“I want you to have somewhere to live which does not require a new argument every time the tide changes.”

Zel looked at the room. There were lime stains beneath the shelf, but they had not risen as far as the ink in the old office. An iron ring in the wall could have held anything, including a person. She asked what it had been used for. Dalia said she did not know, then called someone who did. Washing lines, the keeper told them. Zel was not convinced until he showed the corresponding ring on the opposite wall.

“Will you lock me in?” she asked.

“Sometimes you will need the ward,” Anja said.

“I asked her.”

Her mother sat back. Dalia took the question slowly enough that Zel almost asked it again.

“We cannot promise you can leave whenever you wish if leaving will bring you among people while you are ravenous. We can promise to name who may stop you, what happens then, and who hears you afterwards. I’d prefer something Saikou doesn’t have to remain here forever to explain.”

Zel thought of the shadow of a door across wet boards. She had run towards an opening which was not there. A locked door was at least honest about itself.

“I want the well mentioned,” she said.

“In the account?”

“Everyone talks as if I went looking for this.”

Dalia wrote a note. Anja leaned closer.

“Kes will have to answer for it.”

“Yes,” Zel said. “And I will have to answer for Dren.”

Her mother began saying that one did not excuse the other. Zel told her she knew. For a moment she wanted both women gone. Then Dalia put down the pen and said they could continue in an hour.

Anja remained. She took a little folded cloth from her bag. Inside it were Zel’s wages for the pulley, which Hadrik had brought before the fire, and the thread Zel had used to mend her father’s old coat. Zel touched the coins, then the thread.

“I don’t want to work to earn my next bowl,” she said.

“You needn’t,” Anja answered too quickly.

“You couldn’t pay for it.”

“No.”

“You kept trying.”

“Yes.”

Zel looked at her. “I want to work. I don’t want every useful thing I do to be proof they should let me stay alive.”

Her mother had no ready answer. That helped. She moved the cloth nearer Zel and waited while her daughter sorted its contents.

Saikou came after midday, with his arm bound and his coat hanging strangely where the sleeve had been cut away. He had found the apprentice from the first night, the girl beneath the hull. Her name was Ressa. She had agreed to carry messages within the yard but not blood, because the smell made her sick. Saikou told Zel this as if a person refusing one task might still be expected to do another.

“She’ll hate me,” Zel said.

“She was angry about the cage being carried past her tools. I suspect she’ll begin there.”

Zel laughed. It was a small sound and still possible.

They discussed the feeding without Anja. Saikou had recorded what she drank, how long relief lasted, and the effect of effort. The first proper collection had been much less than her body demanded after months of deprivation. One useful hour had led him to offer a test which should have come later. He said that plainly. She liked him less for a moment, then liked him more for not waiting until she thanked him for the admission.

He proposed three full portions, spaced through the day and night, with more held separately if work or fear brought hunger back early. Someone would remain to observe, not merely to see whether she finished. Varo could supply most days. They needed a second slaughterhouse, someone to carry from it, and a way to pay all of them without Kes deciding she cost too much.

“And when there isn’t any?”

“We are trying an overnight reserve. Unmixed, covered, kept cool. Yesterday’s blood still helped you on the boat. We will find how long it remains useful before we depend on it.”

He did not say the reserve would work because it was necessary. Zel had been doing that for months. She asked him to leave enough time for her to decide whether an hour was good before anybody called it a day.

He wrote that down without smiling.

She asked about the well far away. He told her nothing new. The distance was still distance. Her mother’s face was still something she might lose, along with other things neither of them could predict.

“If I go later,” she said, “I don’t want everyone telling me I betrayed Mother.”

“I won’t tell you that.”

“Will you stop them?”

“I may not be able to.”

She accepted the answer with difficulty. When Anja returned, Zel told her about the possibility herself. Her mother looked away, gripping her bandaged wrist. Then she asked whether there was a map.

“There are several,” Saikou said.

“Which one is good?”

He did not know. Anja told him he had better find out, if he meant to frighten people with such places. Zel sat very still while her mother began asking questions she would once have used to stop the conversation.

At evening Dalia returned with a shorter paper. Zel made her read it twice. She asked for one sentence changed, the one which said her mother could agree on her behalf if Zel was distressed. Distress did not make her mother understand the hunger better than she did.

Dalia replaced it with an undertaking to wait, make the room safe, and hear Zel when she could speak. If she was hurting somebody, the watchers would not wait for permission to stop her. Zel made herself listen to that part without looking at Anja.

Then she placed her mark beneath the terms for a trial lasting three weeks.

She could have signed her whole name. Her hand shook too much. The mark was ugly, not like the elegant shape she had practised as a girl.

Dalia blotted it carefully. It remained hers.

Chapter 10 — The price of the channel

For two days Saikou could not put on his own coat without assistance. He found this more offensive than the wound itself. The surgeon enjoyed it considerably.

“You instruct everybody else,” she said. “Now hold still.”

He held still while she removed the dressing. The cut had gone along the outside of his arm, sparing the tendons by a margin he did not care to hear described. One finger remained swollen from Zel’s grip. He could close the hand, but opening it required concentration.

“The patrons don’t help?” the surgeon asked.

“With some things.”

“Ask one for a better sleeve.”

She stitched the last part which had begun opening beneath the bandage. He looked at the wall. There was a faded picture of a flowering shrub beside a list of debts. Both had been there for years. He knew because he had once sat in this same room beside a friend who would not stop describing where the knife had entered him.

The friend had died later, elsewhere, of something which had nothing to do with the wound. Saikou had been surprised by the irrelevance, as if surviving one encounter entitled a person to end his story there.

When he left, he bought a parcel of hot fish and ate it on the quay. It was the first meal he had chosen in several days rather than accepted while looking at a ledger or a wound. Halfway through, Neral sat beside him.

“You owe me,” the syndic said.

Saikou swallowed. “For sitting on my coat?”

Neral moved it. “For the watch. For that house being available. For the two people who haven’t gone home because you need a third set of eyes when someone changes shift.”

“They are being paid.”

“By money you don’t have after tomorrow.”

Saikou folded the paper round the remaining fish. He hated discussing debt while hungry; every reasonable answer felt like somebody reaching into his supper.

“What do you want?”

“Come to the harbour council. Speak about the broken seal. Kes will say this began as a private illness. I don’t want the port paying a private man’s repair because he had the courage to give it a public name afterwards.”

“That’s your favour?”

“Yes.”

“You could ask him for money directly.”

“He could give it directly and remain the man who decides whether next month’s payment comes.”

Saikou looked at him. Neral rarely raised his voice, and Saikou could see how people mistook that for a wish to keep everything as it was. The syndic wanted Kes weakened. He also wanted the watches and warehouses which had depended on Kes to keep working. Neither purpose made him a disinterested protector of Zel.

“If I speak, I won’t speak only about him.”

Neral nodded once. “I expect to dislike some of it.”

The council met that afternoon in a room which smelled of drying wool and good wax. Isha came with her route records. Dalia came with Zel’s statement. Hadrik came with a figure for the damaged stage, the lost dredging day, and a dead man’s unpaid wages.

Kes stood through the first questions. He seemed to believe sitting would make him look smaller. When Saikou described the curb and the sealing halves shown in his mirror, the dockmaster interrupted to ask whether a reflection could be entered as evidence.

“Mine couldn’t tell you what he knew,” Saikou said. “His signed clearance can tell you what he ordered.”

Kes looked at the sheet. Saikou described Burl’s double payment, Rowan’s purchase of the fragments, the bowls Anja had carried below, and the false quiet which followed. He did not give Tevi’s name. Dalia had asked the wounded people which parts they wanted heard. Tevi wanted the refusal to let him go stated plainly. He did not want every merchant knowing his injured hand before he could decide what work remained possible.

The council questioned Hadrik about the rescue. The foreman made it sound less graceful than Saikou remembered. He described the jumping, the missing oar, the fire, the lighter filling, and the moment Saikou hit Zel with a bin.

“Was she attacking?” one member asked.

“Yes,” Saikou said.

“Then why was she brought ashore?”

“Because she could be stopped without killing her.”

The man tapped a finger against the table. “And can that be done every time?”

“No.”

That answer left more silence than the rescue had.

Dalia unfolded the proposed trial. One council member objected to paying a butcher for blood which had otherwise been thrown away. Hadrik asked him whether he paid dock hands only when they carried valuable cargo. The member replied that the cases differed. Hadrik said the butcher would still need to wash the same bowl and stand in the same blood regardless of its value to a man at a table.

They argued over the collection charge for half an hour. Saikou sat down. His arm hurt; the fish had grown cold. He found he could listen more patiently once he stopped pretending to be comfortable.

The council had authority over Kes’s yard lease. It did not have authority to give his private warehouses away, nor the money to fund permanent care merely by saying it was deserved. They could fine him for opening the sealed well without examination. They could require him to meet the damage caused by his clearance and concealment, once assessed. Those proceedings would take longer than the supply of blood Saikou had paid for.

Kes understood that before anybody else said it aloud.

“I can advance the cost,” he said. “A year. Food, the house, attendants. Against the eventual assessment.”

“And if the assessment is less?” Dalia asked.

“The difference is mine.”

“Do you retain any claim against Zel for it?”

Kes looked at Saikou, then at the council. “No.”

It was a large concession, made by a man who understood he could lose more if he refused. It was not an admission that everything he had done could be measured by the concession. Dalia put the undertaking in terms he could not call a gift later and ask to have thanked.

To find the money, Kes sold his interest in the western bonded shed. Saikou knew because he went with Neral to collect the advance before the council dispersed. The purchaser had already offered less than the share was worth. He offered less again when he saw how badly Kes needed the money that afternoon.

Kes signed anyway.

Outside, he stopped Saikou.

“I did bring you here.”

“Yes.”

“I could have sent men with fire.”

“You could.”

“Will you put that in your report?”

Saikou looked at the dockmaster. The answer he wanted to give would have been satisfying and incomplete.

“Yes,” he said. “Beside the rest.”

Kes let him pass. He was no longer standing as if he meant to address anybody.

Hadrik waited on the landing with a small wooden slat. He held it awkwardly, turning it over until Saikou asked what he wanted.

“My brother’s name,” the foreman said.

The slat had two words cut into it, the second more roughly than the first. Davi Kelm. Saikou knew the surname from the dredge tally. Hadrik used their mother’s name himself.

“He was sentenced with me,” Hadrik said. “He died on the breakwater. The pay record says his work was discharged. Nobody in the family knows where they put him.”

Saikou waited.

“They’re cutting the new channel bed when the lighter is raised. I want that laid beneath the first sound stake. Not to make it holy. To put him somewhere I can point to.”

“Will the keeper let you?”

“He says if an Invoker witnesses it, he won’t object.”

Saikou was tired of discovering which parts of a man became another fee after death. He took the slat, read the name, and agreed to come when the stake was driven. There would be no bargain with Varnek Karn for a man whose resting place he could not find. He could witness where his brother chose to remember him.

Hadrik nodded. “Good. They’re asking whether the bonus includes the recovery work.”

“Does it?”

“It will after I speak to them.”

The foreman went down towards his crew. Saikou unwrapped the cold fish and finished it standing beside the rail.

In the yard, Omi’s daughter was helping her walk home. Omi had been paid for the lost shifts; she would not take more from Saikou, nor promise to return before her head stopped aching. Leto had chosen to go back to ordinary apprenticeship for the rest of the week. He wanted to learn a joint which did not involve being chased by a ghoul or hauled into a boat.

The watchers would have to be replaced.

Saikou went to the quarantine house with the first money. Zel was mending the new shirt where she had torn it in the cage. She asked whether Kes still owned the bowls.

“No,” Saikou said. “Varo owns his until we return them. You have your own.”

She turned it in her hands. It was a plain earthenware basin, large enough to be mistaken for something used to wash feet.

“I’d have chosen another,” she said.

He sat beside the door. For the first time that day he laughed without having to stop because of his arm.

Chapter 11 — The third bowl

On the fifth night the blood came late.

Zel knew it before the watcher checked the little sandglass. The hook inside her had begun tightening, and the man outside smelled less like a person sitting on a stool than like the promise of relief. His name was Perrin. He had two children, a wife who made him carry home whatever he did not eat on shift, and a cough which usually started after the room cooled. Zel knew these things because he had told her. None protected him from what his pulse became.

She struck the pan once, then twice more.

Perrin came to the door. His hand stayed on the handle until he remembered to look through the opening first. Zel saw the mistake. He saw her seeing it.

“Don’t come in,” she said.

He took his hand away.

The third meal was supposed to arrive after the last market killing. A cart had overturned near the stock pens, and two men had spent the evening catching animals which had discovered that a broken fence was not a reason to remain on the correct side of it. The carrier had been told to wait. He had waited, thinking one late journey better than two empty ones. Nobody had sent word.

Perrin went for the reserve. Zel heard him open the little chest. It had been kept in the coolest part of the house, with bowls beneath a wet cloth and a shelf arranged so that each day’s collection stood separately. She knew which bowl he took because he said it aloud to the other watcher, a woman named Gima who had come from the clinic and disliked night work as much as he did.

“Yesterday’s last.”

Zel could hear Gima’s uncertainty. They had tested a smaller portion during the day, when the fresh one was ready beside it. It had worked. That did not make it seem generous enough now.

They brought it through after lowering the ward. Perrin stayed outside. Gima set it within reach and withdrew before the line rose again. Zel drank.

The taste was worse. She could bear the taste. The warmth came more slowly, but it came, spreading beneath her ribs until she could sit against the wall without gripping her knees. She asked for the rest before anybody told her to finish that much first.

Gima brought it. Zel could hear the woman breathing carefully through her nose, as if a strong smell might make her retch. She wanted to thank her and wanted her to stop pretending she could not see the distaste.

“You can say it smells bad,” Zel told her.

“It smells bad.”

“I know.”

“Then we needn’t have a long conversation.”

Gima put down the bowl and went out. Zel laughed, then drank.

When the fresh blood arrived she could look at Perrin again. He came through carrying the empty reserve bowls, eyes on her face instead of her hands. He admitted he had thought of putting his hand on the door because he wanted to do something quickly. Zel admitted she had thought of taking it.

He went back to the stool. The agreement between them was less pleasant than it had been before the delay, and more useful. Zel sat with the new bowl until she knew she could leave part of it without wanting to seize somebody at the threshold.

The carrier was paid for his waiting time. The next morning Saikou went with him to the stock pens and made a separate arrangement for a messenger when collecting would be delayed. Varo complained about another person in his doorway, then pointed out a child who already carried messages for small fees. The child’s mother insisted he go no farther than the outer market after dark. Somebody else would have to carry the rest.

Nobody volunteered to do that indefinitely. They found a woman who wanted the evening work and paid her.

On the eighth day the reserve failed.

It had stood through one day and the whole next morning. The lid had been kept closed; the blood had not spoiled enough to offend Perrin when he lifted it. Zel drank a little and said the hook was still there. Saikou asked whether more might help. She handed the bowl back.

“Not enough.”

He listened. The fresh portion was brought in, and she had it instead. They emptied the older bowl into the waste trench and shortened the time for keeping one in reserve. Zel watched it go. Once she would have drunk every drop because losing blood meant losing what her mother paid for. Now the money had paid for a test whose answer was no.

She was relieved and furious in ways which did not fit together.

The new salt line lasted until Ressa caught a pail against the doorframe and spilled water across it. Zel was in the courtyard at the time, sitting where the dead bean vines cast an irregular shadow. She heard the pail fall, turned, and saw the line dissolve.

Ressa looked at her.

For a moment neither moved. Then Zel walked away from the open threshold and sat beside the far wall. Ressa called Perrin. He came with Saikou, who had been trying to eat behind the clinic. Saikou raised another barrier between the courtyard and the passage. He did not ask Zel to demonstrate her willingness by returning to the room first.

Later he put a narrow silver strip beneath the dry inner sill and set two mirrors facing across it. It was a greater work than the salt line. Zel watched him prepare it over two afternoons, stopping whenever his wounded arm began shaking. Gahri, an Invoker attached to the clinic, came to examine it. She was small and elderly and had no patience with Saikou’s preference for doing difficult things after missing a meal.

“Eat,” she told him.

“I’m nearly—”

“You were nearly yesterday.”

She sent him away and checked the mirrors herself.

The new ward held the courtyard as well as the room. Two watchers could lower it by covering the facing silver, then uncover it once everyone had crossed. The binding beneath the sill remained; it did not wash away with a spilled pail. Gahri would inspect it on her clinic days. Zel was not to discover a damaged line by being the first person who tried to leave.

She asked Saikou whether she could keep one of the mirrors covered herself.

“Then they could not raise it while you were beyond it,” he said.

“I know.”

He looked at her. She could see him beginning to explain how that might be dangerous, then stopping because she knew that too.

“You can ask them to hold it down while you’re in the courtyard,” he said. “We’ll try it when you are fed. Nobody need stand in the doorway to persuade you back.”

The first time she did, the whole afternoon felt like waiting for a mistake. The second time she spent most of it sorting pulley wedges. By the third she had become irritated because Ressa kept taking the sound ones for her own repairs. Ressa said Zel should have marked hers. Zel said she had not been given a separate heap. They disputed it until Hadrik came and told them neither heap belonged to either woman.

Zel enjoyed the quarrel so much she was ashamed afterwards.

Dren did not return with another block.

He sent a message asking for the loss of two working days to be entered beside the surgeon’s charge. Then he sent another correcting the number to three. He could lift things, but the grip hurt, and he could not be expected to wait until pain became visible before anyone believed it. Dalia took his statement. Zel asked to have the correction read to her too.

She wrote him an apology. She wrote it six times, finding each version too eager to describe how badly she felt. The seventh said that she had hurt him after he was told the shed was safe, that she remembered him coming to help, and that he need not visit to hear her say it. She gave it to Ressa unsealed so the girl could tell her if it sounded false.

Ressa read it. “You don’t need that line about remembering.”

“Why?”

“He remembers.”

Zel crossed the line out, leaving the rest. Dren answered with a message thanking her for not coming to his house. He added nothing about forgiveness.

Iven sent Ressa for the ring. He would not come himself. He wanted to know whether the cord was with it, because his wife had woven the cord and he had not replaced it since she died.

Zel could not find it. She had left it below, or it had gone with the water. The ring had been kept while the thing which made it easy to wear disappeared.

She gave Ressa the ring in a scrap of clean cloth. Then she wrote that she had no cord. She did not offer to weave another. Ressa brought back word that Iven had taken the ring and asked nothing else. The silence made Zel angry, then ashamed of the anger. She had hoped being able to return something would make him want to speak to her.

The third person from Kes’s list was Fela, who carried produce from the outer market. Dalia brought her statement with the account of the others. Fela still woke hearing water beneath her bed. She had begun using a longer road home so she would not pass any open drains, and the extra journey meant she could carry one basket fewer. She asked that the missing income be entered, not explained away because she still had all her fingers.

Zel read the page twice. She could remember Fela’s basket rolling down the steps, little hard onions spilling into water. She could not remember whether she had tried to help gather them afterwards. She had been hoping she had. Nothing in Fela’s account mentioned it.

On the fifteenth day Tevi came with Sen.

Zel had put the green button in a bowl of its own, with the ring and the other things kept separately beside it. She had meant to return it before he asked. That was easier while he was not there.

Now he stood beyond the courtyard door, his arm no longer tied to the board. Two fingers curled normally. The others moved together, as if they still preferred to keep watch on one another. Sen carried a bag which smelled of leather.

Zel stayed seated. The ward was raised although she had fed an hour before. Tevi looked at it, then at her.

“You wrote down what I said,” he told Saikou.

“Yes.”

“Is it still there?”

Saikou brought the book. Tevi asked for the sentence, not the whole account. Saikou read it.

She didn’t let me go when I asked.

Tevi nodded. Sen put the leather bag on the ground. Zel knew then that they had come because they wanted something done, not because they had agreed to make the meeting kind.

“My sister makes boots,” he said. “I help. I can still do some of it. I can’t pull the last seam as I could.”

Sen opened the bag and showed Saikou a piece of unfinished work. Zel could see the stitch which needed both hands. She had no skill which would make it easier to hear.

“Dalia said the advance would cover tools,” Sen continued. “There is a clamp which might help. The man who sells it says we should wait until the hand is better.”

“How long?” Saikou asked.

“He can’t tell us. But he can tell us to wait.”

They discussed the clamp. Tevi looked across the line at Zel while Sen was speaking.

“Do you remember?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“I don’t know. I remember holding you. I remember you asking.”

His face changed. Zel wanted him to stop looking at her and could not ask him for another kindness.

“I thought crying meant you needed help,” he said.

She bowed her head. She had no answer which would not have been something he already knew.

He saw the button bowl.

“That’s mine.”

She held it out, then remembered the barrier. She put the bowl on the ground and moved away so Saikou could take it. Tevi picked out the button. The old thread remained in two holes.

“I can use it,” he said.

He put it in Sen’s bag. They left after the clamp was arranged. Zel stayed beside the wall until the shade moved off her feet.

That night she drank the third bowl before she wanted it. She disliked being reminded. She told Perrin so. He said she could dislike him after she finished, and handed her the measure.

She finished. He went back outside. Neither of them called it a good night, but nobody was hurt.

At the end of the three weeks Dalia asked Zel what she wished changed.

The portions were holding, even after work. The reserve had carried them through another delayed killing and one carrier’s fever. Gima had taken a sick day without being told a woman might die because she wanted rest. Gahri had lowered the ward herself during inspection while Zel sat calmly within arm’s reach; that had frightened Zel more than the covered mirrors, and afterwards she asked the old Invoker to warn her before doing it again.

She wanted a bell instead of the dented pan. She wanted the payment for her pulley work entered separately from her food. She wanted Anja to knock instead of assuming the watchers should open whenever her mother arrived. She wanted to walk beyond the courtyard someday. She did not ask Dalia to put a date beside that last wish.

Dalia made the first three changes. The fourth remained, written in Zel’s own hand beneath the rest.

The morning after, Ressa brought a bell with a cracked lip which gave it a sour, indignant voice. Zel rang it once. Everybody in the courtyard turned round.

She laughed and immediately regretted it when Gima asked what she needed.

“Nothing,” Zel said. “I wanted to hear it.”

Gima rubbed her eyes. “Now we have.”

She went back to the stool. Zel put the bell on the shelf beside the letters, where it could be reached from the floor without standing up.

Chapter 12 — A different shift

Four months later Saikou arrived at the quarantine house to find the outer door open and somebody arguing about figs.

Zel sat in the courtyard with a basin on her lap. Anja stood beside the dead beans, holding a small paper parcel away from Perrin as if he might steal it merely by looking. The ward mirrors were covered. A woman Saikou did not know sat within the passage, close enough to uncover them without stepping between Zel and the door.

“She’s entitled to taste them,” Anja said.

“She tasted one,” Perrin replied. “Then she said it tasted like cloth. You’re offering her the rest because you bought too many.”

“I didn’t buy too many.”

Zel saw Saikou and began laughing. Anja turned, angry at being caught, then tried to welcome him and defend the figs in the same sentence.

He helped by accepting one. It tasted excellent. Zel looked offended.

“Don’t make that face.”

“Which face?”

“The one people make when I can’t enjoy something.”

Saikou swallowed. “I enjoy figs. I can attempt to be miserable about it, but I doubt you’ll believe me.”

She grinned. She had gained a little weight at the jaw. The grey remained. Her eyes did not look healthier merely because she smiled; they looked like hers.

He sat outside the courtyard line. The new watcher introduced herself as Dessa. Gima had gone back to more daytime work at the clinic. Omi returned for two nights most weeks, provided her head did not ache. Leto had stayed away for a month before bringing another board game and discovering Zel had learnt from Perrin how to stop losing.

“Unfairly,” Perrin said.

“Only when you don’t pay attention.”

“That describes most of the night.”

They had not become a family. Perrin still had one of his own, whose claims made him cross when somebody assumed he would cover an extra shift. Dessa wanted her wages on the day agreed, and had once refused to remain until a replacement came after that replacement stayed drinking. Neral sent a watchman to sit outside the covered mirrors that night while Omi was fetched and paid for coming early. Zel remembered the watchman’s smell of wine more clearly than anybody’s assurances that he was competent.

Saikou heard these things while he looked at the supply record. He also heard that Zel had put the quarrel about the extra shift on a separate sheet because Perrin kept saying she had been asleep when it happened.

“He forgets,” she said. “I don’t.”

Perrin raised a hand. “I know now.”

The afternoon portion arrived while they were speaking. It came from a second slaughterhouse at the inland market. Varo’s boy had become reliable once reliable delivery brought a separate wage rather than another unpaid errand. On days the killing was light, the two houses shared what was collected, and one of them informed the caretaker before the reserve was opened. There had been a week when no boats brought cattle and the market slaughtered mostly goats. The portions changed in smell and thickness. Zel had reported less relief after the smallest collection. They had increased it instead of waiting for her to hurt somebody and make the complaint believable.

The cold chest held only what had been collected since the preceding night. Once it had emptied completely. Neral had arranged an extra slaughter from animals already sold for the next day’s market, paying the owner for advancing the work and the butcher for reopening his shed. The market price rose briefly. People complained. Zel heard the complaint through Ressa, who said she should stop asking for news if she intended to be frightened by every piece of it.

Zel had been frightened anyway. The following day she asked Dalia whether the money would last the year. Dalia came with the figures. The advance was sufficient at the present rate, with less room for emergency collections than they had hoped. Before the year ended they would need another fund, supported by the well assessment, the port, or people willing to pay for the care outright. There was no clause which made Zel’s ability to earn wages the answer to that problem.

Saikou read the figures himself. He could see why nobody had hidden them from her, and why hearing them had not made her easy.

Perrin placed the covered bowl on a small table beyond the ward. Dessa checked the mark which identified that collection. She had once taken the wrong lid from the chest, believing all the morning bowls the same. Zel had smelled the older blood before drinking and asked for the other one. After that the bowls were marked as well as their lids.

Now Perrin uncovered the mirrors. Zel was still inside the courtyard. The barrier became visible in the passage, a narrow discomfort which made the far door appear slightly removed from the house. Dessa held the bowl while Perrin lowered it, set it within reach, and moved back. They kept the line down while Zel drank, watching her without standing over her.

Zel drank the marked portion. There was no transformation anybody could point to, no light coming into her skin. Her shoulders slowly loosened. She let the basin rest against her knees and looked at Anja, who had gone quiet during the feeding without being asked.

“Better,” she said.

“How much?” Perrin asked.

“Enough that I don’t want you to stop breathing.”

Anja made a small, outraged noise. Perrin nodded as if Zel had reported the ordinary state of a difficult hinge. Dessa wrote the words down. They had stopped asking only whether she felt well.

Saikou remained for the next hour. Hadrik sent a block to be fitted; Zel examined it, said it was past repairing safely, and put it aside despite the promised wage. Ressa arrived with sound timber for another job and a complaint about a new foreman who believed apprentices preferred night shifts because nobody could see their mistakes. She wanted Saikou to speak to him.

“Would that help?” he asked.

“It would make him afraid of you.”

“Temporarily.”

“I only need him afraid until payday.”

He laughed. Then he asked Hadrik to hear the apprentices himself. That would not make them happier tonight, but Hadrik had agreed to the new foreman and could change what happened after payday. Ressa accepted the message reluctantly, as someone who had hoped for a more satisfying thunderclap.

Zel had heard the exchange. She said she would have preferred the thunderclap too.

That evening Saikou visited Tevi’s workshop.

Sen had bought the clamp. Tevi could work a seam with it, slowly, and earn something while his hand recovered whatever it would recover. Two fingers still moved poorly. He had stopped expecting Saikou to arrive with a sudden cure and begun asking him to move things on the bench like any other visitor with an uninjured hand.

“The food?” Saikou asked.

“Less burned. If I put too much pepper in, it still tastes like rope.”

“Don’t put too much pepper in.”

“I had worked that out.”

Saikou smiled. Tevi did too, briefly. He asked whether Zel had hurt anyone else. Saikou said no, since the rescue, and described the months accurately rather than making them a promise about years.

Tevi kept working. The green button was on a coat hanging beside the window. Its enamel caught the light, and Saikou saw a fresh bridge of thread through two holes.

“I don’t want to go there again,” Tevi said.

“You needn’t.”

“And I don’t want her night watch at this shed.”

“There is no arrangement for that.”

“People say she can work.”

“She can. That doesn’t mean she works wherever somebody suggests.”

Tevi nodded. He asked Saikou to hold the leather flat. They finished a difficult inch of seam together, saying nothing about the woman whose survival had made the injury neither less real nor easier to explain.

At the harbour, Kes’s office had another name over its door. The clearance inquiry had removed him from responsibility for wells and sealed works; the yard still employed him to buy timber. He was good at it. Several men who wanted him punished also wanted their next delivery bought by someone who knew how wood behaved at sea.

Rowan remained in custody while the cage, the signed false order, and the testimony of his surviving handlers were heard. His Ashaani buyer had not returned. Isha had provided names from the route list and nothing about customers whom nobody could show she had been obliged to record. Saikou disliked leaving her with so little consequence. Dalia said he could pursue that dislike when he had evidence to put beside it.

The obstructed channel was open again. A stake stood above its new bed. Hadrik’s brother’s name lay beneath the footing, where Saikou had seen it placed before the men covered it. He stood beside the rail for a moment, hearing the dredge chain and the empty beat in the yard song.

When he went back to the house, Anja had gone home. Zel was alone inside the room, although there were watchers in the passage. She had pulled the letters down from the shelf and arranged them by the places where their folds had worn.

“I wrote another,” she told him.

“To your mother?”

“To the man in Ashaan. The one who went to the well. You said you might know where to send it.”

“I do.”

She handed him a folded sheet, sealed with the poor wax available in the house. He took it without turning it towards the lamp.

“I asked about ordinary things,” she said. “Whether he likes music. Whether he regrets going when nobody is asking him to be grateful.”

Saikou put the letter in his satchel.

“And if he says he’s glad?”

“I’ll believe him. Then I’ll still have to choose.”

She touched the old fourth letter, the one about the sailmaker and the smaller bed. Anja had offered her a room again that afternoon. Zel had said no for now. The quarantine house had become somewhere she knew how to live. Her mother could visit without trying to turn an hour into a cure.

Saikou asked whether she wanted anything before he left.

“A pencil.”

He took out the one he used for his notes. It was nearly full length. She examined it with the serious pleasure of someone receiving a tool which was not intended to contain her.

“You’ll need another.”

“Yes.”

“You can buy one.”

“I had considered it.”

She grinned and put it beside the half pencil she had stolen months before. Then she asked him to tell Gahri that one mirror had begun misting on the inside. He went to look. The backing had loosened at a corner; he could mend it for the night, but it would need replacing. Perrin brought a sound spare from the cupboard. Dessa watched while it was set. Zel waited within the room, irritated at the delay and unhurt by it.

There would be another bowl before dawn. The reserve had been checked. Omi was coming for the next shift, her daughter bringing supper because they meant to eat together before the night grew quiet. Gahri would inspect the silver in the morning. The work for Hadrik could wait until Zel had fed and wanted to take it up.

Saikou went out through the courtyard. Behind him Perrin asked whether Zel wanted another game. She said yes, provided he stopped pretending the pieces belonged to the same set. He complained that she was too particular. She told him to bring the good ones, then.

The outer door closed softly.

On the quay the evening tide was coming in. Saikou stood aside to let two carriers pass, each balancing a load which would not have been easy to divide. One recognized him and asked whether the woman was better.

“She is fed,” he said.

The carrier considered that, then shifted the load against his shoulder and went on. Saikou followed the water towards his lodging, where his supper would probably be cold and his coat remained in need of a new sleeve.

The Censor’s Daughter

Chapter 1 — The girl who wanted an orange

Livia had learned to eat with her left hand because the right sometimes lifted the spoon before she wanted it to.

She was fourteen, and this was a ridiculous thing to have to learn. The spoon was horn, its bowl worn thin enough for the lamp to show through. Somebody had cut the handle short so she could not sharpen it against the wall. She understood why. She had watched her own hand try to do exactly that with the previous spoon. Understanding did not make the present one any easier to hold. The chain on her wrist caught under the rim of the bowl every time she reached for the last of the porridge.

Across the cell her reflection ate a mouthful she had not taken yet.

Livia set the spoon down.

The reflection set it down too, a little later. There were six mirrors bolted to the walls, with narrow strips of stone between them. A seventh stood over the door. It had a crack through the middle, and at night the crack looked like a road seen from a hill. She tried not to look at that one. The others showed her a thin girl with dirty hair, a spot beside her nose, and the pale patch on her collar where she had rubbed flour into the cloth before they took her. Her cousin Edda had told her flour would take out grease. Edda had been wrong.

She wanted an orange.

Not because oranges would cure anything, or because her mother had given her one before she died. Her mother had disliked oranges. Their smell gave her a headache. Livia wanted one because there had been a cart of them outside the prison when the guards brought her in, and she had seen a man cut one with a little knife and put half in his own mouth, rind and all. She wanted to discover whether the rind was sweet. She had never eaten the rind.

The guard at the door had a boiled egg.

She could smell it. She could hear him picking the shell off, bit by bit, and putting the pieces into something that rang faintly when they landed. His name was Brio. He was young, though older than Edda, and he had a wife who sent him eggs in a cloth. Livia knew this because he spoke through the door when he forgot to be afraid of her. She had asked him once if he would bring an orange instead, and he had said he would see, and the voice in her had said something in a language she did not know. Since then he had spoken less.

“Is it raining?” she asked.

“No.”

There was a pause. A shell landed.

“Is the Triumph still on?”

Another pause, much longer.

“I’m not the one who tells you that.”

“But you know.”

“I know what’s on my order.”

Her mouth moved before she had decided to answer. The words came out low and measured, with a stop in the middle like a seal being pressed into wax. She did not understand them. She understood that Brio had stopped peeling his egg.

Then he was calling for somebody in the corridor.

Livia held her own jaw with both hands. It did nothing. The words went on between her palms. She could feel the vibration in the bone under her ears. She tried to say orange, because it was a word she had chosen and she wanted there to be one word in the room that was hers.

Her tongue made another word.

When it stopped she was crying with rage, which was worse than crying because she was frightened. Frightened was allowed. Everybody expected frightened. Rage made her look like what they said she was.

A woman came to the door with a lamp on a chain. Sister Nera had a round face and a white line across one eyebrow where hair would not grow. She did not open the door at once. She put her lamp through the little hatch and held it still.

“Livia,” she said. “Can you hear me?”

“Yes.”

“Did you choose those words?”

“No.”

“All right. I’m coming in.”

The sister made Brio turn his shield towards the wall. Its brass boss had been polished. Livia had not noticed that until she saw the woman cover it with her own shawl. Then the bolts moved and the door came open, and Sister Nera came through with the lamp and a cup.

Livia drank. Water, not the bitter stuff they had given her yesterday. Yesterday’s had made her sleep, except that something had stood in her sleep reciting numbers. She had woken with the inside of her cheek bitten through.

Sister Nera looked at the porridge.

“You’ve eaten some.”

“Some of me has.”

The woman’s face changed. Not much, but Livia saw it. She hated making adults do that. They went quiet and looked down and then came up with a voice in which they had hidden something.

“I’ll have it warmed,” Nera said.

“I don’t want it warmed. I want an orange.”

“I’ll ask.”

“Brio said he’d ask. Nobody asks.”

Nera put the cup down. “I can ask about an orange. I can’t promise what answer I’ll get.”

“That’s what my father says about petitions.”

It came out sharper than she meant. Her father had once brought a petitioner into their dining room because the man had waited outside all day and it was cold. He had given him soup. Afterward he had denied the petition. Livia remembered the soup more clearly than the man’s face, and she did not know what that made her.

The sister sat on the stone bench beside her. The mirrors showed them both, six times and one cracked time. Livia kept her eyes on the sister’s hands. There was a burn on the thumb of the left one. Not a sacred mark. A kitchen burn, with the skin peeling at its edge.

“There’s somebody coming,” Nera said.

“A priest?”

“An Invoker. He’s worked with my order before.”

“Is he going to make me drink something?”

“I don’t know. I’ll be here when he asks.”

“Will he ask?”

“He’s usually very particular about it.”

Livia considered that. Being particular could mean a great many things. Her father was particular about the height of the candles on a table. The Inquisitor was particular about the order in which she was to confess. She had not confessed, and he had ordered the clerk to write that she had confessed by omission.

“Does he burn people?” she asked.

“Not as a treatment.”

That was an odd answer. She looked at Nera’s face to see if she was making a joke. The woman looked tired, and there was an affection in the tiredness that was not directed at Livia.

Outside, trumpets began. They were far off, towards the Coin-House, and they were playing the first part of the Triumph march. They always practised that part because it had a difficult turn in it. Edda could whistle the turn without stopping. Livia could not. She used to try until her father put his pen down and looked at her over it.

“Three days,” she said.

Nera did not tell her she was mistaken.

The trumpet missed the turn. Another took it up and missed it differently. In the gap between them something spoke behind Livia’s teeth. It spoke softly this time, in the language of the city.

“Your father is punctual.”

Nera stood up. She moved the cup away from Livia’s hands and took the lamp from its hook. Her burned thumb closed on the chain.

Livia looked at the mirrors. None of them showed her speaking. All of them showed her listening.

“Go away,” she said.

The sister did not go.

“I didn’t mean you.”

“I know.”

And for a while, with the trumpet stumbling over its turn outside and the cup out of reach and the porridge going cold, that was enough.

Chapter 2 — Where the flame stopped

Saikou Ira reached the Coin-House hungry, with half a heel blistered and a child’s accusation hanging on him from the inn.

The child had said he ate like a horse. Her mother had said that was rude. Saikou had said it was inaccurate, since horses generally left the crockery, and the child had laughed so hard she spilled his tea. He had paid for the tea anyway. By the time he reached the marble floor he regretted not asking for another cup.

The room was louder than a shipyard. Factors called the price of wool across men calling the price of grain. Couriers slipped between them with ribbons tied round their wrists. A votive candle guttered beside the melted face of a saint whose name Saikou could not read because somebody had put a ledger over it. The floor was cracked, and soot lay in the cracks as if the marble had been ruled for writing.

He kept his mirror wrapped.

He had learned at Khemesh that seeing could be an injury, and afterward, on a beach where he had no business being alive, that it could also be work. The silver mirror had a crack along its left edge. The crack was older than any account he could give of it. He carried three other Symbols, each answering to a different power, and he took care never to confuse what they could offer. A road was not an answer. A dead witness was not a verdict. A barrier was not a cure.

“Master Ira.”

Sister Nera stood by a column with a boy carrying a basket. The boy looked at Saikou’s boots, then at his mirror bag, then at his face. He was trying to decide what an exorcist ought to look like.

“Have you brought lunch?” Saikou asked him.

“The sister’s brought lunch.”

“An excellent distinction. Has she brought enough?”

Nera gave him a little round bread from the basket and did not smile. That told him more about the case than anything she had yet said.

They crossed to the quieter edge of the hall. A factor intercepted them there with a sheet rolled under a black ribbon. Saikou could not tell whether the man wanted his custom or hers. The factor could, and wanted both.

“Silver, master? I have an order for silver arriving this afternoon. Very advantageous. Thirty below the harbour rate.”

“Why?”

“Because the seller needs money before the Triumph. You need silver. He needs money. There’s no mystery.”

“Then I hope you both get what you need.”

The man held out the sheet. “There’s a name already entered for a hospital sister. Her collector’s left it. If you’re buying for the same house—”

Nera stopped. She looked at the name and held out her hand.

“Who signed that?”

“Brother Oren. Perfectly good signature.”

“He was to buy two mirrors.”

“And he has. At an excellent rate.”

Nera read the sheet. Saikou watched her move her finger down it and stop at the blank between two paragraphs. There was room for a sentence. The space was perfectly clean, the fibres of the paper uninjured.

“What’s missing?” she said.

“Nothing’s missing, sister.” The factor sounded puzzled, not offended. “That’s the form.”

Saikou took the bread out of his mouth. He had tasted burnt clove for a moment, beneath the onions baked into it.

He looked at the floor.

The factor’s shoes stood on either side of a soot-filled crack. His own stood on an unbroken square. Between them the paper shook in Nera’s hands, and she was looking at it as if she had put something down and could not remember what it was.

“Come away from here,” Saikou said.

They went to the column. The factor followed, explaining the rate, which was indeed favourable, and the seller’s solvency, which was not in question. The terms named a delivery hour, a sum, and a collecting house. They also made the goods liable for any unpaid charge against that house. No limitation remained upon what kind of charge.

Nera could not remember whether Oren had meant to add one.

“Don’t sign another copy,” Saikou said.

“It’s already executed.” The factor tapped Oren’s name. “I’m only telling you where to collect.”

Saikou gave the sheet back. He could investigate a whole city’s appetite for clauses, or he could get to a girl who had three days. He disliked knowing which he had to choose before he had even seen her.

Outside the Coin-House a procession had stopped.

It stood at the mouth of a street under an arch, six bearers carrying a brass cage with the sacred flame inside. The flame was blue-white and steady. The front bearers had their feet planted. The rear bearers were looking over their shoulders. Beyond them the crowd kept arriving from the square, people with bread in their hands, people who had come out of shops, children running between knees. The trumpet players were still advancing. They had not seen what was happening ahead.

A beadle shouted that the street was impure.

That was going to become an answer if nobody supplied anything else quickly.

Saikou gave Nera his bread and went to the side of the arch. He put his hand on the cold stone and looked past the flame into the street. A carrier’s cart stood halfway up it with a basket of covered looking-glasses. A woman beside the cart was trying to get the mule to move. It would not. In a window beyond her somebody had hung a round polished plate.

The flame had declined that street. The mule had declined it too. Neither fact told him why.

He took out a little censer, put a pinch of silver filings and ironwood resin on its charcoal, and let the smoke drift under the arch. Saltpeter made it catch briskly. He held it low, away from the bearers’ robes. The smoke showed the cart’s shadow going uphill while the cart stood still.

“Cover the plate,” he called to the woman at the window.

She looked down. “What?”

“The plate. The shiny one. Put a blanket over it.”

The beadle came at him with a hand out. Nera intercepted that hand, chain-lamp raised, and spoke into the man’s ear. Saikou could not hear what she said. He saw the beadle look at the lamp and decide to wait, which was useful and would not last.

The woman covered the plate with a bath towel. The shadow still went uphill.

Saikou went to the cart. There were seven little mirrors in the basket, wrapped separately. The third had wet its cloth. He did not unwrap it. He laid a strip of black linen over the whole basket and asked the carrier where it had been.

“Prison,” she said. “Rejected. They said it wouldn’t hold its silver.”

“Did you polish it?”

“It’s my living.”

“Since the prison?”

“Only that one. The rest are clean.”

He made her move the basket off the cart with him. The wet wrapping was cold through both layers. Under the arch the crowd had begun to press the rear bearers forward. One slipped, the brass cage tipped, and the flame leaned towards a woman’s loose sleeve.

“Back,” Nera shouted. “Give them room.”

Saikou got his shoulder against the cage’s lower rail and held it up while the bearer found his feet. His blister tore. The pain steadied him in a way he did not appreciate. He had not come to Ecktoria to die because somebody sold used mirrors without asking why they had been rejected.

When the basket was entirely covered, the flame moved.

The bearers took one step. Then another. The beadle cried a blessing as if this had been his intention, and the procession went up the street with its crowd behind it. Nera stood against the wall, breathing hard, his bread flattened in her fist.

“Did you see what was in it?” she asked.

“No. I saw where it wasn’t staying.”

The carrier wanted to know who would pay for the cloth. Saikou paid. She wanted to know who would pay for the mirror. Nera gave her the hospital’s name and told her to bring an invoice. That, Saikou thought, was going to be an interesting piece of paper to collect before the Triumph.

They left the basket in a dark store-room, behind a witnessed line he drew with salt while Nera and the carrier watched. The carrier asked if it was safe there.

“Until I come back for it,” he said. “Don’t put another mirror beside it. Don’t improve the light. If the cloth gets wet, fetch the sister. Don’t touch it yourself.”

Nera handed him what remained of the bread.

“The girl?” he said.

“Livia Valerius. Fourteen. Her father’s a Censor.”

“And the schedule?”

“The third morning from now. The Triumph goes to the Basilica steps.”

Saikou ate the flattened bread as they walked. It tasted of onions and Nera’s fist. Behind them the trumpets reached their difficult turn and, for once, played it together.

Chapter 3 — The visitor’s shoes

The man Sister Nera brought had dust on the bottoms of his trousers and a way of sitting that made the bench look narrower.

He was dark-skinned, with grey in his beard and shoulders that belonged on a dock. His left boot was unlaced. He did not explain this. He took the little horn spoon out of Livia’s bowl, turned it over, and set it down again with the handle pointing towards her left hand.

“Is that more comfortable?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“My name’s Saikou Ira.”

“I know. She told me.”

“Then we’re already ahead.”

She did not know what to say to that. The mirrors showed his face correctly, though the one over the door showed him looking at her when his own eyes were on the wall. He had a bag across his knees and kept his right hand on it.

“Are you going to make me confess?” she asked.

“To what?”

“That’s what I said.”

He looked at Nera. The sister’s mouth went thin.

“The record calls her an undeclared vessel,” she said. “Inquisitor Severian regards that as an admission of harbourage.”

“An interesting use of a building word.”

Livia laughed before she meant to. It was a short, ugly noise. For a moment the thing in her seemed surprised that she could still make one.

Saikou took a folded square of cloth from his bag. There was an orange inside it, small and lumpy and green at one end.

“The sister said you wanted one.”

Livia looked at Nera, who was busy setting her lamp on the floor.

“Can I have the rind?”

“If you like. It’s bitter.”

She took it in both hands. The smell came up when she pressed a nail into the skin, and for an instant it was the most wonderful thing she had ever smelled. Then it changed. Cold ashes. She pressed harder and the white underneath the rind smelled of ashes too.

She put it down.

“I was looking forward to that.”

“I know.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you asked for it. I’d be looking forward to it myself.”

Something spoke out of her mouth.

“You are the one who breaks mirrors.”

She felt Saikou’s hand close on the bag. He did not look towards the crack over the door. He looked at her shoulder, where her dress had pulled sideways under the chain.

“Livia,” he said, “did you feel that coming?”

“A little. Here.” She touched the base of her throat. “Like I’m about to cough. Sometimes there’s nothing.”

“And when it talks, can you hear it?”

“Yes. I can’t always understand it.”

“Do you go away?”

“I wish I did.”

His face remained still, but something in the way he was sitting changed. He moved the orange a little further from her bowl and asked whether she could tell him what the mornings at home had been like before she was brought here.

It was a peculiar question. She told him anyway. Rhea woke her by opening the shutters because her father disliked shouting in the house. Breakfast was bread, and cheese if the cook had remembered she disliked the hard kind, and sometimes a cup of milk. Edda came on the days when her mother was in the market. Livia was meant to practise her letters before she went out, but she could usually persuade Rhea to let her practise afterward.

“What did you go out to do?”

“Things.”

“What things?”

“Buy thread. Look at the horse stalls. Edda says she’s going to ride a horse all the way to Acasia. I said we ought to learn how to ride one first.”

“That sounds wise.”

“She said it sounded like my father.”

“Ah.”

She did not know why the little sound was funny. She found herself smiling at it, and then her jaw locked so hard her back teeth hurt.

“The account is overdue,” her mouth said.

Saikou lifted one finger. Nera came closer with the lamp but did not touch her. He asked Livia to point to the bench with her left hand. She did. He asked her to keep the finger there. She could feel the stone under it, cold and rough.

“Can you see your hand?”

“Yes.”

“Keep seeing it. You don’t have to make the words stop.”

She tried. The thing said account again, and another word, and a number. She stared at her hand until the skin of it seemed separate from the rest of her, until she could see the little split beside her nail and the grey dirt pressed into it. Her finger stayed on the bench.

When her mouth stopped she was exhausted.

“Did I do it?”

“You stayed here.”

“That’s not much.”

“It’s enough for the moment.”

He opened his bag and took out a censer. He told her what was in it, and that it would smell sharp, and that she could turn her head away from the smoke. He did not tell her it would be pleasant. It was not. Her eyes watered at once. In the mirrors her shadow stood up while she remained sitting.

Nera breathed in through her teeth.

Livia’s shadow had long fingers. It laid one against the crack in the mirror above the door and, with the deliberation her father used when moving a marker down a column, drew it to the end.

Saikou covered that mirror.

He used an old black scarf with a darn in it. He asked Brio for a ladder. Brio said there was no ladder, then remembered one in the yard and went for it. While they waited Saikou held the scarf in place with the end of his walking-stick. His arms trembled after a while. Livia had not expected that. She had thought someone who came to break mirrors would be able to stand holding a scarf forever.

When Brio returned, the Invoker made him put the ladder down first and cover the polished hinges before he climbed. He went up awkwardly with his unlaced boot. The scarf hid the crack and the shadow’s finger.

“It isn’t just in me,” Livia said.

“No.”

“Does that mean it can leave?”

“It’s already reaching out. Leaving altogether is another matter.”

“Why hasn’t it?”

“I don’t know yet.”

She was beginning to dislike those words, but less than she disliked the Inquisitor’s certainty. Severian had explained everything before he asked her a single question. He said a child’s innocence made a particularly inviting room for corruption. He had said this as if she ought to be grateful for the explanation.

Saikou came down from the ladder. He asked whether anybody had kept her mother’s medicines.

Her right hand moved.

It reached across her body towards Nera’s lamp. Livia caught it with her left and held the wrist. Her thumb dug into the skin. She could feel it pulling under her palm, as if someone on the far side of a door had taken the handle.

“No,” she said. “I’m not doing that.”

Saikou stood between her hand and the lamp. He did not seize her. He set the lamp beyond the chain’s reach, looked at her finger still planted on the bench, and waited until the right hand loosened.

“The medicines,” he said quietly, “are a bad question for something in this room.”

“They’re in the little cupboard. Father kept them.”

“Thank you.”

“Does that tell you why?”

“It tells me where to begin.”

He put the censer out with a lid that fitted tightly. He put it in his bag only after it had cooled. These were ordinary things, and Livia watched them with more attention than she had given the smoke.

He was going away. She understood that as soon as he started lacing his boot. The orange sat beside her bowl, bright and wasted.

“Will you come back?”

“Yes.”

“Before the third morning?”

“Before that.”

She wanted him to say she would live. She wanted it so badly she hated him a little for not saying it. He took the orange, peeled off a piece of rind, and ate it. His face tightened.

“You were right to ask,” he said. “But not about the rind.”

After he had gone Nera moved the cup back within reach and Brio brought hot water for her hands. Livia took the clean cloth and washed the split beside her nail. The finger on the bench had left a small wet mark. She kept looking at it until the stone drank it dry.

Chapter 4 — The street that had always ended

Fabius Valerius would not see him.

The servant delivered the refusal from behind a grille. She was old enough to have known Saikou when he still had knees that did not complain on stairs, and she looked at the bag in his hand as if she knew exactly what was in it.

“Has he been to the prison?” Saikou asked.

“He sends what’s asked for.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“I know.”

She did not close the little hatch. He could see a hall behind her, a runner stretched down it, and above the runner a row of hooks where winter cloaks would hang. One held a girl’s short blue cloak. The hem had been let down twice; the old stitch marks showed.

“What’s your name?”

“Rhea.”

“I spoke to Livia this morning.”

Rhea’s fingers tightened on the grille. “Did she speak back?”

“She did. She told me you open the shutters.”

The woman shut her eyes for a moment. “There’s a loose catch on the left one. It bangs if I don’t hook it.”

“He’ll need to give me the medicine accounts,” Saikou said. “Not every account in his house. The ones for his wife’s last illness.”

“He says you’re to apply through the Hall.”

“Then I’ll apply through the Hall.”

“He says you’re not to come back here.”

Saikou looked at the cloak. Rhea looked at him looking and lifted her chin.

“It’ll get damp if it’s put in a chest.”

“Leave it on its hook.”

He went back down the steps. His heel was worse now, and the bread had been a long time ago. Outside the gate a woman selling roasted beans offered him a paper twist. He bought two and ate one while he walked towards the Hall. A child followed him for a little way asking whether he was the man who had stopped the procession. He said he was the man who had helped get it moving. The child appeared disappointed.

The Hall’s records office closed for the noon meal.

Saikou reached it six minutes after the door had been shut. He found that out from the porter, who had a water-clock and considered punctuality a matter of personal character. The porter could not tell him when the office would reopen. The clerks were in a meeting. Meetings, he explained, did not belong to the water-clock.

Saikou sat on the step and ate the second twist of beans.

A thin man came out of the adjoining door with a measuring rod under his arm. The rod was ivory, or something like ivory, with darker bands along it. The man stopped when he saw Saikou’s bag, glanced at the porter, and asked if he was seeking a route to the Censor’s house.

“Records,” Saikou said. “His house doesn’t want me.”

“Records are this way.” The man pointed down the side street. “The annex.”

“The porter said this door.”

“The porter means requests. If you want to look at the instruments themselves, the annex. I’m Cornelius. I supervise the decumanus measures.”

The porter nodded as if this was entirely clear. Saikou got up, thanked them both, and went where the rod had pointed.

The street was a cul-de-sac. He knew it was a cul-de-sac before he reached the end. There was a fountain at its closed end and a little shrine behind it. There had always been a fountain there. He could almost remember seeing it on a previous visit, though he had not been in this quarter on any previous visit he could place.

A woman washing steps told him the annex entrance was round by the grain arcade.

He went round by the grain arcade. Cornelius was there measuring a drain cover. The rod in his hand looked, at closer range, like a finger bone longer than any finger ought to be.

“Did you find it?” the surveyor asked.

“The fountain.”

“Yes. Quite handsome, before the repairs.”

“Where’s the annex door?”

“Under the third arch. There’s a porter.”

There was. Saikou found the door, and the porter, and a queue of eleven people with folded petitions. He stood at the end of the queue. After a while Cornelius came past him and stopped again.

“You’re Ira, aren’t you? The foreign practitioner.”

“I’ve been called worse things in my own language.”

Cornelius smiled politely. “There’s a name in the Censor’s Hall I’d like you to ask about. If you find yourself with access.”

“Whose?”

“I’ll write it when you have access.”

“Then we can discuss it when I have access.”

The surveyor nodded. He did not look conspiratorial. He looked like a man whose work took him up and down the same streets all day, and who had encountered another man travelling them with a purpose.

Saikou did not catch him lying. He had no lie to catch. He could not retrieve a memory of an open street from beneath his memory of the fountain. The Witness could show a watched hand taking money, a stain beneath a clean surface, the place where a presence had entered. It could not give him a city untouched by all the things that had happened to its remembering.

He moved forward in the queue.

At the desk he met Pell, a copy-clerk with three ink-stained fingers and a spectacles frame repaired with wire. Pell took his request, read it, and turned it over.

“Authority?”

“Sister Nera, Quiet Seal.”

“That’s religious authority.”

“There’s a girl in their prison.”

“That’s religious custody.”

“Her father’s a Censor.”

“That’s civil office.”

Pell took off his spectacles. Without them he looked younger and much more tired. “I’m not making this difficult for pleasure. An account in a Censor’s own keeping is privileged until either the Censor releases it or an examiner opens a cause. Your sister’s cause is against the daughter.”

“She isn’t my sister.”

“Your religious sister.”

“Ah. Yes.”

Pell put the spectacles on again. “If you can give me an instrument number, I can furnish an attested copy of a public assignment. It’ll cost six copper and the search.”

“What can you search without his permission?”

“Assignments registered here. Security transfers. Paid claims. No private memoranda.”

“Medicine purchases?”

“If somebody assigned payment for them.”

Saikou laid six copper on the desk. “His wife was Marcia. She died three years ago. Start there.”

Pell did not touch the coins. “The search costs more if I have to go by relationship. People die every day.”

Saikou paid the search fee. Pell gave him a receipt and told him to wait on the bench. It was the first clear instruction he had received since entering the Hall, and he took pleasure in following it.

By late afternoon Pell had found three entries.

The first was for a syrup of mountain bark, purchased from an apothecary who was now dead. The second assigned the debt to a factor who had left Ecktoria. The third consolidated it with a hospital subscription, an unpaid grain assessment, and a guarantee against a woman’s weaving shop.

“Why the weaving shop?” Saikou asked.

“Fabius acquired the claim. It became security.”

“Did he own the shop?”

“He came to.”

Pell tapped the dates. “There’s something you should notice. That last consolidation isn’t three years ago. It’s last winter.”

Saikou looked at the chain of sums. Each transfer named a price. Each price was plausible. Several were cheaper than the amount owing. The debt ought to have shrunk. Instead the consolidated figure grew.

He did not feel clever. He felt the city’s floor under his feet, cracked with soot, and the clean space in a contract where a protective sentence should have been.

“Can you copy these?”

“Yes.”

“All three. Attested.”

Pell began sharpening his pen. “That’s another charge.”

“I’d guessed.”

As the clerk worked, Saikou took a little smooth road-stone out of his coat and held it in his palm. He asked the Traveler for a path from this knowledge to the next place he needed to be. Not a path through somebody else’s locked conscience. Not an answer. A way to make the distance before the light went.

When Pell looked up from the first sheet, a porter was standing in the door with a message for the foreign practitioner. Sister Nera had sent it by a boy who knew the alleys. The boy waited outside, bouncing on his heels.

The prison’s largest mirror had cracked again.

Chapter 5 — Visitors she had not invited

Edda brought three ribbons and no useful news.

That was what Livia thought when her cousin came into the cell, and she was ashamed of it almost immediately. Edda had got past the prison gate and two guards and whatever Sister Nera had said to her outside. She had brought the blue ribbon Livia preferred, the yellow one she did not, and a new green one bought from her own money. She stood in the doorway holding them together in a little fan and looking at the covered mirror over the door.

“I thought I could do your hair.”

Livia had imagined their reunion several times. In each version she had been dignified, or despairing, or so angry that Edda would understand at once what imprisonment had done to her. In none of them had her hair been the first subject.

“All right,” she said.

Edda came to the bench. Nera stood by the wall with her lamp. The guard did not close the door, though he stayed in it, and this made Edda talk too loudly about things that were not worth talking about. A horse dealer had tried to sell her uncle a mare with a bad foot. The cloth merchant in the square had married for the third time. Their old writing master had gone deaf in one ear and now turned the other towards whoever he was scolding.

“Does he still spit when he says possession?” Livia asked.

Edda stopped combing.

Then she laughed, a small breath, and Livia laughed with her, and for a moment they were in their old classroom with a slate between them and the master spraying the air above it.

The thing inside Livia went still. She felt its stillness the way she felt a person listening behind a door. It was worse than its moving.

Edda found a knot. She worked at it with the end of the comb and pulled too hard.

“Ow.”

“Sorry. Your hair’s impossible.”

“I’m imprisoned. It’s allowed.”

Nera smiled at the floor. Livia saw and felt pleased, which annoyed her. She did not want to be pleased by making a sister smile. She wanted Edda to tell her how they were going to get her out.

“Has Father asked for me?”

Edda held the comb motionless.

“He sent clothes.”

“I saw the clothes. Has he asked?”

“He’s been at the Hall.”

Edda knew nothing, or she knew something she had been told not to say. Livia twisted round too quickly and the chain caught Edda’s skirt. She had to turn back and untangle it. Her cousin lifted the hem with both hands as if it was an awkward part of a dance.

“Are you frightened of me?” Livia asked.

“Yes,” Edda said, and then went red. “Not you. Of—”

“It’s in me. You can say.”

“I don’t want it to hurt you.”

“It already does.”

“I know.”

“You don’t.”

“No. I don’t.” Edda set the comb down beside the ribbons. “I wanted to come anyway.”

That was better than the ribbons. Livia did not say it. She picked up the green one and rubbed its edge between two fingers. It was good ribbon, woven firm enough to keep its shape. Edda had probably paid too much.

Afterward Rhea came, bringing a little pot of the cook’s bean paste. She had been told not to bring a metal lid, so the paste was covered with a leaf tied down by thread. Rhea explained all of this to Nera in great detail, and Livia knew she was explaining because she did not know how to begin speaking to the girl on the bench.

“Is the shutter still loose?” Livia asked.

“Yes. I’ll get it seen to.”

“You won’t. Father hates the noise of the joiner.”

“I’ll get it seen to while he’s at the Hall.”

They looked at each other. Rhea put the pot on the floor and came to the bench and took Livia’s hands. The chain lay between them. The old woman picked it up, found a place where it would not press, and set it down again.

“You look like your mother about the eyes.”

“Don’t.”

“All right.”

Livia had not meant to forbid it. She wanted desperately to be told more about her mother, and hated being offered resemblance instead. Marcia had laughed in the middle of reading when she found a phrase she liked. She had kept shoes under the wrong beds and sent Rhea to look for them. In her last illness she had become polite to everyone, even people she disliked, and Livia had known from that before anyone told her that something was terribly wrong.

Saikou came back while Rhea was still there.

He smelled of rain, though it had not rained in the prison yard, and he carried rolled papers in an oilskin. He greeted Edda by name because Nera had told him who she was, and Rhea as if he had expected to find her. His boot was laced now. Livia noticed the lace because she did not want him to catch her looking hopeful.

He examined the covered mirror without taking off the scarf. Under the cloth the crack made a noise like a cup cooling after hot water had been poured into it.

“How long has it been doing that?”

“Since you left,” Nera said.

He turned towards the six uncovered mirrors. They had a layer of mist along their bottoms. Livia’s reflection in the nearest one had no blue ribbon, though Edda had tied it into her hair.

“We can’t leave her between all of these,” he said.

“The Inquisitor ordered them.”

“I’ll speak to him.”

Livia looked at the door. “Will he listen?”

“He’ll hear me. We’ll find out about listening.”

He sat on the bench at its empty end, put his oilskin across his knees, and asked Rhea whether Marcia had left any letters.

“A few. To her sister. They were never posted.”

“Where are they?”

“With the medicine. In the little cupboard.”

Livia’s fingers clenched on Rhea’s hands. The thing was awake inside her again. Its attention turned to Saikou. Her shoulder drew back, though she had not moved it, and her mouth filled with the taste of copper.

“Let go,” she told Rhea.

The woman let go at once.

Saikou put his papers down and took a little coin of dark metal from his pocket. He did not point it at Livia. He held it between thumb and finger, edge upwards, and asked Nera for the cup of water.

“Not a summoning,” he said to the room, in a voice different from the one he used with them. “A petition across a kept threshold. Marcia Valerius, if you are within hearing, I ask what you chose to leave.”

The water in the cup made a little dent in its surface.

Livia leaned forward. Rhea made a sound. Saikou shook his head, not at either of them, and spoke again.

“Varnek Karn, carry the question. No word is mine to compel.”

For a while nothing happened. Then Livia heard a woman cough.

She knew the cough. She hated knowing it. Not her mother’s laugh, not her voice reading, but the small embarrassed cough she had tried to hide in her pillow. Livia gripped the chain until a link pressed its shape into her palm.

The dent in the water deepened.

A voice said, “The letter to Irena.”

Rhea put both hands over her mouth. Livia could not move. She wanted to put her face over the cup and ask every question that had remained after the bedroom was shut. Why had her mother not told her? Had the syrup hurt? Had she heard Livia in the passage the last night?

Saikou did not ask those questions.

“What is the price of answering?” he said.

“Post it.”

“To your sister?”

“Post it.”

“I will carry it to a living messenger. I cannot promise she receives it.”

The dent became smooth water.

Saikou put the dark coin away. He looked older than he had before speaking. Livia waited for the room to become a room again. Her mother’s cough seemed to remain in the space over the cup, though she knew it had stopped.

“Why didn’t you let me?” she said.

He understood her at once. She saw that, and hated him for understanding without having given her what she wanted.

“She answered one petition. I couldn’t keep her there for ours.”

“But you could have asked.”

“Yes,” he said. “I could have asked.”

Rhea touched her shoulder. Livia moved away.

She had wanted help, and it had come with a dead woman’s cough and another door that would not stay open. She did not want to speak to any of them. Edda gathered the ribbons, hesitated over the green one, and left it beside the pot of bean paste.

Saikou rose to go. At the door he turned back.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “That was your mother before it was my question.”

It did not fix anything. It was better than telling her she ought to understand.

After they left, Livia ate the bean paste with her shortened spoon. It tasted of beans. For the first time that day, something she put in her mouth tasted of what it was.

Chapter 6 — The office that could ask

The Lame King held his audience in a garden because the audience chamber belonged to a commission that had not provided him with a key.

Saikou learned this from the chamberlain. He had asked why three petitioners were standing among cabbages, and the chamberlain had answered with the composure of a man who had explained much worse arrangements. The cabbages were planted by the royal kitchen. The kitchen’s rights had survived four reallocations of the palace estates because nobody had considered it worth drawing up an instrument to transfer them.

“Can he give me access to a Censor’s archive?” Saikou asked.

“He can ask that a cause be opened.”

“Can he require it?”

“No.”

“Can he stop a burning?”

“The Synod holds that power.”

The chamberlain looked at his blistered boot and his dusty bag. “His Majesty will tell you what he can do. People seldom believe the chamberlain.”

The king sat under a vine with his bad leg on a stool. He was neither very old nor very impressive at first sight. His robe had been mended along one sleeve. On the table beside him lay a stack of instruments marked with little strips of blue paper. He had read them. Saikou could tell by the thumb smudges, by the places where corrections crowded the margins, and by the way he did not reach for them when the chamberlain announced the case.

“Livia,” the king said. “Fabius’s daughter.”

“You know her?”

“I know who she is. That isn’t the same.”

He asked Saikou to sit, and Saikou sat with some gratitude because the walk to the garden had been longer than the chamberlain’s gestures implied.

“I can put my name to a request for an examiner,” the king said. “I can also witness your request myself, in public, so no one may say you never made it. I can’t make the examiner hear it tomorrow. I can’t tell the Censor to hand you his books. I can’t tell Severian which doctrine to apply.”

“What price?”

“That you ask me outside the garden.”

Saikou looked at the petitioners waiting among the cabbages.

“Before them?”

“Before them, and anyone who can see the east gate. You need my legitimacy. It’s a real thing. I need you to be seen acknowledging it.” The king moved his leg with both hands. “I don’t need a foreigner to praise me. That’s worth very little here. I need him to ask me for something I can actually do.”

Saikou could imagine the account that would be made of it. The foreign Invoker seeking the king’s permission. The king harbouring an untutored practitioner. The Censor’s property under royal scrutiny. Every faction could use a different piece and none would have to invent much.

“There’s a girl with less than three days,” he said.

“I know.”

“If I ask publicly, can the request name her safety as its purpose?”

“Yes.”

“Not the restoration of any ancient prerogative?”

The king smiled, briefly. “You’ve been warned about me.”

“I’ve been warned about most people.”

“Name the girl. Name the records. Name the cause. I’ll witness those things, and I’ll send the request. That’s all I’m offering.”

They went to the east gate. The king walked with two sticks and refused the chamberlain’s arm until he reached the uneven last step. There he accepted it without ceremony. Saikou stood on the pavement below him with the papers Pell had copied and stated what he had come to ask.

The petitioners followed. A kitchen boy came out wiping his hands. Two passers-by stopped. By the time Saikou finished, twelve people had heard that the account for Marcia Valerius’s medicine continued to circulate after her death, and that the daughter was imprisoned under an execution order issued without an examination of that account.

The king repeated the request in his own words. A clerk wrote it. The clerk read it back. Saikou heard the phrase “without examination” become “pending examination,” and objected. The king made the clerk restore it.

Then he put his seal to the sheet.

It was a narrow thing. It did not spring locks. It did not dismiss guards. By sunset it had made Pell’s superintendent afraid to refuse a public copy search outright, and that was enough to get Saikou back through the annex door.

Pell had four more assignments waiting.

He had made notes on separate scraps, because he did not trust the order in which the registry now remembered its bundles. The amounts were troubling. The witnesses were more troubling. One seller appeared twice as a buyer, and the second time he bought the obligation for less than he had already received in interest.

“There’s a Black Banner receipt here,” Pell said. “Ykrul settlement levy. It got consolidated with the same security.”

“I thought they were being paid to guard the stores.”

“They are. The levy’s older.”

“Where’s the paymaster?”

“Rented rooms by the north gate. Ask for Togan.”

“Does he speak Ecktorian?”

“More precisely than I do.”

Togan’s room smelled of lamp oil and leather. There was no banner displayed, only a ledger on a plain table and an iron cash box under his feet. He was a broad man with hair plaited close to his scalp. He read the copy Saikou handed him and asked what answer was wanted.

“Who paid whom.”

“Two silver for a searched account. One if you give me the day.”

“I have the day.”

“One silver, then.”

“Can the hospital settle it afterward?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Togan sat back. “Because afterward is where people put the money they don’t have. Because my soldiers need bread today, not the gratitude of a respectable house next season. Because Ecktoria agreed to compensate Ykrul families when the old campaign accounts were finally admitted, and three treasuries have since agreed that the fourth ought to pay. Because if I take your promise, somebody under my banner eats less while you decide which of your obligations is urgent.”

He opened the cash box. “One silver. I won’t tell you twice.”

Saikou paid.

Togan searched quickly. His index was a strip of leather with holes punched along it, each hole fitted to a date marker. The receipt was genuine. Fabius had paid the mercenary company in full. The company had cancelled its interest in the consolidated obligation. A copy of that cancellation had gone back to the Censor’s Hall.

Pell’s copy had no cancellation attached.

“Could the interest have been resold?” Saikou asked.

“Not by us.”

“Would your copy say if it had?”

“Yes. It would be a very different account.”

Saikou paid another half-silver for an attested copy of the cancellation. Togan sanded it, blew off the sand, and asked whether this concerned the child in the prison.

“Yes.”

“The procession will come through our store lane on the third morning. The captain has a cordon there.”

“Will he let me through?”

“With a paid pass naming what you carry. No soldiers for hire from that cordon. It has a job.”

Saikou counted his coins. He could buy a pass now, or wait until he knew what he would be carrying, when the price might rise and the clerk might not be available. He asked for a pass allowing himself and one covered container to use the lane from the prison to the Dolmis steps, valid until sunset on the Triumph day.

Togan named a price. Saikou paid it and felt his purse become unpleasantly light.

“Nothing on credit,” he said.

“Nothing,” Togan agreed. “Your container must stay covered. If you bring an armed escort, that’s a different passage.”

Outside, a clerk in a red sash was waiting by the door. He looked at the king’s seal on Saikou’s folded petition and then at the paid pass in his hand.

“You’ve attracted a number of offices,” the clerk said.

“I’m hoping one of them does its work.”

“The Red Vigil takes an interest in missing clauses.”

“So do I.”

The clerk held out a card with an address on it. Saikou did not take it. He had enough doors to visit and no time to discover what a new one would ask him to forget.

Chapter 7 — A room with fewer faces

On the second morning Livia woke with a name in her mouth and a cut on her wrist.

The name was Dama. The cut had come from the edge of the chain cuff where she had twisted it in the night. She knew what had made the cut. She did not know who Dama was. That was almost a luxury, being able to separate one thing from another.

Brio brought her bread and a little dish of oil. He stayed at the hatch while she ate. His wife had sent another egg. He offered it to Livia, looking embarrassed, and she said yes because she was hungry, not because she wanted to make him feel better.

“Do you know Dama?” she asked.

The guard’s hand stopped at the hatch.

“Why?”

“I said it when I woke.”

“My aunt’s called Dama.”

“Does she have a weaving shop?”

The words had come before she thought of them. She felt the thing listening with her ears. Brio took the egg out of her reach.

“Give it back,” she said.

He looked at the egg as though he had not noticed taking it. He set it down again. His face was pale.

“I don’t know your aunt,” Livia said. “I didn’t choose that.”

“I know.”

But he did not know. She could hear him trying to know it, just as she tried, and she wished he would say something else. He shut the hatch gently, which was worse than banging it.

The nearest mirror showed him still standing there.

It showed the inside of the corridor through a stone wall. Brio’s shoulder, the cloth with his own egg in it, the place where he had rubbed the brass of his shield until Nera told him to stop. Then the reflection turned its head and looked at Livia, though Brio had gone.

Its mouth opened.

She called for the sister.

The mirror’s Brio put one hand through the glass. Not all the way. The fingers came out first, pale and flat, moving sideways as if feeling for the air of the cell. A crack ran from the wrist to the mirror’s corner. The noise hurt Livia’s teeth.

She slid off the bench and crawled beneath it. The chain caught around its leg and jerked her back. She had practised finding the length of the chain; she knew where it ended. She had not practised hiding under a bench while a man’s hand came through a mirror.

The door opened. Brio was there, the real one, with Nera behind him. He saw his hand in the glass and stopped so suddenly the sister struck his back.

“Cover it,” she said.

He pulled his cloak off and threw it over the mirror. The fingers caught the cloth. Livia saw them through its folds, a hand searching inside a sleeve. Brio held the corners against the bolts with both hands. His arms shook. Nera went to the door and shouted for help.

Saikou came at a run. He had another man behind him, thin and bald, wearing the white collar of the Inquisition. Livia knew Severian. She pressed herself further under the bench.

Saikou did not look at her first. He looked at the cloth and the hand inside it, took a jar from his bag, and poured a line across the floor below the mirror. Nera knelt at one end of the line. Brio stood at the other, still holding the cloth. Saikou said something quietly, three times, and the hand became a fold of cloak.

Then he looked under the bench.

“Are you hurt?”

“My wrist.”

“Let me see.”

She crawled out a little and held it up. He looked, nodded, and said Nera would clean it. He did not tell her to stand until he had taken the chain off the bench leg.

Severian watched from the doorway.

“You’ve disturbed the confinement,” he said.

“The confinement is offering it seven exits.” Saikou wiped a little salt off his hand. “It’s learning the guard’s reflection. You saw that yourself.”

“The girl draws it towards those who pity her.”

“I asked for an egg,” Livia said.

All of them looked at her.

“That was what I did. I asked for an egg.”

Severian’s face tightened. For a moment she thought he would slap her, and she hated that Saikou had to move a little closer for her to feel safe.

“The voice is skilled at simplification,” the Inquisitor said.

“Then write down which voice you heard,” Nera said. “The girl spoke of the egg. We all heard her.”

Severian looked at Nera’s lamp. The chain on it was blackened, its little links fitted one inside another. The Quiet Seal lamps were different from the ceremonial censers. Livia had once liked looking at them in the Basilica, before anybody brought one into a cell for her.

Saikou asked for a room without mirrors.

Severian said no. He asked for a room with one controlled threshold, two ordinary lamps, covered metalwork, and a place to set three portable mirrors. Severian said that would be an unauthorized rite. Nera took a paper from her sleeve and said her order could authorize the rite if the practitioner accepted its conditions.

Livia listened as they discussed her. She wanted to tell them she was still there. She also wanted them to keep discussing, because it sounded as if they might find a way out of the mirrored cell and she did not want to interrupt that.

“The father must answer the evidence afterward,” Nera said. “He cannot buy the silence of the house with her recovery.”

“He’s not offering to buy it,” Severian said.

“Then it costs him nothing to refrain.”

Saikou held out his hand for the paper. “I’ll deliver what I find to the examiner and your order. I won’t promise a verdict I don’t control.”

Nera nodded. “That will do.”

Severian stood a long time with his hands together inside his sleeves. Livia watched the edge of one thumbnail, clean and square, pressing into the other hand. He wanted the Triumph. She could feel that much without any help from the thing in her. There was to be a girl on the steps, there were to be names read, there was to be a flame that made an answer public. A private answer in a room would not be the same answer.

“The order remains,” he said at last. “If the rite fails, she goes to the Triumph. If the practitioner fails to attend, she goes. If the host is judged occupied afterward, she goes. The transfer is within the prison. No release.”

Nera held the paper steady while he added his mark.

They took Livia down a corridor she had never seen. Brio carried the chain’s free end. Saikou carried his bag. The mirrors were behind them, all covered now, and she kept looking over her shoulder until he told her he was watching the back.

The new room had been used for washing altar cloths. There were stone tubs against one wall, a table, and a barred window high up. Its air smelled of wet linen. She thought it was beautiful.

Nera cleaned the cut and wrapped it. Edda came in with the ribbons and a bundle of clothes. Brio put the chain through a ring in the wall, but Saikou made him move it to the ring beside the bench so she could reach the chamber pot without scraping her wrist.

“Could you have done that in the other room?” she asked.

“Yes,” Brio said.

“Then why didn’t you?”

He looked at the cuff. “I didn’t think.”

She wanted to make him feel worse. The thing in her wanted that too, and because she felt its wanting she stopped.

After they had settled her, Saikou sat at the table with a woman Livia had never seen. The woman had thick fingers and a cloth bag on her lap. She took a little spool from the bag, turned it, and put it back.

“Dama,” Brio said from the door.

His aunt. Livia knew before anybody explained. The woman looked at her, then at the covered cuff, and then away.

“I’ve come about the shop,” she said.

Something under Livia’s tongue laughed.

Livia put her finger on the bench and kept it there. Nera saw, and moved the lamp so its light fell on the hand. The woman with the spool began to tell Saikou what Fabius had taken, and Livia stayed in the room to hear it, because she had nowhere else to go.

Chapter 8 — What the paper held

Dama’s shop had had two looms, three apprentices, and a roof that needed mending every winter.

Saikou listened without interrupting. She spoke of the roof first, and then the apprentices, and only later the loan. He knew people did this. The thing that had been lost was seldom the figure in the account. He had lost a ship once, and knew there had been a cook aboard who sang while he scraped fish. The ship’s name was gone from him now. The cook’s singing remained, though he could not place its tune.

Fabius had advanced money for the roof. The rate had been reasonable. Dama had signed on the Coin-House floor because the factor said the terms would be witnessed there by the house. She had paid through spring, and then one of the apprentices had broken an arm and orders fell behind. Fabius had called the whole principal in at once.

“The grace was supposed to be till the next quarter,” she said. “I knew it when I signed. I can’t remember signing it. I know that doesn’t make sense.”

“The clause isn’t in the registered copy,” Saikou said.

“I know. I asked for the copy. They showed me.”

Fabius took the first loom, then the second. The apprentices found other places. Dama made thread now and sold it by the basket. Brio’s wife sometimes brought her dinner. She put this last fact in with the account as if it was another debt that might be used against her.

Saikou showed her the consolidated instrument.

“That’s my seal,” she said. “But I never owed for somebody’s medicine.”

“You didn’t. Your shop secured a claim that had been joined to it.”

“Is that different?”

“On paper.”

He heard Livia’s breath change from the bench. Nera lifted the lamp. The girl’s finger pressed into the stone, white at the tip. He put the instrument face down and waited. Dama looked from him to the girl, and her hand tightened around the spool in her pocket.

“I’m frightened of what’s in her,” she said.

“So am I,” Saikou said.

That appeared to surprise everyone in the room except Nera. He had no time to be reassuring at the expense of accuracy.

He needed the beginning of the account. Public records showed its transfers, not what had first been promised. Marcia’s letter was in a private cupboard behind a blood-keyed ward. Fabius would not admit him. The examiner appointed upon the king’s request would not arrive before the Triumph. Every office had done enough of its proper work to make another office responsible for the delay.

Rhea was waiting outside the prison.

She had brought a receipt from the house, folded into the back of an old prayer book. Marcia’s medicines had been placed in the cupboard on the day of her death. Fabius had countersigned the inventory, then opened his thumb on the metal clasp and pressed blood beside the signature. Rhea had signed as housekeeper. He had given her this duplicate and told her to retain it against accusations of loss.

“He thought of that,” she said. “He thought of loss.”

The dried blood lay brown in the paper. Saikou did not mistake it for permission. It was evidence of the ward’s making, and a connection he could use to reach its edge. What he would do there remained his decision.

“Will he dismiss you?”

“He may.”

“Where will you go?”

“My niece. If she’s still in the same rooms.” Rhea touched the prayer book. “I’ve sent a boy to ask.”

They went to the house after dusk, Nera with them. The gate was shut. Rhea had a key. She opened it with a hand that shook only when the key came out again. Fabius was at the Hall. She had checked. They entered through the kitchen and went up the narrow servants’ stair, where Saikou struck his shoulder against the wall at the turn.

“Built for thin people,” he muttered.

“Built for people carrying trays,” Rhea said. “You’re going round it wrong.”

He went round it her way. The ordinary correction pleased him. For a moment, standing on the landing to let his heel stop aching, he remembered his daughter as a small weight against his shin. She had been under a table while he mended a strap, pressing her bare feet into his leg and whispering a joke she thought no one else could hear. He could no longer summon her face. He could summon the warm soles of her feet, the whisper, and his own ridiculous effort not to laugh because laughing would give her away.

He let the memory sit with him for a breath. Then Rhea opened the study door.

The room smelled of cedar and dry ink. A scale stood on the desk, its pans tied together for storage. The medicine cupboard was set into the wall beside a window. Its little lock was ordinary brass. Behind it, beyond the wood, a threshold had been made that had nothing to do with brass.

Saikou put his silver mirror on the desk. He called the Witness once, with his hand on the frame, and angled the glass towards the cupboard.

It showed the thumb pressed into the inventory. A hand turning a key. Four bottles on a shelf, a paper packet, letters laid flat beneath them. It showed what had passed that threshold. It did not show Fabius’s intentions. It did not tell Saikou whether the man had cried afterward.

In the reflection there was also a page with a black corner, lying under a wooden weight.

Saikou covered the mirror. “That’s what we need.”

He set Rhea’s duplicate on the floor and made Nera stand where she could see both it and the cupboard. Rhea stood beside the window, away from the reflected line. He drew a narrow bar of salt and iron across the cupboard’s sill, not around the whole room, and laid two fingers on the dried blood.

“An instrument kept by its maker,” he said. “Witness the making. Witness the opening. No claim upon the blood of anyone living.”

The ward pushed back.

It did not flash. Saikou’s thumbnail darkened, and pain travelled from his fingers into his elbow as if somebody had slammed a drawer on it. He kept his hand flat on the paper. Nera said Rhea’s name, and Saikou’s, and her own. The named observers stayed where they were.

He called the Sentinel through his little iron gate-Symbol.

The pressure found his shoulder. He was sweating. He had opened thresholds held by worse things, and thresholds held by much simpler things, and neither fact made this one give. The ward recognized Fabius’s blood. It also recognized that the hand upon it was not Fabius’s. Saikou had to make a witnessed passage that did not depend on pretending otherwise.

He moved the iron Symbol to the sill with his free hand and said what he was taking: the medicine account, the enclosed letter, the page with the black corner. Nothing else. Rhea repeated the names of the objects. Nera repeated them after her.

The lock clicked.

His fingers came away from the receipt with a white blister on each tip. He did not open the cupboard until the pressure was entirely gone. Then Rhea turned the ordinary key, and he took the three objects with her watching.

The page with the black corner was folded into a merchant’s manual. Its heading named Maelstraeus’s terms for obligations whose original holders could no longer be found. The terms were genuinely favourable to a man who needed time. Interest could be suspended. Security could be divided. A creditor could receive full value without awaiting a mortal collection.

Below the terms a sentence had been added in Fabius’s hand.

What is owed may collect itself from one who never promised it.

Saikou sat down.

The first invoice for medicine named a finite price. The next purchase named another. Fabius had signed an open guarantee to continue supplying Marcia while she lived. The protective limit had disappeared from the copy retained on the Coin-House floor. After her death the supply obligation remained, without a living patient to receive it and without an original creditor to close it. Factors bought portions at a discount. Fabius bought some back. Then he acquired other debts, profitable ones, and used their securities to meet the demand that would not finish.

Dama’s roof was there. Togan’s cancelled levy was there. An orchard sold after a missed payment was there. Beside the orchard Fabius had written adequate recovery.

Saikou turned back to the sentence in the manual. No signature of Livia’s appeared anywhere. No promise made by her. The account had entered the house as care purchased for a wife, and had found a child who could be made to carry what care had left owing.

A soft voice spoke behind his shoulder.

“The terms allow adjustment.”

He did not turn. Nera’s lamp had gone pale. Rhea was staring at the empty chair behind him.

“Who is speaking?” she whispered.

“Something with an interest in the paper,” Saikou said.

“The balance can be reduced,” the voice said. “You have witnesses. You have standing. You have property.”

He covered the page with the black corner. The voice remained, courteous and patient. He could feel how favourable the offer would be. A smaller balance, a quiet girl, a father who could keep his house. Someone else would fail to remember a clause on a day when it mattered.

He put the page in the oilskin and tied it shut.

“I’m taking evidence,” he said. “I’m not purchasing a settlement.”

The voice said nothing more. It had not needed to lie.

They left by the tray stair with Marcia’s letter still sealed. At the kitchen door Rhea took her apron off its hook and put it in her bag. Saikou waited while she did it. Outside, the street that led to the fountain had always ended there, and they went the long way round.

Chapter 9 — Seven names

Dama came back the next afternoon and asked Livia if she remembered the shop.

Livia did. There had been a basket of bright scraps beside the doorway, and Dama used to let children choose one if they did not scatter the rest. Livia had taken a red piece long enough to make a cloak for a wooden soldier. She had forgotten that until she saw the spool in the woman’s hand.

“I remember the scraps.”

Dama nodded. “Everybody remembers the scraps.”

“Was the roof already bad?”

“It was always bad. A roof over a loom gets more of the weather than it ought.”

Livia did not understand this, and said so. Dama explained the way damp swelled thread, and how a dry day after a wet one could change the whole web’s tension. She showed it with the thread from her spool, pulling a length between her fingers and letting one end slacken. Livia watched. It was wonderful to be told something that did not concern what might kill her.

The thing in her was quiet. It had been quiet since Saikou returned in the morning with a packet of papers and a burn on his hand. He had not told her everything that was in the papers. He had told her it began with her mother’s medicines, and that her father had done things afterward which would be examined separately from her.

“Will he come?” she had asked.

“I’ve asked him.”

“Did he say yes?”

“No.”

She had wanted more than no. A reason, an insult she could answer in her head, a delay she could interpret as a promise. Saikou had given her the word her father gave him, without improving it, and gone to speak to Nera.

Now Dama sat with her thread while the corridor filled with voices.

Brio’s aunt was going to stay for the rite. So was Brio. So was Edda, whose mother had argued in the prison yard for half an hour before Nera took her aside and said something that made the argument quieter but not shorter. Rhea had brought her bag because she was sleeping at her niece’s. Pell had arrived with a slate and a fresh bottle of ink. Livia had never seen a clerk come into a prison without wanting her to sign something.

The seventh was a woman called Sabina.

She had a leather apron folded over one arm and a wooden case over the other. She was about forty, with a missing tip on one finger. She set the case on the table, opened it, and showed three mirrors wrapped in different cloths. One was Saikou’s own, with the old left-edge crack. The others were different sizes. The larger one had a little dent in its frame.

“I made that frame,” Sabina said, when Livia looked at it. “Then I dropped it on the stairs.”

“Does it still work?”

“It’s a frame. It holds the glass.”

“What happened to the other ones?”

“Taken against the hospital’s arrears.”

“Can they do that?”

“They did it.” Sabina took out a square of soft cloth and cleaned a fingerprint from the dented frame. “The order was cheap. It wasn’t protected from the rest of the house’s charges. The man selling them said everybody knew that. The brother who bought them couldn’t remember whether he’d asked.”

Livia looked at the case. Sabina covered the mirrors again before they could gather her face.

“Did you give us yours?”

“Sold them. The sister paid some and Rhea paid some.”

Rhea, by the window, looked away quickly.

“Don’t go crying about it,” Sabina said. “They’re serviceable stock. I’ll make another pair. I’m here because he wanted somebody who understands what glass does when it’s under pressure. I can tell him before it goes.”

Saikou came in carrying three little dishes and a sack. He had taken his coat off. His shirt stuck to his shoulders where he had been running. Nera followed with a bundle of papers and the expression she wore when she had got permission for something no one else would admit they had permitted.

“The washing room is ours until the dawn inspection,” she said. “Severian will attend afterward. He’ll bring a physician from Mercy and Grace. If there’s no occupying presence, the execution order can be suspended pending his report.”

“And if he says there is?” Edda asked.

“He’ll have to show what he examined.”

That was not the same as saying everything would be all right. Livia heard Edda swallow. She put her hand out and caught a bit of her cousin’s skirt, because the chain would not quite let her reach the hand.

Saikou set the dishes down. In one there was salt. In another, iron filings. In the third, pale powder like ground chalk.

“Bone,” he said, when Livia looked at it.

“Whose?”

“An old hospice attendant. Her name was Tessa. Her family gave her ashes and the remaining bone to the house. Nera’s order kept an account of her dying. She wasn’t afraid.”

“How can you know?”

“I know what the people with her reported. I’ve checked the witness to that account. It’s the best knowledge I have.”

Livia stared at the powder. She wanted no part of somebody else’s death, and knew that saying so would make everyone look at her with those careful faces again.

“Does she mind?”

“Her family permitted its use in the house’s work. I won’t make her speak to approve every use of it.”

She considered the answer. It was unpleasant, and different from the Inquisitor’s answers, which were always very beautiful and made her feel as if she had done something dirty by asking.

Saikou mixed the powder with the salt and iron. Nera wrote the names of the people in the room on a sheet and read them aloud.

Rhea. Edda. Brio. Dama. Sister Nera. Pell. Sabina.

“Seven witnesses,” she said.

“Am I one?” Livia asked.

“You’re the person we’re here for,” Saikou said. “You can tell us what you feel. You don’t have to keep the others in their places.”

He put Rhea near the head of the bench, and Edda beside her. Dama would stand where she could see Livia’s mouth without looking into the mirrors. Brio would be by the door, watching both the room and the corridor. Nera would face Livia, her lamp beside the mirror that was to show the girl her own face. Pell would record what was said from the table beyond the line. Sabina would stand at the far point between the other two mirrors.

“Why her?” Livia asked.

“Because she knows you least, and she knows the glass. She won’t mistake what she hopes to see for what her hands are holding.”

Sabina made a dry noise. “I can still be frightened.”

“I’m expecting it.”

“I don’t know the girl at all,” Pell said.

“You can write without deciding that every word out of her mouth belongs to her.”

Pell looked at Livia. “Yes. I can do that.”

Nobody asked her father’s name. It was not one of the seven. She was glad until she was not, and the change happened so quickly she could not keep up with herself.

Saikou sat beside her before they drew the line.

“It may speak with your voice. It may say things that are true and things you don’t know about. You don’t have to defend your father. You don’t have to confess for him. If you can keep a hand on the bench, do. If you can’t, Nera will watch where you are.”

“Will it hurt?”

“It can. I can’t tell you how much.”

“Will you hurt it?”

“I’ll take its hold away. It won’t like that.”

She looked at his bandaged fingers. “Does it get to hurt you?”

“It will try.”

She wanted to tell him not to do it. She wanted to tell him to begin at once. Neither wish made the other less strong.

“Can I have the green ribbon?” she asked.

Edda fetched it. She tied it over the blue one because there was no time to undo the braid properly. The knot sat crooked behind Livia’s ear. Saikou watched, then got up and moved the lamp so the knot would not be near its flame.

Livia put her left finger on the bench.

The thing in her opened both eyes.

Chapter 10 — The second call

Saikou began while there was still daylight in the high window.

He had intended to begin earlier. The seizure of the hospital’s mirrors had cost three hours, a quarrel with a factor, and most of Rhea’s saved wages. He had begun to protest her contribution. Nera had pointed to the darkening window. Sabina’s two mirrors had been polished under the previous night’s moon. One had been waiting for an order that was not yet paid. The other she had intended to keep as a demonstration piece for apprentices. Both were suitable. Neither had been bought free of consequence.

The prison’s washing room could not be made safe by declaring it safe. He tested the door hinges, covered the wet patches on the tubs with coarse cloth, and made Pell empty his ink into a narrow-necked bottle rather than leave an open reflecting dish on the table. Brio took his shield outside. Nera put a blackened pin through the latch so it would not rattle loose when someone struck the door.

Then Saikou drew the circle.

He walked it three times against the turn of the sun. On each circuit he spoke the obligation in full: the medicine bought under Marcia’s name, the promise extended beyond her death, the assignments without a surviving original claimant, and the false right to collect from a daughter who had made no promise. He did not substitute an abyssal word for the account. It was this opening he meant to close, not a hole he could improve by inventing a grander name.

The line tightened on the third pass.

Livia’s shoulders rose. She breathed shallowly and pressed her finger into the bench. Rhea moved as if to go to her. Saikou held up his hand, and Rhea stopped outside the powder.

He set the mirrors.

The host’s mirror faced Livia. The demon’s mirror, unwrapped from black cloth only now, faced her eyes. His own faced that second glass, its old left-edge crack angled away from the lamp. Sabina stood at the far point of their triangle, with her feet on the marks he had made in chalk. A strip of linen ran behind her shoulders so she would know if she stepped back. It was not tied to her. She could leave. If she did, someone would have to keep the place she left, and he had told them that before beginning.

He set his iron gate-Symbol beside his mirror.

Outside the barred window the trumpets practised again. He heard one take the turn too fast. The music had become a measurement of how much evening remained, and he disliked it for that.

“I name you by what you took,” he said. “The care purchased for Marcia, carried past her death, assigned beyond a creditor’s claim, collected from Livia who never promised. You are seen. You are witnessed.”

The girl’s body arched.

Her heel struck the bench. Edda made a sound, and Nera answered with a short prayer in the ordinary language Livia knew. The faith represented there was the girl’s, the little household flame she had prayed beside with her mother, not Severian’s sentence upon it. Nera named the saint of that household and asked for the child to remain with the living people who knew her.

The demon mirror showed a face.

It was not a magnificent face. It was the shape made by blank spaces on paper when names had been removed. Its mouth looked like a seal cut away from a document. Its eyes looked like the holes where somebody had taken binding cord out of an old account.

Saikou kept his eyes on it.

Livia’s mouth began to speak in old Utaran. Pell’s pen moved, then stopped. He knew enough to recognize a formula but not enough to record the whole passage. Saikou told him to mark the language and the number of phrases he heard, not to invent a translation.

The line hissed.

“Livia,” Rhea said.

Saikou turned one palm towards her. She swallowed the next word. The hissing moved around the circle towards the place where her voice had crossed it, and stopped there, searching.

The girl’s left hand stayed on the bench.

He held the first call for an hour. It was longer than he wanted. The reflection found every little break in their attention: Brio looking towards footsteps in the corridor, Edda staring at the blood on Livia’s cuff, Sabina testing her bruised heel against the chalk mark. Each time he had to bring the witnessed line back into agreement with the people who stood around it. It was not their moral worth that mattered. It was whether the thing they saw remained the same thing long enough to deny another version of it.

At the second call the room became cold enough to show their breath.

“I name the same claim,” he said. “No consent was hers. No promise was hers. You are bound. You are contained.”

The demon mirror sweated.

A drop rolled down its face, paused at the bottom of the frame, and fell towards the floor. It fell too slowly. Sabina saw it before he did and put a covered tray beneath it without changing her feet. The drop hit the cloth with a hiss. A little black mark appeared, shaped like a thumbprint.

Livia began reciting names.

Dama’s was first. Then a man whose orchard had been sold, a cooper who had pledged tools against a levy, two sisters whose father’s burial fee had been assigned to Fabius, a carter whose guarantee had followed him across three districts. The voice used Livia’s breathing precisely. It stopped at the end of each name as if waiting for someone to acknowledge the debt.

Dama cried without moving her head.

Brio had tears under his chin. They fell onto his collar and made the wool dark. He could see his aunt across the circle. Saikou could see him seeing her, and knew the thing was using that line between them as surely as it used a silver surface.

“It knows,” Dama said.

“It has the account,” Saikou answered. “Stay with what you can see.”

The voice recited the price of her roof. Then the price of the first loom. Then the second. It made the sums sound small, manageable, a sensible settlement between sensible people. Saikou had heard a merchant speak that way beside the body of a man whose widow could not afford a coffin.

Livia said, in her own voice, “Stop it.”

Nera lifted her lamp a finger’s breadth. “I hear you.”

The demon called Nera by a name she had used before joining her order.

Her hand shook. Her lamp did not fall. The flame inside it leaned away from the second mirror and she shifted the lamp to keep it from throwing the shadow across Livia’s face.

It fought hardest there. Saikou lost the ordinary order of time. His hands burned from the cupboard’s ward. His back ached. Sweat cooled under his shirt though the room’s air had turned winter-cold. Twice he had to ask Pell how long the candle on the table had burned. Pell answered in lengths, not comforting estimates.

A blow came at the door.

Brio went to it without crossing the powder. Severian’s clerk wanted entry to inspect the room. Brio told him the signed permit prohibited interruption except in case of fire. The clerk said the Inquisitor’s authority preceded the permit. Brio asked if there was a fire.

Another blow struck the latch. The blackened pin bent.

Saikou raised his hand towards the door and brought the Sentinel’s line across it, witnessed by Brio on one side and Pell beyond the table. For an instant the whole room’s geometry had to hold two barriers at once. The demon saw the opening in that effort and took Livia’s arm.

Her hand reached for the host mirror.

Edda cried out. Rhea cried out too. Saikou stepped between the reaching hand and the glass, too late to stop her fingers striking its rim. A crack ran across it. The sound was low, like a tooth breaking.

“Hold the frame,” he told Sabina.

She could not reach it from her place. He saw that as he spoke. He corrected himself at once.

“Nera. The frame. Sabina, keep your mark.”

Nera caught the host mirror by its back edge and tilted it a little towards the girl. The crack held. A white bead appeared along it and trembled there. Saikou reset his own mirror a thumb’s width to keep the triangle, and the reflected face took on an expression of intense, ugly satisfaction.

The door stopped shaking. Brio’s captain had arrived in the corridor and was shouting at the clerk. Saikou heard the words signed order, and then the name of the king, and then another name he did not know. Whoever won that argument would matter later. Inside the room he had an arm to return to its owner.

He called Livia’s name.

Not the debt. Not the thing’s name. Hers.

He named the finger she had kept on the bench, the orange she had asked for, the green ribbon crooked behind her ear. Nera named the household prayer. Edda named the horse with a bad foot, and stumbled over it, and named it again. Rhea spoke of the shutter catch. The hand on the mirror opened.

Livia’s arm fell into her lap.

Saikou did not permit himself relief. The second glass was full of water that could not be water, and his own reflection had turned its head a little further than his body. He had to make the third call before either of those things became a path.

He took one breath. He looked at Sabina. She nodded once, with her eyes wide and fixed on the glass.

The window had been dark for hours.

“I name you,” he said, and gave the whole claim again, every living and dead name that made the opening particular. “You are unmade.”

The demon mirror broke inward.

Saikou saw the pieces fly towards the face in it. Silver flashed without catching the lamp’s light. The sound came from all around him at once, a door slammed in a place where there were no walls to stop its echo. Livia screamed, and the scream belonged to her.

His own mirror turned black.

There was a weight inside it. He felt the frame drag at his hand, felt the old crack strain along its left edge, felt something press towards the surface from the wrong side. He wrapped the glass before it could show anyone a face and laid it inside the lined box Sabina had prepared.

“Contained,” he said.

He had to say it twice before his mouth could make the whole word.

Nera was on her knees beside Livia. The girl lay on the bench with her eyes open, breathing. Edda held her hand. Rhea was pulling a blanket over her feet. Pell wrote the time by the candle and had to sharpen his pen again because its nib had split.

Saikou looked at the seven witnesses, counting them one by one.

Beyond the door the trumpets had begun their dawn preparation. The rite had lasted through the night. The work was not finished.

Chapter 11 — After the voice

Livia’s first word afterward was water.

She had meant to say Edda, because her cousin was holding her hand and crying in a way that made no noise. Instead her body asked for water. Nera brought it, held the cup to her mouth, and stopped after two sips. Livia wanted to push the cup up and drink the rest, but her arms would not answer properly. They were hers. She knew that before she knew how to move them.

There was a broken mirror on the floor.

She looked at it and found she could look away. No one turned her head back. No one finished the looking for her. She turned towards the window. The bars were black against a sky that had not yet decided what colour it would be.

“Did it go?” she asked.

“It’s in the covered mirror,” Nera said.

“Can it come back?”

“Master Ira has more to do.”

The Invoker was at the table with Sabina. They had a box between them and a cloth laid over it. He held his bandaged hand flat against the lid while the woman fitted a strap around it. His face was grey beneath its brown, and there was a little tremor in his jaw. She had seen people exhausted before, the cook after a feast, Rhea after staying up with Marcia. This was more than that, and it frightened her until she remembered it did not have to mean anything about her own body.

Dama sat on the floor against the wall. Brio had brought her a stool, but she had set it aside. She was holding the spool with the loose end wound around one finger. Pell was still writing. Edda’s green ribbon had come off Livia’s hair and was tied around the bench leg, where it would not get lost.

The door opened.

Fabius stood there.

He looked wrong in a prison corridor. His robe was the one he wore to ordinary morning hearings, dark cloth with a narrow band of gold at the cuff. She had seen him wearing it at breakfast a hundred times. Here there was no breakfast table, no pen laid neatly beside a plate, no servant waiting to tell him a petitioner had arrived early. His hands were empty. She had never noticed how rarely he stood with empty hands.

He looked at the floor’s powder, then at the broken glass, then at her.

“Livia.”

She waited. She had imagined this too. In some versions he came in and took her out. In others she told him what she thought of him, in words so exact he could not misunderstand. Now he had said her name and she had nothing ready.

He stepped towards the line.

Saikou lifted his head from the box. “Wait there.”

Fabius stopped. He looked at the Invoker’s hand on the lid and something passed across his face that Livia could not read.

“I was told she lived.”

“She does,” Nera said.

“I have come to take her home.”

Nera looked at the chain ring beside the bench. The cuff had been opened during the rite. It lay empty now, but the ring remained in the wall.

“She’s still in the prison’s custody pending inspection.”

Fabius reached inside his sleeve. He had a paper after all. Of course he had a paper. He unfolded it and held it out, but Nera did not take it.

“My acknowledgment of guardianship.”

“We know you’re her father.”

Livia began to cry.

She did not want to. She had not chosen it. But this time the not choosing was only how crying happened, and she could feel the difference. Her chest hurt from the screaming. Edda wiped her cheek with a cloth and did not tell her to stop.

Fabius put the paper away.

“I thought I could close it,” he said.

Saikou had gone back to fastening the box. He did not make the confession begin. Fabius was speaking to the floor between them, and Livia heard him because he was not speaking softly enough for her not to.

“I bought the assignments. When they were discounted, I bought them. It was cheaper than allowing the penalties to fall due. Every time I bought one, another appeared. Another charge attached. I had to recover it somewhere.”

Dama stood up.

“You recovered my looms.”

Fabius looked at her. Recognition came slowly, not because he had forgotten her but because he had not expected her in this room.

“You signed security.”

“There was a grace.”

“The copy—” He stopped.

“There was a grace,” she said again.

He held his own hands together. “I enforced the copy.”

Saikou looked up.

“Did you know she had paid against different terms?”

Fabius’s eyes moved to Livia. She wished they would not. She wished he would look at the woman whose looms he had taken, since he had already looked at Livia for years without seeing what was happening.

“I knew she believed so,” he said.

“Did you investigate?”

“No.”

Pell’s pen scraped. Fabius heard it. He looked towards the clerk and then back at Dama.

“There were others,” he said.

Pell reached for a clean sheet.

Fabius told them names. Some were names the thing had spoken through Livia’s mouth. Some were new. He had bought poor securities cheaply, enforced their harshest surviving terms, and credited the recoveries against the obligation that would not finish. He had used his office to prevent an examiner taking the medicine account from his keeping. He had told himself the daughter was ill, then that she had been influenced, then that the Inquisition would put matters in order.

When he reached that part, he stopped.

Livia was staring at him. “They were going to burn me.”

“I didn’t believe they would.”

“They tried twice.”

“The flame refused.”

She remembered the yard, the wood under her feet, a guard trying to light it with a little taper that bent away from the kindling. She remembered being taken back inside while officials argued about whether to put that refusal in the record. She had not felt protected. She had felt a thing in her preventing another thing from taking its place.

“That wasn’t you,” she said.

“No,” her father said.

His voice broke at last. She had wanted it to break. When it did, she wanted him to go away because she did not know what to do with it.

The physician came with Severian and two officers of the Hall.

Severian examined the broken glass first. The physician examined Livia. She was an older woman with warm hands who asked where it hurt, waited for the answer, and then asked a different question when Livia said everywhere. Saikou showed her how the censer smoke now followed the girl’s shadow rather than running ahead of it. Nera read the witnessed record. Pell supplied the times, the separate statements, and Fabius’s confession, which had not been part of the rite’s authorization but was part of the room’s evidence now.

Severian said the residue might remain.

Saikou agreed. Livia’s fingers were mottled where she had gripped the stone. The lines on one thumb did not match the lines on the other.

“Residue is what you will examine,” he said. “An occupying presence is what your order condemns.”

The physician put her own mark beside Nera’s. Livia watched her blot it. The mark was plain, almost clumsy, and it changed more than the Inquisitor’s beautiful phrases had changed.

Severian suspended the execution order. He did not apologize.

The Hall’s officers asked Fabius to come with them. He looked at Livia. She had nothing to say that would release him from that request. He took one step towards her, stopped at the powder, and then turned and went.

When the door closed she asked for the orange.

Saikou found it in the little cupboard where Nera had put it. He left the covered box on the table with Sabina’s hand upon its lid, peeled the fruit with his fingers, and gave Livia one section.

It tasted of orange.

She cried again, with the section in her mouth. Edda leaned her forehead against Livia’s shoulder. Nobody asked which kind of crying it was.

Chapter 12 — Four separate waters

Saikou had contained a will that was made of an obligation. Now he had to prevent that will from learning to become an agreement between its fragments.

The mirror in Sabina’s box was whole except for its old crack. The demon mirror’s broken pieces remained inside the circle, silver dead and dull after their inward flight. Those were not the pieces he needed to distribute. They could be collected later, watched and counted. What pressed beneath the lid was in his own glass.

He asked Pell to read back the deposition record. Fabius’s confession was clear enough to submit. Dama’s claim was identified. The cancelled Banner receipt was attached. Marcia’s sealed letter remained in a separate pocket of his bag; no one had opened it to enlarge the confession.

Then he asked Sabina for her leather gloves.

She knew what he meant to do. They had arranged it before the rite because an exhausted man with a hungry mirror should not have to improvise a hammer.

The box had four compartments made of lead-lined wood. Beneath the mirror was a soft bed of black cloth. Sabina had scored its back along three lines, shallow enough not to weaken it during the rite, deep enough to guide its breaking afterward. The lines did not guarantee four pieces. They gave him somewhere to put the force.

Nera stood by the table. Pell watched from the door. Sabina put the gloves on Saikou’s hands, one at a time, because his burned fingers were slow.

He held the gate-Symbol against the box’s hinge and named the containment before he struck.

The first blow did nothing.

The second made the lid jump. A voice inside the box offered him every name he had lost, in an order designed to sound accidental. His ship. His daughter’s face. The first room where he had slept safely after Khemesh. He did not answer. The third blow divided the glass along Sabina’s scores with a sound that made the lamp go out.

Nera relit it from hers.

Saikou did not open the lid until the box had stopped trembling. Inside were four large pieces and a little spray of powdered glass. He kept the cloth between himself and the four silver faces. Beneath it, the pieces strained towards one another.

Sabina folded the loose dust into the wrapping of the first piece. No fragment was left on the lining. She counted the compartments, then the bundles, then the compartments again. Pell recorded the count. Nera repeated it aloud.

Four.

They had chosen the waters before beginning. The Dolmis at the lower steps. A disconnected cistern under a Mykkielan chapel. A disused well beyond the southern wall, above the floodplain. A bottle of water-based ink, narrow-necked and stoppered, to remain in Saikou’s keeping. The current could carry a piece away. The cistern and well could not drain into it. The ink would travel, which made it both useful and the thing most likely to get him killed years hence if somebody mistook it for ordinary ink.

He labelled that bottle before he allowed himself to be tired.

At the Dolmis steps the first difficulty was people.

The Triumph stores were being opened. Carts stood nose to tail. Soldiers of the Black Banner cordon held the lane while carters shouted about who had paid for which loading place. Saikou showed Togan’s pass. The sergeant read it in the light of a shuttered lamp, counted Saikou and the covered container, and let him through without a flourish.

Nera stayed outside the cordon. The pass did not name her. Saikou had known that when he bought it and disliked it now. He needed a witness at the water.

He asked the sergeant for one man.

“To carry?”

“To watch. Nothing enters his keeping.”

The sergeant called a soldier whose boots were wet already. His name was Orduk. He had been carrying sacks at the lower steps and was pleased to stop. Saikou told him what to watch and what not to touch. Orduk stopped being pleased but came anyway.

The water was high. A night tide had pushed brown water over the bottom landing, leaving a slick strip of weed across the next step. Saikou set the covered box above the wet line and took the first bundle from it. The cloth moved in his gloves. Something inside was turning towards the other compartments.

Orduk held the lamp.

Saikou went down one step too far. His heel slipped on the weed. He caught the rail with his elbow, slammed the box against his ribs, and felt the bundle twist in his fingers. Orduk reached for him. Saikou told him to take the coat, not the bundle. The soldier got a fistful of cloth at his shoulder and hauled him back onto the dry stone.

For a moment they stood breathing like men who had carried something heavier than either expected.

“The river,” Orduk said, “doesn’t care what your business is.”

“No,” Saikou said. “It doesn’t.”

He knelt on the dry step. He laid the bundle on a little wooden tray and slid the tray beyond the weed with his stick. At the edge he tipped it. Cloth and silver sank together into brown water.

“You are distributed,” he said. “You are forgotten. You are no longer one.”

The Sentinel’s iron Symbol went cold against his palm. Orduk saw the surface close without a splash. He gave his name for the record and went back to carrying sacks.

At the chapel, the keeper unlocked the cistern hatch. Sabina had come by another lane with Pell. She set a wooden block beside the opening and had him help her lift the hatch. It weighed more than Pell expected. He let it tilt, and a corner struck the box in Saikou’s arms.

From inside came a small sound of greeting.

All three remaining bundles shifted towards one another.

Saikou set the box down flat at once and pressed his gate-Symbol across its three closed compartments. Nera stood on one side. The keeper stood on the other. He had to make them both name what was happening before the barrier held: three separate pieces seeking the same space, a box divided, a seal struck but not opened.

The sound stopped.

Pell sat down against the wall. His face was white.

“Take the hatch’s weight on the block,” Sabina told him. “Wood. Not your hands. You don’t have to be brave at a hinge.”

They lowered the second piece by a cord into the cistern. The cistern had not supplied drinking water for a generation. Its inlet was bricked shut, its overflow sealed. Nera inspected both seals with the keeper before Saikou released the cord. He spoke the distributing words again. The water went dark where the bundle entered and returned to its own old green.

Pell recorded the deposit while Sabina helped the keeper lower the hatch.

They reached the disused well after sunrise.

The procession drums had begun within the walls. Nera could still go back for the morning inspection; Saikou could not. She had taken the report of the first two deposits and would return with it while he and Sabina finished the others. He trusted her to keep Severian from turning an unfinished task into a failed rite. Trust was unpleasantly dependent on a woman who had not slept.

The well stood in an abandoned orchard beside a village whose surviving houses had been absorbed into the city’s tax ward. Its upper courses leaned. Nettles grew around its base. The farmer who held the orchard’s present lease came out with a spade and asked what they were doing.

Saikou told him enough to make him angry.

“That’s a well.”

“It isn’t used.”

“It could be used. I mean to clear it.”

The preparation report had called it abandoned. The report had been accurate about the water and wrong about the man who intended to claim it.

He paid for the lease of the well’s immediate ground, in coin, and made the farmer write that the shaft was not to be opened for drawing. Sabina witnessed. They agreed on where a warning stone would stand. The farmer insisted upon a sum for loss of future irrigation. It was not outrageous. Saikou paid it and had to use the emergency coins sewn into his coat.

The well rope snapped on the first lowering.

The bundle stopped against an old projecting stone below the rim, hanging over black water. Saikou could see a little corner of silver where the cloth had torn. It reflected the sunrise in the wrong direction.

He got down on his stomach with Sabina holding his belt. He could not reach. The wall’s mortar came away under his arm. He took out the Traveler’s road-stone and asked for a path for one thing to arrive below without bringing anything else after it.

A root crossed the inside of the shaft, thin and living, where he had seen only stone. It did not become a ladder. It gave his stick something to press against. He worked the bundle off the projecting course with the stick’s end, slowly, while Sabina kept both hands on his belt and swore at the stones dropping beside his head.

The bundle fell.

He spoke the words, saw the water close, and felt the third seal take. The root remained where it was. The well remained dangerous. They put the cap across its mouth and fitted the agreed warning.

Only the ink remained.

He sealed the fourth shard in the bottle’s water-dark body with Sabina watching. It was a small piece, the one from the mirror’s old left edge. It sank through the ink as if the ink was deep enough to hold a whole city. He stopper-waxed the bottle, wrapped it, and put it in the separate inner case he had bought for that purpose.

The pressure left his chest.

He sat on the grass beside the capped well. There were drums in the distance and a bird somewhere in the orchard making an ordinary, badly timed noise.

For a moment he wanted the memory he had rested in on Fabius’s stair: his daughter’s feet against his shin, the joke whispered under a table, his effort not to laugh. He knew the table. He knew he had once mended a strap there. He knew what the memory had meant to him.

There was nothing where the warmth had been.

He tried once more, which was a mistake. He could remember the case in terrible detail: Livia’s voice reciting Dama’s name, the hand coming through Brio’s reflection, the third call and the inward breaking. He could remember Sabina’s curse at the well. The shard had left him all of that.

Sabina offered him water.

He drank. He did not tell her what had gone. She had her own injuries from this night, and a shop to open, and he had no wish to make her comfort the man who had been paid to do the work.

After a while he got up. His knees hurt. The ink bottle was heavier than it ought to be.

They went back to the city separately, so Sabina could fetch her apprentice and Saikou could find Nera before the Triumph passed the prison gate.

Chapter 13 — The copies sent out

Nera had fallen asleep sitting beside the washing-room door.

She woke when Saikou’s shadow crossed her lap, looked up at him, and reached for her lamp before she remembered it was daylight. The lamp had burned dry. Her thumb was red where the old kitchen burn had opened under the chain.

“Four?” she asked.

“Four. Separately held.”

“The inspection report’s lodged. Severian wanted another examination at noon. Mercy and Grace said she needed sleep more than another censer. He accepted it.”

“Did he?”

“He put it in writing.”

Saikou sat beside her on the step. The Triumph passed the end of the street beyond the prison yard, flags first, then the bearers with their blue-white flame, then musicians trying not to step on one another’s heels. The crowd moved steadily. There was no girl being led between guards. There would be other sentences on the Basilica steps. There would be speeches which used words larger than the things they described. He had neither stopped the Triumph nor made it just.

Livia slept in the washing room. Rhea sat by her feet with a mending basket somebody had fetched from the house. Edda had gone home to her mother and would return in the afternoon. Dama had gone to sell thread. Brio was on the gate instead of the cell corridor because his captain said he had had enough of mirrors for one shift.

Pell brought the clean copies at midday.

He had made three sets. One for the examiner, one for Nera’s order, one for the office that had registered the original assignments. The last office might dislike its set, which was why he had entered delivery in the other two copies. Fabius’s seal did not authorize any of them. The king’s witnessed request appeared at the front, identifying how the cause had begun, not pretending to have determined its outcome.

“Where’s the fourth set?” Saikou asked.

Pell looked at him.

“For the people whose claims are named,” he said. “Dama and the others. They’ll want to know where to apply.”

“That’s many copies.”

“Yes.”

Pell took off his spectacles and wiped them on his sleeve. “The office will charge.”

Saikou looked at his nearly empty purse. Nera said the Quiet Seal would pay for notices naming the cause and its examining desk, not for reconstructing every individual account. It would pay a clerk to sit twice a week in the hospice vestibule and help people read the notice. Pell nodded. He could arrange that. He did not volunteer to do all of it himself.

Before evening he returned with Nera to the store-room where they had left the carrier’s rejected mirror. The cloth was dry. The witnessed line had held. He did not uncover the glass to celebrate either fact. Nera entered the store-room in the Quiet Seal’s custody register, naming the carrier as its owner, and paid her for the days it would remain withheld. Sabina would examine it after the next dark moon, with the cloth kept on and a new barrier drawn before any silver was stripped. Until then, Nera held the key. The carrier accepted the receipt, asked whether anyone would pay for the ruined basket lining, and had that added too.

Before evening the examiner reached the prison.

She was a heavy woman called Mistress Varro, with a rain cloak despite the dry weather and a habit of sharpening her pencil while people spoke. She read the accounts in front of Saikou, asked for the objects removed from the archive, and compared the blood inventory with Rhea’s deposition. She spent longer over the cancelled Banner levy than over the page naming Maelstraeus. The former could be traced through living offices. The latter required a caution she was not prepared to abandon because a foreign exorcist looked tired.

Fabius was held under a civil warrant for coercive recovery, misuse of office, and obstruction of an examination. His hearing would not be the same hearing as the one that had condemned his daughter. His daughter would remain in the hospital’s care until a guardian could be examined who was neither the accused Censor nor the order that had attempted to burn her.

Rhea was not eligible by herself. Edda’s mother was. They would apply together, the aunt responsible in law, the housekeeper to remain employed if there was money to employ her.

“How much money?” Saikou asked.

Varro tapped her pencil against the desk. “That depends what assets survive the civil claims.”

“Can his daughter’s things be released now?”

“Clothes and ordinary personal effects. The house’s silver is attached.”

He thought of the blue cloak on its hook. That was a thing someone could fetch without pretending an entire household had been restored.

Marcia’s letter went out with the next ordinary carrier.

Rhea knew her sister’s address. She had written to it before the illness and twice afterward. Saikou paid the carrier in front of Nera, gave him the sealed letter, and took a receipt naming the destination. He could not make the dead woman’s sister be alive to receive it. He could meet the price he had promised.

The letter’s weight left his bag. The ink bottle’s did not.

On the following morning he went to Sabina’s shop.

Her apprentice was polishing a little mirror under a covered porch. Sabina made him stop and put the cloth away when Saikou came through. He did not want the boy seeing a stranger in an ink bottle and mistaking it for a demonstration of the trade.

She had two cuts on her arm from the well stone. They were clean and wrapped. She was planing the back of a new frame with her injured hand lifted out of the way.

“Did they let her out?”

“Out of the execution order. Not the hospital yet.”

“Better than the other way round.”

She showed him the bill for the two rite mirrors and the box. Nera had paid her promised share. Rhea’s contribution was recorded separately. There was still an amount outstanding. Saikou paid what remained of his fee advance against it. He would have to collect the balance from the Quiet Seal before he could leave Ecktoria, and he would have to spend part of that on a new mirror of his own.

Sabina took a small sheet of silver from a drawer.

“This one has a flaw at the corner. Not a crack. A thick place. I can polish it down. It’ll cost less than the other stock.”

“Will it show what’s in front of it?”

“That’s usually what mirrors do.”

He smiled. She smiled back, very slightly, and the room became a workshop again.

He ordered the mirror. He did not ask her to reproduce the old crack.

When he returned to the hospital, Livia was sitting by the window with Edda. They had a slate between them. Edda was drawing a horse and doing it badly, giving it knees that bent in the wrong direction. Livia took the chalk and corrected one leg.

“You’ll never get to Acasia on that,” she said.

“You don’t know. It might go faster.”

Saikou put the blue cloak on the chair beside them. Rhea had fetched it while he was at the shop. Its hem bore the old stitch marks and the left clasp was missing.

“Your shutter’s being repaired,” he told Livia.

“Whose house am I going to?”

“Your aunt’s, for now.”

“Then it’s a waste of a shutter.”

She sounded so like an ordinary girl annoyed by an ordinary adult’s arrangements that he almost laughed. He did not.

At the gate Cornelius was measuring the street’s drainage fall. The surveyor greeted him and asked whether the Hall had furnished the records he required.

“Some,” Saikou said.

“And the name I mentioned?”

“I never agreed to find it.”

“No. You didn’t.” Cornelius looked at the girl’s blue cloak visible through the window, then went back to the drain. “Mind the third step outside the gate. It’s higher than it seems.”

Saikou minded it. It had always been that height. He went back to the inn for a bowl of soup, and the child who had accused him of eating like a horse asked if he was going to eat the spoon this time.

“Only if the soup’s disappointing,” he said.

It was not.

Chapter 14 — The thumb upon the page

The first time Livia’s fingerprints changed, she thought she had got glue on them.

She was at her aunt’s kitchen table repairing a little tear in Edda’s drawing book. There was glue in a cracked saucer and a strip of paper under her left thumb. When she lifted the thumb, the lines in its pad had become circles instead of their usual loops. She rubbed it against her skirt. The circles stayed.

Her aunt fetched Nera. Nera fetched the physician. Edda stayed at the table and finished gluing the strip because somebody had to, and this was how Livia remembered the afternoon afterward: everyone alarmed, her thumb strange, Edda bent over the book trying not to get glue on its pages.

The circles were gone by morning. A different pattern had taken their place.

There was no voice with them. No mouth saying what she had not chosen. No taste of ashes in her food. The physician wrote down what she saw and gave Livia thin gloves to wear when handling hot dishes until they knew whether the skin’s sensation had changed. It had not. The gloves were useful anyway. They kept other people from taking her hands to examine them without asking.

She had nightmares for months.

Sometimes they were about the mirror hand. Sometimes they were about her father at breakfast, putting his pen down while trumpets played outside. Those were harder to explain. The physician listened to both kinds and did not insist they were evidence of a remaining demon. Nera inspected when something warranted inspection. Not every bad night.

Her father’s hearing began in the winter.

Livia went once, with her aunt and Rhea. The room smelled of wet wool. There were so many claimants that some had to wait on the landing. Dama sat near the front with the spool in her lap. Fabius wore a plain robe and no office band. He had lost weight. He looked towards Livia when she came in and then away, because the examiner was asking him a question.

He did not say he had loved Marcia as an answer to that question.

Livia had feared he would. She had also wanted him to say it somewhere, because she remembered his hand smoothing her mother’s pillow and did not want that hand to have been pretending. She understood neither wish well enough to speak of it in the courtroom.

He admitted the recoveries. He disputed some of the penalties proposed against him. His advocate disputed more. The hearing did not turn one confession into a clean result. Dama’s looms had been sold and could not simply be returned. Some buyers had acquired goods knowing exactly how they had been taken. Others had not. The accounts became thick enough to require another table.

Fabius was stripped of his office and sentenced to imprisonment. Several claimants recovered money from the sale of the house. Dama received enough to purchase one used loom, not the two she had lost, and the new loom needed a beam she could not yet afford. Rhea found work with another household and came to Livia’s aunt on her evenings off. The shutter in the old bedroom was repaired before the house sold, which Livia still thought was a foolish thing to have accomplished.

She wrote her father a letter.

It was short. She told him where she was living, that the orange had tasted right after the rite, and that Edda was still planning to ride to Acasia. She did not tell him she forgave him. She did not tell him she never would. She asked why he had not come before the mirrors broke.

His answer took two weeks.

He said he had believed an official answer would be better than his own fear. He said he had been wrong. Then he spent a page explaining the assignments, and she became angry enough to put the letter away before finishing it.

Later she read the rest. It did not make her less angry. It let her know what he thought an answer was.

She began visiting the copy office because Pell needed someone to carry completed sheets to the drying racks, and because her aunt wanted her out of the house for part of the day. At first she hated both reasons. She thought she ought to be permitted to sit by a window until she was ready to do something larger than carry papers. Then she discovered that the office had a box of spoiled copies and that Pell would let her take clean-backed sheets from it for drawing.

She drew horses. Bad ones, then better ones. She drew Brio’s shield with the boss covered. She drew the orange, and found that making an orange look round was much harder than making a face look frightened.

Pell taught her how to sort duplicate instruments by registration rather than subject. Things filed under medicine might be found later under grain. Things filed under a dead person’s name might have living people depending on them. He did not make these observations into warnings about her father. He made her put the bundles on the right shelf.

One day she found two copies with different endings.

The earlier one had a grace extending repayment to the next quarter. The later one did not. She called Pell. He called the registrar. The registrar took both copies upstairs, and Livia never learned whether that particular disagreement ended well. But Pell entered the transfer of the copies in the daybook and made her check the entry the following morning.

“Otherwise,” he said, “we’ve only discovered a thing and handed it away.”

Her father died in prison in a later winter. The notice named an illness and the day his body could be claimed. Livia went with Rhea to collect it. She kept his letters, including the one that had made her angry. Dama was still paying for the beam of her replacement loom.

Years passed. Not all of them were full of incidents worth writing down.

Livia grew taller. Her aunt died, and Edda married a man who owned no horse but was willing to hire two for a journey. They went only as far as the eastern grain towns. Edda said this was a rehearsal for Acasia. Livia learned to ride badly and enjoyed it more than Edda thought such a bad rider should.

She became an archivist.

She wore gloves at the desk, fine ones with the fingertips replaced more often than the rest. When a fingerprint was required on a delivery, she used the office seal instead and wrote why beside it. Some visitors found this interesting. Some found it upsetting. Most wanted their records and left once she furnished them.

Saikou came through Ecktoria again on a damp spring afternoon.

He had more grey in his beard and a new mirror case. She recognized his walk before his face: the care with the right heel, the shoulders turned to let somebody with a tray pass. He stood in the archive doorway looking for the clerk who had brought him copies, and found her behind the desk.

“You’ve grown,” he said.

“I was fourteen.”

“So you were.”

She gave him a chair. He lowered himself into it with relief and looked at the shelves, the blotting sheets, the ribbons tied around open bundles. He did not ask first about her father. She was grateful and then annoyed at being grateful, and decided both feelings could sit there without her settling them.

“Does it still change?” he asked, looking at her gloves.

“Sometimes. Less often in winter. I don’t know why.”

“Does anything else come with it?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She asked about his work. He told her of a ferry landing that had acquired the habit of putting passengers on the wrong bank, and a ferryman who resented paying for a second inspection of the rope. She laughed. He laughed with her. The mirror case remained closed on his knee.

When he rose to leave, she took an orange from the bowl beside her desk. She had brought three for the afternoon staff. One was enough to give away without making everybody else’s tea worse.

“For the road,” she said.

He held it in his broad hand, looked at it, and nodded. “Thank you.”

“Don’t eat the rind.”

“I’ve been advised.”

After he went she returned to a petition for an orchard claim. The first sheet had been copied in a hand she liked, firm and slightly sloping. The attached testimony was untidy. She put them side by side, checked the names, and reached for the daybook.

Through the open window she could hear somebody teaching a child to whistle the Triumph march. The child missed the difficult turn, tried again, and missed it differently. Livia listened for a moment. Then she put her gloved thumb on the page to hold it flat, and wrote the number where the next clerk would find it.
