Part One The approach

Chapter 1 The First Paid Mile

The wheel went down without much noise. That was the part Sariel disliked afterward. No crack, no warning shout, only the back of the cart sinking gently while the horse took another hopeful step and found that it had been attached to the earth.

Rada pulled him up.

“Nobody push,” she said.

Benjamin, already moving, stopped with both hands out.

“I hadn’t.”

“Keep it that way.”

The road had been repaired with brush and a generous layer of pale gravel. From the rise it had looked better than the older stretch beside it. Sariel had walked ahead on the older stretch because it was dry, then come back to tell Tema the bend was clear. She stood now with the sunlight warm on the back of her neck, looking at her own clean footprints beside the cart.

Rada climbed down on the high side. The horse put its ears back. Its traces creaked when it tried to lower its head to a tuft of grass.

“We ought to get the load off,” Benjamin said.

“We ought to unhitch him first.” Rada passed Sariel the reins. “Stand there. If he starts moving, go with him. Don’t discover how strong you are.”

He had a broad forehead and a damp, warm smell, different from the narrow city horses Sariel remembered from Silkstrand. She put a hand against his neck. His skin moved under it. He went on looking at the grass.

Behind them the other three carts had stopped before the new gravel. Mera was turning her horse across the road to prevent Tovin from bringing the fourth cart closer. Tema came forward carrying his gloves. He had taken them off to eat an apple, and the apple remained in his other hand, bitten once.

“How far down?”

“As far as it is going,” Thane said.

“That sounds hopeful.”

“I meant don’t help it.”

Thane set his tool roll on the verge. Linett had moved the fastening twice before he was satisfied with it. It opened toward his left hand now, leaving the straps clear of the small saw. His right hand rested against his thigh, two fingers more curled than the others. Sariel looked away before he could notice her looking.

Iset was already under the shafts, releasing the harness. She had a strip of red cloth tied around her hair and one front tooth set a little across its neighbor. Rada had introduced her that morning as the person to ask before blaming a horse. Iset had added that the answer might be insulting.

“Take him forward,” she told Sariel.

Sariel led the horse clear. Free of the cart, he went directly to the grass and began eating with a concentration she envied.

Thane knelt beside the wheel. He did not try to touch the axle. He picked up a stick, pushed it into the gravel behind the rim, and withdrew it coated in black mud.

“There’s a drain under this.”

Rada looked at the bank. “There was.”

“Still is. It’s trying to be a pond.”

He worked the stick farther along the edge. Sariel watched his shoulder move. He had spent the winter learning to kneel without putting his right palm down first. He usually remembered. When he forgot, nobody was supposed to tell him.

“That fallen branch,” he said to Benjamin. “The straight one. Bring it over, but don’t put it under anything yet.”

Benjamin brought it and began searching for a stone to rest it on.

“I said not yet.”

“I can hold it while you—”

“You can bring the branch.”

Benjamin put it down.

For a moment all Sariel could hear was the horse tearing grass. Then Tema tossed his apple core far into the ditch, put on his gloves and asked which sacks they wanted first.

They spread a canvas on the old road. Kestra counted while Rada and Dennet lowered the grain. Twelve sacks, each with two dark stitches in one corner and a different buyer’s mark on the neck. Sariel had watched them loaded in Millhaven. She had admired the tied knots and the tarred covers, the readiness of a journey in which nobody had to leave a bed behind because the building was burning.

The sixth sack came down hard. Grain shifted inside with a sound like heavy rain.

“Careful,” Tema said.

“I am being careful,” Dennet said, breathing through his mouth.

Elena moved the canvas so the sack would not lie against a sharp stone. “Then be careful from a little lower down.”

He laughed, although he did not seem to want to. Dennet was all elbows and the long nose of someone who had not yet grown into the rest of his face. He had introduced himself twice that morning, once to the company and once to Sariel alone, apparently afraid she had not heard. She had pretended the second introduction was necessary.

When the cart was empty, Thane put his shoulder against the high side and looked underneath.

Benjamin reached for the branch again.

“Ben. Leave it.”

“You shouldn’t be down there.”

“I’m beside it.”

“If it moves—”

Thane straightened. Mud had reached one knee and the end of his sleeve. “If it moves, I want you at that wheel. Not beside my ear.”

Benjamin’s expression shut for a moment. Sariel knew the look: he had been preparing to accept blame and had been given an instruction instead. He went to the other wheel.

Thane found two broad stones, had Dennet bed them in the sound ground, and laid a short plank over them. The branch would lift against that plank. Rada and Iset chocked the high wheel. Two people held the cart steady while Benjamin leaned on the branch. Thane slid a packing block beneath the raised axle with his left hand.

“Down.”

Benjamin eased the weight off. The block held. A little black water came up through the gravel.

They did it again. This time the wheel lifted enough for Iset to work brush underneath, followed by another plank. Nobody was beneath the cart. The whole operation looked disappointingly ordinary when it succeeded.

Thane stood and watched the wheel settle.

“Now we can move it backward.”

Benjamin wiped his palms on his coat. “I could have done that.”

“You just did.”

He looked at Thane, then began to laugh. It came out more loudly than the occasion required. Rada smiled and went to fetch her horse.

Sariel kept the reins long enough to feel the warm leather slide through her fingers. The animal had eaten a neat hollow in the verge. Beyond its ears Millhaven’s smoke was no longer visible. She could still have walked home before dark, however, and knew precisely where her key was.

That helped more than she had expected.

They ate early because the sacks were already off and the ground dry. Mera brought out a flat bread folded around chopped greens, cut it into pieces on the tail of her cart and offered Sariel one. There was sour cheese in it and something bitter which made her take a second bite before she was sure she liked the first.

“Young dock,” Mera said.

“The plant?”

“No. An entire harbor. Take another before Tovin wakes.”

Tovin opened one eye from beneath his hat. “I heard that.”

“Then you’ve had your share.”

Sariel sat on the grass with the bread across her knees. Her bag rested against her calf, fastened. She did not need to keep touching it to know the money was there. She touched it anyway.

They had been paid forty marks before departure, half their fee. Part had gone into medicines, food they wanted in addition to the road allowance, and the ordinary expenses of keeping somewhere to return to. The rest was divided between the common purse and their own pockets. Sariel could say which coins were hers. That felt different from the little pile they had made in the first cattle shelter after Silkstrand.

Kestra was making a mark in her working book.

“You aren’t charging for the wheel?” Sariel asked.

“First day. Weather and ordinary delays are within the agreed span.”

“The weather didn’t put brush over a drain.”

“It isn’t Tema’s drain either.”

“I know. I was only hoping you’d found a reason.”

Kestra turned the book slightly away from a crumb. “I was writing that we should check repaired ground before driving over it.”

Sariel took a mouthful of bread. It had become very dry.

“You could put my name next to that.”

Kestra looked up. “Would it make the ground better?”

“No.”

“Then it would take up space.”

She closed the book and asked Mera what the greens were. Mera told her, this time without the harbor.

Sariel ate the rest slowly. Across the road Benjamin and Thane were arguing over the branch. Benjamin wanted to carry it. Thane wanted him to imagine carrying every useful thing they encountered between here and the Ford. Elena had taken Rada’s reins long enough for her to eat with both hands. Rada stood looking at her freed hands as though uncertain what had become of their occupation.

Tema sat down beside Sariel.

“The bend?” he asked.

She swallowed. “I’ll walk beyond it.”

“I know you walked it already.”

“I looked for men.”

He waited.

“This time I’ll look at the road.”

“Good.”

He did not soften the word. She preferred that, once she had got past wanting him to.

The repaired stretch ended at a culvert whose western mouth was blocked by a willow trunk. Water had found a way along the road instead. Sariel followed the wet line to the next bend and put her boot against the outer edge. A piece the length of her foot broke off into the ditch.

She tried farther in. The gravel held, but a crack ran through the weeds beside it. Above, winter water had cut a narrow runnel through a field. A fence post hung in the air with its lower end bare.

She went back.

Tema had them nearly loaded. The horses faced forward. Dennet was pulling the last rope tight, leaning his whole weight away from the cart.

“Wait,” Sariel said.

Tema raised his hand. “All of them?”

“All of them.”

Rada let her reins fall slack. Tovin complained to no one in particular about the cost of standing still. Sariel told Tema what she had found, using her hands to show the width left between the crack and the bank.

“Could you get through?” he asked Rada.

“Maybe.”

“Would you?”

“Not with this load.”

He looked at Sariel. “Another way?”

She knew a track had left the road near the fallen willow. She did not know where it went. Yesterday she might have said she could find one. Today there were twelve sacks per cart, four horses and twelve people who would act on her answer.

“There’s a track. I haven’t walked it.”

Tema rubbed his forehead with his thumb. “Then we wait while you do.”

She took Dennet because he knew which farms had rights across the upper field. It cost the rest of the afternoon. The track climbed, crossed a stony pasture, and came down through a gate whose owner was repairing its hinge. He required them to shut it afterward and avoid the soft ground near his sheepfold. Tema paid him for the use of the track. The farmer made the gate wider before accepting the money.

On the descent Sariel walked beside the second cart. Its driver, Varn, had said little since Millhaven. He wore his beard trimmed close to the jaw and had a dark patch on the shoulder of his coat where a fastening had been moved. When the cart struck a stone there was a dull sound beneath the sacks, wood meeting something more solid than the load above it.

“Loose board?” she asked.

“Old repair.”

He leaned forward to quiet his horse. She had no reason to disbelieve him. Every cart in the train had been repaired somewhere, by someone who had possessed different timber.

They camped before the light failed, in a dry place behind a bank. The horses stood together chewing. Rada and Iset checked harness while Thane inspected the rescued wheel for a second time. Sariel fetched water with Benjamin. He carried the full bucket in his right hand and swung the empty one she had brought in his left.

“You don’t have to carry both.”

“I’m enjoying the empty one. It makes the other seem less unreasonable.”

She took it from him. He did not resist.

At the fire Elena put a pot on two stones and discovered that she had packed the ladle under the clean linen. She stared into the bag for a moment, then laughed at herself and asked Thane for a stick.

“I have an excellent branch,” Benjamin said.

“You left it.”

“A thing I am coming to regret.”

Thane looked up from the wheel. “We’ll send for it.”

Later, after the food, Sariel checked the first part of the morning’s road. She went only as far as the next rise. It was sound, wet in places, and nobody seemed to be waiting behind the gorse to kill them. She came back and said all three things. Tema asked about the gorse before deciding that, on balance, it could remain where it was.

Benjamin moved his folded coat so she could sit beside the fire. Their shoulders touched. She watched a drop of fat flare on the coals and thought, with a pleasure almost embarrassing in its size, that tomorrow was another paid day.

Chapter 2 The Muddy Stilt

Elena had been told the waystation was close enough to smell before they saw it. She smelled wet wool, horse dung, onions frying in old fat, and a ditch which had not quite decided whether to become a stream. The building remained behind the hill.

“It has to be near,” Benjamin said.

“It may simply be very large,” she said.

The lower step appeared first. It stood in muddy water with two planks laid across to it, and one of the planks had a split which opened when someone heavy stepped in the middle. Above it rose an inn built crookedly against a storehouse. A stilt supported the upper floor at the corner nearest the road. It was not the only stilt, but it was the one a traveler would notice while wondering whether to stand beneath it.

Tema sent them round to the yard.

There were already seven carts there, a wagon with one wheel removed, a litter occupied by a woman who was not asleep despite what its bearer said, and a row of people sitting under the eaves with their boots on the rail. Chickens pecked between the wheels. One stood on the disabled wagon’s axle and watched Thane as if it expected professional assistance.

“We won’t all fit,” Rada said.

“You won’t,” a woman called from the pump. “They’ll put two of you beyond the washing.”

Elena looked toward the line of shirts and stockings stretched between the storehouse and a cart. A child was turning a shirt with a stick so both sides would catch the wind.

“Are you leaving?” she asked the woman.

“I would have left yesterday if anyone had found a use for the road.”

She worked the pump handle again. Water emerged in a brown cough, then cleared.

Tema greeted the innkeeper and began the discussion of where twelve people might sleep. Elena stood beside the third cart until Mera passed down her bag. She had let the bag ride that afternoon. Her shoulder still ached where its strap usually crossed, as though carrying nothing had been insufficient notice of a holiday.

“Your bottles?” Mera asked.

Elena shook the bag gently. Nothing clicked against glass. “Still bottles.”

“A satisfactory outcome.”

Under the eaves a man rose so quickly that he struck his head against a hanging pan. He caught it before it could strike him again, swore with a considerable command of syllables, and came forward holding the pan in one hand.

“Tema.”

“Sorian. You look prosperous.”

“I’m carrying the inn’s silver.”

“Put it back before she sees.”

Sorian hung the pan on a different hook. His nose had been broken toward one cheek and set without much success. It gave him an expression of patient interest even while he was glaring. He clasped Tema’s forearm, glanced past him at the carts, and visibly counted them.

“All four?”

“You asked for four.”

“I asked for them on the other side of the Ford.”

Tema’s hand remained on his arm a moment longer. “Come and tell me.”

They ate in a room where the ceiling was low enough for Benjamin to encounter a beam he had already been warned about. The innkeeper brought bowls of bean soup, a board of sliced meat, and bread whose crust carried a pleasant amount of soot. The meat was good. Elena had been trying not to look relieved by this when Sorian said the crossing had closed three days before.

“Both ways?” Tema asked.

“For carts.”

“The horses?”

“Depends whose horse.”

Sorian broke bread into his soup. He wore a wool waistcoat with small bone buttons, several of which differed from the rest. Elena could see a pale strip of skin at his wrist where he had stopped wearing something. A bandage, perhaps. She made herself listen to the answer instead of constructing an injury.

“Voss has chained the west barrier,” he said. “The Gray Ash have roped the horse approach. Rusk is paid to stand between them, or would be if anybody had paid him. At present he stands where the food is.”

“Why the rope?” Kestra asked.

“Last season a carrier ran over a herder and didn’t stop. Sarnai’s nephew. They’ve come for the spring passage and found iron in the ground where they used to lead the mares.”

“On the ford?”

“At the approach. To stop carts slipping round the toll. Horses haven’t learned to tell the difference between iron intended for a wheel and iron intended for a foot.”

Thane put down his spoon. “Who drove it in?”

“Voss’s people.”

“Then he can take it out.”

“I suggest you open with that. They’ve all had three days to overlook it.”

Thane picked up his spoon again. Elena was pleased with Sorian for answering him. She was less pleased when he turned the same patient face toward her.

“You’re the healer.”

“I treat people.”

“Good. There’s a man at the Ford—”

“Is anyone here hurt?”

He stopped. “Not that I know.”

“Then tell me about him when we can do something.”

Tema slid his folded cargo list out of his inner coat pocket and laid it between their bowls. Sorian wiped his fingers, unfolded it and read the buyer marks down the left side. He touched two of them.

“They’ve sent their own carts to meet us.”

“They weren’t to collect until the receiving yard.”

“They also weren’t to have ploughed three fields before the seed arrived. Here we all are.”

Elena took another mouthful of soup. The beans had been mashed against the side of the pot; there was onion through them, and cumin, which she had not tasted since Silkstrand. She had bought a small packet once for a woman who could not bear the smell of her medicines. The woman’s husband had used half of it in a stew and apologized with such sincerity that Elena had never told him its price.

“Can we go around?” Sariel asked.

Sorian looked at Tema before answering. “Light traffic has gone by the lower track. A few people are paying to be told where it is.”

“And loaded traffic?”

“I’m here, aren’t I?”

“You might like the soup.”

He considered her, then laughed. “I do like the soup. I don’t like it enough.”

There was talk of the western bank, of the condition of the road past Ironbound, and of a monastery whose steward would be writing polite letters until the last day he could afford to be polite. Elena let some of it pass. She was warm. Her feet were dry at the toes and wet at the heels, an improvement over the reverse. Above the ceiling someone dragged furniture across the floor, then put it back with a thump which made dust fall onto the unused edge of the table.

Sariel looked up.

“Beds,” Tema said.

“I hope so.”

He folded the cargo list and returned it to the same pocket. Elena watched because Sorian had reached for it a moment too late, and neither man chose to mention that.

The eggs were being sold from a basket beside the disabled wagon. Elena had meant to ask about the woman in the litter, but the basket lay between them, and three eggs sat apart on a cloth. Their shells were pale blue. She had never seen pale blue eggs at the Coin.

“Are they different?” she asked.

The seller looked at her. “Inside?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Then why do they cost more?”

“People ask whether they’re different.”

Elena laughed and bought the ordinary ones. She bought six, then returned two because she could not think how to carry them without taking linen out of its bag. The seller found a little box and asked whether she wanted all six after all.

She did.

The woman at the pump was watching from the neighboring cart. She had a heavy brown skirt tucked up at one side, exposing boots worn almost white across the toes. Her empty sacks lay folded beneath the seat.

“You’ll break them in that,” she said.

“Apparently they’re no different inside.”

“Put straw round them.”

“I was going to.”

“Under them as well.”

Elena had not been going to. She accepted a handful of straw and introduced herself.

“Orsa.” The woman gave her name as if setting down something that might be wanted later. “Are you with the grain?”

“I’m with the people guarding it.”

“Same road, then.”

Orsa had four fields, or four strips of other people’s fields; Elena needed a second explanation before she understood. They lay beyond the Ford, with different slopes and different ideas about when spring had arrived. One was ready. One would not be ready for days. The lowest had flooded, and Orsa had come to meet the grain while the water left it.

“Can you wait?” Elena asked.

Orsa pointed to her pony, which was eating from a small bag. “He’s waiting beautifully. It’s the eating which needs attention.”

Elena looked at the folded sacks again. “Do you know Sorian?”

“I know where he’s sitting.”

That seemed a fair distinction.

The woman in the litter stirred and demanded to be put somewhere from which she could see people doing useful things. Her bearer, a broad young man with a solemn face, looked at the disabled wagon and then at the pump, unable to choose.

“Ask her,” Elena told him.

He did. They moved the litter three feet.

Her name was not offered, and Elena did not require it. The woman’s ankle had been wrapped tightly after a fall. Too tightly: the toes were cold, the skin above the binding swollen. Elena explained what she wanted to do and waited for permission before loosening it. There was no broken skin. The woman had been trying to persuade her nephew of precisely that for half the morning.

“He keeps asking whether it is bleeding.”

“He can’t see through the cloth.”

“Neither can I, but I remember what I brought into it.”

Elena laughed. She supported the ankle while the nephew placed a rolled coat beneath the heel. She told him what changes to watch for and made him repeat the instructions, which his aunt enjoyed more than she should have.

“What do I owe you?” the woman asked.

Elena named a small fee. The nephew looked relieved. He had been holding a copper in his palm and seemed to have feared she would ask for something he could not offer.

“For this visit,” Elena said. “If it swells or the pain changes, find someone again. Don’t pay me now for work I haven’t done.”

The woman took the copper from him and added another. “For making him listen.”

“That part may not last.”

“Then I’ll spend it while it does.”

By the time Elena returned to the carts, Benjamin had decided her egg box would fit beneath a rolled blanket. Kestra was explaining that it would fit there because it was being compressed, which was an objection Benjamin had mistaken for assistance. Elena heard a small crack.

All three looked down.

“One?” Benjamin asked.

She lifted the blanket. Two shells had split. A little white was escaping into the straw.

“Two.”

“We could eat them now.”

“You can eat them now.”

“I would rather we shared the consequences.”

That made her laugh despite herself. They borrowed a small pan from the kitchen and bought a spoonful of fat. The eggs spread to the edges, and the innkeeper scraped them into four uneven portions because Thane had appeared during the cooking. Sariel arrived in time to complain about the empty pan. Elena gave her half her portion.

They ate standing beneath the eaves. The rain began and then stopped before anyone had decided whether to move. Orsa passed carrying fodder she had paid too much for. She accepted a piece of bread, kept walking, and called over her shoulder that they ought to pack the remaining eggs themselves.

They left the next morning. Sorian rode with them as far as the turning past Ironbound, where he had people to find and a letter to send. He promised to meet them at the Ford and made Tema repeat which yard he meant.

The road went up into stone country. The fields narrowed, the hedges thinned, and the light gained a pale clarity which made distant things look near enough to reach before supper. They seldom were. Elena learned which trees grew beside drinkable water and which merely suggested it. She learned the sound of the fourth cart when Tovin was awake, and the softer sound when Mera had to call back and wake him.

They stopped one afternoon at a shrine built into a milestone. A fresh bunch of yellow flowers stood in a cup too small for their stalks. Kestra straightened the cup, but Rada moved it back.

“It drains that way.”

Kestra examined the hole in its side. “So it does.”

She left a thread from the loose edge of her sleeve. Elena put one of her remaining eggs in a neighboring hollow after making certain it was sound. She could not have said whom she meant it for. At the next farm a boy asked whether they had passed the cup and whether anything had been left for him to collect. She was pleased by the answer he supplied to a question she had not quite asked.

On the sixth evening Benjamin helped Thane loosen his coat. Thane had worked too long at a rattling fitting and his right hand had swollen enough to make the fastening awkward. Elena sat close enough to see, far enough away to let them manage. Benjamin held the sleeve open without pulling. Thane worked his hand through it, swore at one fold of wool, and thanked him.

“It’s going to rain,” Thane said.

“Your hand?” Benjamin asked.

“The sky. Look at it.”

Elena laughed into her cup. Benjamin leaned back and examined the sky with exaggerated care.

They did not reach the Ford that evening. They could hear the river from the last rise, and some of the waiting fires showed through trees below, but Rada wanted daylight for the descent. Tema agreed. The company spread their blankets in a farmer’s empty lambing shelter, where old wool caught on the boards and a blackbird kept returning to protest their presence.

Elena lay awake after the others had settled. She had been imagining the room with shelves she meant to rent in Millhaven. Shelves of different depths, not the same depth for every bottle simply because the carpenter preferred it. A window she could open. A door that shut.

Below them, beyond the dark slope, something heavy struck against stone. The river carried the sound up clearly. It came again after a long interval, then stopped.

She reached for her bag in the dark, found its fastening, and left it closed.

Chapter 3 Two Rights to One Bridge

The bridge appeared between the trees in pieces. First the western tower, then a strip of parapet, then the water beneath it, brown at the edges and bright where the morning caught its broken surface. Kestra counted arches while the cart descended. Trees interrupted her at six. When they cleared she began again.

“Eight,” Rada said from the shafts ahead.

“I haven’t finished.”

“No need.”

Kestra looked across the river. There was a narrow arch at the east end, partly obscured by the bank. She could see light through it. Beyond that were eight larger openings, some filled almost to the shoulder with water.

She put her book away without writing the number.

The waiting yard lay on their left, south of the road. It had once been a field, and its owner had spent the last few days collecting payment from people who were destroying it. The wheel ruts were deep near the entrance. Farther in, carts stood in two crooked rows, with fires between them and ropes stretched wherever cloth could be dried. A woman had planted a stick beside her cooking place so people would stop reversing over it.

Rada waited for a small wagon to clear the entrance, then drove in. The other three followed. Across the road, a path went north beside the river, toward a low reach where horses stood among pale stones. Beyond them rose the round roofs of felt-covered wagons. Something white hung along a rope at the camp’s edge.

Kestra had expected the dispute to be visible. Men facing one another with weapons, perhaps. Instead there were people waiting for other people to finish things. A clerk stood outside a shed with a board beneath his arm. He raised it whenever someone approached, as if the answer might be written on the underside. Two mercenaries were playing at flicking stones into a broken cup. A third was asleep against a sack, his mouth open to the sun.

“The fifth row,” Sorian called.

There were only two rows. He pointed toward a space that might eventually become another.

Tema got down and looked at the mud. “Not there.”

“It’s the space available.”

“It drains toward the sacks.”

“Then put the sacks above it.”

They moved a stack of empty hurdles, negotiated with the owner of the wagon beside them, and found enough room for all four carts on ground that would remain almost dry if the weather behaved. Kestra helped carry the hurdles. One left a long pale scratch across her palm. She examined it before remembering that Elena was watching her examine it.

“I’ll wash it,” she said.

“A splendid idea.”

Sorian waited until the horses were unhitched before bringing the clerk. The man carried a little pot of sand and a roll of thin paper protected in an oilskin wrapper.

“These are the Millhaven people,” Sorian said. “No flag, no claim to the Ford. They’ll speak to both sides.”

Kestra put down the bucket she had been carrying.

“We’re employed by Tema.”

“Naturally.”

“Then we aren’t without a claim. We want his carts delivered.”

Sorian’s expression did not change. “Which would require the Ford to open.”

“It would also be true if we got them through and everyone else stayed here. I would rather people knew who pays us.”

The clerk looked from one to the other with increasing interest. Tema, who had heard the last part, came over wiping his hands on a cloth.

“Tell him,” he said to Sorian. “He’ll ask somebody later.”

The clerk wrote their names, then asked whether the five had a company seal. Benjamin looked pleased by the possibility of one.

“No,” Sariel said.

“A registered name?”

“Our names.”

He took a longer piece of paper.

Foot passage was permitted when the bridge crew opened the narrow gate beside the cart barrier. They went over in two groups. Tema and Sorian first, then Kestra and Thane with an Aeler woman whose leather apron had been patched with a different shade at the knees. Her name was Dorra. She carried no weapon except a short measuring rod with a brass cap, although Kestra thought it would have served.

“Keep to the southern side,” Dorra told them. “Don’t step over a rope because you can’t see why it’s there.”

Thane looked at the ruts worn in the stone. “How long have they been running the heavy loads across?”

“Long enough to call it a right.”

“I meant this spring.”

She glanced at him. “Since the first thaw. Before we were ready.”

The bridge rose toward its middle. On the northern side someone had lifted two paving stones and covered the opening with boards. A rope ran between short posts around them. Through a gap Kestra saw darkness and the glint of moving water. She kept to the side she had been told.

Thane stopped once to look over the parapet. Dorra let him look, then said, “The joint above your left boot.”

He crouched. A thin line of pale mortar had broken away from the older stone.

“New?”

“Since last autumn. It opens under the loaded grain wagons and closes when they pass.”

“How much?”

She held her thumb and finger a small distance apart. He looked up sharply.

“Yes,” she said. “That’s why we closed it.”

On the west bank the operating winch stood beneath a roofed shelter. Its chain passed through a slot in the masonry and out toward the heavy cart gate. A brass padlock secured the handle. Fine marks ran across the hasp, and somebody had added a thin twist of wire after the marks were cut. Kestra bent to look.

“Don’t,” Dorra said.

“I wasn’t going to touch it.”

“Then continue.”

The tollhouse had a warm room behind its public counter. It smelled of oiled wood, damp coats and onions simmering somewhere below. Aeler letters covered a slate beside the hearth. Kestra could make out several measurements, a list of names, and a sum which had been rubbed away and rewritten so often that the new figures lay in a gray hollow.

Voss stood at a high desk with a stool pushed aside. His beard was divided into two short braids to keep it clear of his work. He wore spectacles with a leather loop behind his head, and one lens had a flaw near the lower edge. He was reading Tema’s cargo list.

“Forty-eight,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Grain throughout.”

“Seed grain. The buyer marks are there.”

Voss tapped the list with the blunt end of his pen. “These two marks are the same buyer using two hands.”

“Two farms under one purchase.”

“Then two places to send a claim if the sacks differ.”

“They don’t.”

“You must find that comforting.”

Kestra waited until he looked at her. “Could we see the maintenance grant?”

“Why?”

“Because I’m being told you can close the crossing until people acknowledge it. I’d like to know what acknowledgment is missing.”

He stared for a moment. Then he took off his spectacles, polished them on a cloth from his waistcoat, and put them back.

“Dorra. The lower box.”

She brought a flat wooden box with a clasp that worked. Kestra noticed that without intending to. Sariel had made her aware of how many important things were kept behind broken locks.

Voss lifted out a parchment wrapped in blue cloth. He spread the cloth first, placed the parchment on it, and set two smooth pieces of iron at its corners. The seal had lost its outer edge, but the inner mark remained. Beside it were later witnesses in different inks.

“The original?” Kestra asked.

“The working copy. Do you expect me to keep the original beside a kitchen?”

She began to like him, which made it important to pay attention.

The grant gave the guild responsibility for the bridge’s stonework, lifting gear and approach on each bank. It authorized tolls by load, with exemptions for certain local traffic and a schedule for exceptional repairs. The authority came through the old holding’s market charter and had been renewed by later occupants who disagreed about almost everything except their desire to use the bridge.

Kestra read the clause about approaches twice.

“This says the built approaches.”

“It does.”

“Does that include the horse ford?”

Voss looked toward the window. “Carts go there to avoid inspection, then ask us to pull them out.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No. It is what happens.”

She pulled her notebook closer but did not write. “I don’t doubt it. I’m trying to understand whether the ford belongs to the maintenance.”

Dorra answered before he could. “The ford belongs to whoever is prepared to mend it. At present that appears to be no one.”

Thane had been listening beside the hearth. “If you drove spikes in, you’ve maintained something.”

“A refusal,” Dorra said. “Not a road.”

Voss took his pen from behind the iron weight and laid it across the list. “The horse passage predates the bridge. The agreement was renewed when the stone approaches were built. The carriers pay for the bridge, or they should. The herds use the upper water. We agreed not to raise a barrier across their access.”

“But there is a barrier.”

“Against wagons.”

“Does a horse know that?” Thane asked.

Voss looked at him for the first time. “I am told you are a carpenter.”

“Among other disappointments.”

“Then you may be interested to learn that the last timber bill was refused because an auditor in a dry room decided wet oak should cost what it had cost four summers earlier. Three wagoners paid for the timber themselves. Seven crossed on it and promised to pay later. Two went round by the ford. One broke an axle and complained that the slope was unsafe.”

His voice had grown louder without becoming less precise. He stopped, seemed to notice where he was standing, and returned to the desk.

“We built the spikes high enough to catch an axle and low enough for a man to see. At the time there was no grass.”

Dorra said nothing. Thane looked toward the window.

Kestra wrote down the terms of the horse passage. She asked who kept its copy. Voss gave her a name she could not pronounce, corrected her attempt once, then told her Sarnai would know whom she meant.

They were not opposite claims. One was about maintaining stone and one about access to water. She could already imagine putting them side by side on a clear sheet, each with its limits visible. The thought gave her a lift of pleasure which she tried not to show too plainly.

“Tema,” Voss said, “I could permit these four loads after weighing, if you acknowledge the temporary inspection charge.”

Sorian sat forward. Tema did not.

“For these loads?”

“As a carrier using the crossing.”

“Which would mean the next loads too.”

“It would mean I have inspected them. That is generally the purpose of an inspection.”

Kestra looked at the words on the page in front of her. She had been pleased too soon.

They crossed back before the noon bell. The bell hung above the eastern shed and had a flat note which carried well against the river. Its rope had been tied high enough that no passing child could ring it. A second rope, thinner, ran along the wall toward the weighing stage.

“For the scales?” Thane asked their escort.

The Aeler worker nodded. He had introduced himself as Harn on the way out, and now added that anyone who rang it for entertainment would be given entertaining work.

The stage stood beside the bridge approach, with its outer end on short piers over the sloping bank. A ramp led onto it from the wagon lane. Underneath, a maintenance crawl opened toward the yard at one end and toward a narrow shelf above the river at the other. A boy emerged carrying a length of rotten wood while an older man told him to keep his head down. The boy raised his head to answer and struck it.

Thane winced in sympathy.

Harn showed them the scales, then the cleared space where sacks could be set on trestles for inspection. There was nothing sinister about it. The apparatus was good, although one of the chains had been replaced with a heavier kind. Kestra wondered how often Voss had been told he was charging too much by people who had never stood here.

Sariel came from the north path with mud on her boots.

“You can’t just walk into the camp,” she said. “There’s an opening in the rope, but you wait there. A girl told me where to stand.”

“Did you stand there?” Thane asked.

“For once.”

Kestra told her about the charter and the permission that had almost been offered. Sariel listened with one foot against a timber so she could scrape mud from the other heel.

“Would it get us across?”

“Yes.”

“And the people behind us?”

“They’d be asked to accept it too. Tema thinks our signature would make it harder for them to refuse.”

“He’s probably right.”

Kestra felt an unexpected wish to defend Voss. “The repairs are real.”

“I didn’t say they weren’t.”

“Everyone talks as if he invented the problem in order to charge for it.”

Sariel looked at the stage. “Maybe he only invented the charge.”

It was a good answer and not a sufficient one. Kestra resented both qualities.

They went back to the carts. Elena had found a wash place behind the field owner’s shed and was making Dennet hang a cloth across it. Benjamin was helping Rada move her horse out of the wind. Thane wanted to go back to the bridge immediately and had to be reminded he had not eaten.

At the fire, Tema read Kestra’s copy of the grant. She pointed to the phrase about built approaches. He rubbed his thumb along a stain on the paper.

“They have been arguing over that word for six years.”

“Then perhaps someone could have told me before I spent a morning finding it.”

“Would you have believed them?”

She opened her mouth, then shut it. He smiled, not unkindly.

After they ate, she copied the horse agreement on a separate sheet. The paper was rougher than she liked; the pen snagged at the end of a line and left a small blot. She turned the blot into a mark showing where a witness would need to confirm the wording.

Benjamin leaned over her shoulder.

“Does it help?”

“It tells me what not to ask them to surrender.”

He read the two sheets slowly. “That seems useful.”

“It will be, if I remember it when they start shouting.”

He laughed and sat down beside her. He stayed while she finished, holding the empty cup off the ground so a gust would not roll it into her ink. When Tema came back she put the two claims before him and asked for the names of the people empowered to change either one.

He took a seat. This time he put his gloves away.

Chapter 4 A Road Below

Sariel found the first track by following a man who was trying not to be followed.

He did not make it difficult. He looked back whenever he reached a turn, then hurried through the turn so the person behind him would have something to wonder about. At the edge of the waiting yard he stopped to tie his boot. Sariel stopped to admire a damaged wheel. He straightened, saw her, and began tying the other boot.

She walked past him.

“I don’t want whatever you’ve got,” she said.

“I haven’t got anything.”

“Then you’re carrying it carefully.”

He had a wrapped package under his coat, held with one arm while the other performed the business of being innocent. Sariel kept walking. Beyond the yard the track descended behind a hedge and turned back toward the river. She heard him start after her, slow down, then decide to wait until she had gone farther.

She liked being alone for a little while. She liked the knowledge that Benjamin knew where she was going and had not asked to come. Those were separate pleasures. In the city she had sometimes wanted a companion only so she could prove she was able to lose one.

The track was lower than the road but still on the east bank. Water stood in the ruts. On one side the hedge grew from an old stone wall; on the other lay patches of rough ground where people had cut willow. Sariel could see the bridge between the stems, its eastern end above the weighing stage. The stage had a riverward opening beneath it, only a dark slit from here. Someone had pushed a long pole through and was levering at a piece of timber.

She went on until the track forked. The wider way ended at a riverside clearing marked by old wheel circles. The narrower went north through willow, turned inland around a wet hollow, and rejoined the horse approach above the bridge. Somebody had laid cut branches over its worst ground. The branches were not settled into the mud yet.

A loaded cart might get through. An empty cart certainly had: the marks were recent and sharp at the edge.

Sariel crouched beside them. The ground gave under her heel. She put her hand on a root to stand and found a strip of leather tied around it, nearly the same color as the wet bark. Another strip showed on a branch farther ahead. Markers, for somebody who did not mean to ask directions.

“You want the far one.”

She turned with her knife half clear of its sheath.

The man behind her had been standing very still. He was broad through the chest and carried a sword whose scabbard had a patch near its mouth. His black surcoat had been mended with brown cloth beneath one arm. There was a little river mud on the back of one glove, and no mud at all on the other.

“The far root,” he said. “The one you’ve found pulls out.”

Sariel let the knife settle. “Then why mark it?”

“I didn’t.”

He stepped past her, put his clean glove against the farther root, and tested his weight. It held. She disliked that he had shown her the use of his hands without giving her a reason to take offence.

“Rusk?”

“They’ve told you the worst of me already.”

“They’ve told me your name.”

“You’ll find somebody willing to improve on it.”

He looked toward the bridge. She saw now that he had been watching the space beneath the stage rather than the track. The boy working there had come out onto the shelf with a rotten board. Rusk waited until he was clear, then turned back.

“You came with Tema.”

“Yes.”

“How many swords?”

“How many are you counting as swords?”

“People who can keep hold of them.”

“One more than you might expect.”

He smiled. “That’s usually the answer when there aren’t enough.”

He did not ask again. She had expected him to enjoy making her say it. Instead he took off the muddy glove, folded it into the other and put both through his belt.

“I have people watching this track. If you find one asleep, wake him before you decide to step over him. He may misunderstand.”

“What are they watching for?”

“People who want to go around the bridge.”

“To stop them?”

“That depends what they’ve brought.”

The man with the package appeared at the lower fork. He saw Rusk and stopped. Rusk beckoned him over with an impatience that seemed older than their present meeting.

The package contained a pair of boots. Good boots, with the soles stitched recently and the leather treated against water. Rusk examined them, pushed a thumb against the heel and gave the man a coin.

“Both?” the man asked.

“Unless you’ve found a customer for the other foot.”

“That isn’t what we said.”

“You said the left one was sound.” Rusk held it up. The seam beneath the ankle had opened. “It isn’t. I’ll pay for a sound left one when you bring it.”

The man took the coin. When he had gone, Sariel said, “I thought you weren’t being paid.”

“My men aren’t being paid. That’s why I buy the boots.”

“Out of your own money?”

“Out of money I’d prefer to use for something else. You’re very interested.”

She was. She had expected a simpler man from the descriptions at supper. That did not make him a better one, but it meant she had to look again.

“Would the track take four carts?”

“Not full.”

“If we divided the loads?”

“People have.”

“And your watch?”

“People have settled that too.”

He placed the boots beneath a dry part of the hedge. “Don’t take that as an offer. You haven’t asked one.”

Sariel studied the ground beyond him. The track widened where it met the horse lane. A pale strip of gravel led down to the water, broken by darker patches. On the opposite bank she could see a landing place and a path climbing west through scrub.

“I haven’t decided what I’d be buying.”

“Good. It saves us both a tiresome conversation.”

He turned toward the bridge. The meeting was over as far as he was concerned. Sariel remained at the fork until he had gone, then followed the narrow track north.

The iron showed where the grass had been trampled away. Flat blades had been driven into a timber buried across the approach, each tilted against a wheel coming downhill. From the upper path a driver would see them. From the water a horse would meet their reverse edges while looking for firm ground.

Sariel stood above the line and imagined a wagon trying to turn around it. The ruts told her where the drivers had tried. One wheel stayed high and the other cut deep into the soft shoulder. Farther along, willow had been laid to widen that shoulder. The marks she had followed were part of a way round the spikes, made by people unwilling to go back to the road.

She could use it, perhaps. Two people leading, one at the wheel, half the load waiting on dry ground. Then another crossing, another wait. With four carts they would be working through most of a night.

A girl on a gray pony watched from beyond the white rope.

Sariel had seen her that morning. She wore her hair in two thick braids tucked into her collar, and one of her boots had a new strap considerably brighter than the other. She did not raise a hand. Sariel stayed where she was.

After a time the girl rode to the opening in the rope and pointed at the white stone beside it.

“There.”

Sariel went to the stone.

“You came before,” the girl said.

“I wanted to see which way the path went.”

“It went there before too.”

“I didn’t want to assume.”

The girl’s mouth twitched. The pony lowered its head to rub its nose against her knee. She pushed it away with the easy familiarity of somebody who had been interrupted by the same animal for years.

“I’m Sariel.”

“Yelü.”

“We’re taking grain across.”

Yelü looked at the bridge. “Many people are taking grain across. It stays on this side.”

“That’s what I’m trying to improve.”

“By going there?” She indicated the churned shoulder beside the spikes.

“By finding out whether we could.”

Yelü considered her. “Bring the woman with the medicine.”

“Elena.”

“If she has time tomorrow.”

“Is somebody hurt?”

The girl looked at the iron and then back at Sariel. “Yes.”

“I’ll tell her.”

“Tell her we can pay something. Not everything they ask in the town.”

Sariel nodded. The pony rubbed its nose against Yelü’s knee again. This time she let it.

On the way back Sariel stopped at the timber in the ground. A lock was a device that meant someone could not go through a door. It could be well made or badly made, justly placed or not; her fingers did the same work on it either way. She knew how to think about that.

These blades were easier to remove than a good lock. She could see where to put a lever. The thought came quickly and stayed with her, irritatingly useful. She could picture the timber up, the lane clear, four carts over the water before anyone finished arguing.

Then she looked at the wheel ruts beyond it, where the earth had already slid a little toward the river. She took the long way back so she could see the bank from below.

Benjamin was mending a strap when she returned. He had a needle between his lips and the expression of someone who had discovered he should have started at the other end.

“Don’t speak,” she told him.

He removed the needle. “I wasn’t going to.”

“You always are.”

She sat on the back of the first cart. He moved the open awl box away from her hand, then looked at her boots.

“Found a way?”

“Found a place people have tried.”

He waited for the rest. She told him about the lower track, the marked roots and the iron. About Rusk’s boots. She did not try to turn any of it into an answer before she had finished.

“Would you go across it?” he asked.

“On foot, yes.”

“That’s not what we need.”

“I know.”

He drew the thread through his needle. “Did he threaten you?”

“Rusk? Not particularly.”

“That isn’t no.”

“He said not to step over sleeping men. It seemed good advice.”

Benjamin looked at her for a while, then nodded. She had expected another question and found she was grateful for the lack of one.

He resumed the seam. The needle entered the wrong hole and came back out.

“Give it here.”

“I can do it.”

“I can see that. It’s the length of the afternoon which concerns me.”

He gave it to her. Their fingers touched on the leather, and neither of them moved quite as quickly as the transfer required. She bent over the stitching before he could see her smile.

“You’re cheerful,” he said.

“It’s your workmanship.”

“I’ve heard it has that effect.”

She finished the seam and trimmed the thread with her knife. On the road a child was running with a wooden hoop, trying to keep it clear of a rut. Every time it fell, the child scolded it as if it had chosen poorly. Benjamin watched with an expression which made her think of the room upstairs in Millhaven, the place beside his bed where she had put a bowl after he could eat without assistance.

She had enjoyed looking after him. She did not like admitting that to herself without immediately adding that she wished he had not needed it. Both were true, and neither made the other comfortable.

“Here,” she said, handing back the strap.

He tested the seam. “What do I owe you?”

She almost said something clever. Instead she leaned against his shoulder for a moment. He went still, then put his cheek against her hair.

The hoop fell again. The child’s verdict was loud and complete.

Sariel started laughing and could not stop before Benjamin did.

Before dark she gave Tema her account, with Thane there to hear the parts about the ground. Rusk could be paid to overlook a crossing. The lower track joined the same approach the Ykrul needed. A cart would have to take its load over in stages, and somebody would need to test the bank under weight.

“Tomorrow,” Thane said.

Tema looked west over the river. Smoke rose from the receiving yard beyond the tollhouse. A cart stood there already, empty and waiting for sacks which remained on their side.

“Voss has offered weighing in the morning.”

“Then I’ll look afterward,” Thane said.

“Could you look before?”

“I could look in the dark now. It wouldn’t improve what I saw.”

Tema smiled without much pleasure. “In the morning, then.”

Sariel told Elena about the invitation from the camp. Elena checked how much linen she had ready and asked whether she ought to take a second pair of hands. Sariel offered hers.

“You need to know what hurts before you start moving him,” Elena said.

“I was going to carry your bag.”

“You could have said.”

“I’m leaving room to impress you.”

She went to check the horse lane once more from the road. A lantern had been placed near the iron. Whoever had put it there had shielded the flame from the wind with a broken pan. The light made the spikes visible, and the dark beyond them seemed deeper by comparison.

A horse called from the camp. Another answered from the waiting yard. Sariel watched the lantern until she could see the wick burning unevenly. Then she turned back and found Rusk’s man awake at the fork, eating something from a cloth.

“Still looking?” he asked.

“Still being paid to stop me?”

He gave a short laugh. “Not yet.”

She left him enough room to get up without feeling hurried, and took the road home to the carts.

Chapter 5 The Horse Lane

Yelü was waiting by the white stone with two cups and a look which suggested that visitors usually arrived later than they said they would.

Elena had arrived when she had been asked. She decided to preserve that distinction privately.

“Drink first,” Yelü said.

The cup was warm, its outer surface rubbed smooth by hands. Elena smelled tea and milk before tasting salt. She took another sip, more cautiously. Sariel had accepted the second cup and was looking into it.

“It’s meant to be like that,” Yelü told her.

“I wasn’t going to say anything.”

“You were holding it as if you might pour it away when I turned round.”

“I was looking for somewhere to put the bag.”

Yelü took Elena’s medicine bag from her and carried it through the opening in the rope. Both women followed. The camp was much noisier on this side of its quiet boundary. A baby was protesting a change of clothes. Someone hammered a wooden peg while another person told them where the peg ought to have gone. The horses shifted in their lines, stirring the smell of damp hide and trampled spring grass.

Felt walls had been pegged to the ground beneath the wagons so the wind could not come through. At one of them a man knelt beside a stove, trimming a strip of dough before putting it against the hot iron. He looked up, saw the cups and gestured for them to wait. Three little cakes went into a cloth. Yelü accepted them with a familiarity that left Elena uncertain whether they were breakfast or a theft which everyone permitted.

“He asks what you put in them when you make them at home,” Yelü said.

“Mostly I ask someone else to make them.”

The man understood that much Vhasian. He laughed and handed Elena a fourth cake directly.

It was dense, hot through the middle and pleasant with the salted tea. Elena ate it before approaching the patient. The man by the stove watched to make certain she did.

Törun lay on folded bedding beneath the roof of a wagon. A bowl of cold water stood beside him. His right foot rested on a rolled piece of felt. He had shaved his chin recently but missed a patch beneath one side, and the untidy place gave him a younger face than his voice suggested.

“You can tell her I am not asleep,” he said when Yelü lifted the hanging.

“She has eyes.”

“The last one waited until I spoke.”

Elena sat on the step. “Does it hurt more when you sit up?”

“It hurts more when people touch it.”

“Then I’ll ask before I do.”

He examined her for a moment, then moved his blanket aside. The foot was wrapped in cloth darkened by an old stain. She put her cup beyond reach of the bag and washed her hands with the water Sariel had brought.

“When did it happen?”

“Four days.”

“The morning?”

“Near evening. Why?”

“Because four days can mean three nights or four, and I want to know which.”

He considered this reasonable. It had happened near evening, four nights before. They were bringing horses up from the water. The lead mare met the iron and turned across the others. Törun had been at her shoulder. He had not let go of the rope soon enough.

“He got the mare clear,” Yelü said.

“She got herself clear. She weighs more than I do.”

Elena asked whether he had been able to walk afterward. He said yes. Yelü said he had been carried. Törun corrected her: he had walked as far as the flat stone.

“How far is the flat stone?” Elena asked.

Yelü pointed. It lay just outside the wagon.

“I meant the other flat stone,” he said, but without much conviction.

She loosened the cloth slowly. The first turn came away easily. The next had dried to the damaged skin. Sariel poured a little clean water where Elena pointed, and they waited while it softened.

Törun watched the roof.

“Will you need to cut?”

“I can’t tell yet.”

“The other man said he would.”

“Did he tell you what he wanted to cut?”

“No.”

She kept her voice even. “Then let’s find out what there is to do before we borrow his answer.”

The swelling was greater across the outer foot. Two toes lay at a slight angle which might have been his ordinary shape; he said it was not. There was a split in the skin where the hoof had caught him against stone. The edges were hot and tender. Elena asked him to move what he could, then checked the places he said he could no longer feel properly.

She had dressed worse wounds in Millhaven. She had also dressed small ones that had gone bad despite everything she knew to do. Here there was no Vennit in the next room. She could imagine the older woman’s fingers at work, the patient silence while Elena named what she saw, and had to stop herself answering the imagined questions aloud.

“I want to wash this,” she said. “Then support the foot so you’re not moving it whenever someone moves the bedding. You’ll need someone to watch it with you. If the heat spreads, or you become feverish, I want to be told.”

“I have to ride.”

“Not today.”

“I didn’t mean today.”

“Good. We agree about today.”

He looked down at the foot. She let him look before covering it again.

Yelü fetched hot water and a woman who could remain after they left. The woman spoke to Törun first, briskly, and he answered with the resignation of someone accustomed to losing this particular argument. She was his cousin. He had told her he would not need assistance before the sun was properly up.

“He thinks I enjoy hearing him ask for the bowl every time,” she told Elena.

“I would prefer to need it less,” he said.

Elena smiled. “That would be convenient for everyone.”

They worked for most of an hour. Sariel held the foot when asked and kept her face so carefully neutral that Elena knew she was remembering another treatment. Thane’s hand had been swollen like this after the raid, too large for the shape she remembered. Sariel had kept offering to fetch things then. Now she waited until a thing was needed.

When the dressing was on, Elena showed the cousin how to support the foot with folded cloth and two light splints. She did not ask her to remember a list. She made her do it once while Törun complained about being used for practice. The second arrangement was better than the first because the cousin noticed where the knot pressed against the bedding.

“Yes,” Elena said. “Keep it there.”

Törun settled back. Sweat had dried beneath his jaw. He looked exhausted and angry, and she was glad he did not feel obliged to thank her immediately.

He asked what she would charge.

She told him for the materials and this visit, then said a later visit would depend on what was needed. He asked her to repeat the materials. She did, naming each one. The cousin went to fetch a small pouch.

Sariel stood to make room. Outside, a bell sounded faintly from the yard.

“The weighing,” she said.

“Go,” Elena told her. “I’ll bring the bag.”

“Yelü can help.”

Yelü, who had not offered, raised an eyebrow. Sariel apologized. The girl accepted the apology with satisfaction and went to wash the cups.

Sarnai came while Elena was packing. She had been working with horses: a light coating of hair clung to her coat, and one sleeve was damp above the cuff. Her face resembled her daughter’s most closely when she was trying not to smile. She spoke to Törun’s cousin, listened to the answer, then asked Elena whether she had eaten.

“Yes.”

“He feeds everyone before they work. He thinks it improves the work.”

“It improves mine.”

Sarnai nodded as though recording a useful point in the cook’s favor. They walked a little way from the wagon, to where two women were sorting lengths of rope. Each coil had been marked with a knot near one end. Elena began to ask about the knots, then remembered she had been brought here for a reason.

“Will he walk?” Sarnai asked.

“I hope so. I can’t promise what the foot will be like afterward.”

“And the mare?”

Elena looked at her. Sarnai’s mouth curved.

“Not your work. I know. People ask it of anyone who arrives carrying a bag.”

“Usually they ask me to look at a mule first.”

“Our mules have very good advice.”

One of the women sorting rope made a remark Elena did not understand. Sarnai answered without looking round. Both women laughed.

From this part of the camp the bridge was visible above the willow. The horse approach lay nearer, its churned ground catching light where water stood in the ruts. Elena could see the timber carrying the spikes. Up close it would be ugly; from here it was barely distinguishable from an ordinary piece of work left unfinished.

“You come from the grain train,” Sarnai said.

“Yes.”

“To make the crossing open.”

“To bring those carts across. We were asked to see what was wrong.”

“You have seen some of it.”

Elena looked back at the wagon. “Yes.”

“The people who put the iron there have told you why.”

“They’ve told Kestra. I haven’t spoken to them.”

“Then when you do, ask why it is still there.”

There was no heat in the sentence. Elena found she had been preparing to answer anger, perhaps to assure Sarnai that she understood it. The quiet question gave her nowhere useful to put the assurance.

“I can ask,” she said.

“They may have an answer. They have had several.”

A small child came between the rope coils carrying a broken wooden horse. He held it up to Sarnai and showed her the missing leg. She examined the break, asked where the leg had gone and received a detailed account which appeared to implicate another child and a dog. A young woman followed him, taking care not to step on the rope.

Sarnai handed the toy back. “Bring the leg. No one can mend the part you have hidden.”

The child went at once. The young woman stayed.

“Oyun,” Sarnai said to Elena. “My nephew’s wife.”

Elena knew which nephew. She wished for a moment that she did not, so she could ask an ordinary question without wondering whether it sounded like an evasion.

Oyun saved her the effort. “Törun says you made him pay for being told to lie down.”

“He was lying down when I arrived. I charged him for the rest.”

“Good. He has been telling us that for nothing.”

She had a square, capable face and a scratch on her wrist, recently washed. Her sleeves were rolled well above her elbows. There was nothing in her dress that Elena could confidently identify as mourning. She decided not to search for it.

“He asked me to look at the mare,” Elena said.

“She has someone with her. Someone who hasn’t been arguing about whether she ought to put weight on the foot.”

Oyun looked toward the bridge, then at Sarnai. “They are weighing grain.”

“They weigh everything.”

“They can begin with what came over us last year.”

Sarnai touched her arm. The gesture was brief and accepted. Oyun did not appear soothed by it, nor did Sarnai appear to expect that. They spoke a few words in their own language. Elena waited, uncomfortable with the sudden importance of where she looked.

The child returned without the missing leg and began an explanation. Oyun picked him up. He was large enough that the weight pulled at her shoulder, and she shifted him rather than put him down.

“I should go back,” Elena said.

Sarnai nodded. “Törun is being paid for work he cannot do. That ends when the horses leave. He knows it. Tell me if he asks you to make the foot well by then.”

“I’ll tell him I can’t.”

“Tell me too. He won’t.”

Yelü accompanied her to the opening with the bag carried on one shoulder. Elena had offered to take it twice. The second time the girl said that if she did not want it carried she should stop making it so heavy.

“There are bottles.”

“I can hear them.”

“They shouldn’t be touching.”

Yelü stopped at once. Elena opened the bag and moved one fold of linen. They went on.

At the white stone a young woman stood waiting with an empty water jar. She wore a man’s coat shortened at the hem, the cut edge sewn badly enough to be her own work. She asked Yelü a question in careful Ykrul, received an answer, and turned to Elena.

“Is he worse?”

“Do you know Törun?”

“He brought us a horse when my father’s couldn’t finish the lower field. He said he would collect it when he passed through again. My father keeps asking when he’ll come.”

“He needs to rest.”

“Then I’ll tell him not to walk down there and ask.”

“Your father?”

“Both of them, if necessary.”

Her name was Senn. Her family lived beyond the bend, in a house Elena had mistaken for an abandoned shed on the descent. The good part faced away from the road. The jar was for clean water from a spring above the camp; the water in the yard was muddied by the waiting animals.

Elena asked whether there was somewhere cloth could be washed without people leading horses through it.

“At ours. If you don’t mind the climb.”

“Would your family mind?”

“They’d mind if you expected us to do it for you.”

“I meant to pay for someone to help.”

Senn held the jar against her hip and looked more closely at the bag. “My aunt used to have me boil cloth. She said I was the only one who would leave it alone long enough.”

“What did she use it for?”

“People. Mostly babies and the people who had them.”

Elena asked a few more questions. Senn answered without improving the amount she knew. She could keep water hot, fold clean dressings and recognize when somebody who had been speaking stopped responding. She did not know which of Elena’s bottles she would use for anything. That was an answer Elena liked.

They agreed on work for that afternoon only: wash the used cloth, dry it properly, and help prepare another supply. Elena named what she could pay for the hours and the fuel. Senn thought about it and asked for a little more if she had to carry water to the yard as well.

“For that trip, yes.”

“Then I’ll come after we’ve eaten.”

Yelü had been listening. “She doesn’t spoil the cloth,” she said.

Senn looked pleased and tried not to. Elena recognized the effort.

They walked together as far as the yard. From the higher ground Elena could see the weighing stage, a line of sacks across its planks and the second cart backed against the ramp. Men stood around something on the ground. One was Voss, recognizable by the pale flash of his spectacles. Tema faced him with his gloves in one hand.

Sariel was speaking. Her voice did not carry clearly, but the way she stood did: feet apart, shoulders level, no attempt to make herself smaller in the space between the two men.

“Your people?” Senn asked.

“Some of them.”

“They’ve found something.”

Elena took her bag from Yelü. There was enough time to set it in the dry place, wash her hands and find out what had happened. She wanted those things in that order, even if the men on the stage had other preferences.

She thanked Yelü. The girl nodded and started back toward the camp, then stopped.

“You should come early tomorrow,” she said. “Before Törun decides how well he is.”

“I will.”

Elena carried the bag to the third cart. Mera moved a folded blanket to make a dry space for it and asked whether the patient would mend. Elena gave her the same answer she had given Sarnai. It was becoming no easier to say.

Above them, on the stage, Tema raised his voice. She could hear him now.

“That is not the load you were asked to weigh.”

Elena closed the bag, checked the fastening and went to see which part of the job had changed while she was away.

Chapter 6 The Load Test

Earlier that morning, while Elena and Sariel were still in the Gray Ash camp, Kestra had expected the scale to settle when the men stopped touching it. It continued moving, a little less each time, while Harn stood with his hands behind his back and everyone else waited for permission to become impatient.

“Now?” Tema asked.

Harn lifted one finger.

The beam passed the mark, returned, passed it again. At last he lowered his finger and moved the smallest weight along its bar.

“Now.”

Kestra wrote the figure in her working book. Voss’s clerk wrote it on his board. Tema, whose grain they were weighing, looked as though he would have preferred to keep the information between himself and the grain.

The twelve sacks from Rada’s cart stood on trestles beside the scale. They had been weighed in pairs, the buyer marks checked and the ties left intact. Dorra had inspected the cart itself while it was empty. She crawled beneath it without asking Thane to join her and called out what she found. He listened with his head tilted, left hand resting on the shaft.

“Sound axle,” she said. “New pin. Someone’s packed the near bearing too tight.”

“Rada had the wheel off in winter.”

“Then Rada can loosen it.”

Rada knelt beside the wheel. “How much?”

“A quarter turn, then see.”

Nobody offered to do it for her. Kestra watched her work the wrench, test the play and ask Dorra to look again. When they were satisfied, Harn slid a small wooden stand beneath the shaft and the cart remained level without anybody holding it.

“I want one of those,” Thane said.

Harn looked at the stand. “So do I. It belongs to the stone gang.”

They began putting the sacks back. Four at first, spread low across the floor, then the next four. Dorra made Dennet move one forward before the last row went in. He lifted it, put it down again with a grunt and waited for her approval.

“That,” she told Thane, “is what they won’t do if I leave the yard.”

“I’m standing here,” Tema said.

“Yes. You can tell them tomorrow.”

He took this better than Kestra expected. He had been offered a witnessed load test rather than another argument over a charge, and was trying to remain grateful for it. He had even brought out the copy of his previous carriage weight. It lay on the clerk’s board, protected from the wind by a lump of smooth iron.

Voss arrived from the west bank after the first cart had been reloaded. A boy opened the foot wicket for him and followed with a covered cup. The factor took the cup, tasted its contents and handed it back.

“Hotter, please.”

“It’s just boiled.”

“Then give me the cup it boiled in.”

The boy went away muttering. Voss put on his spectacles and compared the figures.

“We take the first cart to the near rise. One horse, no passengers. Nobody rides on the sacks.”

“We don’t ordinarily—” Tema began.

Voss raised his eyes.

“No passengers,” Tema said.

Kestra stood on the southern edge of the bridge with a measuring cord against her knee. The wind brought the smell of the river up through the open joint beside her boot. She could hear water striking the pier below, but she could not see it without stepping outside the place Dorra had assigned her.

Thane had made a small gauge from two thin scraps of wood and a wedge. Harn held it against a joint in the parapet while Dorra watched the paving. Rada waited at the eastern entrance with her horse. The heavy cart barrier remained shut; two workers had opened a shorter service rail onto the first part of the span, far enough to admit the cart for the test but not to permit a crossing.

“How far?” Rada called.

“Until Harn lifts his arm,” Dorra said. “Then stand.”

The horse stepped up onto the stone. Rada walked at its head. The cart followed, rattling over the first joint, and the sound traveled through the bridge into the soles of Kestra’s feet.

She had imagined a bridge as an object that remained still while things crossed it. Now she felt a faint yielding, nothing that would have frightened her had she not been told to notice. Harn’s thumb moved on the gauge.

“Stand.”

Rada stopped. Her horse flicked its tail and looked back at the cart as if wondering why everyone was staring at its work.

Thane leaned over Harn’s shoulder, careful not to touch him.

“Again,” Dorra said.

They moved the cart another few paces. This time the wedge shifted enough for Kestra to see it from where she stood. Harn lifted his arm. Rada stopped immediately.

“There,” Thane said.

“I see it.”

“I meant the sound.”

Dorra crouched, put her hand flat on the stone and waited. Somewhere below them came a dry little click, almost lost beneath the water.

“That isn’t the arch,” she said.

“The facing?”

She nodded. “And the packing behind it. You were looking at the mortar yesterday.”

“You showed me the mortar.”

“To find out whether you’d look at anything else.”

Thane laughed, openly delighted. Kestra saw Dorra’s expression ease before she returned her attention to the paving.

They backed the cart off under Rada’s patient instruction. The horse disliked being asked to reverse uphill and made that dislike known in several ways. Benjamin, waiting beside the service rail, kept the shafts clear while Iset steadied the trace. The cart came down onto the approach. Nobody applauded. Kestra wanted to, a little, and was relieved when she discovered Dennet grinning at her.

Thane and Dorra compared the gauge with the mark they had made before the load. They disagreed over how much of the movement had returned. Harn settled it by fetching a second wedge and making them hold both against the light.

“It will take that load?” Tema asked.

“It took it,” Dorra said. “We haven’t finished.”

Kestra wrote the two measurements rather than a conclusion. She had once ruined an entire day’s work at the Academy by choosing the result she expected from two results she did not understand. The memory did not often arrive so helpfully. Usually it arrived while she was trying to sleep.

Voss looked over her shoulder. “Put the cart’s weight beside it.”

“I have.”

“Including the vehicle.”

“I have that as well.”

He adjusted his spectacles. “So you do.”

It pleased her absurdly. She turned the page before the pleasure could make her volunteer to copy anything else for him.

The second cart did not agree with its own paper.

They had unloaded Varn’s twelve grain sacks and found them close enough to the first twelve that Tema began to look relieved. The trouble appeared when Dorra checked the empty vehicle against the old carriage weight. She looked beneath the bed, came out again and asked for the paper.

“When was the floor replaced?”

“Last autumn,” Varn said.

“With what?”

“Boards.”

Dorra waited. Varn looked at Tema, who was speaking to Sorian on the far side of the stage.

Thane tapped the floor with the end of his measuring stick. The sound changed near the rear.

“That’s a second floor.”

“An old repair,” Varn said.

Kestra remembered hearing that phrase on the road. Sariel had mentioned a noise beneath the load while they ate, more interested in the loose road than in the answer. Kestra had not written it down. Why should she have? Half their equipment was an old repair.

Harn climbed onto the cart and felt along the joint between two boards. There was a little iron fastening, recessed and packed with dirt. He scraped it clear with a blunt nail.

“We need this up.”

“You’ll split it,” Varn said.

“Then show me how it opens.”

Varn did not move.

Sariel came onto the stage from the yard, carrying Elena’s empty water flask. She had mud on one knee and a streak of dried grass along her sleeve. She stopped when she saw the men around the cart.

“What’s wrong?”

“An uncommunicative floor,” Thane said. “Can you persuade it?”

She gave the flask to Kestra. “Varn?”

He looked toward Tema again.

Sariel saw the look. She stepped onto the wheel hub, bent over the rear board and pressed the fastening with her thumb. The pin moved sideways. Harn caught the board before it dropped.

The space beneath was shallow. Six flat packages lay fitted between the crosspieces, their cloth dark with old damp. One had been pinched under the edge of the board. When Harn lifted it, the corner tore and a little white material ran across the floor.

Sariel put her hand down to stop it falling through a crack. For an instant her palm covered all of it. She could have brushed it into the gap, made the discovery something less definite while Tema came to explain. Kestra knew she could have because she herself thought of doing it.

Sariel lifted her hand.

“Tema,” she called.

He heard her over the noise of the yard. He looked at the open cart and stopped speaking to Sorian. The two men came toward them, Sorian half a pace behind.

Voss put his pen away before he approached. Kestra noticed that. He intended both hands to be free.

“Salt?” he asked.

Tema looked into the floor. “Yes.”

Nobody needed to taste it. One of the torn package’s seams carried the merchant’s mark that had been omitted from the grain list.

Voss held out his hand for the list. The clerk brought it from his board. The factor read the heading, then the items beneath it, although he had examined them twice already that morning.

“Seed grain,” he said.

“That is the consignment.”

“And this is?”

“Another.”

The word fell badly. Tema knew it as soon as he said it. Sorian turned away and rubbed his mouth with the back of his hand.

“Declared where?” Voss asked.

“It was bought outside your collection district.”

“We collect for passage, not purchase.”

“At a rate higher than the schedule.”

“Then you might have appealed the rate while telling me what you carried.”

Tema’s voice rose. “That is not the load you were asked to weigh.”

“It was carried on the vehicle I was asked to admit.”

Tema did not answer. Around them other people were finding reasons to be close. Rada stood at the bottom of the ramp, her reins loose in one hand. Mera had not left her cart, but she had turned on the seat to watch. Varn remained beside the wheel with both hands in his coat pockets.

Kestra felt the warmth go from her face. Their names had been written with Tema’s the day before. She thought of the two documents she had laid out so carefully, and of how easy she had found it to believe that the obstacle was a failure to explain them properly.

“Get the grain covered,” Sariel said.

Everyone looked at her.

“It is going to rain. You can argue over salt without wetting forty-eight sacks of seed.”

Voss glanced up. The sky had darkened beyond the western tower. He nodded to Harn.

“Cover it. Nothing leaves this stage until I’ve entered the weights.”

“The grain belongs to the buyers,” Sorian said.

“And it will continue to. I said weigh it.”

The first drops struck the plank at Kestra’s feet. She put her book inside her coat and helped carry a cover. Tema remained standing beside the opened floor while rain darkened his shoulders.

There was more waiting after the discovery than Kestra would have expected from the words undeclared cargo. Nobody was put in chains. Nobody drew a weapon. The six packets were weighed, including the salt swept carefully from the cart floor onto a clean cloth. Voss’s clerk made two copies. Tema refused to sign one until a phrase had been crossed out and replaced. Voss agreed to the replacement, then refused Tema’s proposed change to the next line.

The grain was weighed and returned to its carts. The four drivers stood beside their loads as if permission to resume owning them might be revoked. Only the salt remained on the stage.

Kestra wrote the weights beside her earlier figures. The second cart had been asked to carry more than its paper admitted. The grain itself was sound. Both facts needed to remain where they could be read.

Varn helped Harn lift the packets into a wooden chest. The torn one went into a loose spare bag, still distinguishable from the others. Harn took the chest into the east weighing shed. Voss shut it with a small iron key and gave Tema the description of what had been retained. No salt was to be sold or discarded pending the assessment. Tema read the description, folded it into his inner pocket and kept his hand there a moment.

Thane stayed at the second cart. With the secret load removed, he could see where the false floor’s support had rubbed against the axle housing. Dorra crouched beside him. Their heads were close together beneath the rear plank.

“That won’t have helped,” Thane said.

“No.”

“It isn’t the reason for your joint moving.”

“I know.”

She sounded less angry with him than with the fact. Kestra was grateful. It would have been easy for everyone to let one discovered wrong explain every broken thing in sight.

She brought Thane his tool roll when he asked for it. He selected a thin wedge, set it between his teeth, changed his mind and gave it to her instead.

“Hold that, will you? Clean end.”

“It’s all dirty.”

“The end that is less dirty.”

She held it while he adjusted the packing. His left hand moved slowly, with none of the ease she remembered from the Coin’s workshop. Dorra watched the adjustment rather than his fingers. When it was finished she asked where he had learned to test a moving joint with scraps instead of a proper gauge.

“Where there weren’t proper gauges.”

“There are some here. Come tomorrow if they let your employer out of this argument.”

Thane looked up. “I’d like that.”

The pleasure in his voice caught Kestra off guard. She had been thinking of what the discovery would cost them, and he had found something in the same morning that he wanted to return for.

“Bring your drawing,” Dorra added. “The one you were pretending not to make.”

He glanced at the scrap protruding from his sleeve. “It isn’t finished.”

“Neither is the bridge.”

She went back to the workers. Kestra gave Thane his roll and carried her book to a dry corner. She took out the page on which she had written the company’s names, checked the copied weights against the clerk’s figures, and added the discovery beneath them in language Tema could not reasonably call an accusation.

The pen still shook a little. She waited until it stopped before finishing the line.

“You might have sent for me quietly.”

Sariel had been expecting the sentence since the board came up. Hearing it gave her an unreasonable satisfaction. She was angry enough to want him to make the argument easy.

“I know where I was.”

He looked at the loaded cart. The rear edge of the repaired floor was visible beneath the sacks, with pale fresh scratches around its fastening. Sariel could no longer see the space beneath it. She knew exactly how deep it was.

“One moment,” Tema said. “You might have given me one moment to speak to him before half the yard knew.”

Sariel’s anger found something less easy to hold. It was possible he would have said something useful. He knew Voss, knew which argument had already failed, knew whose witness mattered. “I’m staying for the grain,” Benjamin answered.

“I hope so,” Sariel said. “Another one would be excessive.”

The hem of his cloak had been folded up for riding, and he took the time to release it before speaking. When a clerk offered to hold his gloves, he gave them over without seeming to notice the offer.

“Four days closed?” he asked Sorian.

“Longer by the time the first report reached you.”

“I came when I received it.”

“I didn’t say otherwise.”

Morvain let that pass. He asked where Voss was, and a runner went over the bridge through the foot wicket. Then he asked who spoke for the carriers. Tema named three people who were not present. Morvain asked that they be sent for too.

“My office can provide an interim arrangement,” he said. “Inspection of loads, security at the approaches, repairs certified for reimbursement. No need for everyone here to settle the history of the valley before supper.”

Several people laughed in relief. Sariel understood why. She had spent the morning wanting one difficult thing to become simple enough to do.

“Whose reimbursement?” Tema asked.

“The road office at Ironbound, against verified expenditure.”

Morvain looked at him. “When the expenditure is verified.”

Rusk gave a small cough.

The prefect turned. “Your situation is different. I understand your command has received nothing for its current service.”

“You understand generously. We’ve received several assurances.”

“Until regular passage resumes.”

“Then you’ll want us paid by the day.”

For the first time Morvain smiled. “A reasonable safeguard.”

“Among others.”

“No one has proposed changing its ownership.”

“With the agreement of those responsible. That is why I am speaking to them.”

He was patient. He made Kestra sound like somebody objecting to a conversation already in progress. She colored, but stayed where she was.

“Naturally.”

“Varn was paid to know.”

“With whose horse?”

“The cart’s mine. Horse belongs to Tema’s partner. Harness is partly mine, partly replaced against the last journey. Leaving is an expensive way to explain that I’m cross.”

She offered Sariel the brush. Sariel took it and continued the line while Rada worked oil into another strap. The work gave her something to do with anger which had grown less satisfying as the day went on.

Beyond the river, light appeared in the tollhouse windows. Upstream a rider came to the white stone and waited until someone noticed. Yelü, bringing a message. Morvain’s clerk started toward her, but she remained where she was and called for Elena instead.

Elena went with her bag. Sariel put down the brush.

“She can walk to the rope without you,” Rada said.

“I know.”

“Then finish that bit. You’ve left the dirt where the buckle folds.”

Sariel finished it. When Elena returned, she said Törun wanted to know whether the men from Ironbound were being paid to remove the spikes.

“What did you tell him?”

“That I would ask.”

They looked toward the shed. The clerk was folding his page, satisfied with the day’s work. Sariel rose before she could think of a more comfortable way to spend the evening and went to put the question to him.

Chapter 8 Three Beds

Törun had prepared an argument by the time Elena reached him. It involved the width of a saddle, the shortness of the journey to the horses and the fact that he would not be using his injured foot while sitting on an animal.

She let him finish.

“How will you get on?”

He pointed to the low stool beside his bedding.

“And down?”

“The same stool.”

“Is the stool coming with you?”

Yelü put her face into her sleeve. Törun looked at her, then at Elena.

“Someone could bring it.”

“Someone could bring the horse here for you to look at.”

He considered that. Elena was careful not to speak while he did. Senn stood beside her holding the clean cloth they had washed the previous afternoon. She had folded it into a basket lined with an old sheet, the ends tucked over the top. Elena had checked it before they came uphill and found nothing to improve.

They were beyond the white stone, in the part of the camp where voices resumed without anyone having to announce that they could. A woman was stirring grain into a pot near the wagon. Children ran past with an empty feed bag between them, each pulling as though the other were the obstruction to its being carried properly.

Törun’s right foot lay on folded blankets. The swelling had not increased overnight. The skin remained hot, and Elena disliked a darkened patch near the outer edge, but she could find no change which justified giving the others her fear before she had examined it properly. She washed her hands, uncovered the splint and asked him where the pain had moved.

“It hasn’t moved. It’s very pleased with the place.”

“Show me the worst part.”

He did. She worked slowly. He watched her face with an attention that made her aware of each expression before it reached her mouth.

“It’s not worse this morning,” she told him.

“Then I may—”

“That is all I’ve said.”

He shut his eyes, more irritated than frightened for a moment. She was glad to see irritation. It belonged to a man who could still imagine doing something else with his day.

Senn gave her what she asked for, then held the clean dressing away from the used cloth without needing a reminder. When Törun shifted, she steadied the blanket beneath his calf rather than taking hold of his foot. Elena noticed and waited until the dressing was secure before saying she had done well.

“My aunt was particular about where I put my hands,” Senn said.

“Your aunt had sense.”

“She had sisters. They supplied the need for it.”

Törun laughed, then swore because laughing had made him move. Yelü offered to fetch the horse. He gave her a description so particular that Elena wondered whether the animal possessed a name at all. Yelü repeated the relevant portion and left before he could add anything.

When Elena had finished, Törun asked what the new men at the bridge intended to do.

“I don’t know,” she said. “They are offering inspection.”

“Of my foot?”

“Of loads.”

“Then they can inspect the iron after someone puts it on a cart.”

She packed the used cloth in its separate wrapping. He watched her do it.

“You don’t have to tell me it will be fair,” he said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good. I would have disliked having to argue with you before breakfast.”

The woman at the pot handed him a bowl. He took it without shifting his leg. Elena accepted a smaller bowl because it was offered and she was hungry. The grain was cooked thick, with sour milk stirred through it and a little fat floating at the edge. Törun ate carefully, keeping the bowl level against his chest.

They stayed until Yelü brought the mare. Törun’s whole expression changed when the animal put her head under the wagon cover. He scolded her for stealing the last of a feed bag, stroked the place above her eye and asked Yelü whether the small cut on her foreleg was clean. He did not ask for the stool again.

Senn’s home faced the wrong way for visitors from the road. What Elena had taken for its rear wall was in fact the rear wall; the door opened toward a patch of cultivated ground, with a spring beyond it and two narrow apple trees trained along a fence. There was a bench beneath the window, occupied by a man stripping the bark from a stick.

“Father,” Senn said, “this is Elena.”

He began to rise. Elena told him not to, which made him rise more quickly.

“Nothing wrong with my legs.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“She tells everybody to sit down,” Senn said. “She wants the work.”

Her father laughed and offered Elena the bench. She sat because it was the simplest way to end the matter.

Inside, Senn’s mother had cloth hanging above the hearth. The room smelled of damp linen and toasted barley. A small cradle stood beside the wall, empty except for folded bedding. Senn explained that her married sister brought the child when she worked in the lower fields. Elena found herself looking at the shelves above the hearth: broad enough for pots, narrow enough that nothing could disappear behind them.

“You made these?” she asked Senn’s father.

“My brother. I can ask him what you dislike.”

“I like them.”

“That’ll confuse him.”

Senn put the used cloth in a covered bucket and took the basket through to the wash place. Elena followed, then stopped herself from reaching for work Senn had already agreed to do.

“Could I employ you for more than the washing?” she asked.

Senn leaned the basket against the wall. “How much more?”

“I don’t know yet. That’s part of why I’m asking.”

There had been two people waiting at the carts when they left that morning, neither belonging to Tema’s train. One had a cut hand. The other wanted to know whether Elena could see his mother, who had not been able to keep food down since the day before. If she visited everyone where they lay, she would spend the daylight walking between them and the evening trying to boil cloth in a cooking pot. Törun could remain where he was; his people knew how to care for him. Others had no dry place except the space beneath their carts.

“There’s the old store beside the yard,” Senn said.

“The owner’s shed?”

“He keeps hurdles in the wet half. The other half used to hold wool. It has a door that shuts.”

“Would he rent it?”

Senn’s expression suggested that the harder task would be preventing him from renting it twice.

They went down together. The owner showed them the space without removing the bundles which occupied it. Elena ducked under a hanging rope and found a dry floor, one high window and a bench wide enough to lay a person along if that person did not mind losing circulation in both legs.

“You could put three there,” the owner said, pointing at the floor.

“I could put six there. It would still be a floor.”

“What do you want?”

“Raised beds. A place to wash away from the clean cloth. That window opened. No harness mending beside people with open wounds.”

He looked at Senn, who was examining the window catch. “She wants a good deal.”

“She’ll pay for what she uses,” Senn said.

They settled a daily rent, ending when the crossing opened or Elena gave a day’s notice. The owner’s family would carry away the waste water to a place below the yard, not empty it beside the pump. Senn would do a morning and evening period, with washing arranged separately at her home. Another woman in the yard, experienced in tending her husband’s damaged leg, agreed to sit with people while Elena was away, but refused to lift anybody alone. Elena was grateful for the refusal. It was one task she would not have to discover had been attempted badly.

The money took longer. Tema’s reserve covered medicine consumed in the company’s work, not every person waiting at the Ford. Elena brought him a proposed division: a small charge to people who could pay, contributions from carriers who wanted their workers attended, and reimbursement for actual supplies. Sarnai’s people would pay for Törun’s care as they had agreed. No one would be charged merely for asking whether Elena could help.

Tema read the figures while someone shifted a hurdle behind his chair.

“This is another business.”

“For the time we’re stuck here, yes.”

“And our commission?”

“If it needs me, I come. Which is why I need someone who knows whom to call while I’m away.”

He looked up at her. “How much are you paying yourself?”

She pointed to the line. He followed her finger, then nodded.

“Good. Don’t ask me to believe you’re cheaper to feed when you’re helping strangers.”

She laughed in surprise. He gave her back the page and agreed to the train’s limited contribution. She wished she could stop being angry with him as easily as he had made her laugh.

Thane came to look at the beds and was immediately sent back for Benjamin. He had offered to build frames. Elena wanted three sound sleeping surfaces before nightfall, not the best beds in the marches next week.

“Doors,” she told him. “On trestles. With something comfortable over them.”

“The doors will flex.”

“People flex too. We manage.”

He examined the available boards, found two disused doors and one broad store panel, and spent half an hour proving that Elena had been right only after he had changed several details. Benjamin carried the pieces in. Harn brought spare wedges from the stage and asked whether the third bed was being reserved for him.

“Do you intend to need it?” Elena asked.

“I like to know what I’m working toward.”

She made him hold the panel while Thane fixed the crosspiece. He complained cheerfully until Thane handed him the hammer. Then he worked without talking, the blows falling firmly where they were wanted.

Elena and Senn carried in straw mattresses borrowed against payment from two waiting households. The cloth covers had been washed. The straw was dry. Elena ran her hand across each mattress, searching for the stiff stems that would become unbearable beneath a patient who could not turn freely.

“You’re making a nest,” Benjamin said.

“Then stop shedding wood in it.”

He swept the floor. She watched him work around the beds, lifting each trestle leg in turn while Thane checked its seating. It pleased her to see the room acquiring an order she had chosen. She had spent so long finding a space at the edge of someone else’s work.

By late afternoon the first patient lay on the bed nearest the window. He was the man with the cut hand, and he remained chiefly because Elena wanted him to stop using it for an hour. He stared at the ceiling with suspicion.

“I could sit outside.”

“You could. You would also see something that needed carrying.”

He gave her an offended look which became agreement after Senn handed him a cup.

The sick woman arrived with her son. Elena examined her, listened to what she had eaten and what she had been able to keep, and arranged small drinks while they watched for a change. Senn moved a bowl within reach without being asked. The woman’s son sat on the floor until Benjamin found him a stool.

The third bed remained empty. Elena liked that best.

She was washing her hands at dusk when Benjamin came back with wood. He stacked it near the door, counted the pieces, then moved three farther from the wash bowl.

“If that splashes,” he said.

“Thank you.”

He did not leave. She dried her hands and waited.

“Thane was over the bridge again.”

“I know.”

“With Dorra. They have a set of instruments. He came back talking about them.”

“He told me too.”

Benjamin looked toward the empty bed. “He’s happy there.”

Elena hung the cloth on its peg. She could have said something reassuring, but the particular worry in his voice deserved more care than that.

“He was happy at the forge.”

“Not always.”

“No. Not always here either.”

Benjamin sat on the stool beside the door. There was a little pale dust on his sleeve from the last board he had carried. He brushed it away, discovered more and stopped.

“I keep remembering the raid,” he said. “When he’s doing something difficult, I remember what happened before I’ve seen what he’s actually doing.”

Elena knew the feeling. Sometimes a person turning too quickly beside her became Tom in the first instant after the arrow, and she had to wait for the present face to return.

“Does he know?”

“He knows I’m watching him.”

“Those aren’t quite the same.”

Benjamin leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. “If I tell him, it becomes another thing he has to look after.”

“It might. Or he might tell you to stop standing where he needs to put his tools.”

He laughed once. “He has.”

“Then there is somewhere to begin.”

They sat without speaking for a little while. Inside, Senn was asking the sick woman whether the last drink had stayed down. The answer came clearly. Elena listened until the exchange was finished.

“I don’t want him to stay because of us,” Benjamin said.

“You can want him to stay.”

He looked up.

“I do,” she added. “I’m allowed to be disappointed if someone goes. I don’t have to make it their punishment.”

The sentence surprised her. She thought of Tavia’s room with a door she could shut, the better paper, the pleasure of wide margins. There were still evenings when Elena wanted to be angry at her for having those things somewhere else.

Benjamin rested his head against the doorpost. “I miss having everybody under one roof.”

“We were awful under one roof.”

“We were less damp.”

That was true enough to make her smile. Senn came out to ask whether she should warm another cup, and Benjamin rose to fetch the water before being asked. Elena watched him go, then went inside to see whether the second bed could be empty by morning.

When she finally ate, Sariel had saved her a bowl with a cloth over it. There was barley, meat cut small and a piece of onion browned almost black on one side. The food was warm. Nobody asked her to explain the day’s work before she took the first mouthful.

Chapter 9 The First Meeting

The ink had dried before Kestra noticed she had left no place for the people who would be moving the sacks.

She had named the charter holders, the carriers, the Gray Ash, the road office and the guards. The loading work appeared as something that would happen once permission had been given. She read the sentence again, then drew a small mark beside it and went to find Rada.

“How long to unload four carts?”

“Onto what?”

“The stage.”

“Dry sacks, or ones you’re going to ask us not to tear? Do we put them back afterward? Are the horses harnessed while we’re doing it?”

Kestra opened her book.

Rada looked at it and sighed. “Bring that here.”

They sat on the cart shaft while the driver took the plan apart in a manner which made Kestra grateful and increasingly late. Dennet would help; Iset would not be available for lifting while animals were moving; Varn had been told to stay with his cart until the last part of its inspection was settled. Rada was not willing to begin another unloading with no dry cover prepared. If they shifted grain twice, the people doing the shifting would need food between the two occasions.

“People always feed horses before asking them to pull again,” she said. “Try remembering we have stomachs too.”

“I was going to arrange food.”

“Then write it before the part about taking the covers away.”

Kestra did. By the time she stood, the page was crowded with corrections. It looked less impressive and more possible.

The meeting was to take place outside the east weighing shed, beneath an awning borrowed from Sorian. The awning required four poles, which meant four people had opinions about where the table ought to go. Benjamin found a place clear of the wagon lane. Morvain’s clerk wanted a place nearer the shed. Dorra wanted everybody away from the scale. In the end the table stood at an angle to all three, with one corner in sunshine and the rest in shade.

Kestra set down her papers, her pen and the two smooth stones she had borrowed for weights. She had put the documents in order of proposed action. A separate sheet listed the points no one had yet agreed to, so nobody could accuse her of having concealed them.

Sariel came over carrying a stool.

“For you,” she said.

“I can stand.”

“You can sit too. It’s one of your less celebrated talents.”

Kestra accepted the stool. Sariel stayed while she checked the names against the places at the table.

“Is it readable?” Kestra asked.

Sariel leaned over the first sheet. “The words are. I won’t pretend I know what everyone will do when they hear them.”

“Neither do I.”

“You look as if you might.”

Kestra touched the edge of her clean copy. She had slept badly and risen with the certainty that one clear sequence could make the day better. She still thought it could. She did not see any point in arriving with less hope than the work required.

“I know what I’d like them to do,” she said.

Sariel nodded and put her stool on the side of the table nearest the yard, leaving the road clear behind it.

Sarnai came on foot with Yelü and two adults Kestra had seen at the camp. She stopped beyond the awning while her companions looked at the men standing near Morvain. Nobody announced her. Sorian went to meet her and offered the shaded place at the table.

She took it after moving the stool so she could see the horse lane.

Voss brought the working grant in its box. Tema had his carriage papers and the retained-salt inventory. Morvain’s clerk laid out the prefect’s authority beside them. Rusk brought nothing written and stood where the awning did not oblige him to bend.

Kestra waited until the ordinary movements of arrival had ceased. The yard did not grow quiet, but people closer to the awning began speaking more softly. She could hear a hammer striking iron on the stage and a child insisting that a cup belonged to him.

“I have copied the claims as their holders gave them,” she began. “If the copies are wrong, I want that corrected before we use them. The agreement about the horse passage and the grant for maintaining the bridge concern different uses of the crossing. Neither requires the other to be surrendered.”

Voss inclined his head. Sarnai looked at the two sheets without touching them.

“Then we can begin with what has to happen before loaded carts use the span,” Kestra said. “Dorra has given us the work required for a temporary safe limit. That work requires access to the approach, freedom to move the timber, and people who will be paid for doing it. The carriers can contribute toward the immediate work without accepting the proposed inspection charge for every future load.”

Tema said, “For this work, verified.”

“Yes.”

Morvain folded his hands. “My office can verify it.”

“The workers can verify what they used,” Dorra said.

“And the office can ensure their account is recognized.”

Kestra let the exchange finish. It was not yet an objection that prevented the next point.

“The horse approach must also be cleared. The iron will be removed under supervision so that no one leaves exposed ends or holes. Until the road is sound, carts will remain on the bridge approach rather than widen the horse lane.”

Sarnai spoke for the first time. “When is the iron removed?”

“Once the inspection is complete and the workers can move freely.”

“They can move now.”

Rusk shifted his weight. “Not if your riders decide they’re strengthening the barricade.”

“Have my riders stopped your workers?”

“They’ve watched them with bows.”

“Your men watch our horses with swords.”

Kestra lifted one hand, then lowered it because she had no authority to silence either of them. “The proposed first step is a withdrawal from the immediate approach by both armed parties. The Gray Ash would lower the rope at the near end and bring the riders back toward the camp. Captain Rusk would keep his people on the road side of the yard. Benjamin and one representative from each party could witness the start of the work.”

Sarnai’s face had changed very little. Kestra felt the first warning of her mistake in that lack of change.

“We lower the rope,” Sarnai said. “Then you inspect.”

“Then the inspection takes place without the risk of a confrontation.”

“And after that?”

“The iron comes out. The repairs begin.”

“And Arsai?”

Kestra turned to the separate sheet. “The claim for compensation remains open. We haven’t proposed an amount because we don’t yet have agreement on who accepts responsibility for the carrier who fled.”

“So after the rope comes down.”

“It would remain a recorded claim.”

Yelü looked away toward the horses. One of Sarnai’s companions spoke quietly in Ykrul. Sarnai answered without taking her eyes from Kestra.

“Who puts the rope back if they refuse?” she asked.

Kestra looked at the first page. She had written temporary withdrawal, without prejudice to the claims. Tavia would have known how to explain that in a sentence which someone might trust. Kestra could explain the words, but suddenly she could not make them describe the day after a refusal.

“You would retain the right to dispute the settlement.”

“From where?”

Morvain leaned forward. “No one proposes to deprive you of a hearing.”

“I have one now.”

It was not said loudly. People at the next table stopped speaking anyway.

Voss adjusted the position of the grant’s box. “The iron cannot remain where it is. I agree to that. But if we remove it and the carts begin using the ford again, we return to the same damage. We need an enforceable limit on their use.”

“Then ask the carriers for it,” Sarnai said.

Tema looked at Sorian. Sorian looked at the other carriers standing behind him. A woman in a green felt cap shook her head before anyone had asked her a question.

“Some of us have paid for the bridge twice,” she said. “Once to build it and once to pass it. Now we’re to promise never to use the water beside it?”

“Not never,” Kestra said. “Until the approaches are sound.”

“That could be all spring.”

“It could be less if the work starts.”

“With what money?”

The questions moved faster than her pages. Tema named an amount he was prepared to contribute against identified repairs. Voss said it would cover less than the timber. Morvain offered recognition of the full expense for reimbursement if the holders accepted temporary imperial administration. Rusk asked whether that recognition could be spent in the food yard before the month ended.

There was laughter again, but this time it had an edge. Kestra pressed the heel of her hand against the first page to keep it from lifting in the wind. She had included every disagreement. She had not arranged a way through them that anyone wanted to take first.

“Elena has seen the injury at the horse lane,” she said. “She can tell you why waiting will continue to cost people—”

“I can describe Törun’s foot,” Elena said. “I can’t tell him this arrangement makes it less likely another horse will step on him.”

Kestra looked at her. Elena’s face was tired, and neither apologetic nor hostile.

“I need to know what happens before I offer to stand beside it,” she added.

Sarnai rose. “Your healer was allowed into our camp. Your scout was shown the approach. Your workers can come and look at the ground. I will not call those refusals because it makes your first step easier to write.”

Kestra felt the words across her face like heat.

“I didn’t mean—”

“I know what you meant to begin with.”

Sarnai put one hand on the back of the stool. “Speak to the people who set the iron about lifting it. Speak to the carriers about the man who died under their wheels. Then ask what we will do while the work is done.”

She left with her companions. Yelü paused long enough to move the stool clear of the lane before following.

The awning flapped. At the edge of the yard a man arrived with a tray of hot cups, saw the departing party and stopped, uncertain where his payment would come from.

Kestra stayed beside the table while the others separated into smaller arguments. Morvain remained with Voss. Sorian called the carriers together beyond the stage. Tema went to the salt shed with the clerk. Sariel had followed the edge of the dispersing crowd, looking for somebody; Benjamin was speaking to Yelü near a horse which kept swinging its hindquarters toward the road.

“Would you like me at his head?” Benjamin asked.

“No,” Yelü said. “Stand there so the cart doesn’t come closer.”

He stood where she pointed. The horse settled after a while. Yelü loosened one strap and spoke into its mane. When she looked up again, she thanked Benjamin without inviting anything more. He stayed until she was mounted, then came back to the table.

Kestra could not decide which papers to put away first. Their arrangement had seemed obvious in the morning. Now every page looked as though it had been placed on top of the one that mattered.

“We could have stopped the discussion before that,” Benjamin said.

“Before what?”

He hesitated. “Before she had to leave.”

“I didn’t know she had to leave until she was leaving.”

“I know. I’m not accusing you.”

She put the pen in its case too forcefully. Ink marked the rim.

“I’m aware.”

He gave her room. That almost made her cry, which was unreasonable and would have made the afternoon worse.

Elena sat beside her after checking the station. “Senn can manage for a while,” she said. “The sick woman’s kept her last drink.”

Kestra nodded. She wanted that to be enough for a conversation and knew it was not.

“I shouldn’t have asked you to speak for the arrangement.”

“No.”

“I thought if we established the need—”

“They know the need. Törun knows it every time he tries to stand.”

Kestra pressed her thumb against the ink on the pen case. “You don’t have to be kind about it.”

“I’m not. You also don’t have to make me your punishment.”

That made Kestra look up. Elena’s expression softened a little, but she did not withdraw the sentence.

They sat together while the man with the cups decided to sell them to whoever remained. Elena bought two. The drink was bitter and very hot, with a little honey sunk at the bottom. Kestra had to stir with the end of a clean twig because the man had forgotten spoons.

“I put the withdrawal first because it was the only thing that cost no money,” she said at last.

Elena looked toward the camp. “It cost them something.”

“Yes.”

She drank carefully. The sweetness arrived late, after she had stopped expecting it. She did not feel better enough to mistake it for an answer.

Morvain’s clerk brought her the record of the meeting before the light changed. He had written quickly and neatly. The claims were summarized; the proposed sequence appeared beneath them; the objections had been added in a narrower hand. At the bottom was a place for witnesses.

Kestra read it twice.

“This says the parties accepted inspection in principle.”

“They did not object to inspection.”

“Sarnai asked who restored her position if the agreement failed. That is an objection to this inspection.”

“The question is recorded.”

“Under other matters.”

He took the page back and looked at it. “Where would you put it?”

“Before the statement that she accepted.”

The clerk’s face grew carefully blank. She knew the expression from people who believed they were being asked to do a day’s work again because someone else had thought of a word too late.

“I can add that no settlement was concluded,” he said.

“And strike the acceptance.”

“That makes the record almost entirely objections.”

“It was almost entirely objections.”

He looked past her toward the prefect. Morvain was speaking to Rusk and did not turn. After a moment the clerk crossed out the disputed phrase, wrote the correction and sanded it.

Kestra signed as witness to the discussion. She made a copy of the correction in her working book and let him see her do it. Then she returned Voss’s grant to its box and handed the box to the waiting Aeler boy.

“To Voss himself,” she said.

“That’s where it came from.”

“I know. I’d like it to get there.”

The boy took it with both hands. Kestra gathered the failed plan, folded it along the old crease and put it in the pocket where she would have to see it when she reached for fresh paper.

Chapter 10 Four Carts in the Dark

Rusk named his price while Kestra was still arguing over the record of the meeting.

Twelve marks. Four carts, their drivers and the people needed to handle them, between the late watch and dawn. If they had to divide the loads, the empty returns were included. He would keep his people from interfering. He would not carry sacks, repair an axle or explain the arrangement afterward to anyone who had not paid for it.

Sariel listened until he had finished. They stood beside the fork in the lower track, where the roots she had examined on the first day were beginning to dry. A strip of leather still marked the wrong one.

“Tema pays,” she said.

“Someone pays.”

“You’ve accepted Morvain’s advance.”

“For his inspection on the road. This is not the road.”

“He may think differently.”

“He may employ me to think differently tomorrow.”

Rusk watched a man carrying a bucket down toward the river. The man had new boots. Sariel recognized the patched left one from beneath the hedge, and was annoyed to find the recognition softening her view of the captain.

“Do you know whether the bank will take them?” she asked.

“Light loads have gone through.”

“That’s not an answer about ours.”

“No. Ask your carpenter.”

At least he was not selling her certainty. She looked up the track toward the place where it joined the horse lane. The river made a low continual noise, broken now and then by the clack of stones as a horse shifted at the water’s edge. There was a way to finish the job here. She could almost feel the paper being signed in the receiving yard, the rest of their wages counted out, the turn east toward Millhaven.

“Until when can we decide?”

“Before the late watch is assigned. Don’t wake a man to tell him he’s being paid to sleep.”

She laughed despite herself. Rusk did not smile back. He had made his offer and was waiting for a useful response.

“I’ll bring you an answer.”

“Bring Tema if the answer is yes.”

She left him at the fork and took the narrow track slowly enough to look at what she had wanted to overlook the first time. The soft shoulder was drying at its surface. Beneath a crust the mud still moved when she pressed her boot into it. Somebody had laid a plank over the worst place. One end rested on a root, the other on gravel which had rolled down from the upper bank.

A horse could put a foot there. A wheel would ask a different question.

She went back to fetch Thane before the light failed.

He was helping Harn return the borrowed wedges from Elena’s new beds. They had used fewer than he had expected and were arguing over whether that meant the floor was better than it looked or the beds worse.

“Have you time?” Sariel asked.

“For what?”

“The lower track. Rusk’s made an offer.”

Thane put the last wedge in Harn’s bag. “Now?”

“While we can see it.”

He glanced at the sun, then took his measuring stick. Harn asked whether he ought to come.

“Not yet,” Thane said. “We may only be looking at an expensive mud hole.”

“Most of mine are cheap. Tell me what improves it.”

They took Benjamin to help test the ground and Varn because he knew the second cart’s balance. Sariel asked Varn herself. He looked surprised, then uncomfortable, and asked whether Tema had agreed.

“To looking? Yes.”

“Did he tell you to bring me?”

“No.”

Varn put down the harness he was brushing. “I’ll come.”

He did not thank her. She was glad of that. She wanted information from him, not a small reconciliation he could carry away in place of answering for the cargo.

On the track, Thane asked him where the grain lay in relation to the axle when the cart was half loaded. Varn answered readily. He crouched, drew a wheel and two shafts in the dirt, and placed six small stones where he would put the sacks. Thane moved one stone.

“The horse would hate you,” Varn said.

“The axle would like me.”

“The horse has to take it there.”

Thane moved the stone back halfway. “There?”

“Better.”

Sariel watched the two of them arrive at a load neither would have chosen alone. Benjamin stood with his boot beside the drawing, careful not to erase it. Varn’s manner had changed since they left the carts. Asked about the thing he did well, he had stopped looking for the safest answer in somebody else’s face.

“I didn’t know you were going to weigh the empty vehicle,” he said after a while.

Sariel looked at him. “Would you have told us if you had?”

He stared at his drawing, then rubbed it out with his heel.

“I don’t know.”

The answer disappointed her less than a confident lie would have. She led them to the narrowing above the water.

Thane took his time. He put the stick into the shoulder at three points, asked Benjamin to stand where a cart’s near wheel would run, then had him move two paces farther downhill. The crust broke beneath Benjamin’s boot and a small slide of gravel went into the reeds.

“Stay there,” Thane said. “Don’t climb up yet.”

Benjamin stood still while Thane found a place for him to put his next step. There was no drama in it. The water was well below, the slope not steep enough to carry a standing man far. Sariel could nevertheless see the cart going over that edge, one shaft lifting while the horse tried to pull away.

“The plank?” she asked.

“Bridges the soft patch. Its lower end sits on the part that’s moving.”

“So another support.”

“Into what?”

He handed her the stick. She pushed it down beside the plank until the wood met something firm, then pushed harder. The firm thing moved too. A buried branch, perhaps, or a stone resting on more mud.

Varn came down beside them, using the places Thane had marked. He looked at the width, then at the turn onto the horse approach.

“Half load,” he said. “I’d lead him, not drive.”

“Once?” Thane asked.

“If you want the other half, more than once.”

“The first cart may get over it. So may the second. Every wheel works the edge farther loose.”

“Could we put the weight on the upper side?” Sariel asked.

Varn shook his head before Thane answered. “Not on that turn. You make it lean the other way when the wheel comes up.”

Benjamin climbed back onto the track. His boot was black to the ankle. “What happens when one sticks?”

Nobody answered immediately. They were all looking at the same narrow place.

“You unload,” Varn said at last.

“Where?”

Varn pointed uphill, then let his hand fall. There was space for two people beside a cart. A pile of sacks would fill it. To get around the stopped load, anyone following would have to go through the grass beside the spikes or down toward the river.

Sariel thought of the small line of horses she had seen there that morning, their handlers waiting for a clear approach. She had been thinking of the night as a time when nobody else would need the lane. She did not know that. She had never asked when the herds moved or where they waited if passage failed.

“Can you make it safe?” she asked Thane.

“I can tell you it isn’t safe for eight loaded journeys now. To make it safe, we’d need to know more about the bank, and we’d need material. Dorra should look at it.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow.”

The word took the clean escape out of the evening. Sariel could feel herself resisting it, trying to find a different question which would bring back the answer she wanted.

Thane sat on a sound stone and flexed the fingers of his left hand. He had been holding the measuring stick too tightly. His right remained tucked against his coat, protected from the cold wind coming off the river.

“I’m not saying that to keep us here,” he said.

“I know.”

“You look as if you don’t.”

She sat beside him. There was enough room if she kept one foot lower than the other. Benjamin and Varn went back up to look at the turn, arguing quietly about how much room a led horse required.

“I wanted it to be a thing I could do,” she said.

Thane watched the two men above them. “It still might be. It just isn’t the thing Rusk is selling.”

She leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. A loose strand of hair blew across her mouth. She pulled it away and laughed once, without much humor.

“I thought that was the part I understood.”

Thane did not offer consolation. He gave her the measuring stick to carry back, and she took it.

They met beside the third cart because Elena was drying cloth near the first and Kestra had claimed the fourth for a flat writing surface. Nobody had intended this distribution. It was simply what their belongings had done while they were busy.

Tema came when Sariel asked him. She told all of them Rusk’s price, the terms about divided loads and the time by which he wanted an answer. She drew the lower track with Thane’s stick in a patch of dry ground. Benjamin put a pebble where the bank had slipped beneath him.

Kestra looked at the pebble. “That close to the horse lane?”

“Yes.”

“I thought you had found another crossing.”

“I found another way to reach the same crossing.”

Tema bent over the drawing. “It would still take us past the inspection.”

“It might take the first load,” Thane said. “I can’t promise you the last.”

“Nothing on a road comes with a promise.”

“The description of our work did,” Sariel said.

Tema straightened. For a moment she thought he would be angry. Then he looked down at the pebble again.

“How bad?”

Thane explained what he had tested and what he had not. He was precise about the difference. Varn had stayed close enough to hear; Tema called him over and asked whether he would drive it. Varn said he would take a lightly loaded cart in daylight with people to help, but not four carts back and forth until morning without first improving the bank.

“You told me you knew the way,” Tema said.

“I know where it goes.”

“That has been an expensive distinction.”

Varn’s face closed. Sariel had wanted him made uncomfortable in the morning. Now she wanted Tema to stop before the discomfort turned into another answer intended only to please him.

“He looked with us,” she said. “That’s what he saw.”

Tema nodded after a moment. “All right.”

Elena had been quiet. She held one of the small jars she had washed, turning it against her palm to feel whether it was dry.

“If we did get across,” she said, “what would happen to the lane behind us?”

Thane looked at the drawing. “It would need work.”

“And the people who need it before somebody does the work?”

“We’d be on the other side,” Sariel said.

The words made her angry as she spoke them. They were true, and she had known they were true when she first pictured the receiving yard. She wanted the job finished. She wanted the money that remained hers to become a coat, a repaired boot, rent for the loft. She wanted to stop carrying a problem large enough to make any decision look small.

Benjamin sat on the cart’s tail and rested his hands on his knees. “We aren’t taking the bank down for four carts.”

“You make it sound as if that was the plan,” Sariel said.

“I know it wasn’t.”

“Then don’t make it sound like that.”

He looked at her, startled by the force of it. She looked away first.

Kestra touched her sleeve. “I thought my plan would keep everyone safe while we got started.”

Sariel met her eyes. Kestra looked exhausted, with ink in the crease beside one fingernail and a small red mark where her collar had rubbed. She was not asking to be forgiven for the meeting. She was offering Sariel somewhere less lonely to stand with a bad idea.

Sariel put the stick down.

“I don’t think we should take it tonight,” she said.

Tema rubbed his palms together, warming them. “Then I won’t pay him.”

The decision was made more easily aloud than it had been inside her. It did not make her pleased with it.

Mera had cooked lentils with a piece of smoked meat bought from the waiting traders. The pot stood on a low fire, too hot at one side. She asked Benjamin to turn it while she stirred. He turned the handle instead of the whole pot and received an explanation which made Dennet laugh so hard he had to put down his bowl.

Sariel ate without much appetite at first. The lentils were softer than she preferred and very good with the sharp chopped leaves Mera scattered over them. Tema sat a little apart speaking to Varn. Their voices were low. Sariel could not hear whether the driver was being paid, rebuked or asked to do something else. She let herself not find out immediately.

Across the fire, Thane used a piece of bread to clean the last sauce from his bowl. His spirits had improved since the inspection. The ground had been bad, but he had been able to say why; he was always easier in himself when something had ceased to be a vague possibility and become a problem with a shape.

“What would go over it?” Elena asked him.

He looked up. “The track?”

“The water. Without a cart.”

“People. Horses. I haven’t tested where they put their feet.”

“Yelü knows that,” Sariel said. “Or someone in the camp does.”

Tema stopped speaking to Varn.

“How much on a horse?” he asked.

“Depends on the horse,” Iset said from the other side of the pot. “And how you mean to put it there.”

“Pack loads.”

“Then someone who knows packing should look at the sacks. You don’t put a rider’s weight on one side and hope the animal is grateful.”

Thane set down his bowl. “You’d still need a sound approach, but the weight comes down differently. We wouldn’t be asking a wheel to hold the edge while the other wheel stands higher.”

Sariel looked at the little drawing still visible beside the cart. “The narrow place would take a led horse.”

“In daylight,” Thane said. “After it’s checked.”

“I’m not trying to send one now.”

“I know. I’m trying to get the conditions spoken before you become pleased with me.”

She laughed. He smiled back, and some of the frustration she had been holding since the meeting loosened enough to let her taste the food.

Sorian, who had come to ask Tema a question, stood listening with his hand on the cart side. “How many sacks?”

“We don’t know,” Kestra said.

“A few would matter to the lower buyers.”

“Which buyers?” Elena asked.

He gave her a look. “The ones who are ready to sow.”

“That isn’t a name.”

“No. We’ll need names.”

Kestra fetched a clean page, then stopped with it in her hand. She looked at the meal, at the people still eating, and put the page under her bowl until afterward.

They talked while the light went. Who could lend pack gear; whether the sacks needed dividing; who could accept delivery on the west bank before the carts arrived; whether Sarnai would permit grain to use a passage currently closed by an unresolved grievance. That last question slowed them. Sariel was glad it did. She could see the white rope from where she sat, and knew they would have to speak at its opening rather than arrive with another completed plan.

“Ask to look first,” Benjamin said.

Thane nodded. “That’s all I can do yet.”

When the bowls were gathered, Sariel went to the lower fork and told Rusk they were not crossing that night. He asked whether the price was the trouble.

“The ground.”

“Usually more expensive to change.”

“Yes.”

He accepted the refusal without pretending he approved of it. One of his men was already settling beneath the hedge with a folded cloak. Rusk told him he would be on the early watch instead. The man objected, then asked whether there would be hot food before it began.

Sariel left them arranging the practical consequences of her answer. Back at the carts, Benjamin had kept a place for her beside the fire. She sat, put the measuring stick where Thane could find it in the morning, and asked Kestra for the names of the people they needed permission from.

Chapter 11 Seed for the Lower Fields

The milk had turned in the night. Elena discovered it after she had poured a little into her cup, and spent several annoyed moments deciding whether she could bear to drink it anyway. Senn watched her from the far end of the shed.

“There’s another pot.”

“That was mine.”

“It will probably remain yours.”

Elena took the cup outside. A dog came to investigate, considered what she tipped onto the ground, and went away. She found the rejection more offensive than it deserved.

The morning was bright. The river had a different sound under clear weather, less of a continuous rushing and more of water striking particular things. From the treatment shed she could see the weighing stage above the bank, where an Aeler worker was taking a hammer to a peg. Beyond him the bridge stood empty. The absence of people made its curve seem steeper.

Senn brought out a second cup.

“I put some water in.”

“I can taste that.”

“Good. Otherwise it would have been wasted.”

Elena smiled and sat on the step. She had slept badly, woken by a quarrel between drivers and then by her own conviction that someone had called from the shed. Nobody had. The older woman with the stomach illness had gone home with her son the previous afternoon. The three raised beds were empty except for a blanket and a child whose mother had needed both hands while repairing a basket. The child was now sitting up, industriously pulling a loose thread from the blanket.

“That one belongs to the owner,” Elena said.

The child examined the thread, then carefully pushed it under the blanket.

Senn leaned against the wall. She had washed her hair, which lay in dark damp ropes against her collar. Without the scarf she looked younger, though she was older than Elena had first thought. Her sleeves were rolled to the elbow. A pale scar ran across one wrist, old enough for the skin to have lost its shine.

“Did your meeting go badly?” she asked.

“It ended early.”

“I’ve been to some very good meetings that did that.”

“Nobody stayed for the food.”

“Ah.”

Elena looked toward the road. Kestra was crossing the yard with her notebook under her arm and a piece of bread in her other hand. She had plainly forgotten the bread was there. Sariel caught up with her, said something, and took a bite from it before Kestra could object. The two continued toward Tema’s carts, arguing. Benjamin followed carrying a rolled length of rope which kept slipping apart.

“They’re going to look at the horse crossing,” Elena said. “There may be a way to get a little grain through.”

“Would that open the bridge?”

“No.”

Senn nodded. “My aunt’s flour will last three days. She asked me whether you had heard anything useful.”

“The cargo is seed.”

“I know. She wants to know whether to borrow flour or buy it. If the bridge opens, buying might be cheaper.”

Elena rubbed her forehead. She had been ready to explain why eating the seed would be a mistake. Senn had not needed that explanation. Her aunt was trying to decide how much of next week’s money to spend this morning.

“I’ll find out what Sorian thinks. I can’t promise he’ll know.”

“She’ll take a guess from someone who has seen the road.”

The child held up a little gray bird made from the loose thread. It was a surprisingly recognizable bird. Elena admired it before making him bring back the blanket.

Törun had placed his foot on a sack of horsehair and was pretending the sack was there for some other purpose. He sat in the open side of the camp wagon with a leather strap across his lap. Yelü was holding its other end while he showed her how to pare a stiff place without making it thin.

He saw Elena and pushed the knife into its case.

“I haven’t walked on it.”

“Good morning to you too.”

“She would tell you if I had.”

Yelü kept looking at the strap. “I would make him tell you. It’s more work.”

Elena washed her hands from the water he had set aside and loosened the covering. The swelling had lessened around the ankle, though the top of his right foot remained discolored. There was heat, but less than she had feared. She asked about the night, the ache and the feeling in his toes. He answered each question seriously, then asked when he could ride.

“I don’t know yet.”

“When will you know?”

“After I’ve seen how this changes.”

“It changes very slowly.”

“Yes.”

He glanced at Yelü. “She agrees with you. You might try agreeing with her about something else.”

The girl laid down the strap and brought a bowl for the used cloth. She did not smile until her back was turned.

Elena checked the place where the skin had broken and asked him to show her how he was reaching the ground when he had to move. He demonstrated with an annoyance she understood. He was careful. That mattered more than whether he enjoyed being asked.

“They’re considering a few packhorses,” she said. “To get seed over before the repairs are done.”

Törun rested his head against the wagon frame. “Of course they are.”

“What does that mean?”

“That I should have hurt the other foot. I could have been useless on the other side of the river and not had to watch.”

“You might tell them what to look for.”

“They know what to look for. They don’t always look.”

Yelü took the bowl outside. Elena began putting clean cloth around the foot, keeping the pressure even. Törun watched her fingers. He had good hands himself, broad and scarred, with a careful way of catching an end of thread without looking directly at it.

“The gray mare,” he said. “She’ll stand in water until the hill comes down, but if a strap knocks beneath her belly she starts thinking she has been saddled by fools. Use the bay if they’re lending them.”

“Tell Yelü.”

“I have.”

“Then tell her again while I’m not here. She may prefer it that way.”

He made a sound which became a laugh. She finished the binding and showed him where she wanted the edge kept loose.

Before leaving, Elena asked whether he wanted Senn to visit later so someone nearby knew the dressing. He considered it, asked whether she had worked with injured feet before, and agreed when Elena explained what she had done. He wanted to be awake when she came. He wanted his own cloth kept separate from the shed’s supply. He wanted someone to ask before shifting the sack beneath his leg.

She repeated the requests until he was satisfied. Then she took the bowl outside and found Yelü waiting with a small wrapped piece of cheese.

“For your breakfast,” the girl said.

“I’ve had breakfast.”

“You look as if it was poor.”

Elena accepted it. The cheese was hard enough to require work and tasted sharply of smoke. She ate it on the walk back, carrying the used cloth in its own covered pail.

Sorian had arranged the inspection beneath the only sound awning in the waiting yard. A table and two scales stood there, with three people telling him that he had promised to meet them somewhere else. He assured each of them that he would be delighted to resume that arrangement at the receiving yard across the river as soon as someone allowed the grain to reach it.

“He says I can wait.”

“I have become excellent at it. That’s not the question.”

“I dislike the look of the waiting,” Sorian said. “The grain can speak for itself.”

“Does that leave a hole?” she asked.

Tema held up a needle and thread. “Some of us considered that.”

Chapter 12 The Marked Approach

Kestra’s first line was too straight.

She had drawn it from the place where the east-bank track reached the water to the forked willow on the western landing. Thane looked at the page, then at the river, and put his left forefinger on the middle of the line.

“Then put an arrow on it so nobody mistakes it for work.”

He was enjoying himself. She could tell by the care with which he kept his face still. Benjamin, standing on the other side of the table, failed to help either of them by laughing.

Kestra drew an arrow, rather more sharply than she needed to. “There. Shall we go and do the work?”

They took poles, a knotted measuring line, chalk, a small mallet and an unmarked staff Dorra had allowed them to cut. She had made them pay for the timber from the repair provision rather than let it vanish among the larger needs of the bridge. Kestra respected that until they had to find the payment, when it became mildly irritating.

At the camp opening Yelü was waiting with two horses and an older woman who held the poles as if she would have preferred everybody to arrive knowing where to put their hands. She wore a felt cap pulled low and a dark strip of cloth over one ear against the wind. Yelü said she knew the spring bar better than anyone present.

“That includes you,” the woman told her.

“I said everyone.”

“You sometimes mean other people.”

Sarnai held the broken strap and listened until he had run out of variations on the same complaint, then asked him how long it had been cracked. He looked down at it.

Kestra tried not to enjoy the answer too visibly.

“You want to cross.”

“We want to test whether six light loads could cross,” Kestra said. “With your permission and handlers who know the water. Nothing is moving today except us and the trial weight.”

“That is different from what you wanted yesterday.”

“Yes.”

“If the approach is damaged by your test?” Sarnai asked.

“We stop,” Thane said. “We don’t call the damage proof that it needs widening.”

She looked at him, then at the mallet. “Are you building something?”

“A mark to show when the water is too high.”

“The river can be too high before it reaches a mark.”

“Yes. The mark is one reason to stop, not all of them.”

The older woman gave a short nod. Sarnai stepped aside, allowing Yelü to lead the first horse through the opening.

“Do not open the lane for anyone behind you,” she said. “They will see you cross and say it has been settled.”

Kestra turned to Benjamin. He was already looking toward the waiting yard, where several people had noticed the poles. He went to tell them what was happening before a procession could form.

The older woman took them to a starting stone farther north than Kestra had expected. It stood above a gravel slope, broad enough for one horse and a handler beside it. From there the shallow bar could be seen beneath the water as a pale curve, broken in three places where darker channels crossed it.

“The willow is where you finish,” Yelü said. “It isn’t where you point your feet.”

Kestra put her notebook in the oilskin wrapper and left it beneath a flat stone on the bank. She had considered carrying it inside her coat. The river was persuasive about the disadvantages.

They crossed first without a horse. The older woman went ahead with her pole, testing each darker place before shifting her weight. Benjamin followed carrying the line loose in his hands; it was for measurement, not a tether. Thane remained on the eastern slope with Yelü and the horses, watching where the water changed around the stones.

Kestra rolled her trousers above her knees and stepped into the first run. The cold seized her shins so sharply that she stopped breathing for a moment. A little water came over her boot tops before she found the next foothold.

“Keep moving when she moves,” Benjamin said.

“I am moving.”

“Yes. You’re doing well.”

That was worse. She followed the woman and made herself look at the pole, at the pressure of its end against the bottom, rather than at the broad moving surface beyond it.

The canal had been dark. She could still remember putting a foot down and failing to find the place where a foot ought to go. This water was clear enough to show the stones. The difference should have helped more than it did.

At the first bend the current pressed across her legs. The older woman stopped, reached back, and offered the thick end of her pole. Kestra took it. The woman did not pull. She let Kestra feel something steady while choosing her own step.

They reached a patch of gravel that rose almost above the water. Kestra stood there while Benjamin unwound another length of line. On the eastern bank Thane lifted one hand and moved it slightly north. The woman looked where he indicated, tested with her pole, and chose that line through the next shallow channel.

The crossing took longer than its width suggested. At the western landing Kestra sat on a stone, took off one boot and poured water out of it. Benjamin turned away to give her space, though there was no reason she could not wring a stocking while being seen.

“I can manage,” she said.

“I know.” He faced her again. “Would you prefer I watched?”

“No.”

He grinned. She threw a little water at him, most of which fell well short.

The measuring line gave them the length of each change of direction. Kestra walked the route back with the older woman, counting ordinary paces where footing allowed and checking against the line where it did not. From starting stone to willow landing, keeping to the safe curve, it was ninety-two. She wrote the number after drying her hands.

Thane examined the line. “Within yours?”

“Yes. The marked section. People would still need guides to get here and away at the other end.”

“Good. I’d rather not ask a light in the water whether it knows the road to St. Arilan.”

They tried the first horse with empty panniers. The older woman led; Yelü walked beside its head until the first turn, then let it follow. It went reluctantly but without panic, placing its feet where the woman had already tested. Its breathing sounded loud above the water.

On the return they put stones in the panniers, balanced to the weight of one grain sack. The sacks themselves remained on Rada’s cart. Tema had been very clear about that. The horse crossed again, slower at the second dark channel. At the west landing it shook hard enough to spatter everyone within reach.

Yelü came back smiling. She tried to remove the smile before Sarnai’s messenger, who had come to watch, could notice it. She failed. Kestra was unexpectedly glad.

They tested the next line with a longer pole, found soft ground beside the eastern bend, and shifted the entry uphill by the width of a person. That took away the simplest approach for a cart. Thane drove the depth staff beside the starting stone, where its base would remain fixed against a lodged rock. He cut a notch at the agreed stopping height and tied a strip of blue cord into it so it could be seen from the track.

“If the water touches that, nothing goes,” he said.

The older woman put her pole beside the staff. “If the bottom moves, nothing goes before that.”

“Yes.”

Yelü repeated both instructions to the two people who would help her with the packhorses. They asked their own questions. Kestra liked the sound of the discussion: what to do with a frightened horse, where a handler could step aside, who would tell the east bank to stop sending another animal.

By the time Kestra began preparing the Waymark, the sun had moved behind a narrow cloud. Her boots remained wet. She wanted a warm cup and a place to sit, and was irritated by how much those modest wants intruded on the thing she had been eager to try.

She walked the route again. Chalk would hold on the dry upper faces of three stones at the changes of line; the lowest mark would have to be checked after every passage if the water rose. At the beginning she made the mark where a traveler would enter, not where a spectator might stand to admire it. The end lay above the wet gravel beneath the forked willow. She took her time with the turns. The pattern had to describe the route they had actually tested, including the awkward bend around the soft ground.

Benjamin carried the sandglass in both hands, keeping it level.

“You look as though it’s an infant,” she said.

“I’ve never been asked to turn an infant upside down to tell the time.”

She laughed and lost the place in her own sequence. He waited without speaking while she began that part again.

The preparation took a little more than ten minutes. She checked the bronze disc beneath her collar, untwisted the blue wool so it lay flat against her skin, and stood at the starting stone. The marks were intact. No one was in the water. She had walked the whole way herself.

For the minute of invocation she kept her eyes on the first turn. Mareth had made her practice the words when she was tired, annoyed and distracted, then refused to tell her which condition they considered most common. She had thought that unfair. Now she could remember the cadence even while her toes ached.

The light came quietly. A pale seam lay over the moving water, sometimes visible only where it crossed a darker stone. It turned with the tested line. Nothing about the current changed.

Yelü stepped to the starting mark and went still.

“You see it?” Kestra asked.

“I see where it wants me to go.”

“It doesn’t want. It’s the route we made.”

Yelü gave her a sideways look. “Then I see where you want me to go.”

That was true enough to be uncomfortable. Kestra nodded and handed her the pole.

Yelü crossed on foot with the older woman. They followed the seam, testing the dark places as before. At the western landing the girl turned and raised her hand. Benjamin watched the glass. Kestra waited until she could no longer see the pale turn by the second stone.

“Gone,” she said.

“Nearly all the sand,” he told her.

Yelü came back along the physical markers with no magic to help her. She was breathing hard and laughing at something the older woman had said. The woman’s felt cap had been drenched by the horse on the last crossing, and her covered ear now hung at a different angle from the other.

“If I brought somebody you disliked,” Yelü asked, “would it show them?”

“If they entered at the mark and followed willingly. I can’t choose who sees it.”

“Good.”

Kestra looked at her.

“My mother will want to know whether the horses’ owners need your approval.”

“They don’t.”

“I’ll tell her that part first.”

They returned by the bridge with Dorra’s permission, carrying the wet line and the stones used for the trial. The narrow foot gate admitted one person at a time. Kestra had changed into dry stockings but still felt the water in her bones. Her skin was warm now, her hands slightly unsteady with the work and the lack of food since morning.

Dorra took the sketch. This time the line bent in three places, and Thane did not improve it.

“Ninety-two paces,” Kestra said. “For the part I can mark. Six horses, each carrying one sack. We haven’t asked for any grain to move until the other terms are agreed.”

“And the water?”

Thane showed her the notch on his own measuring stick and explained the stop condition. Dorra asked where the staff had been driven, who would inspect the bed and which people were being paid to lead. Kestra answered what she knew and left two spaces in the notebook for what they still needed to settle.

Harn brought a mug from the tollhouse kitchen. It held hot water darkened with a bitter leaf and sweetened more generously than she expected. She thanked him and drank before asking what it was.

“What we give people who turn gray and tell us they’re all right.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You were getting ready.”

She smiled into the mug. From the western parapet she could see the east waiting yard and Tema standing beside the first cart. Sorian was pointing to six marks on a sheet. For once they appeared to be arguing about something that could happen.

On the walk back, Kestra stopped at the narrow eastern arch. From this angle its recess looked deeper than it had in the morning. A small square opening stood above the waterline, perhaps where a timber had once rested. She had noted it while counting the spans. Now she could not find the opening in the corresponding place on the inner face.

She leaned closer. The river below the recess lay strangely smooth. Elsewhere little sticks spun past the stones; here one leaf rested with its pale underside upward, turning only a fraction while she watched.

Behind her, Benjamin said her name.

The sound seemed to come from the recess first. She turned sharply enough to spill the last of the hot drink onto her sleeve.

He was standing where he ought to be, two paces behind her, holding the wet rope away from his coat.

“Dorra wants the mug.”

Kestra looked back once. The leaf had gone.

She brought the mug to the tollhouse door and returned to the east bank. That evening she copied the surveyed route onto a clean sheet. She put the depth limit beside it, the names of the people who had tested it and the three changes of direction. She did not add the little opening beneath the bridge. It belonged to a different inquiry, one for which nobody had asked her to risk a horse.

Thane came to inspect the copy. He turned it toward the fire, examined the first bend and nodded.

“That one is a survey,” he said.

She flicked his ear with the dry end of the pen. He laughed and stayed to help her make the lettering large enough for a wet person to read.

Chapter 13 The Nephew’s Household

Oyun had spread a horse blanket over two boxes and was cutting a damaged corner from it. She held the cloth down with her bare foot while she worked. Her son sat beside the wagon step with his wooden horse between his knees, explaining to it why its missing leg was inconvenient.

Sariel stopped where the shade began. Sarnai had brought her through the camp without announcing that a discussion was about to occur, and now seemed prepared to leave her there.

“She has the carriers’ offer,” Sarnai said.

Oyun glanced at the folded sheet in Sariel’s hand. “Does it need cutting down too?”

“Probably.”

The young woman smiled briefly. She finished the cut, laid her knife beyond the child’s reach and stood. There was a pale scratch on her wrist, almost healed. Sariel remembered Elena mentioning it as the sort of thing one noticed when trying not to stare at a widow.

“Sit.” Oyun indicated the step. “Not there. That’s where the water comes from the roof.”

Sariel sat at the dry end. The boy lifted his horse away from her boots and studied the knife at her belt. She moved its sheath behind her hip. He looked disappointed but made no attempt to negotiate.

Sarnai stood for a moment listening to something farther along the wagons. A man called her name. She held up a hand, waited until Oyun nodded, then went to answer him.

Sariel had expected the khatun to remain and make the right terms apparent. Without her, the folded sheet felt prematurely official.

“Shall I read it?”

“Can you tell me what they mean first?”

“Money from the carriers using the crossing. Paid directly to your household. They want your acceptance to let the trade begin again while the other arrangements are completed.”

“Is the money collected?”

“Some of it. The remainder is promised before the first grain crosses.”

Oyun picked a thread from the damaged blanket. “Everything happens before the grain crosses, except the things they want us to do first.”

Sariel unfolded the paper. “That was our mistake at the meeting.”

“Was it yours?”

“One of ours.”

“You may say whose. I won’t have her beaten.”

“Kestra wrote the proposal. We let her take it into the room.”

Oyun looked up. “She came and told my aunt where she had been wrong. I liked that better than the proposal.”

Sariel had not known. She looked down at the sheet again, careful not to turn the discovery into a reason to defend Kestra now.

There were words about acknowledgment, injury, passage and the proper settlement of a claim. The carriers had used a phrase near the end which required the payment to be accepted in full satisfaction. Sariel read it aloud.

Oyun’s hand stopped moving.

“They can change that.”

“They want to know the same demand won’t be made when they return.”

“Then say which demand. I won’t be satisfied.”

The boy had begun making the horse walk on its three remaining legs. Each step required him to hold its front end higher. He kept trying different arrangements, as if one might restore the missing piece by balance alone.

Sariel set the paper on her knee. “Do you want the driver found?”

“Yes.”

“I don’t know how to do that.”

“Neither do I. He didn’t stop to introduce himself.”

She spoke without raising her voice. Sariel could hear a pot lid being tapped against its rim behind the next wagon, somebody knocking dust out of bedding, a horse pulling at a rope. It was difficult to fit the account she had been carrying into this busy place. Arsai had died here and these people still had washing to finish.

“Were you there?” Sariel asked.

Oyun looked at the child. “He was inside. I had gone to take a bowl to my husband’s mother. When I heard the shouting I thought a horse was loose.”

She reached for the blanket, then left it.

“The cart had gone over him. That part was finished. The other carts had room to stop. They didn’t.”

Sariel waited.

“A man called that they would bring help from the next house. They were taking wool. I could see it through the boards. One bale had white wool pressed out of the cloth like the inside of a split seed.”

She rubbed the scratch on her wrist.

“When the first cart stopped at the bend I thought they had changed their minds. A wheel was caught. They got it free and kept going.”

Sariel tried to imagine asking her which part of that a payment could satisfy. She folded the bottom of the paper so the offending words disappeared beneath the rest.

“I can say they acknowledge he was struck, and that the train left him. I can say accepting money doesn’t mean anyone has identified the driver or forgiven him.”

“Can you say it, or will they?”

“I’ll ask them.”

Oyun nodded. “Ask that first.”

They moved the boxes into the sun because the blanket needed drying before it could be patched. Sariel helped hold one edge. The child carried a much smaller box with loud instructions for the adults about where it belonged.

Oyun mended saddlecloths and wagon covers. Arsai had handled horses and taken work with households who needed an extra pair of hands during movement. Their money had not arrived evenly. In winter he had done jobs for two carriers at the Ford, both of whom had been willing to confirm his skill while maintaining that the man who killed him had been driving for somebody else.

“They may have been,” Oyun said. “I don’t know all their marks. I know which of them still ask whether I’ll repair a roof before they go.”

“Do you?”

“Repair it? Yes. They pay.”

She seemed to find Sariel’s question more bewildering than insulting. Sariel thought of the repaired locks she had sold to people whose voices she would cross a street to avoid. She let the matter go.

The household owned two animals. One was a mare with a good back and a tendency to dislike every load except the one she had just put down. The other had been lamed by an illness during winter and could still carry a light pack, but needed grazing that would not require long movement each morning. Oyun showed Sariel the remaining fodder beneath the wagon.

“The river meadow,” she said. “The one below the road, on the west side. Its owner lets cattle in after the first cut. If we could use part of it through summer, I could work here and keep them fed. The money would last longer.”

Sariel knew the meadow. She had looked down on it from the bridge and thought how easily a person could disappear among its willows. She had not considered who cut the hay.

“Would its owner agree?”

“For money. He agrees to many things for money.”

“The carriers may prefer to give you the sum and let you arrange it.”

“The meadow’s owner would prefer to charge me more because I have few choices. Let people who tell me they know the prices go and ask.”

Sariel smiled. “I can carry that request.”

“You can improve the manner of it. Keep the meaning.”

They discussed the use of the meadow, how much of it would be needed and where the animals could be brought in without crossing a standing crop. Oyun knew the gate and the ditch. She knew the name of the woman who actually managed the grazing, which was not the name her husband used when describing his property. Sariel wrote that name in the margin of the offer.

Then Oyun asked who had asked the carriers to pay.

“Sarnai.”

“And who is paying because they fear her?”

Sariel looked at her.

“It will matter when she moves on,” Oyun said. “If a man agrees because my aunt is in front of him with thirty riders, I want the money before she leaves.”

“So do I.”

“Then we agree about something easy.”

Sarnai returned while they were discussing the order in which payment, acknowledgment and removal of the iron would happen. She heard the request for the meadow without interrupting. When Oyun finished, Sarnai said she would have asked for another mare.

“I would have to feed it.”

“It could work.”

“So can I, if I don’t spend the morning searching for grass.”

Sarnai studied her for a moment, then nodded. “Ask for the meadow.”

Sariel folded the sheet with the new notes inside. The boy held his horse up to her. She admired it and asked whether its leg had been found.

“Yes,” he said.

“Where is it?”

He looked at his mother.

Oyun said, “Somewhere he considers safer than the horse.”

Sariel laughed. The child laughed too, pleased with the success of his arrangement.

Tema was not pleased about the meadow. He was less pleased when Sariel told him which woman to ask.

“I know who owns it.”

“Then you’ll have a shorter walk.”

He stared at her. She had intended that to be helpful and heard only after speaking how much it sounded like Hest when an apprentice had declared a job beneath her.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “That wasn’t necessary.”

“No. It wasn’t.”

He read the folded sheet. Sorian leaned over his shoulder, close enough to be irritating, and asked about the grazing gate. Tema moved the paper so they could both see it.

“We can ask,” Sorian said. “If the owner has cattle promised, there may be no room.”

“Oyun knows the gate by the willows. Only that side.”

Sorian rubbed his broken nose. “That’s not much land.”

“It’s enough for her two.”

Tema looked again at the crossed-out phrase. “This is harder.”

“It’s what she needs them to say.”

“I wasn’t there.”

“You aren’t being asked to say you saw it.”

“There will be men who hear an acknowledgment as an admission that any carrier can be charged for another’s crime.”

Sariel sat on the edge of a cart. She was tired of people being right in ways which left nothing possible.

“Then speak about the train. The train struck him and left him. Several people saw the bales. You can say the carriers using the Ford are paying to settle this demand for passage. You can also say it doesn’t give the driver a clean name if someone finds him.”

“You make that sound simple.”

“It took most of the morning.”

Sorian laughed once. Tema did not, but his expression eased a little.

They took the altered wording to two other carriers. One agreed quickly and complained more about the cost of the paper than the proposed acknowledgment. The other asked whether a witness could identify the mark on the wool bales. Sariel answered that the visible mark had been incomplete. He made her draw the part Oyun remembered, then spent a long time looking at it.

“I don’t know it,” he said at last.

She had not accused him. He seemed to need her to hear the denial anyway.

The meadow took less time. Its manager came to the western gate, listened to Sorian, and named a price which made Tema turn away briefly to look at the river. They agreed on a smaller enclosed portion, access after the first hay cut, and a supply of cut grass until then. Payment would be made to the woman managing it. She required Oyun to inspect the boundary herself before any money changed hands.

“She knows what she wants,” Sorian said.

“Then she’ll know if it isn’t this.”

Sariel liked her. Later Oyun went across the foot passage with the meadow manager and walked the proposed boundary. Sariel waited at the western gate while they settled which water gap would be kept open and who would cut the first grass. Oyun came back with burrs on her skirt and one change to the written terms. She would accept that piece of meadow if its near gate could be mended before her animals used it. The manager agreed, provided the carriers supplied the hinge.

They carried the terms back across the foot passage. At the eastern gate Dorra was waiting for Harn to finish moving a stack of boards. Sariel told her that the acknowledgment was nearly agreed.

“Then we can start pulling the iron?”

“When the payment is ready and they’ve seen the written terms.”

“I can have the crew there before noon tomorrow.”

“Sarnai will want to know the lane stays clear.”

“Then help me keep carts out of it. Iron was a poor answer. A gap people can widen whenever they are impatient will be a poor answer too.”

Sariel looked at the wide shoulders of the road, already cut by wheels. “A post on either side. Wide enough for a loaded horse. Too close for a cart.”

“With the gate above it opened for work only,” Dorra said. “And someone paid to say no.”

“I’ll tell them.”

“Tell them the posts can be lifted for a genuine need. Not every late delivery is one.”

They met at Oyun’s wagon before the light went. Tema and Sorian brought the offer, the meadow manager’s mark and the first collected money. They did not make a mound of it on the ground. Tema placed the small purse on the step and left his hand beside it until Oyun had read the amount with Sarnai.

Sariel read the terms aloud. She had asked Kestra to copy them clearly, with each promised thing given its own line. The copied page lay in her hands; Tema retained the carriers’ counterpart. Sarnai held the grazing agreement while Oyun asked two questions about the date the hay would be cut.

Then Tema read the acknowledgment himself.

He stumbled once over Arsai’s name. Sarnai corrected him. He said it again, properly, and continued. The train had struck Arsai at the crossing and had left without aid. The present carriers accepted a stated payment and grazing provision to settle the household’s demand against their passage. It did not identify or absolve the driver. The same demand would not be raised against those signatories after they had performed it. No promise was made on behalf of a man nobody could find.

Oyun listened with one hand on the boy’s shoulder. He had grown quiet because the adults were quiet, and was looking up at her face.

“That will be said where people hear it?” she asked.

“At the east approach tomorrow,” Tema said. “Before the work begins.”

“And the rest of the money?”

“Before the first load goes. You can count it with Sarnai’s witness and mine.”

She looked at the purse, then at the paper. Sariel had imagined a visible release when the terms were accepted. Instead Oyun asked for a place to put her mark, pressed her thumb where Kestra had left room, and told Tema to move the purse away from the child’s feet.

When they stood to leave, she caught Sariel’s sleeve.

“He could mend a harness while the horse wore it,” she said. “Arsai. He said taking it off gave the animal time to form an opinion.”

Sariel smiled because Oyun was smiling.

“Was he right?”

“Often enough to become unpleasant about it.”

She let go. Sariel walked back through the camp carrying the carriers’ signed counterpart for Tema, who had stayed to discuss the order of the morning. She handed it to him at the white stone when he caught up. He put it into the inner pocket where his cargo list had been, with the agreement kept separately inside its own fold.

At the carts Benjamin was trying to take a splinter out of his thumb. Sariel sat beside him, took the needle and asked him to hold still. She did not tell him what the afternoon had meant. When he asked how it had gone, she said the iron could come out tomorrow.

“Good,” he said, and gave a small involuntary flinch as the splinter came free.

“You could have warned me.”

“I asked you to hold still.”

“That sounds like a different service.”

She wiped the needle and gave it back. He held his thumb toward the fire, admired the absence of the splinter, and made room for her to stay.

Chapter 14 A Meal Interrupted

The first iron blade came out with a lump of earth attached to its lower end. Harn held it upright while Dorra scraped the mud away, looking for the place where it had bent. The people watching made no sound. Elena had expected applause from somebody, or a shouted insult now that the thing could be held harmlessly in one hand. Instead a man behind her said he had thought it would be longer.

“Most of it was in the timber,” Törun told him.

He sat on a wagon bench brought close enough for him to see without standing. Elena had helped him get there after he asked, and then regretted not choosing a position farther from the dust. He had tucked the covering around his right foot himself. Every time she looked down to check it, he moved his heel a little to prove he remembered.

Tema’s acknowledgment had been read at the eastern approach before the workers began. He had said Arsai’s name correctly. Two other carriers had stood with him, and Oyun had listened beside Sarnai with her son on her hip. The last of the cash was being counted now in the east weighing shed, with witnesses from the household and the carriers. The grazing payment would go to the woman who had agreed the meadow, with its boundary described separately.

Nobody had asked Elena to witness the words. She was glad of that. She could look at Oyun without wondering which opinion her presence was being used to support.

Dorra laid the blade in a barrow and went to the next one. The first had been the easiest. This one came out only after Harn and another worker levered the timber loose enough to expose its fastening. Thane stood to one side watching the movement of the beam.

“Stop a moment,” he said.

Harn stopped. The other worker did not hear and pushed harder until Dorra tapped the lever with her rod.

Thane pointed to the end nearest the slope. “There’s a peg beneath it. If you lift there first, the timber twists against it.”

“We put it in,” Dorra said. “I remember.”

“Then you remember why it’s taking so much strength.”

She looked at him, then squatted to find the peg. When it came free, the timber moved easily. Harn grunted and set down the lever.

“I was warming up,” he said.

“You’ll have all morning,” Dorra told him.

Yelü was leading horses along the upper lane, keeping them away from the noise. The woman who had tested the bar with Kestra helped her set two posts where the new horse-width opening would be. The posts would keep carts from widening the track without trapping a horse against hidden iron. A broad rail above could be lifted when the maintenance crew needed access. The same people who had demanded removal stood deciding how to keep the replacement from becoming another obstruction.

Elena watched a rider dismount to help dig. His horse nuzzled the back of his coat until he moved a pouch out of reach. He passed the pouch to Yelü, who looked inside and took something before returning it. He protested. She pointed to the labor he had asked her to perform. Elena could not hear all the words, but the argument ended with both of them eating.

Beside her, Törun shifted against the bench.

“Do you want to go back?” she asked.

“I want to tell them they’ve put the lower post where a frightened mare will rub her load.”

Elena looked at the post, then at the man who could see its position from the bench.

“Tell them.”

He called to Yelü. She came over, followed the line of his finger, and went back to the hole. The post moved a little farther uphill. Törun settled again without requiring Elena to admire the change.

Senn had opened the shed’s high window and wedged it with a piece of wood. The room smelled of washed cloth and the sun warming old boards. On the nearest raised bed a young driver was holding a folded pad against his palm while insisting that he had not struck the iron with his bare hand.

“Something struck it,” Senn said.

“The hammer slipped.”

“Then you can blame the hammer while you hold that.”

Elena examined him. The cut was shallow. He was more frightened by the amount it had bled than by the pain, and became embarrassed when she asked him to say so. She gave him a task with his other hand—hold the clean strip where she could reach it—and explained what he would need to keep dry.

He wanted to go back to the crew immediately.

“You can carry light things,” she said. “Don’t keep taking this off to see whether it’s better.”

“How will I know?”

“You’ll be able to think about something else.”

When he had gone, Senn held up the pad he had been using. “Wash or discard?”

“Wash first. We’ll see what remains.”

“My aunt would have answered before I asked.”

“Your aunt knows more about cloth than I do.”

Senn looked pleased. Elena had not meant the remark as praise and was glad it had worked that way.

They set the used cloth in its covered pail. Elena checked the medicine reserve, wrote what had actually been used, and put the paper beneath the small box holding money for replacements. The owner of the shed had supplied more straw. She owed him for that, separately from the space. It would be easy to let all these small payments dissolve into people’s willingness to help. She had promised not to do that, and wanted the room with shelves enough to resent each extra line she had to write before she could stop working.

Senn saw her rubbing her wrist.

“Go and eat.”

“There’ll be another person as soon as I do.”

“Then I’ll ask the woman sitting with us to stay until you get back. She can send for you if I don’t know what to do.”

Elena looked toward the doorway. The experienced woman from the yard was trimming a child’s nails with a small knife, absorbed in the work. The child was explaining why one finger required special treatment. Nobody in the room was waiting to be abandoned.

“Where will you be?” Senn asked.

“At the bridge crew’s meal.”

“Then there are worse places to be sent for.”

Elena washed her hands again, changed the stained cloth at her waist and went.

They had set the food out behind the weighing stage, far enough from the wagon lane to leave room for people to pass. Someone had borrowed two long boards and made a table whose middle sagged slightly when a pot was placed there. Thane moved the pot six inches toward a support. Dorra moved it back because it was then too far from the people meant to eat from it. They settled on moving the support.

Elena sat between Rada and an Aeler mason who had worked at the crossing long enough to know which winters everyone else remembered incorrectly. Benjamin brought her a bowl before taking his own. She thanked him and let him enjoy having anticipated a need correctly.

There was mutton cooked with onions, a mound of flat cakes kept warm beneath a cloth, and a dish of curd with chopped green leaves through it. The curd was sour in a way that made Elena want another mouthful. Rada saw her expression and asked whether it was good.

“Very. I had some milk turn a few days ago and spent the morning feeling cheated. Apparently I should have added leaves.”

“You should have waited until it meant to turn,” the mason said.

“How would I know?”

“It would invite someone useful.”

Rada laughed. Elena took another piece of cake and decided the distinction could remain with people who made cheese.

For a little while they ate without discussing the bridge. The mason’s daughter had married a man who could mend clocks but was unwilling to admit he did not understand chickens. The trouble concerned a gate, a nesting box and a conviction that anything which opened could be improved by making it open automatically. Dorra began asking questions. Thane looked interested. Rada put down her bowl.

“Don’t encourage them. It’s the daughter I want to hear about.”

The mason explained that his daughter had removed the mechanism and used it to hold up a flowerpot. He spoke with considerable pride. Thane asked whether the clock-mender had objected.

“He said it was no longer doing what it was made for.”

“And she?”

“She said it was holding the flowerpot beautifully.”

Thane laughed so hard that he had to set down his cup. Elena had not heard that laugh for several days. It changed the table around him. Benjamin leaned back, smiling openly; even Tema, who had come to ask something, remained standing with his mouth slightly open until the mason finished.

The conversation moved to houses. Rada liked a door that opened away from the prevailing wind. The mason maintained that people built rooms for the season in which they happened to be building them. Elena said she wanted a window in the room she meant to rent, and then found herself describing the shelves.

“All the same depth?” Thane asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

“And no fine little rail that makes every bottle difficult to lift out. The bottles won’t leap off by themselves.”

“Not with that attitude.”

She kicked his boot beneath the table. He smiled and asked which wall would carry the shelves. She knew the room well enough to answer. For several minutes the room became something people could picture, with a basin by the window and a chair for the person who had brought a patient and needed to sit down more than the patient did.

Elena had not intended to tell so much. She did not mind afterward.

Across the table, Sariel was dividing the last cake with a worker who had misjudged how much he wanted. She made no ceremony of giving him the larger part. He offered her the last of the onion gravy and watched closely while she chose whether to accept. She did, tipping her bowl so he could scrape it in without spilling.

The river sounded beyond the boards. A horse called. A hammer struck twice on the western bank and stopped. Elena ate the last morsel slowly, letting its warmth remain in her mouth before swallowing.

Tema had waited through the story and most of the meal. When the bowls began to clear, he asked Dorra whether the first carts could be weighed and sent over at the same time as the pack train.

“One thing after another,” she said. “Not all the same thing at once.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“The packhorses go first, with the east lane clear. We can start weighing while they cross if the wagons remain out of their approach. Then one cart over the bridge at a time. No stopping halfway. No crowd following to see whether it falls.”

“It won’t fall,” Thane said.

“I know that. I’d still rather not provide an audience.”

Harn took Tema to the ramp and showed him where the first cart would stand. Sariel went too, carrying an empty bowl she had forgotten to put down. Elena followed as far as the end of the table. She wanted to see where a person could be brought clear if someone were hurt in the work.

The crawl beneath the stage was open while the braces were checked. A bolt had rolled through a gap and lodged on the bank shelf below. Harn was about to go after it when Sariel handed him the bowl and ducked through the yard mouth.

“Mind your shoulder,” he called.

“Which one?”

“Whichever you choose to lift.”

They heard her laugh under the boards. She emerged on the shelf, picked up the bolt, and came back the way she had gone. Mud marked one knee. She looked up at the stage and asked whether the crawl would remain clear tomorrow.

“It has to,” Dorra said. “We need access to the braces.”

Sariel handed back the bolt. Benjamin reached for her bowl, discovered Harn was still holding it, and stood for a moment between them with his hands empty. Elena smiled at the confusion, then turned as someone called for the factor.

One of Morvain’s clerks had entered the yard. He was the younger one, carrying his writing case tucked beneath his arm to keep it out of the mud. Two of Rusk’s men came behind him. They had not drawn weapons. They had placed themselves where anyone looking at the clerk would have to see them too.

“A notice for the factors and carriers,” the clerk said.

Dorra wiped her hands on her apron. “Voss is across.”

“Copies are to be posted on both approaches.”

He unfolded the notice. Tema read it over his shoulder before the man had finished smoothing it. The slight movement in his face brought Kestra over from the table.

Elena could not read the whole page from where she stood. She could see a seal, a date and a large space left for further signatures. The clerk explained that the Prefect would provide inspection and protection for the reopening. Loads would be held until the necessary particulars were recorded. The arrangements were temporary and intended to prevent renewed disorder.

“What particulars?” Tema asked.

“Origin, weight, ownership and intended destination.”

“You’ve been looking at our sacks for days.”

“Then the inspection should be brief.”

Dorra held out her hand for the paper. The clerk hesitated before giving it to her.

“Does this stop the repair crew?” she asked.

“No.”

“Does it authorize your men to stand in the lane while we lift a loaded cart?”

“It authorizes a controlled reopening.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Kestra touched Dorra’s arm lightly. “May I take this to Voss?”

The clerk produced another copy. “He has been invited to attend the Prefect this afternoon.”

Tema looked at the signatures still missing. “Invited.”

“Yes.”

One of Rusk’s men shifted his weight. His boot heel sank into the mud and made a small sucking noise when he pulled it free. He looked down, annoyed. Elena found the ordinary gesture more alarming than a threat would have been. The men expected to spend time here.

Kestra folded the copy carefully. The clerk kept the one intended for the east approach and went to find a dry place to post it. Harn carried the recovered bolt back beneath the stage. Work resumed in short bursts around the place where the notice had been read.

Elena returned to the table. The mutton pot was nearly empty, but there was enough gravy to soften a piece of bread. She put it in a bowl and took it to Kestra, who stood studying the copy beside Tema’s cart.

“Eat before you go across.”

Kestra glanced up. “I already ate.”

“Then this is for when you forget that you did.”

She waited until Kestra took the bowl. Then she went back to the shed, where Senn had a question about a man’s blister and no interest in postponing it until the Prefect had finished arranging tomorrow.

Chapter 15 The Temporary Order

Voss read the notice standing up. He did not offer Kestra a chair until he reached the second page, and then waved toward the stool without looking away from the words.

“There’s no end date,” she said.

“I can read.”

She sat. The bowl Elena had given her was empty, and she had brought it across because she had forgotten to leave it anywhere. She placed it on her knees. Beneath the desk, a small heap of wood shavings showed where somebody had planed the edge of a drawer which no longer closed in damp weather.

“Temporary,” Voss said, tapping the page. “Until the approaches have been certified secure.”

“By the office receiving the inspection charge.”

“I said I can read.”

Kestra folded her hands around the bowl. She had come expecting him to object as she did. His irritation made her suddenly aware that she was asking him to refuse something useful.

Outside the window Dorra and Thane stood over the drawing of the bridge facing. They had lifted the loose outer stone, found where the packing had washed from behind it and rebuilt the support against sound work. New timbers carried a temporary brace. It would permit a limited load, one cart at a time, while the longer repair waited for lower water. Dorra had made the limits plain enough for even an anxious carrier to understand.

Voss turned the second sheet over. “Recognition of the guild’s accounts. That part is useful.”

“It is.”

“My own auditors have asked for testimony from an authority they acknowledge. They do not acknowledge a carrier whose interest is to make the repair cheap, or a forewoman whose wages came out of the repair. They might acknowledge this.”

He touched Morvain’s seal with the end of the pen. Kestra imagined the man trying to explain the river to people who would never cross it. She had been ready to make his choice sound like vanity. It was easier to be patient with that thought before she said it aloud.

“Can you accept his witness to the work without giving him the crossing?” she asked.

“That is what I intend to find out.”

He folded the notice and put it inside the maintenance box. His own copy of the bounded agreement lay beneath it, with the carriers’ payments entered beside the actual materials. Tema’s salt duty had now been paid from a sale of the retained stock to a local provisioner. The provisioner had the purchase paper; Voss’s clerk would release the six packets to that buyer. The grain commission no longer needed to pretend there was nothing beneath a cart floor.

Kestra looked at the newly entered sum.

“Enough for the immediate work?”

“With what was already subscribed. Not enough to make me stop caring whether next month’s users pay.”

“I wasn’t going to ask you to stop.”

He shut the box. “You might be the first person this week.”

Morvain had taken a room above the eastern counting shed. It had a good view of the approach and a poor hearth. Smoke drifted across the ceiling in a flat layer. The Prefect’s coat hung on a peg near the window, with a dark damp mark at its lower edge. He had been walking the site himself.

Kestra was glad to see the coat. She had begun constructing a man who only issued paper from dry rooms, and it would have been convenient to dislike that man instead of the one she actually had to speak with.

The older clerk admitted Voss, Tema and Kestra. Sorian was already there. Rusk stood at the window with one boot on a low stool, tightening a strap. He lowered the boot when Voss entered but did not leave.

“The work is proceeding,” Morvain said. “Good.”

Voss set his box on the table. “The immediate work is paid for. It will be tested again before a loaded crossing. I will accept your office’s witness to what has been done.”

“That can be arranged within the reopening order.”

“It can be arranged separately.”

Morvain offered him a chair. This time Voss took it. Kestra remained standing until the younger clerk fetched another stool from the wall. Its seat was warm from someone else’s use.

Tema put his counterpart of the household agreement beside the maintenance paper. “The other terms are settled. The iron is out. The first six sacks have been selected and will be accepted by Sorian at the western landing.”

“Six sacks are a beginning,” Morvain said. “They are not a system for all the traffic waiting below.”

“No,” Tema said. “The bridge is.”

Rusk looked up from his strap. Morvain did not.

“The bridge has been present throughout this dispute. What it lacked was an authority able to ensure that the arrangement lasted beyond the next dissatisfied party.”

Kestra waited for Voss to answer. The factor looked at the paper in front of him instead. His silence was not agreement, but it gave the words room to become the room’s working assumption.

“Who would certify that the approaches were secure?” she asked.

“My officer responsible for the reopening.”

“Against which conditions?”

“The absence of armed obstruction, stable control of loads, and confidence that lawful users can pass.”

“Does confidence have a date?”

The younger clerk looked at her. Morvain’s expression remained courteous.

“The condition may be satisfied quickly. If it is not, it would be irresponsible to withdraw protection merely because an arbitrary number of days had passed.”

“And if the inspection is what holds the loads?” Tema asked.

“Then everyone will benefit from prompt particulars.”

The conversation had found a smooth surface on which every objection slid back toward the person who made it. Kestra recognized the sensation from the Academy. She had once kept arguing long after the person across the desk had ceased to hear anything except a student refusing the terms of her dismissal.

She took out the correction from the failed meeting. It was copied in her working book, beneath the date and the list of people present.

“Your clerk struck the statement that all parties accepted inspection,” she said. “I witnessed that correction. Sarnai did not accept. Neither did these carriers.”

“They accepted that inspection was necessary.”

“Dorra’s inspection of the repaired span. Not a new imperial charge on both banks.”

Voss opened his box. “I will sign the account of the repair. I will not sign away the guild’s maintenance grant in order to have someone certify that I have maintained it.”

Morvain looked at him more closely. “Nobody has asked you to sign it away.”

“Then remove the paragraph which makes my use of it subject to your continuing permission.”

For the first time the Prefect reached for the paper himself. He read the paragraph, although Kestra did not think he needed to. The younger clerk sat very still with his pen poised above a clean sheet.

“I can add that existing grants remain recognized.”

“Then let the existing grant govern this reopening,” Voss said. “With the load limit Dorra has given and the payments the carriers have made.”

“A guild grant does not command a captain.”

Rusk put his strap down.

Tema turned toward him. “We are offering a defined guarding payment. Keep the wagon lane clear during weighing and crossing. Prevent theft while the loads are divided. No collection of disputed claims. No occupation after the work ends.”

“Pay when?” Rusk asked.

“At the start, and the balance when the agreed traffic is through.”

“There are men who would need feeding while you discussed what counts as through.”

“Then write the last load and the last hour,” Kestra said.

He looked at her as though he had forgotten she was there. “That’s a useful answer. It doesn’t tell me who’s paying me tonight.”

Morvain drew a small purse from the drawer beside him. He did not open it. Rusk watched him place it on the table.

Kestra felt angry with the captain for allowing such a plain thing to influence him. Then she remembered the men at the fork, the patched boots and the carriers who had promised payment while waiting for one another to begin. Her anger had to find a more exact place to stand.

“What service does that buy?” she asked.

“Security on the eastern approach,” Morvain said.

“Does that include stopping the grain?”

“It includes ensuring the grain can move without renewing this dispute.”

Tema exhaled slowly. Voss put the unsigned order back on the table, leaving his maintenance account beside it. The documents no longer looked like parts of the same transaction.

“Our repair can be witnessed without this,” he said. “That offer remains.”

They left before anyone called the meeting finished.

On the stairs, Tema asked Voss whether refusing the order would cost him his post.

“Not by itself.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“It wasn’t intended to reassure you.”

The factor descended carefully, his box held close against his coat. Kestra followed with the empty bowl beneath her arm. She had brought it into the meeting and spent the whole discussion hoping nobody would ask why.

At the foot of the stairs Sorian took it from her.

“You’ve eaten, then.”

“Some time ago.”

“Good. I’d hate to think all that was done on an empty stomach.”

She laughed because he sounded tired rather than amused. He carried the bowl back toward the meal boards. Tema went to speak to the other carriers. Voss crossed through the foot wicket to the west, where Dorra was waiting with the results of the final test.

Kestra went with him.

The gauge had held its mark under the tested load. Dorra explained what had been changed, where the temporary brace sat and which movement would stop traffic again. Voss signed the limited reopening on the back of the maintenance account. He named the permitted order: the authorized light pack loads first, then weighed carts one at a time, no passengers riding and no unnecessary people on the span. The guild’s ordinary toll remained. The extraordinary contribution was for this repair only, recorded against its actual cost. His clerk copied the instructions for the carriers. Voss signed that copy too and gave it to Kestra, keeping the maintenance account in his box.

“The horse passage?” Kestra asked.

“For Sarnai to authorize within the terms already acknowledged. We aren’t buying it by repairing the bridge.”

She wrote that down because it was the sentence people would otherwise improve later.

Thane stood beside Dorra while she inspected the last fastening. His right hand rested in the loop of a strap to keep it from swelling while he talked. Harn brought over the measuring wedge and made him confirm the reading. Thane did so, smiling. The three had begun using names for pieces of the repair which meant nothing to Kestra. She listened anyway, pleased that he had an entire conversation in which she was unnecessary.

When they were done, Dorra gave him a drawing to carry back.

“Bring that in the morning. I want your mark beside the test we made.”

“You won’t want my writing.”

“I asked for your mark.”

He tucked the drawing into the flat pocket of his tool roll. Kestra waited until he had secured it before asking him to walk with her to the horse lane.

Sarnai’s authorization came beside the new posts, with Yelü and the two handlers there to hear it. The household payment was complete. The iron lay in the barrow waiting to be taken to the forge. The hay arrangement had been accepted by the people who would perform it. Six sacks could pass on the tested route in the morning, if the water and footing remained suitable.

“Six,” Sarnai repeated when a carrier beyond the posts leaned closer. “This is not a signal for everyone to bring a horse.”

Kestra showed Yelü the copied route. The girl recognized it without needing the names written beside the turns.

“We inspect before the load comes down,” she said. “If the water has reached the blue cord, we stop. If the bed has moved, we stop before that. I know.”

“I wasn’t going to repeat it.”

Yelü glanced at the page in Kestra’s hand.

“I was going to repeat part of it,” Kestra admitted.

The girl grinned. They agreed on the simplest signal: an arm raised and held to stop the next horse at the east entrance, ordinary spoken instructions while within hearing. No one was to send the next animal merely because the previous one had vanished into morning mist. A handler would receive each at the far landing. Sorian would be there before the first load, having used the footbridge, with the sheet listing the six sacks and their buyers. He would inspect and accept those sacks separately from the forty-two still in the carts.

Thane checked the post bases. Benjamin tested the room left for a frightened horse to turn without driving another onto the rough shoulder. The arrangements had become small enough to fit the ground. Kestra wanted to preserve that quality when they returned to the yard and everyone began discussing the meaning of tomorrow.

She found Sariel above the weighing ramp, watching Varn check the second cart’s wheel. The concealed salt was gone. He had fastened the removable floor properly and laid the twelve grain sacks back in their places.

“Has somebody told him tomorrow’s opening is already authorized?” Sariel asked, nodding toward the counting shed.

Kestra leaned beside her. “He knows Voss’s terms. I’ll send a copy of Sarnai’s permission over too. I don’t know what he’ll do about either.”

“I’ll watch the men. They may tell us sooner.”

She told Sariel about the purse on the table. Sariel looked toward Rusk’s men, who were moving their cooking fire closer to the eastern shed. One was carrying a table between the hitching posts.

“I preferred it when we knew they weren’t being paid,” Sariel said.

Kestra did not answer. The man’s end of the table caught a post. He swore, lifted it higher, and kept going toward the bridge approach.

The company met at the first cart after the horses had been fed. Rada listened while Kestra set out the order for the morning. Six sacks would be removed from her load. Weighing could begin while the packhorses crossed, with the horse lane kept clear; no cart would enter the bridge until all six sacks had been accepted west. Dorra would then send the weighed carts over one at a time. If the water was wrong, the pack crossing would wait. If Morvain detained the lane, nobody was to force a cart between armed men merely because the paper allowed passage.

Tema objected to the last point and then, before anyone replied, said that he understood it.

“I don’t have to like waiting,” he added.

“No,” Sariel said. “You can be unpleasant while the carts stand still.”

He gave her a tired smile. “At last, a task suited to my gifts.”

They divided the places they would be. Kestra and Yelü at the east start after the fresh walk and preparation. Sorian at the west landing. Benjamin keeping the approach clear with the drivers. Thane with Dorra and Harn at the weighing stage. Elena and Senn ready at the shed, with clean water already carried. Sariel moving between the lane and the loading work, reporting changes rather than solving them invisibly.

Kestra had written that last instruction in more formal language. Sariel read it and crossed out three words.

“That says the same thing,” Kestra protested.

“Now I can remember it while I’m annoyed.”

They laughed. For a little while tomorrow seemed no more than a difficult piece of work they had prepared to do together.

After the others dispersed, Kestra checked her chalk and the bronze disc. She wrapped the sandglass in the cloth Vennit had given her and placed it in the pocket she could reach without opening the scroll case. Her account of the Coin remained dry beneath its own cover. She had not added to it during their days at the Ford. She wanted, when there was time, to write something which did not begin with a thing they had lost.

Outside the cart cover, someone dragged a pole across gravel.

She looked out. Rusk’s men were laying it beside the eastern approach, with two short stands ready to hold it. The clerk who had brought the notice was pointing to a position nearer the bridge. Sariel stood on the other side of the yard watching them.

Kestra took Voss’s signed reopening from its wrapper, checked that the ink was dry, and put it inside her coat. Then she pulled on her boots again and went to fetch the drivers.

Part Four The return

Chapter 16 The Blocked Lane

Varn had washed his coat.

Sariel noticed because the dark patch on its shoulder had lost the shiny edge where rain and grease had gathered. The rest of the cloth was still damp. He stood beside the second cart, blowing into his hands while his horse nosed at the fastening on his sleeve.

“You could have waited for a warmer day,” she said.

“I wanted it clean.”

She looked toward the six sacks being secured on the packhorses, then at the road between the carts. “There will be opportunities to regret that.”

“There generally are.”

He passed her the rein while he tightened the last strap. She took it. They had managed not to speak alone since the salt came out from beneath his floor. There had been plenty to do together, but Rada or Tema or somebody else always stood near enough to make conversation unnecessary.

“You knew,” Sariel said.

Varn pulled the strap through its buckle. “Yes.”

“All of it?”

“I knew what was under my feet.”

She waited. He checked the buckle rather than look at her.

“He told me we were carrying salt. He told me he would settle it at the Ford. I didn’t ask how.”

“Did he pay you more?”

“A little.”

The horse shifted its head, and Sariel let the rein move through her fingers before gathering it again. Varn had a house somewhere beyond the western road. She knew that because he had spent one evening asking Sorian whether the turn toward it remained passable. She had not asked who lived in it.

“I won’t tell you I wouldn’t have taken it,” she said. “But I would have liked to know what I was guarding.”

“I thought you did.”

She looked at him then. He met her eyes.

“You’re his guards,” he said. “You sit with him over the papers. I thought that was the sort of thing people put in papers.”

There was no useful answer ready. Sariel handed the rein back. “Next time ask me.”

He nodded. “I will.”

Beyond the carts, Kestra had taken off her boots to walk the beginning of the marked route with Yelü. They had gone over it once already that morning. Now they were checking the chalk on the first turn, where the damp air had made its edges spread against the dark stone. Kestra held the sandglass in one hand and her book under the opposite arm, looking like someone who expected the river to submit to a comparison.

Sariel liked the sight of her there. She liked knowing where everyone was.

Elena stood at the treatment shed with Senn, arguing over a stool which had one short leg. Thane was on the weighing stage with Dorra. Benjamin had stationed himself at the mouth of the wagon lane, where he could see both the road and the approach to the horses. He had left his sword in its sheath. Sariel had seen him check that it would draw before putting his coat over the hilt.

She went to him.

“Varn thought we knew about the salt.”

Benjamin considered that. “I suppose we looked as if we ought to.”

“We should charge more for looking so well informed.”

He smiled, then glanced north. One of the packhorses had turned to examine the sack on its own back and was being persuaded that this could wait.

“Are you worried about the crossing?” he asked.

“I’m worried about people bringing something new to it.”

“Morvain?”

“Among the possibilities.”

Benjamin shifted so she could stand beside him without being in the way. The morning was cold enough for her breath to show. She put her hands under her arms and watched a small boat farther downstream struggle against the current. Its rower had placed his coat over a basket near the bow. Every few strokes he looked at the basket instead of where he was going.

“When we’re back,” Benjamin said, “I’d like two mornings without boots.”

“At all?”

“I can do the work which doesn’t require them.”

“What work is that?”

“I’ll ask you to find me some.”

She laughed. He looked pleased with himself. It was an ordinary plan, and for a few breaths she let herself live in it: the loft warm enough to sit on the floor, the square making its morning noise, nothing happening which required a sword.

Then a clerk in a pale coat came down the road carrying Morvain’s folded order.

The official escort lifted the two long poles laid out overnight and brought a roll of red canvas from the counting shed. Sariel had seen the canvas the evening before while a man repaired its hem. She had thought it a screen against the wind. It had become an inspection barrier.

They laid one pole across trestles at the eastern end of the bridge. The other remained against the wall beside the foot wicket. No cart could enter without moving the first pole. Anyone on foot could still pass through the narrow gate, provided an escort chose to stand aside.

Rada stopped the first cart before its horse came within reach of the canvas. Varn halted behind her, leaving enough room to unhitch if necessary. The third and fourth carts waited farther back in the yard. There was room for all of them if nobody tried to occupy the narrow strip between the weighing stage and the turn toward the horse lane.

Morvain chose that strip for his clerk’s table.

Sariel went to the table before they had finished opening its legs.

“You can’t put that here.”

The clerk looked at her, then at the man holding the other end. “It is level.”

“It’s the way out.”

“The road is there.”

“For a person. We have carts turning.”

He glanced toward Rada’s horse. “They are to wait.”

“While waiting, they continue to be carts.”

Rusk came up behind the clerk and took one end of the table. For a moment Sariel thought he was helping her. Then he set it six feet farther toward the yard, still in the lane but clear of the immediate turn.

“That will do,” he said.

“It won’t.”

He looked down at her. “I’ve moved it.”

“You’ve moved the problem six feet.”

“Then we’ve made progress.”

Morvain arrived while she was considering how much of that answer he had meant to be funny. The Prefect wore a dark riding coat with a fur collar he had not possessed at their first meeting, or had not worn. He stopped to greet Tema. He seemed genuinely pleased to find the merchant ready to move.

“We can get these inspected and across before the hour is out.”

“Voss’s people have already inspected them,” Tema said.

“And I have no wish to duplicate their labor. We require an acknowledgment of passage under protection.”

Kestra was too far away to hear. Sariel wished for her, then disliked herself for it. She had read the proposed wording the evening before. She knew the part they had refused.

“For these carts?” she asked.

Morvain turned. “For the traffic we are protecting.”

“Until when?”

“Until the threat is resolved.”

“Which threat?”

He looked past her toward the camp. The rope was still up. Beyond it Sarnai stood with two riders, watching the packhorses being led into position.

“I should not need to explain that to an experienced guard.”

Sariel felt herself grow hot under her coat. He had chosen the word neatly. She could argue and become inexperienced, or agree and lend him the people he meant to govern.

“Then explain it to your clerk,” she said. “He’s going to write it down.”

Tema touched her sleeve, not to silence her but to draw her a little closer. He spoke quietly. “I won’t sign it.”

She looked at him. “Good.”

“I would prefer we didn’t make refusing more expensive than it already is.”

Morvain had heard enough to know the direction of the conversation. He asked Rusk to hold the lane while Voss came over. Rusk turned to his men, named two of them and pointed to positions along the road. Their boots splashed in the mud as they went.

Sariel looked toward Benjamin. He had seen. He raised one hand, palm down, to the waiting drivers. Nobody moved a cart.

Upstream, Kestra began her invocation.

The pack crossing had been agreed separately. Six sacks, six horses, three buyers waiting on the western landing. Sariel could see the pale shapes of the sacks above the mist. The first horse entered the water behind its handler. Yelü waited with the second, holding its head straight while the current found its legs.

For a little while the arrangement worked despite everybody watching it.

Then Morvain’s clerk asked whether those loads had passed inspection.

“Yes,” Tema said.

“By whom?”

“Voss’s people. With the marks you can see.”

“I cannot see them from here.”

“Then you’ll have to walk.”

The clerk looked at Morvain. The Prefect gave a small nod. Two of Rusk’s men started up the lane.

Sariel stepped into their path.

“They’ve begun,” she said. “You wait until they’re across.”

The older mercenary halted. The younger looked over her shoulder toward the horses and seemed to calculate that there was room beside her.

Benjamin came from the yard. He did not draw his sword. He stood where the younger man wanted to go and asked, with an amiability that would have annoyed Sariel if he had used it on her, whether the man could hold a rope.

“What?”

“When the next horse turns. We could use another pair of hands here.”

“I’m not here for your work.”

“Neither am I,” Rada called from the first cart. “But we’re all getting older.”

Somebody laughed. The younger man flushed. Sariel saw his hand move toward his belt and felt her own hand answer before she stopped it.

Rusk called his name. He took the hand away.

“The loads are to be checked on the far bank,” Rusk said. “You can get there over the bridge like an ordinary man.”

The older mercenary went toward the wicket. The younger followed. For a moment Sariel thought the danger had passed.

Behind her, Varn was being told to bring his cart onto the weighing stage.

“Why?” he asked.

The clerk pointed at the table. “So the first can be turned out of the inspection lane.”

“The first is waiting to cross.”

“It cannot cross until we have—”

Sariel turned back. “Leave them where they are.”

Varn had already lifted his reins. A man holding the red canvas stepped backward to open a space. Wind caught the loose end, filled it abruptly and slapped it against the side of Varn’s horse.

The horse shied toward the stage.

It did not bolt. Sariel would remember that clearly afterward. It took two hard sideways steps, found a cart resisting its movement and pulled again. The outer wheel rode the edge of the ramp. The cart tilted. Varn came off his seat on the lower side, still holding the rein, and the shaft struck the stage’s timber rail with a report as loud as a shot.

Rada’s horse backed into its traces. Somebody shouted for it to be held. The packhorses upstream heard the shouting and answered it. One of the waiting riders stood in his stirrups with a bow in his hand.

“Put that down,” Sarnai called.

Sariel did not see whether he obeyed. Varn had gone beneath the tipped cart.

She ran.

The near wheel had jammed against the ramp. The far wheel hung over the bank, with only a narrow ledge of earth beneath it. Grain sacks pressed against the low side. One had split at its neck, spilling a pale trickle through the slats.

Varn’s head showed below the cart bed. He was wedged between its side and the stage’s outer framing. His coat had caught around a projecting iron strap. One leg lay clear; the other disappeared into the dark beneath the bed.

“Don’t pull him,” Thane shouted.

Two men had already seized Varn’s shoulders. They let go.

Iset reached the horse first. She took its head and spoke close to its cheek while Rada released a trace. The animal trembled hard enough to shake the shaft. Each movement made grain fall from the open sack.

Sariel knelt where Varn could see her. “Can you move your foot?”

“Which?”

“The one I can’t see.”

He tried. His face changed.

“Don’t,” she said. “Just tell me.”

“It’s under something.”

“I know.”

He breathed in sharply. “My coat’s caught.”

“We can lose the coat.”

“I washed it.”

She almost laughed. It came out as a breath against her teeth. “Then it’ll be a respectable loss.”

She made herself look at the water around them rather than the light she had put there.

He started down toward her. She moved into his way, holding the sandglass in both hands because she suddenly feared she would drop it.

“What are you doing?” he asked.

“Keeping you off the marked stones.”

“Several people. Not all waiting patiently.”

“That’s better than people who don’t mind.”

Oyun said it as a practical preference, then went to discuss the meadow with the man who had accompanied her. Elena finished folding her cloths. She kept enough with her for the return road and left the station’s share in the cupboard Senn had chosen.

When she lifted her bag, it was lighter than when they arrived. She had expected that to worry her. Instead she found herself thinking of what would fit in the empty space.

Chapter 19 Separate Purchases

The coat had a blue lining which Sariel said was unnecessary.

“Then don’t look at it,” the seller told her.

“It makes it dearer.”

“It makes it lined. An unlined coat is cheaper.”

Kestra stood beside the stall holding two books and trying not to help. Sariel had already established that the coat was too long, the left cuff had been mended and the fastening ought to sit higher. The seller had replied that she could shorten it, the cuff was sound and the fastening was where its previous owner had wanted it.

“Why did the previous owner sell it?” Sariel asked.

“Because her sister made her another.”

“Better?”

“I didn’t ask to see the work.”

The outer wool was dark gray with a greenish cast where the light caught its nap. It was good cloth, heavy enough to turn rain without becoming a burden. Kestra could see that Sariel liked it. She could also see that Sariel had decided liking it was a reason to distrust the purchase.

The seller held it open again. Sariel put her arms through, settled it across her shoulders and fastened the clasp. Her old coat had pulled at one side since the crawl beneath the stage. This one fell evenly. She looked down at it with an expression which made Kestra turn away for a moment.

“You can move in it,” the seller said. “Try.”

Sariel lifted both arms, bent, reached behind herself and found the edge of her knife sheath. The seller watched without comment. Then Sariel walked to the edge of the stall and came back.

“The hem catches.”

“I said I’d shorten it.”

“Today?”

“If you buy it today.”

Sariel named a price. The seller named another. They moved closer together without ever agreeing that either had moved. At last the woman held out her hand and Sariel put coins into it.

“Blue,” Kestra said when they had left.

“Inside.”

“It still counts.”

“Only if I fall over.”

“Then I’ll watch for it.”

Sariel looked at the books. “Did you buy both?”

“One is for drawing.”

“I haven’t seen you draw.”

“I could.”

The pleasure in saying it was quite disproportionate to the little book. Its paper was plain and the binding ordinary, but there were no names she had to save in it, no amounts owed, no account of a night she could only remember in pieces. She had bought it because a page of blank paper looked like room.

Sariel took it, opened it at the middle and tested whether it would lie flat. “Use the first page before you become afraid of it.”

“Why?”

“Hest always did something wrong on the first page. She said it stopped the rest of the book being frightened of her.”

Kestra laughed. “That sounds like Hest.”

“It does.” Sariel closed the book and gave it back. “I think I’ll keep the old coat for filthy work.”

“You could mend it.”

“I intend to. I paid for that one too.”

They found Elena at the potter’s stall with three jars arranged before her. Two were brown. The third had a blue rim. She looked up when she saw Sariel and said, “Don’t.”

“I haven’t said anything.”

“You are preparing.”

“I was going to say your jars will go with my coat.”

That broke Elena’s composure. She laughed, then made the potter demonstrate the lids again because enjoyment did not make a poorly fitted lid acceptable.

Thane was still at the tollhouse when they returned.

He sat at Voss’s desk with a sheet of paper held beneath a strip of iron. Dorra stood behind him, pointing at a line. His left hand moved slowly. He drew a short curve, stopped, rubbed it out with the edge of bread provided for the purpose and tried again.

“It’s the same curve,” Dorra said.

“It is not.”

“Then make the difference large enough for the rest of us.”

Kestra put her purchases down. “Would you like me to ink it once you’ve done?”

“Possibly.” He did not look up. “Don’t improve anything.”

“I never improve your drawings.”

“You make my letters readable.”

“A known defect.”

Dorra moved aside so Kestra could see. The drawing showed an arrangement for holding a fitting while it was worked, with two positions for the handle and a movable brace. Thane had made something related to it for his own tool board. This version was heavier and could be fixed to the bridge workshop’s bench.

“For Harn?” Kestra asked.

“For whoever wants it,” Dorra said. “Harn will be sent home to learn to be irritating in another room. We’ve a lad who can reach everything only by standing on a box, and an old man who says the bench was lower when he began work here. We may as well make one of them right.”

Voss came from the public counter with a small account book. He set it beside Thane’s drawing and asked how long the alteration would take.

“If you have the timber, two days.”

“We have the timber.”

“The right timber?”

“Dorra selected it.”

“Then two days. Unless you want the second handle too.”

“We do.”

“Three. And I want someone else fitting the iron.”

“There are other hands in the workshop,” Dorra said.

Thane smiled down at the paper. Kestra had heard that said to him before, kindly, angrily, as reassurance and as rebuke. This time Dorra was merely telling him what resources were available.

Voss turned a page. “After that, the smaller winch wants its drum checked and the replacement frame laid out. Eight working days altogether, if you will stay. Daily pay, a bed and meals. The work is under Dorra.”

“I had gathered that.”

“You would be amazed how often it needs saying.”

Thane laid down his pencil. He looked at Kestra, then toward the open door where the others had gone to put their purchases away.

“I want to,” he said.

Voss nodded. “Tell me by this evening. I have to ask someone.”

He returned to the counter. Dorra went to speak to a waiting worker. Thane remained seated, his good hand flat beside the drawing.

“You should,” Kestra said.

He looked up. “I wasn’t asking you to decide.”

“I know. I was agreeing before you gave me reasons.”

That made him laugh. He took up the pencil and added the second handle.

They discussed the return outside, where the sun had warmed the tollhouse wall and somebody had set out stools of several unrelated heights.

Benjamin came late carrying the repaired strap from Sariel’s field bag. He put it beside her and sat down without asking whose discussion he had interrupted. Thane told them about the work.

“Eight days,” he said. “Working days. If Dorra closes the shop for weather, I may be longer.”

“You could write,” Elena said.

“I intend to.”

“I mean about your hand, if it—”

She stopped. Thane waited.

“You know what I mean,” she finished.

“Yes. I’ll ask for help before it’s so bad I can’t ask.”

She nodded. He reached across with his left hand and touched the new jar wrapped in her lap.

“Good lid.”

“I checked.”

“I assumed you would.”

Benjamin looked toward the workshop. “They have somewhere you can sleep without climbing that ladder?”

“Yes.”

“And the return?”

“A carrier is going east when the work ends. I’ll pay for a place and walk when it suits me.”

“I could stay.”

The words came out quietly. Kestra watched Thane’s face. He did not look wounded by them. He looked as though he had been expecting them and was trying to choose an answer which would not make either man regret speaking.

“Do you want work here?” he asked.

Benjamin considered the question. “Not especially.”

“Then go home. Tell Linett I’ll have something worth looking at when I get back.”

“She’ll tell me she already has.”

“She will. Don’t agree with her too quickly.”

Benjamin smiled. He sat back, stretching his legs into the sun. After a while he said, “I’ll make certain our room hasn’t been given to somebody with better manners.”

“That would be most people,” Sariel said.

“Yes. I expect difficulties.”

Kestra took out the note she had made about Three Oaks. The shrine lay off the direct return road, south of the ridge they had followed west. She needed to take back the disc and wanted to ask about obtaining one of her own. She had written from the Ford, but a reply had not yet reached them.

“I’ll come,” Sariel said.

“You don’t need to.”

“I know.”

Kestra waited for an explanation. Sariel rubbed a finger over the newly mended strap.

“I haven’t seen that road.”

It was enough. Elena wanted to get home, put the jars somewhere safe and tell Vennit about Senn’s way of arranging the washing. Benjamin would travel with her, accompanying a small carrier returning east. Kestra and Sariel would take the slower road through Three Oaks. Thane would follow after his work.

They agreed who would carry news ahead and what to do if the others were delayed. No one promised a precise day they could not control. Elena took out paper to write to Senn about the medicines left behind, remembered Senn was still across the bridge and went to tell her instead.

Thane picked up his drawing. Benjamin walked with him to the workshop, talking about a tool he had borrowed and not returned. Kestra stayed in the sun with Sariel until the wall’s warmth began to leave their backs.

“Did you want me to come?” Sariel asked.

“Yes.”

“You looked as if you were waiting for a better reason.”

“I was waiting to hear yours.”

Sariel nodded. After a moment she said, “That was most of it.”

Three Oaks had four trees.

The fourth was a sapling planted beside the remains of an older trunk, and Kestra decided not to make an observation she suspected the keeper had heard before. Sariel did not share her restraint. The keeper said she was pleased somebody had finally noticed and sent them to wash before the evening food.

Her name was Helit. She was a broad woman with a white scar beneath one eyebrow and ink on the side of her middle finger. She wore no visible emblem while working in the yard. In the little room behind the shrine she took a bronze disc from a hook and put it around her neck before asking Kestra to show what she had brought.

Kestra laid Mareth’s borrowed symbol on the table, blue wool gathered beside it. Helit inspected the disc, checked the edge and the hole through which the wool passed, then opened a book to record its return.

“Mareth said you might bring it.”

“You’ve heard from them?”

“A letter. Not a prophecy.”

Kestra felt herself blush. “I didn’t mean—”

“I know. People ask as though they expect them to arrive out of the wall.”

She entered the date and asked Kestra to make her mark beside it. Kestra signed her name. The borrowed disc lay between them, suddenly more separate from her than it had been at any point on the road.

“May I ask about another?”

“You may.”

Helit waited. Kestra realized she would have to say the rest.

“I’ve learned one Waymark. Mareth taught me in Millhaven. I’ve used it, but I don’t know how much of what I do is the way they taught me and how much is something everyone learns.”

“Usually both.”

“I’d like to keep learning. I can pay for the disc and whatever work preparing it requires.”

Helit sat down. “Tell me what you’ve used it for.”

Kestra began with the crossing at Millhaven and found herself moving too quickly toward the Ford. Helit stopped her twice. The questions were about distance, direction, who could see the mark and whether she had checked the ground after the rain. They were not questions designed to expose her as a fraud. That made it easier, eventually, to answer the ones to which she had no tidy reply.

When she reached the horse that had hesitated in the current, Helit asked what she had done.

“Nothing useful. The handler knew what was needed.”

“And the mark?”

“It held.”

“Then something useful had been done.”

Kestra looked at the borrowed disc. She did not reach for it.

They stayed five days. Helit had a consecrated disc which could be transferred to a practitioner after they had worked together, and the house charged for the bronze and its preparation. There was no secret collection of further workings inside it. Kestra walked short routes around the shrine, checked what happened when a mark was obscured, and learned that she had been drawing one turn more elaborately than necessary. Once she finished an invocation and found Sariel standing at the designated start with an armful of washed clothes, waiting to see whether carrying a basket made any difference.

“It doesn’t,” Kestra called.

“Good. Then you can carry it back.”

They paid for their lodging. Sariel helped mend a shutter one afternoon because its catch irritated her, then spent the next afternoon asleep beneath a tree and refused to feel obliged to improve anything else. Kestra bought ink and used the first page of her new book to draw a terrible horse.

Helit looked at it and said, “I hope that isn’t part of the working.”

On the morning they left, the new disc lay cool against Kestra’s chest. She had threaded it on a cord of plain brown wool. The blue cord remained with the borrowed symbol in Three Oaks’s keeping.

Sariel waited at the road with her pack on, the new coat unfastened in the mild air. She held two apples and offered Kestra the larger one without making anything of it.

They took the eastward road. By midday Kestra had stopped touching the disc to make sure she had not left it behind.

Chapter 20 The Table Upstairs

Sariel knew they were near Millhaven when Kestra began pretending not to hurry.

She had done it for the last hour. Whenever the road climbed, she slowed enough to make a sensible remark about the heat. Whenever it leveled, her steps lengthened. Twice she asked whether Sariel wanted to stop. Twice she sounded relieved by the answer.

“You can say you want to get there,” Sariel told her.

“I have said it.”

“You said it would be prudent to reach the village while the shops were open.”

“It would.”

“You’re carrying two books and enough ink to frighten a clerk. What do you need to buy?”

Kestra thought for a moment. “Bread.”

“We have bread.”

“Better bread.”

Sariel could not argue with that. The loaf in their bag had spent two days acquiring the character of a building material. They had eaten some that morning by dipping it into hot water, which improved the water more than the bread.

The fields had changed since they left. Young growth stood in rows where the earth had been bare. A hedge which Sariel remembered as a tangle of brown sticks had filled with leaves. At one gate a woman was spreading wool over a hurdle to dry. She looked up, recognized them after a moment, and called for them to shut the gate if they came through.

They stayed on the road. Sariel liked being recognized without the fact stopping anyone’s work.

The last stretch past the mill stream had been cleared of fallen branches. A new length of rail protected the turn. She stopped to inspect the fastening, found it sound and made herself continue without testing it a second time. Kestra was ahead of her now, watching smoke rise beyond the roofs.

Sariel had imagined their return in several ways while they were away. In the least respectable version people came out to meet them and knew what they had accomplished without requiring an account. In another, their room had been given away and nobody could quite remember the terms under which they left it. She had not admitted either version to Benjamin. The one she found was a dog asleep in the sunny part of the square and Mira leaning out of the bakery to tell a boy he had forgotten his change.

The boy came back. Mira put the coin into his hand and made him close his fingers before letting go.

Then she saw Sariel.

“You’re late.”

“For what?”

“Being back.”

Sariel laughed. It surprised her how much that helped.

Mira came to the door wiping flour from her hands. She looked from Sariel to Kestra, taking in the packs, the new coat and the fact that both were standing under their own power.

“Any more of you behind?”

“Thane should be on the road,” Kestra said.

“Thane is at the forge correcting people. He got in yesterday.”

Sariel looked toward the western side of the square. The forge door stood open. She could hear hammering, too steady to be his left-handed work. Relief came before she had decided there was anything to be relieved about.

“Benjamin?”

“Upstairs, when I last sent him somewhere.”

Mira took Kestra’s pack off one shoulder and set it inside the bakery before Kestra could explain that she could carry it. “Leave those a minute. I’ve something for you.”

It was bread, still hot enough to make them pass it from hand to hand. Mira cut two thick pieces and put butter between them. She did not ask how much money they had brought back. She asked whether the roads were passable and whether the seed had reached its buyers.

“Most of it,” Sariel said.

Mira looked at her. “People?”

“All alive.”

“Good. Sit there until you’ve eaten. You’re dropping crumbs where I just swept.”

They sat on the bench inside the door. Kestra closed her eyes over the first bite. Sariel ate slowly enough to taste the butter as well as the hunger.

The boy with the change returned to ask whether she had found anyone worth fighting. Mira told him to take his bread home before it became evidence of neglect. He went reluctantly, looking back once.

“They heard we went west,” Sariel said.

“They have ears. Their use is intermittent.”

“There wasn’t a battle.”

“Then he’ll have to survive the disappointment.”

Mira went back to her oven. Sariel watched her lift the cloth over the next rising batch and remembered a night when the bakery’s light had seemed more improbable than any magic Kestra could make. She had been so tired she could scarcely hold a bowl. Now her new coat lay over her knees and there was a key in her pocket which she had bought the right to keep.

She took another bite. Kestra reached for the butter with no sign of intending to apologize for it.

Their stairs were blocked by a woman carrying the far end of a table.

Sariel stepped aside. The woman said, “Thank you,” without looking at her, then called down to the person at the other end that he was lifting too soon. He lowered. The table struck the wall. They both stopped and reconsidered their arrangement.

“That’s our table,” Kestra said.

“For the moment it’s everybody’s,” the woman answered.

Sariel recognized Thorne’s sister when she turned her head. She had a pencil behind one ear and a strand of thread caught in her sleeve. The table was draped with a length of heavy cloth whose lower corner dragged on the step.

“We said you could use it,” Sariel said.

“And we have. Give us another minute.”

They waited. The woman explained that the lower room had been needed for a meeting, rain had kept the cloth from being spread outside, and the loft had been empty except for people who could move themselves out of the way. They were cutting covers for the wool carriers. Now the meeting was over, and they wanted the table beside the lower door so buyers could measure their finished pieces before taking them away.

Sariel could not see why that required moving it before finishing, but she had spent enough days watching outsiders improve work they did not understand. She held the cloth clear of the stair and let the people carrying the table decide.

At last the way was open. Kestra took up her pack and climbed. Sariel followed more slowly, one hand on the rail.

The loft smelled of wool, soap and the familiar dry dust above the beams. Their beds stood where they had left them. Her chest had been moved away from the window so the cloth could be laid out in the light, and the space where the table had stood was full of pale scraps. Somebody had put a bowl of water on the sill with a sprig of green leaves in it.

Benjamin was gathering the scraps into a basket. He looked up, saw her and dropped the basket.

“Oh,” he said.

She put down her pack. He crossed the room and embraced her so enthusiastically that the fastening of her bag pressed into her ribs. She laughed against his shoulder and then stopped laughing, keeping her face there for a little while.

He smelled of clean shirt, wood smoke and the outdoors. His arms were solid around her. She had thought about that on the road more often than she intended to tell him.

“You got back,” he said.

“I had a key.”

He laughed, but did not let go at once.

When they separated, Kestra was pretending to examine the leaves in the bowl. Benjamin embraced her too. She made a surprised sound and then held him tightly enough that Sariel looked away to give them the same kindness she had just received.

“A better road?” he asked when they had all remembered what to do with their hands.

“Longer,” Kestra said. “Some of it was very good.”

“She drew a horse.”

“Sariel.”

“You’ll want to see it.”

Benjamin looked delighted. Kestra took her new book out of her bag and put it in the chest before anyone could ask.

Elena came upstairs with an empty basket. She saw the packs and stopped on the top step, smiling so widely that Sariel felt herself smile back before either spoke.

“There you are.”

“Were we expected somewhere else?” Kestra asked.

“I’ve had to be sensible with only him to argue with.”

“I have been extremely reasonable,” Benjamin said.

“I know. It’s been exhausting.”

They talked over one another for a while. Elena had found a room she might be able to rent for two afternoons each week. The window stuck, but the light was good. Benjamin had been helping Halen with the village watch and had discovered that Halen now possessed opinions he had not personally supplied. Thane had arrived with a bundle of drawings wrapped more carefully than his spare shirt and a story about a man who could damage any fastening merely by being asked not to.

“Where are the jars?” Sariel asked.

Elena pointed to a shelf behind the screen. The three stood in a row, each with something in it. The blue-rimmed one held the cleanest cloths.

“They’re beautiful,” Sariel said.

Elena looked briefly shy, which Sariel had not expected. “I think so.”

Kestra touched the new brown cord at her throat. Benjamin noticed.

“Yours?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She let it go. There would be time to tell him how the visit had gone, what Helit had corrected and what she wanted to try next. For now she sat down on her bed and took off her boots with an expression of uncomplicated relief.

Sariel went to the forge while Elena and Kestra unpacked. Benjamin came as far as the door with her, then stayed to help carry the table back up because Thorne’s sister had changed her mind about where the last pieces should be cut.

“You could refuse,” Sariel told him.

“I did. Then she explained.”

At the forge Linett stood over an account with the visiting smith. She held up one finger when Sariel entered. Sariel waited. The smith was disputing the quantity of iron used in a repair. Linett made him go and weigh the offcuts before she would finish the calculation.

“He knows,” she told Sariel when he had gone. “He hopes I won’t.”

Thane sat at the bench, his right hand resting in a shallow tray of warm water. A drawing lay beyond it. His hair was still damp from washing and had fallen across his forehead in an arrangement he would object to if anybody mentioned it.

“You stayed,” Sariel said.

“I did.”

“How was it?”

He looked at the drawing. “Good.”

She sat on the stool beside him. He began telling her about the second handle, the grain of the timber Dorra had selected, and the old worker who had refused to try the altered bench until everyone else stopped watching. Once he began, the account grew. There were mistakes he enjoyed describing because they were no longer his problem, a fitting he would make differently, a morning when his hand had hurt and Dorra had sent him to measure something instead of pretending she had not noticed.

Sariel listened. She understood perhaps two parts in three. That had been true of Hest’s explanations too, and she had sometimes discovered months later that a part she thought she had missed was still available when she needed it.

“Would you go back?” she asked.

“If the work was worth the road.”

He said it without looking to see what it did to her. She was glad of that, once the first small sting had passed.

“You should tell us before we hire ourselves somewhere else.”

“I intend to.”

“Kestra will want dates.”

“Kestra always wants dates. We can give her some and let the road disappoint her.”

Sariel laughed. Linett came past and looked at the drawing over Thane’s shoulder. He moved the warm-water tray before she could tell him it was too close to the paper.

“Better,” she said.

“The drawing?”

“The placement of the water.”

She left them a cup each. Tom’s old cup stood on the shelf behind the bench, full of short pegs. Sariel saw it, and saw Thane looking at it when Linett turned away. Neither mentioned it. He resumed his account at the point where the old worker had tried the handle and pretended he had always intended to.

When they went back to the loft, Thane carried the drawing himself.

The table had returned. Its contents had multiplied.

Elena’s basket occupied one end. Kestra’s books occupied another. Benjamin had put a loaf, cheese and a dish of onions in the middle, leaving a narrow clear strip which he described as more space than they had enjoyed at the Ford. Sariel laid her old coat on her bed to mend later and hung the new one on the peg beside her chest.

Thane stood at the table with his palms resting on its edge, the right hand light, the left bearing most of his weight.

“It’s too high,” he said.

“It was that high when we left,” Elena told him.

“I know.”

“And when we ate at it last winter.”

“Yes.”

“Have we changed height?” Benjamin asked.

Thane ignored him. “If the work surface could be lowered at this end—”

“We’re eating,” Kestra said.

“Afterward.”

“You said afterward about the bowl cabinet,” Sariel reminded him.

“That was Hest.”

“You helped.”

He smiled. It was a pleased, unguarded smile, and she found she was glad she had said the name. It belonged in the room as well as anywhere else.

Benjamin pulled out a chair for her. She sat, accepted the bread and reached for the cheese. The lower window was open. From the square came the sound of Halen trying to finish an instruction while three people offered improvements. Mira called somebody in before the next batch burned. A cart went by with one wheel making a noise Thane immediately began listening to.

“Eat first,” Sariel said.

“I haven’t moved.”

“You’ve left in your head.”

He returned his attention to the table. Elena passed him the onions. Kestra opened the drawing book and, after a moment’s hesitation, showed Benjamin the horse.

His face did something heroic. He failed to maintain it.

“It has a very definite opinion,” he said.

“About what?”

“Being a horse.”

Kestra snatched the book back, laughing despite herself. Sariel leaned across to see whether it was as bad as she remembered. It was worse. The head had acquired another line which made it seem to be arguing with the legs.

They were all still laughing when Thane reached beneath the table to test the brace.

“If you take that out,” Sariel said, “you can hold the cheese yourself.”

He withdrew his hand and took another piece of bread.
