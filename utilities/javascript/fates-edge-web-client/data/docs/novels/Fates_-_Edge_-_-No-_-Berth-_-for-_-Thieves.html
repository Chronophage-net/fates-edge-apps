Part I — An excellent purchase

Chapter 1 — The other tow

Rosceline put her hand on the scraped rail and acquired a splinter. For a moment her indignation was entirely sincere.

“This was polished before your men touched it.”

The yard foreman looked at her hand. He had a folded slice of bread in his own, with a black olive pressed into the middle. He had been trying to eat it since she arrived.

“It will be polished again.”

“By the men who have just demonstrated their qualifications?”

She held out the splinter. Behind his shoulder, the White Lark lay against the repair berth, her short mast naked against the morning sky. New wood shone at the rudder head. The ship smelled of tar, hot pine and the sour water trapped beneath a hull that had not moved for three days. Beyond the basin gate, sunlight broke into knives on the channel.

The foreman took a step aside. Rosceline moved with him, obliging him to look at her rather than at the gatehouse.

“I have a carpenter,” he said.

“I have several. What I require is evidence that yours intends to do the work.”

On the deck behind her, Captain Ivenne Rasc tested the tiller. She leaned on it, released it, tried again. She had tucked her skirt into a broad belt, leaving her boots free. Her hair was cropped close to her head except for a narrow plait behind one ear. Nothing about her suggested a woman who had come to discuss the shine on a rail.

Rosceline wore a clerk’s dark green coat, very good wool at the elbows and conspicuously poorer at the collar. She had made the collar herself. A purchasing clerk too well dressed invited questions; one whose employer had supplied a coat but neglected to pay her properly could be trusted to create a dispute about a piece of wood.

The name on her instruction was Mirelle Soss. No such clerk existed to be dragged from a bed later.

At the basin mouth, Briska crouched beside the chain housing. Her tool roll lay open on the stone. Daska stood over her in a yard apron, holding a bucket of grease. The monk’s sleeves were rolled above her elbows. She looked pleasantly willing to help anybody who would go somewhere else.

A watchman remained beside them.

Rosceline had expected two men there. One had gone to watch a load of expensive stair timber being damaged with great confidence. The other had a face like a clenched fist and no interest in timber. He carried a short iron-shod club. His free hand rested on the gatehouse door.

The foreman tried to look past Rosceline again.

“Your master accepted the repair charge.”

“My mistress accepted a rudder. She did not order this.”

She ran one finger beside the pale scar in the rail. On the far quay, Nescor put his shoulder to the capstan bar and pretended to find resistance. He was a square young man with a sailor’s brown neck and a disastrous attempt at a mustache. He had shaved it off for the job, leaving a white line above his lip.

The capstan took up the slack in the tow. The rope lifted from the water, shedding little showers.

“Who told him to start?” the foreman said.

“Did you want the ship moved before the timber scow arrives, or did you not?”

“I want the gate opened first.”

“A remarkably sensible preference. I shall note it.”

She opened the little book in her hand. The first page held a list of cloth delivered to a woman who had died nine years earlier. Rosceline had bought it for its worn cover. The next held a wholly invented history of the rail.

She heard the gate chain move once, then stop.

The foreman heard it too. He started toward the basin mouth.

Rosceline caught the loose sleeve of his coat. She had paid attention to this sleeve when choosing him as her audience. Its seam was already failing.

“You are not leaving me to explain this to her.”

The seam gave with a soft ripping sound.

He looked down. For a long moment they regarded the exposed patch of his shirt together.

“Oh,” Rosceline said.

He set the bread on the rail with extraordinary care.

Ilaro stood beside a stack of short planks, hat brim turned against the glare. Rosceline could see his face beyond the foreman’s shoulder. He had been watching the customs stair. Now he took his notebook out, shut it without writing, and began to walk toward them.

The runner was coming.

They had watched the yard for three mornings. On all three, the same clerk had taken the movement orders up to the repair office while his assistant went for hot milk. Today the assistant had returned without milk and had begun looking for the clerk. Rosceline could see him now, a thin man squeezing between the stair timber and a wagon, one hand protecting a folded sheet inside his coat.

It was not the direction he usually took.

The foreman’s complaint about his sleeve had reached the matter of payment. Rosceline gave him her full attention for another sentence, because a man who believed himself ignored would insist on accompanying her.

“I will pay for the seam,” she said. “I will not pay for an entire new coat merely because the old one has inconvenienced us both.”

“Six marks.”

“For sewing?”

“For the cloth.”

“You might begin by showing me where it was good cloth.”

Ilaro came alongside. “The office needs the captain’s signature.”

“That can wait,” the foreman said.

“It cannot,” Ilaro answered. He looked at Rosceline. “The other instruction has come down.”

She closed her little book.

“Well. I suppose we must all be inconvenienced.”

She stepped onto the gangplank. The foreman reached after her, then stopped when she turned and held up the book.

“I am taking the description to the captain. You will have your opportunity to dispute it.”

Ilaro followed her. His hand slid along the rope rail. It was the wrong hand for his notebook; he had to stop once and move the book into his coat before he could step aboard. Rosceline waited without looking back at him. She asked Ivenne a loud question about responsibility for the varnish.

At the gate, the watchman pushed Daska’s grease bucket aside.

“Come out,” he told Briska.

His voice carried over the basin. Briska’s back did not move.

Daska said something Rosceline could not hear. The watchman raised his club and struck the stone beside Briska’s head.

The sound brought the yard’s attention around.

Rosceline saw Briska flinch. One of her hands came out of the housing, bloody at the thumb. Her other arm stayed inside. The hanging chain shivered, lifted, and pulled tight.

Daska set the bucket down.

The watchman swung at her. She stepped close enough that the club’s shaft hit her shoulder instead of its iron head. Her right hand took his sleeve. She turned beneath his arm, low and fast, and his boots scraped across the stone.

He did not fall. He was stronger than she had allowed for, or more frightened. His free hand caught Briska by the back of her coat.

Daska struck him in the throat with the heel of her hand. His grip opened. She bent his left wrist against the edge of the housing and brought her weight down.

He made a short, astonished noise.

The foreman shouted. Men were running from the stair timber.

“Now,” Rosceline told Ivenne.

“The gate is shut.”

“It is becoming open.”

Briska hauled her arm out. A little block of dark wood remained wedged beneath a pawl. She kicked a lever. Chain thundered into the housing, and the basin gate began to swing.

Nescor drove the capstan. The White Lark moved.

The gangplank slipped off the quay and struck the ship’s side. Ilaro caught the rope fixed to its inner end. Rosceline took it with him. Between them they hauled the wet, awkward thing across the rail while the foreman leaped back from its descending corner.

“You are stealing her!” he roared.

Nobody answered. Agreement would have been discourteous.

The watchman was on his knees, coughing. Daska pulled him clear of the chain and pushed the grease bucket beneath his uninjured hand so he could brace himself. A woman from the timber crew reached him. Daska let her take his weight, then ran for the gate’s outer ledge.

Briska was already there, her tool roll clamped beneath one arm. She took a running step and jumped for the cutter’s rail.

Her boot struck below it.

Rosceline caught the leather strap of the roll. Ilaro caught Briska’s coat. For an instant all three leaned out over the bright strip of water between hull and stone. Then Briska got a knee onto the rail and came over, swearing with a concentration Rosceline admired.

“Leave the tools,” Ivenne said. “Get her feet clear.”

Daska jumped behind her. She landed on the balls of her feet and put a hand on the deck. The scraped knuckles left four small marks.

The runner reached the foreman. He was still protecting his folded paper.

Beyond the gate, Pello’s Mallow waited on the slack side of the tow. He pulled her against the quay while Nescor slipped the hauling rope off the capstan and threw its free end into the skiff. Nescor jumped after it. For a moment the two men and the heap of rope filled the little boat so completely that she looked to have acquired a second, untidy deck.

Pello shoved off.

A yard man reached for Mallow’s stern. Nescor brought an oar down across his fingers. He did not stop to discover whether the man had let go voluntarily.

The cutter’s bow passed the gate. Her stern followed more slowly. Ivenne held the tiller against the pull of the water; a tendon stood out beneath her ear.

“Your wedge,” Rosceline said to Briska.

“It can stay.”

“What if they pull it out?”

Briska looked at the two gate leaves, each held against its outer stop by the current. “Then they can close it behind us.”

A man threw the foreman’s abandoned bread. It struck the sail bundle at Ivenne’s feet and fell open. The olive rolled across the deck.

Nescor came aboard by the low waist. Daska held his collar while he climbed. Pello kept the skiff clear as the tow rope was hauled onto the cutter, then tied Mallow astern.

Only when all of them were aboard or safely behind did Ivenne look at Rosceline.

“Tell your people to make sail.”

“They have names.”

“Use them.”

Rosceline turned. Daska was breathing hard, a red patch rising on her shoulder. Briska had her bleeding thumb in her mouth. Ilaro crouched beside the recovered gangplank with both hands on his knees.

“Daska, Nescor—”

“We heard,” Daska said.

They took the halyard together. Canvas climbed, bellied, and cracked above them. The cutter leaned into the channel.

On the quay the foreman was still shouting. A few words reached them between the slap of the sail and the water beneath the bow.

“Do you know whose—”

Rosceline leaned on the scraped rail. Her pulse was running much too fast for the casual posture she had chosen.

“Yes,” she said, softly enough that only Ilaro heard. “That was rather the attraction.”

Lyra’s ship gathered way beneath her hand.

Chapter 2 — Twelve boxes

Briska made Nescor wash his hands before touching the cargo. He looked at the blood on her thumb.

“Mine is clean,” she said.

“It is blood.”

“It came out of me recently. Yours has been on a capstan.”

He stared at her for a moment, then dipped his hands in the bucket.

She followed him down into the hold. The ladder was fixed to the after face of the hatch, four solid rungs and a fifth that had been replaced by someone who understood the difference between a repair and a promise. She tested it with her heel anyway. The air below was cooler, smelling of raw timber and the heavy damp of sailcloth that had been folded too soon.

Twelve chests stood low on the cargo floor, six on either side of the central passage. They were strapped to ringbolts through their iron bands. The numbers had been burned into the lid boards. No decorative carving, no family device. A chest was most useful when the people lifting it did not have to accommodate the owner’s imagination.

Nescor held the lamp while Briska checked the first row. The ship moved under their feet with a slow, easy rise. Somewhere forward a loose hook tapped the bulkhead. She listened long enough to place it, then continued.

“Have you carried this before?” she asked.

“Silver?”

“Cargo.”

He gave her a wounded look. “I have worked boats since I was twelve.”

“Then stop putting the lamp on the lids.”

He lifted it.

The chain across the hatch had come with a yard padlock. The key had hung from a hook behind the cabin door, beside the master’s spare cap. Their guard on the quay had been meant to prevent precisely what had happened. Briska had used the key, and now wore it on a cord inside her waistcoat. She did not intend to spend the afternoon making another.

She took the little assay slip out of its oiled wrapper. Ilaro had copied the cargo marks from the buyer’s original. Twelve numbers, one weight against each, an uneven flourish beside number seven where the assayer’s pen had caught. The mark on the band matched.

“Lift here,” she told Nescor.

Between them they raised the first chest just far enough to feel the weight. Her thumb hurt where the cut opened against the band. She set her end down without making a sound.

“Is that enough?” he asked.

“No. It could be lead.”

“I was hoping you would say enough.”

She opened the lid with the key supplied by Bastienne, which worked on the first attempt. That was pleasant. Beneath a folded pad of coarse cloth lay the dull white bars. They had none of the liquid beauty people expected money to possess. One had a thumbprint in the surface where someone had touched the casting too early, leaving an ordinary carelessness permanently expensive.

Nescor reached toward it.

Briska caught his wrist.

“I only wanted to—”

“I know what you wanted. Use both hands.”

He lifted the bar. Light from the lamp ran down its edge. His mouth opened slightly.

“There,” Briska said. “You have held it. Put it back in the same place.”

He obeyed with more care than he had used on the lamp.

They checked the remaining chests by their numbered bands and seals, opening the seventh, opposite the first, to compare the contents. Briska marked the disturbed seals in pencil on the back of the slip. The buyer had required an inspection; Lyra could hardly be expected to be pleased with the manner of it, but Briska saw no reason to be inaccurate on her own account.

At the ninth chest, a leather pad beneath the iron strap had been dragged sideways. She knelt to put it square. The strap was sound. The pad had been cut too short and kept working toward the edge of the board.

“Bring another piece when you come down,” she told Nescor.

He put two fingers beneath the loose end to remember it, nodded, and held the lamp higher.

Above them, Daska laughed.

Briska looked up. She had not heard that particular laugh during the gate business. It was the loose one the monk used when she had enough room to move and no immediate intention of apologizing.

“Are we there?” Nescor asked.

“We have scarcely left.”

“I meant the landing.”

“So did I.”

Pello was peeling an orange on deck. He had taken it from a bowl in the captain’s cabin and sat with his back against the hatch, placing the pieces of rind in a neat pile beside his hip. The coast beyond the harbor entrance was a blue haze. Nearer, the city shone in patches: saffron cloth drying from a balcony, a tiled roof slick with pigeon droppings, a red parasol moving along a quay.

The White Lark carried herself differently from Mallow. Briska could feel it even standing still. The larger vessel yielded slowly, her weight taking time to answer a change in the water. Pello had spent the morning watching that answer. He glanced at the little boat towing astern, then back at the sail.

Ivenne stood at the tiller eating the foreman’s olive.

Rosceline had removed the clerk’s coat. Beneath it she wore a loose cream shirt and a fitted waistcoat with far too many buttons for work aboard a ship. A narrow red line ran across her palm where the splinter had entered. She inspected it as Briska came up.

“The silver?”

“Silver.”

“All of it?”

“Twelve chests. I opened two and checked the seals on ten. I can spend the afternoon opening the others if you would like to postpone receiving the money.”

“No. I am content to trust you.”

Briska put the cargo slip into Ilaro’s hand. He read the penciled additions, folded it on the old creases and returned it to its wrapper.

“Eighteen thousand,” Nescor said behind her.

Rosceline held out a finger. “In value. Our share is six.”

“Thousand.”

“Yes. Do try to retain the important part.”

Ivenne turned the tiller a little. “Our share is three hundred and twenty.”

“Your hire,” Rosceline said, “is three hundred and twenty.”

“Our hire has become our share now that we have stolen a ship.”

There was no heat in the captain’s voice. She watched a barge crossing ahead of them, judged its pace and let the cutter pass behind its stern.

Daska came out of the companionway carrying a round board piled with food. Bread, a wedge of white cheese, onions in vinegar, slices of cold pork threaded with fat. Somebody had left a small crock of mustard in the cabin, and she had brought that too.

“You cannot eat all that,” Briska said.

“I have made an effort to include you.”

She set the board on the hatch and stretched out beside it. The sun caught the fine hairs on her forearm. Her right hand had swollen across the knuckles; she kept that hand clear of the mustard.

Briska sat.

For a while they ate without discussing how close the gangplank had come to taking the foreman’s foot. There was room on the hatch for all of them, though Ilaro preferred the shaded side. Pello passed him the orange. Ilaro took one segment and returned it. When Rosceline reached, Pello gave her a piece of peel.

“Is that an opinion?” she asked.

He handed her a segment.

The pork was excellent. Briska had been expecting ship’s stores with the flavor of their container. This had cloves in the rind and a sharpness she could not place until Daska said apple. She ate another slice slowly. At her own bench there would have been yesterday’s bread, the little pot of dripping, and someone knocking before she had finished spreading it.

Rosceline lay back on her elbows. “I intend to have a coat made.”

“You have a coat,” Briska said.

“I intend to have one made without thinking of the woman who will be disappointed if I cannot find it cheaply.”

“Who is she?” Nescor asked.

“Usually me.”

He laughed, relieved to have encountered a difficulty he understood.

Daska reached for the mustard. “I am having a room with a bath.”

“For a night?”

“For as long as the water stays hot.”

“I could make your present bath easier to fill,” Briska said.

Daska looked at her with affection so plain that Briska wished she had kept quiet.

“I want someone else to fill it.”

Ivenne called her name. Daska rose at once.

“You are lying on the sheet.”

The monk looked down. A loop of working rope had disappeared beneath her hip.

“It was comfortable.”

“It is about to become less so.”

Daska moved. Ivenne let the sail draw another handspan of line. The cutter answered, heeling just enough to make the crock slide. Pello stopped it with his foot.

Briska went forward to look at the mast heel.

The hinge was ordinary, stout iron through a timber cheek, with a removable pin holding the mast upright. A tackle ran from the lower fitting to a pair of blocks. The wear told her it had been used, not merely installed to impress a buyer. Beneath the mast lay a set of low wooden crutches, their upper hollows padded with old leather.

“You have had her through the canal?” she asked Ivenne.

“Not this one. Others like her.”

“Why leave the tackle rigged?”

“So I can lower the mast without searching for it.”

Briska looked back. The captain’s mouth had moved at one corner.

“That,” Briska said, “was not my question.”

“It is the answer you are getting while I steer.”

She accepted that and tested the timber around the pin with her thumbnail. Sound. Grease had hardened along the bottom of the cheek; the joint would need cleaning before use. The spar would lie aft when lowered, taking most of the center of the deck. There was room to work on either side, if people refrained from lying on the ropes.

A smell of scorching drew her to the galley.

Nothing was burning. A kettle sat on the small charcoal stove, the fire banked beneath it. The lid of the stove had been mended with a strip of iron so thin that it had curled in the heat. One corner caught the kettle’s foot and held it at an angle. The ordinary inconvenience was waiting patiently to become a scalding.

She fetched pliers from her roll.

“You can leave that,” Ilaro said from the companionway.

“I could.”

He watched her flatten the strip. He did not repeat himself.

When it lay properly she put the kettle back and tested it with one finger. Stable. She had paid for a lifting bench once, years before, with three companions who had all required the same money for different urgent things. The bench had remained at the maker’s, sold eventually to a person who had paid for all of it. She still sometimes walked past the window.

This time there would be enough. A proper bench, with a screw she could raise without bracing her knee beneath the load. She would not improve somebody else’s equipment for a week merely to earn the price of a part for her own.

She shut the stove door harder than necessary.

Above, Nescor called that he could see the buyer’s quay.

Bastienne’s landing had yellow posts and a crane with its boom folded toward the wall. Briska recognized the painted pulley case from Pello’s description. A boy stood beneath it eating something from a twist of paper. Behind him, an open doorway showed a stack of empty carrying trays.

There was space. No other vessel lay at the berth.

Ivenne took in sail, letting the cutter’s weight carry them gently toward the posts. Pello untied Mallow and went ahead with the bow line. Nescor stood in the waist with the second coil.

Rosceline put on her green coat again. She brushed a crumb from its sleeve and settled into an expression Briska had come to recognize as the beginning of somebody else’s inconvenience.

The boy under the crane saw them. He lowered his food.

“Catch,” Pello said.

He threw the light end of the line. It landed almost at the boy’s feet.

The boy stepped backward.

Pello waited. Behind the boy, a woman appeared in the open doorway. She wore a dark apron over a blue dress and had a short chain of keys looped through her belt. Briska had seen her twice during preparations. Bastienne Corval did not usually allow a useful rope to lie unattended.

“Take the line,” Rosceline called.

Bastienne looked up at her.

“No,” she said.

Chapter 3 — The buyer has reconsidered

Ilaro had expected Bastienne to try to reduce the price. He had prepared for an objection to the broken seals, a dispute about the assay and the discovery of an entirely new meaning of the word delivery.

He had not expected her to leave the silver in the channel.

The boy backed into the crane post. Bastienne took him by the shoulder and put him behind her. She did it without looking, as a person did who had moved the same child away from machinery many times.

Pello’s line trailed through the water.

“We have an appointment,” Rosceline said.

“I have cancelled it.”

“You seem to have neglected to tell the ship.”

“Keep her off my posts.”

Ivenne called an instruction. Pello took a boat hook to the nearest post and pushed. The cutter slid another few feet, slow enough for the change to seem harmless until Ilaro saw the crane’s projecting ironwork directly above the rail. Daska caught a hanging fender and thrust it between ship and stone. Its rope tightened with a creak.

“Speak from there,” Ivenne told Rosceline. “I will hold as long as I can.”

Bastienne had the original cargo notice in her hand. A corner of it showed beneath her fingers. Ilaro recognized the red assay mark. The day before, she had let him examine the whole sheet at her table while she ate almonds and discussed weather on the eastern coast. She had answered his questions readily. Not eagerly, which would have concerned him. Readily.

“Someone has been here,” he said.

Bastienne looked at him. “Yes.”

“Who?”

“A woman who knows what her employer owns.”

Rosceline laughed once. “A professional distinction we all hoped to preserve.”

Bastienne did not answer her.

Ilaro got closer to the rail, where the sail’s shadow cut the glare. He could see the woman’s left ear now. An earring hung there. She had worn a pair yesterday.

“What happened?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

He waited.

“She told me the ship’s name. I had not asked.”

“Did she strike you?”

“No.” Bastienne touched her bare ear, noticed him notice it, and dropped her hand. “I took that off myself. The wire caught on the cupboard.”

He accepted the correction. There had been a time when he would have put the earring into a report as evidence of violence and made the rest of the room agree with it.

“Vaska?” Pello asked from the skiff.

Bastienne’s eyes moved to him.

“I told you whose cargo it was,” he said.

“You told me whose cargo. You did not tell me she would be in my counting room before it arrived.”

“I could not.”

“You could have priced the possibility.”

Pello looked down at the wet line in his boat. He began drawing it in, a foot at a time.

Rosceline leaned out. “You are receiving the vessel for six thousand against eighteen thousand in cargo alone. We have made a considerable allowance for your anxieties.”

“I have a warehouse full of pepper she does not own. Two lighters she does not own. A cousin carrying wool who has to use Blackglass next week. I made the allowance before she put names to it.”

“She cannot confiscate all your trade.”

“No. Some of it she can delay until I have to sell something else to keep it.”

The boy started to come around her. She moved him back again.

Ilaro watched the open doorway. Two carrying trays had been taken down from the stack. The crane’s lower hook was already fitted with the lifting basket appropriate to a heavy chest. Bastienne had expected to receive them. Whatever she had decided now, the original offer had been real.

“Did Vaska order you to refuse us?” he asked.

Bastienne pressed her lips together.

“Precisely what did she say?”

“She asked who I would be insuring the voyage with. Then she asked if I would prefer to discuss it while I still had the choice of a voyage.”

“Was she alone?”

“One woman with her. Outside.”

“Armed?”

“Everyone is armed. You have brought a monk.”

Daska, still holding the fender clear of the crane iron, looked over her shoulder.

“I can leave the apron on if it helps.”

The boy smiled. Bastienne saw it and looked suddenly much older than she had at the table yesterday.

“Go inside,” she told him.

He took his paper of food and went. She did not watch him all the way.

Ilaro felt the beginning of his headache at the bridge of his nose. The bright water beneath the quay sent a wavering stripe up the wall. Each movement drew his eye despite the effort to keep it still.

“What did she ask you to—” He stopped. The question had gone. He put his thumb against the edge of the cargo slip in his coat. No. That was the wrong paper.

Pello said, quietly, “Did she say we were coming here?”

“Yes,” Bastienne answered.

“How?” Rosceline demanded.

“She did not tell me.”

Ilaro found his place. “And you did not ask.”

“I wanted her out of the room.”

Ilaro pictured her yesterday, breaking almonds at the table where Vaska had since sat. He had liked how little she seemed to need the six thousand. It had looked like security.

He turned to Rosceline. “The sale is gone.”

“Only if we let it go.”

Bastienne folded the cargo notice. “Keep the advance.”

Rosceline’s expression altered. It was a very small change. Ilaro knew how much work went into making it small.

“Six hundred is not the purchase price.”

“It is what I can afford to lose.”

“You signed.”

“I signed that the advance was at my risk. You insisted.”

Rosceline had. They had all been present. Ilaro remembered the pleasure with which she had proposed the clause, as though obtaining a concession from a fence were evidence that the rest of the venture had become safer.

The cutter’s rail groaned against the fender.

“I need a decision,” Ivenne said.

“We can take the cargo off here,” Rosceline told Bastienne. “You need only allow us the crane. We can arrange a different buyer from land.”

Bastienne moved back from the edge, toward the empty trays.

“Do you imagine refusal will make you invisible to her?”

Bastienne’s hand closed around the folded notice.

“No,” she said. “It will give me something true to tell her.”

Pello laid the last of the wet line in Mallow. He looked up at Rosceline, waiting.

Ilaro stepped close enough that she would not have to turn her head to hear him.

“We cannot sell it to a woman who will not take it.”

“I had noticed.”

“Then stop giving her reasons to keep talking. The ship is moving.”

For a moment she looked at him with simple dislike. He preferred it to the smile she used when she intended to return to an argument later.

She pushed away from the rail.

“Captain.”

Ivenne gave the order before Rosceline could improve it. Daska released the fender. Pello pulled Mallow clear. Nescor took the sheet, and the sail filled as the cutter’s bow swung into the channel.

Bastienne stood beside the yellow post until they had gone past. Then she picked up the empty carrying tray and took it inside.

The six hundred had arrived in two leather bags, one new and one so worn that Ilaro had made Pello tie it in a cloth before carrying it away. They had spent eighty-six on the preparation: lines, clothes, the false instruction, food, a replacement pin, the use of a room where seven people could talk without a child being sent to listen at the door. Ilaro had kept the remaining five hundred and fourteen in the strong pocket of his coat. The weight pressed against his thigh when he sat down now.

Rosceline paced once from the hatch to the stern. Ivenne asked her to stop crossing the tiller’s reach.

“There are other buyers,” Rosceline said.

“There are,” Pello answered.

“Then we should visit one.”

He looked at the ship around them.

“They generally want to speak before we bring it.”

“Would a little initiative kill them?”

Nobody answered that.

Ilaro opened his notebook. The pages had swollen slightly in the morning damp. He smoothed one flat and put his pencil beside the three landing places they had agreed to use if Bastienne needed a delay.

A delay. They had not been fools enough to omit alternatives. They had been pleased enough with their preparations to imagine that the reason for needing an alternative would remain small.

“Lantern Quay first,” he said. “They can take the draft.”

Ivenne glanced at the sky outside the breakwater. Low cloud lay beyond the sunlight, moving fast over the outer water.

“And after?”

“We find out whether they will.”

She nodded. “That is a better answer than telling me to sail away.”

“Could you?” Nescor asked.

“Tonight, with this weather and seven people who have brought one day’s food and three different opinions about rope? I could take her out. I would rather be paid before you discovered what that meant.”

Rosceline sat at last. Her green coat caught beneath her, and she dragged it free with a sharp little movement.

Daska passed her the remaining orange.

She took it without making a joke.

Chapter 4 — Water sold separately

Lantern Quay was receiving a cargo of onions. Briska could smell them before she could read the name on the stone. Their papery skins blew across the water and caught beneath the cutter’s bow.

A man in a straw hat walked to the edge as Pello brought Mallow ahead. He held up one hand.

“No room.”

Behind him lay a space long enough for two vessels of the White Lark’s size.

Pello looked at it. The man looked at Pello looking.

“Booked,” he said.

“Until when?” Ilaro called.

“Until you have gone.”

Briska leaned on the rail. The man had decided not to pretend very hard. That saved time, at least.

“Who told you?” Pello asked.

“A runner in a brown cap. From Harrow. I have the message if you want to argue with it.”

He tapped his coat. A boy carrying an onion basket stopped beside him and stared at the ship. The man turned the boy bodily toward the warehouse.

“No argument,” Ivenne said. “Keep clear of the posts.”

They went on.

The next landing was a narrow timber stage beside a cooper’s shed. Briska knew the cooper slightly. He had once asked her to make a hooping lever that would require no effort from the man using it. She had offered to make him a chair instead. He had bought neither.

He came out wiping his palms on a cloth. No runner had reached him. She could tell from the way he regarded the ship, interested at first and then doubtful as he recognized Pello.

“You have acquired something.”

“Temporarily,” Pello said.

The cooper looked toward Briska for a fuller account. Ivenne was already bringing the cutter alongside the outer piles, stopping short of the stage.

Pello sounded with his pole. His first test gave him enough water; his second struck mud much sooner.

“How far does your sill run?” the captain asked.

The cooper pointed. “Old stair under there. Stage was built over it.”

Briska could see a pale line beneath the green water. Stone steps, wide enough to have served a vanished house. She had noticed them from a skiff without ever needing to think about what lay at their foot.

Pello sounded again. Ivenne watched the mark on the pole.

“We cannot lie here through the fall,” she said.

“We would not stay long,” Rosceline called.

“You do not know that.”

The cooper had begun studying the name on the cutter’s bow. His cloth stopped moving between his hands.

“Who are you bringing her for?”

Rosceline leaned out to answer. The captain pushed the tiller, and Briska reached down with the boat hook to keep the stern away from a broken pile. The whole arrangement had become too close. She could hear the rudder brushing something beneath the water, a soft scraping that was much worse than a clean knock.

“Off,” Ivenne said.

Pello pushed. Nescor took the line. The cutter moved outward, and the scraping ceased.

The cooper remained on his stage. He raised his cloth in an uncertain farewell, then seemed to remember that he had not agreed to be friendly.

Rosceline watched him go into the shed.

“Could we lighten her?” she asked.

“Enough for that stair?” Ivenne said. “Yes. By unloading the silver.”

“Which requires a landing.”

“Now we have both arrived.”

The water seller would sell them water.

Briska found this so refreshing that she nearly paid before he told her the rest. He stood at the head of a flight of narrow steps with his hands folded across his stomach. Three barrels rested in the shade behind him. The brass tap on the nearest shone from use.

“Five marks, filled into your own jars.”

“Brought down,” Nescor said.

“Filled into your own jars.”

“It is four flights.”

“Then take your time.”

There were only two flights, but each turned back upon itself, and Nescor had already carried the first empty jar to the top before learning the condition. He put his hands on his knees. His shaved upper lip had begun to shine with sweat.

Briska looked at the seller. “You deliver to the onion quay.”

“I have people there.”

“You have people here.”

He glanced toward the cutter, held off the foot of the stairs by a line Pello had looped around a public mooring ring.

“Here they are busy.”

One of his assistants sat on an upturned tub eating melon. She did not look up.

Briska had no desire to watch Rosceline discover this conversation. She took five marks from the small purse Ilaro had given her and put them on the barrel.

Nescor fetched the second jar.

When he came up again, the seller had begun filling the first. Water struck the inside with a clean hollow sound. Briska rinsed her thumb beneath the overflow. The cut had clotted badly under its hurried binding. She unwound the strip, washed it and tore a clean piece from the cloth around the jars.

“Your people are very calm,” Nescor said.

He said it quietly, with his back to the seller.

“We are buying water.”

“You know what I mean.”

She tightened the new bandage with her teeth, then let go. “I am trying to keep the blood off my tools. I would prefer to be at the buyer’s table.”

“Captain says she will have to charge for another day if this goes past morning.”

“She should.”

He looked surprised, then relieved, which annoyed Briska more than the surprise.

“We agreed to hire you,” she said. “We did not purchase you.”

“People have tried to make the distinction afterward.”

The seller turned off the tap. Nescor lifted the jar with both hands and started down. Briska followed with the other, taking the steps sideways. At the turn she saw Rosceline leaning out from the deck to offer an opinion on how the jar should be carried.

“Come and do it,” Briska told her.

Rosceline closed her mouth, considered the invitation, and came down the last flight.

Between them they got the first jar into Mallow. Pello held the skiff close to the steps while Nescor brought the second. Daska helped him lift it aboard the cutter. Water slopped over his wrists. He shut his eyes for a moment when it touched the raw patch the capstan bar had left in his palm.

Ilaro sat in the shade of the sail, examining the face of his watch. The little instrument had stopped. Briska had offered to repair it twice. He kept forgetting to leave it with her.

She crouched beside him.

“Five for the water.”

He wrote it down, then looked at the stairs. “Who told him not to deliver?”

“No one he would admit to.”

“Did he refuse the sale?”

“You are sitting beside it.”

He smiled faintly. “I am.”

She looked at the watch in his hand. “Is it helping?”

“No. But I know where to put my eyes when I hold it.”

She had been about to explain the spring. She sat beside him instead. On the steps, Rosceline argued with Nescor over whether her helping had improved the process. He insisted that the jar had remained equally heavy. She conceded the weight while disputing his account of the companionship.

Briska took a mouthful of water. It tasted of the jar and a little of the limestone through which it had come. Warm already. She drank again.

A narrow towboat passed the steps, its rower keeping close to the shade. Pello called to him. The man slowed, recognized the cutter and began rowing again before Pello had finished asking for a tow to an outer berth.

“I’m not taking charge of her,” he said. “Or anything from her hold.”

“Who said you would be?” Pello asked.

The man gave an irritated pull, then rested his oar. “I have work. Don’t make me stop to explain that I don’t want yours.”

He went on. Briska watched his empty boat disappear around the next reach. Pello put the unused line back beneath his seat.

By late afternoon the shine had gone off the theft.

The green clerk’s coat lay in a heap. Daska had washed her knuckles and sat with her feet in the shade, working her bruised shoulder through a small patient circle. Nescor had gone below to square the pad beneath the ninth chest’s strap, taking a longer strip from the galley’s spare leather. He came back with the old one and threw it into the kindling box.

Briska watched him replace the hatch cover. A proper correction. She would have liked to make it herself, but finding that he had done it without needing to be asked again was better than that.

Pello said, “Ors.”

Rosceline turned her head.

“My mother’s cousin. The old rope quay.”

“Will he take us?”

“He can.”

“Will he?”

Pello let the question sit. He was rubbing a dark streak from Mallow’s gunwale with a rag. It had come from the repair basin’s greasy wall. The wood beneath was sound and pale. Briska had helped him fit that strip after he bought the boat; he had rejected the first timber she offered because he did not like the grain.

“He knows Bastienne,” Pello said at last.

“So do we,” Rosceline answered.

“He knows me longer.”

Ivenne asked where the quay lay. Pello showed her with his hand, along the north side of the loading basin and through a short reach partly sheltered by ropewalk sheds. He gave her the depth at the outer berth, the position of an old anchor below the steps, and the fact that the nearest piling would not hold a heavy line despite looking better than its neighbor.

The captain listened to all of it. When he finished she asked one more question about the wind beneath the sheds. Pello answered without looking at anyone else.

“Then take me there.”

He untied Mallow.

Rosceline watched him climb down into her. “You should have mentioned your cousin earlier.”

Pello paused with one hand on the rail.

“I have mentioned him now.”

He stepped down, sat at the oars and pulled away. Ivenne brought the cutter after him.

Briska stayed by the hatch. Beyond the ropewalk, the roofs gathered close enough to hide most of the sky. She could smell hemp dust and boiled tar, the dry bitterness of long fibers being combed. A bell rang somewhere inside a shed, followed by the clatter of a meal being set out. She could see only a pair of hands carrying a bowl past an open door.

Ahead, Pello shipped one oar and raised his arm to a man standing beside a coil of rope almost as tall as himself.

The man recognized him. He smiled before he saw the ship.

Part II — The owner’s harbor

Chapter 5 — A place until noon

Ors Tregard put his foot on the arriving rope.

He did not pick it up. He stood with his hands in the small of his back and examined the White Lark, beginning at the mast and ending at Pello. Behind him, lengths of new rope lay drying beneath a patched awning. The quay smelled of wet hemp and the sweet black stuff used to dress it. A woman at a winding frame kept turning her handle. She looked at the cutter each time the frame brought her round.

Pello waited in Mallow. The rope between his boat and the quay made a pale sag above the water.

“Your mother writes,” Ors said.

“She does.”

“To me.”

Pello rested the oars across his knees. Ilaro could see only the back of his head from the cutter’s rail.

“She says you have a boat now.”

“This one.”

Ors raised his chin toward the cutter.

“Then whose is that?”

“Lyra’s.”

The winding frame stopped. The woman adjusted something at its base and began again, more slowly.

Ors took his foot off the rope.

He stooped, gathered the end and made one turn round a post. His knees gave him trouble. Pello half rose to help and sat again when Ors looked at him.

“Come up. Just you.”

Ivenne let the cutter drift close enough for Pello to step from Mallow onto the lowest stone. Then she checked its movement with the stern line. Nescor stood ready to fend off, perspiring through the back of his shirt despite the breeze.

Ilaro went to the shade under the mainsail. The white faces of the buildings across the water had begun to hurt. He could still hear the conversation at the quay, provided Rosceline did not decide to assist it.

She came to stand beside him.

“His mother’s cousin,” she said. “A useful relation, I am given to understand.”

“By Pello?”

“He has spoken of him.”

“That wasn’t quite—” Ilaro stopped. A hammer struck a shackle behind him, and the question he had meant to ask went with the sound. He looked at Pello, who stood with his shoulders drawn back while Ors spoke into his face. “Has he said the man owes him anything?”

“No.”

“Then let them finish.”

Rosceline’s fingers moved on the rail. Ors pointed down the quay. Pello shook his head once. The old man said something Ilaro missed, louder this time, and Pello answered without raising his voice. Ors looked at the cutter again. His gaze passed over Daska’s scraped knuckles and the bandage round Briska’s thumb. He did not linger on either.

When Pello came back, he held the end of the bow line.

“Thirty. Until noon tomorrow.”

“For a berth?” Rosceline asked.

“For this berth.”

She glanced toward the strip of broken paving beside the hemp frames. “I had hoped the price included somewhere to put our feet.”

“It does,” Pello said. “Until noon.”

Ilaro took out the purse. Pello watched him count, then went down to the stone with the money in his hand. Ors counted it again. He gave the rope a second turn and called to the woman at the frame to clear the inner bollard.

The cutter came alongside with a long, agreeable creak. Nescor made fast. Briska tested the first mooring post, found its iron strap loose and began looking for a wedge until Ivenne told her the second post was sound. Daska went ashore carrying the water jar. She moved so carefully over the sloping plank that Ors watched her feet.

“You could use the steps.”

“They’re wet.”

“You’re on a ship.”

“I’ve noticed.”

The old man’s mouth twitched. He pointed her toward a level slab where the jar could stand.

Ilaro followed Pello to the little office built under the winding shed. There was room for a desk and two people who did not mind one another. He remained outside while Ors found a pencil in the hair above his ear.

“No cargo on my stones,” Ors said. “No change of crew. If you want bread, my girl can fetch it while she’s getting ours. You can pay the baker yourself.”

“We have food,” Pello answered.

“Then I haven’t offered you any.”

He wrote on the scrap in front of him. Pello leaned on the doorpost. For the first time that day he looked younger than Ilaro had been expecting him to look.

“Tomorrow’s boat?” Pello asked.

“Rope for the long yard. Promised before you thought of whatever you’ve thought of.”

“We can lie outside after?”

“Outside is water. You can ask the water. You can’t keep your plank across my loading place.”

“Has she sent to you?” he asked.

Ors turned the pencil in his fingers. “Which she?”

“Lyra.”

“Not yet.”

“Will you tell us if she does?”

“I’ll tell you whatever changes the use of my quay.”

On his way back Ilaro stopped beside the woman at the winding frame. She had a red line on the bridge of her nose where spectacles had rested. The spectacles themselves hung from a cord at her neck.

“Did a man in a brown cap come through here?” he asked. “With a paper for the landings?”

“He wanted a length of cord.”

“Did he leave anything?”

“Three pennies.” She held out the end she was feeding onto the frame. “Mind that.”

Back aboard, he put his notebook on the cabin roof and held it open with his palm. The first landing’s keeper had named a runner from Harrow. The towman had refused to take charge of the ship or anything from its hold, without saying who had told him to refuse. The water seller had said something different. He had been quite willing to sell, provided someone else went down to the boat. Bastienne had not spoken of orders at all. She had spoken of what Vaska could do to a business which continued receiving cargo every day.

He wrote the three refusals separately.

Briska stopped beside him. “What are you making of that?”

“A smaller problem.”

“It still occupies the whole ship.”

“She hasn’t closed the harbor.” He tapped the first line. “These people were told. These thought about it themselves. Ors has thought about it and taken thirty marks.”

“For less than a day.”

“Which is longer than the rest.”

She read over his shoulder. “If you’re suggesting we try every landing until someone makes a different calculation, we haven’t the time.”

“No. I’m suggesting we stop asking people who need her loading crews.”

Briska looked toward the warehouses beyond the bend. A narrow towboat came down the channel carrying a bundle of cane poles. The boatman had refused them an hour earlier. He glanced at the cutter, kept his eyes on the clear water beyond it and went on.

Pello joined them at the rail.

“Where’s he going?” Ilaro asked.

“Mulberry yard.”

“Whose?”

“The Court’s.”

Rosceline heard that from the companionway. Her face tightened before she made it pleasant again.

“We are not going to spend our afternoon begging at the Countess’s tradesmen,” she said. “Bastienne has had time to recover her judgment. She knows the cargo is here.”

“She knew it was there,” Briska said.

“Fear can be remarkably responsive to a better offer.”

“How much better?”

Rosceline looked at the boat disappearing beyond the stone shoulder.

Ilaro closed the notebook. The shade had moved while they spoke. He shifted after it, taking his hand off the bright brass rail.

Before Rosceline could answer, Nescor called from the bow.

A workboat had entered the bight. Three people rowed. A fourth sat in the stern, eating a small dark fruit. She spit the stone into her palm, looked for somewhere to put it, and dropped it into her pocket.

“Don’t throw me a rope,” she called. “I might be tempted.”

Pello had already taken his hand off the coil.

The boat lay a little beyond reach of the cutter’s rail. Its rowers held it there with small movements of the oars. The woman in the stern had a scarred hand and a coat cut short enough to leave her knees free. The pale damage caught Ilaro’s eye before he had a clear look at her face.

Vaska looked at the new moorings.

“Ors. You are a hospitable man.”

Ors stood at the shed door. “Until noon.”

“I’ll try not to keep him longer.” She looked up at Rosceline. “She’ll see two of you in the morning.”

“Bastienne?”

Vaska laughed. It was a full, delighted laugh, and Ilaro disliked her more for enjoying the question.

“No. You’ve finished with Bastienne.”

Ivenne came forward from the tiller. “Where?”

“Harrow. My boat will wait to bring them back. Yours stays here.”

Ivenne stood with one foot on the rail, looking down. “You won’t touch the cutter or the people left on her until you’ve brought the two back?”

“That’s what I’m offering.”

“Then say it that way.”

Vaska’s smile remained, though its amusement changed. She repeated the terms, slowly enough for Ors to hear from the shed. Rosceline and Daska would be collected after the morning unloading at the outer steps. They would be returned to the cutter. Until then, Lyra’s people would leave the ship and those aboard it alone. The five would not use the meeting to unload the silver or send the vessel out of the harbor.

“I can agree to hearing an offer,” Rosceline said. “I cannot promise that it will improve anybody’s mood.”

“You don’t have to.” Vaska set her scarred hand on the gunwale. “She’s already curious about the kind of woman who steals a ship and then complains that someone else has been unreasonable.”

Daska leaned beside Rosceline. “You brought that description with you?”

“I’ve been improving it all afternoon.”

The rowers backed their boat away. Vaska lifted two fingers toward Ors. He did not return the gesture.

When she had gone, Nescor let out a breath so long that Ilaro turned toward him.

“What?” the deckhand said. “I thought she’d ask to come aboard.”

“So did I,” Ilaro told him.

Nescor looked from him to the others, offended by the discovery that their composure had not contained any better information.

Chapter 6 — The captain’s bottle

The bottle was behind the master’s clean shirts, wrapped in a stocking too fine for the rest of his clothes.

Rosceline took it out with both hands.

“Leave those where you found them,” Ivenne said from the companionway.

“The shirts or the expectations?”

“The shirts.”

Rosceline restored the folded linen. The stocking she put on the bunk, beside a little book of coastal approaches she had already been told not to use as a coaster. The bottle was heavy and square at the shoulders. Wax had run down its neck in dark red fingers. She held it to the lamp and saw no sediment moving in the wine.

“A man of neglected attainments,” she said.

“He has a ship,” Ivenne answered. “That usually keeps a man busy.”

“He has entrusted it to us.”

“He has not.”

Rosceline smiled at her. The pilot refused the invitation to smile back, though her eyes went briefly to the bottle.

“We’ll leave you some,” Rosceline said.

“You’ll put the cork back. I have the first watch.”

The cabin was too narrow for the company to sit gracefully. Its table had been bolted a little too close to the hanging bunk, perhaps by a shipwright smaller than the present master. Rosceline’s knee pressed against the varnished edge. Above the table, a rack held three cups. There were five of them, so Daska went to fetch the two pewter mugs from the galley. She returned with a dish of olives as well.

“Those were meant for tomorrow,” Briska said.

“They’re still good tonight.”

Briska put down her rule and held out a hand for one.

Pello took the place nearest the door. He had washed and combed his hair back with wet fingers, making himself look for a moment like a guest waiting to be introduced. Ilaro sat on the bunk with a cushion behind his shoulders. The lamp was below his eye line, and he had turned its bright chimney toward the wall. Rosceline reached past him to lower the little shutter over the stern window. The boat rocked less here than it had in the open channel. Beyond the closed glass, something tapped occasionally against the hull.

She cut the wax and worked the cork free. It gave a soft wet sound which made Daska sit forward.

The first smell was dark fruit, then something warm and bitter underneath. Rosceline poured enough into one cup to test it. “Well?” Daska asked.

“It deserves kinder company.”

“Then it’s going to be disappointed.”

Rosceline filled the cups. She gave Pello the one with an earthen handle broad enough for two fingers, and he moved it away from the edge before she had finished pouring. Briska inspected the hairline crack in her own cup and turned it outward.

For a little while they drank without discussing the ship.

Footsteps passed on the deck above. Ivenne spoke to Nescor, telling him where to lay the spare line. The winding frames had stopped ashore. In their place came the sound of supper being made under the shed: a lid striking stone, someone calling a child away from the water. A woman began to sing and forgot the words after the first verse. Another voice supplied an insulting alternative. Daska laughed with them through the planks.

Rosceline leaned back as far as the bench allowed. Her hip had settled into a deep, dull ache. She eased the leg beneath the table and felt the pressure diminish.

“You could put it up,” Ilaro said.

“On what?”

He looked at Briska’s tool case.

“No,” Briska said.

“I had not asked.”

“You were going to.”

Pello pushed the empty water bucket beneath her foot. Rosceline rested her heel on its rim.

“Thank you.”

He nodded and returned to his wine.

She had not intended to begin again with Bastienne. The woman had paid six hundred marks in advance. She could be reminded of what she had wanted badly enough to pay for.

“If we reduced the remaining payment,” she said, “we would still—”

“Please stop selling us the ship,” Briska said.

Rosceline looked at her.

“We already have it.”

Daska bent over her cup. Rosceline could see her shoulders moving.

“I am entitled to consider our options.”

“Consider quietly for a moment. I want to taste this.” Briska drank. Then she looked at the cup with a small, involuntary surprise which Rosceline found almost forgiving.

Pello asked for the bottle. He read the narrow label before pouring himself a little more.

“You know it?” Rosceline asked.

“I know what it costs.”

“How very practical of you.”

“My uncle sold six bottles to a man who said he couldn’t drink anything else.”

“Was that true?” Daska asked.

“He bought seven the next year.”

Ilaro smiled into his cup. The smile softened his tired face without making him look younger. Rosceline had grown accustomed to checking whether light hurt him, whether a room had a place to sit. “What were you going to buy?” Daska asked him.

“A room.”

“You have a room.”

“A different room.” He rubbed his thumb over the cup’s glaze. “Mine faces the locksmith’s yard.”

“I thought you liked the locksmith.”

“I do. I know his whole family. His youngest has learned to strike a nail.”

Daska winced in sympathy.

“There’s a place behind the old herb market,” Ilaro went on. “The window faces a wall with a vine on it. I went twice, to hear it at different hours.”

“Was it quiet?” Rosceline asked.

“Not entirely. A woman keeps birds. But they stop.”

Rosceline drank and let the picture remain: a wall, a vine, birds making whatever kind of noise birds made when they were not above a stolen ship.

Daska pulled an olive apart with her fingers and spat the stone neatly into her palm.

“I’m still having my bath,” she said.

“Even if it costs twelve hundred marks?” Briska asked.

“I’ll have some left for a dress. A yellow one I saw near the dancing rooms. It would be a dreadful color on Rosceline.”

“Most colors are dreadful on people with dreadful intentions toward them.”

“It has sleeves I couldn’t fight in.”

Rosceline found herself looking at Daska’s hands. The scraped skin over the right knuckles had darkened around the edges. Her nails were clean. She was holding the olive as delicately as if it were a small bead she meant to sew onto something.

“Would you wear it?” Rosceline asked.

“To dance.”

“And leave your purse?”

“With Pello.”

Pello lifted his eyes. “How much in it?”

Daska laughed. It filled the little cabin and went up the companionway, where Ivenne told them to keep the lamp clear of the hanging sleeve. Rosceline moved the lamp. For once the warning did not spoil anything.

Briska wanted her bench. She described its lift in sufficient detail that Daska’s gaze began to wander, then caught herself and asked whether it would raise something with a person standing beside it. Briska took that seriously. She showed the screw’s action with two fingers and the stem of her cup, then explained why the lifting surface had to remain supported when a worker put a hand underneath.

Rosceline listened longer than she had expected to. Briska’s voice changed when she described a machine which did not yet exist. The impatience remained, but it had somewhere to go. At the end, her hand rested on the table as if she could feel the screw turning through the timber.

“Your parent could get you one,” Daska said.

The hand came away.

“My parent could obtain six,” Briska answered. “Three for people with rooms large enough to install them, two for people who wished to own what the first three had, and one for me on terms I would spend the following year learning.”

“Right.” Daska ate the olive. “Your own bench, then.”

Briska looked at her for a moment, expecting more. When nothing came, she reached for the wine.

Pello had set his cup down. Through the opening above him, Rosceline could see the dark shape of Mallow moving beside the cutter. The skiff’s bow line rose and fell with a patient little creak.

“You’ve seen the place,” she said to him.

He nodded.

“The covered berth?”

“A roof with a loft above. At the back of a cooper’s yard. He doesn’t use it now.”

“Sounds dry,” Ilaro said.

“It is.”

Pello picked at a ridge in the table’s varnish. “I went after rain.”

Rosceline waited. “I keep waking,” he said. “If the rain starts hard. She’s covered, but water gathers at the middle. You have to lift it before it gets heavy.”

“Put a ridge under the cover,” Briska said.

“I did.”

“A higher one.”

“It’s a small boat.”

She considered that and looked up toward the dark outline beyond the door.

“I’ll come and see it,” she said.

“I want the roof.”

The answer had more force than anything else he had said that evening. Briska set her cup down.

“All right.”

Rosceline could have promised him the roof. The shape of the promise was immediately available: one good delivery, one better buyer, one more conversation in which she found the correct opening. She took another drink instead. The wine was warmer now and richer. Someone ashore had begun washing dishes. The water struck the side of a basin in short, irregular slaps.

“And you?” Ilaro asked her.

“A great many things.”

“Choose one.”

Rosceline turned the cup between her hands.

She wanted a room people asked to enter. She had several rooms people would ask to enter if she paid for them long enough, and she knew how quickly that could become another form of waiting. She wanted to have a dinner and choose the company for the pleasure of hearing them, without discovering what any of them could do for her first. She also wanted a necklace she had seen under glass, with pale stones which would look expensive even at a distance. The two wishes sat together without either feeling ashamed.

“A supper,” she said. “A very good one. Somewhere I have not had to flatter the owner.”

“Could you manage?” Daska asked.

“I could pay someone to flatter him.”

Pello’s mouth moved. It was almost a smile.

“Would we be invited?” Briska asked.

“That would depend on whether you began inspecting the furniture.”

“Then buy sound furniture.”

Rosceline laughed. The laugh surprised her by lasting. She had to put the cup down before she spilled it, and Daska laughed too, though nothing new had been said. Ilaro leaned back into the cushion. For a while the five of them occupied the cabin as if it belonged to a friend who had gone ashore and trusted them with the bottle.

A hard knock came through the hull.

Pello was on his feet before the second. He went up the companionway. Briska followed, catching the lamp before her sleeve brushed it. Rosceline straightened her leg too quickly and waited for the pain to pass while the others climbed.

On deck, Nescor held a boat hook against a drifting spar. It had come down from the yard with a length of rope caught round it. The rope had looped the cutter’s stern line and was drawing tight as the tide pulled.

“Don’t haul it,” Pello told him. “Let me get below it.”

He stepped into Mallow, crouched, and worked the hook under the floating timber. Ivenne eased the stern line while Daska held its turns. The spar swung away, scraped down the cutter’s side and went out into the bight.

Nescor wiped his hands on his trousers. “Sounded like someone coming up.”

“It did,” Daska said.

He looked at her, perhaps expecting a joke. She was watching the timber disappear into the dark.

Rosceline went below to cork the bottle. There was enough left for Ivenne and Nescor. She set it beside the cups, took the stocking off the bunk and folded it into the master’s drawer.

Chapter 7 — The truce boat

The wine bottle stood empty beside the galley mugs when Briska came on deck. Ivenne had finished her share after the watch; Nescor, who smelled faintly of it, was washing the cups.

Briska found the mast’s hinge pin beneath paint which had never been intended to come off.

She scraped a small patch with the back of her knife. The iron showed sound. Beside it, the securing wedge had been driven from the wrong side, where the pin’s shoulder left too little room to strike it out cleanly. Someone had then painted over that as well.

Ivenne crouched beside her.

“Can you lower it?”

“The man who fitted this could. He probably had a friend with a very long punch.”

“I asked about you.”

Briska put her knife away. “I can remove the wedge. I need to examine the tackle before I trust the rest.”

“Then examine it. Don’t lower anything yet.”

“I had understood that.”

The pilot stayed where she was until Briska looked at her.

“Have you?”

Briska drew a breath. The mast rose between them, its warm wood smelling faintly of the dressing which had been rubbed into the lower part. “I will ask,” Briska said.

“Good.”

From the quay came the sound of Ors’s frames beginning their morning work. It had rained briefly before dawn. Drops still fell from the awning onto the same dark patch of stone, though the sky above the shed was pale and clear. Pello had lifted the sag out of Mallow’s cover before anyone else came on deck. Now he sat in the skiff, making a new eye in the short line he had used during the night.

Rosceline emerged from the cabin in clothes Briska had not realized she brought. The skirt was darker, the collar higher. She had brushed her hair until it caught the light in one continuous sheen. Daska wore what she had worn yesterday. She had washed the knuckles and left them uncovered.

“You should put something over those,” Briska told her.

“They’ll want to see.”

“I meant because they’ll split.”

Daska flexed the hand, looked at the small opening in the skin and went to find a strip of linen.

Rosceline watched Briska measure the distance from the hinge to the aft crutch. “You appear to be planning a journey.”

“I’m establishing whether we have one.”

“Let us hear what the woman offers before we start crawling under bridges.”

“We can do both.”

Briska wrote the measurement on a scrap of board. Paper had become disagreeable in the damp. Her thumb throbbed beneath its bandage each time she closed her hand round the rule.

Vaska’s boat came round the stone shoulder while Nescor was eating the last of his bread. He tried to finish it before she reached them and had to swallow twice. Vaska noticed, gave him an interested smile, then turned to Ivenne.

“Same terms?” the pilot asked.

“Same terms.”

“And you bring them back before you do anything to us.”

“I have remembered since yesterday.”

“I want your rowers to remember.”

One of the rowers looked up. He was a broad man with a healed split in his lower lip. “We heard.”

Ivenne nodded. Rosceline stepped down into the boat without taking Vaska’s offered hand. The movement pulled at her hip; Briska saw her pause before she sat. Daska went after her and accepted the hand, keeping her own bandaged knuckles out of the grip.

“Nothing promised for us,” Briska said.

Rosceline looked up. “I do know how to listen to an offer.”

Daska settled beside her. “I’ll listen too.”

That was enough. Briska moved away from the rail before the exchange became something Rosceline would have to win.

The workboat turned into the channel. Its wake lifted Mallow against the fender. Pello steadied the skiff with one foot and finished his splice.

“Bring your pole,” Briska told him.

He looked at the board in her hand, then at Ilaro, who had come on deck wearing his broad-brimmed hat.

“The cut?”

“I’d like to see what you call low.”

The old salt cut began behind a shed whose roof had collapsed inward and then been used to store whatever would not spoil in rain. Briska had passed its street face often enough. From the water the building showed a different history: blocked doors, the outlines of two raised loading sills, and a wooden staircase which ended a hand’s breadth above the present bank.

Pello took Mallow through the entrance without changing his stroke. The boat’s oars had less room now. He shortened his grip and let the current do more of the work.

Ilaro sat forward with his back to the morning sun. He had brought no case, only the notebook inside his coat. Briska kept her tools between her feet and the measuring pole across the thwarts.

“How often have you come through?” she asked Pello.

“Enough.”

“With a cargo boat?”

“Not that one.”

She waited.

“Shallower,” he said. “Open boats. Barrels, mostly.”

“Then say that when you tell me it’s passable.”

“I said we should look.”

He had. Briska checked the answer she had been preparing and watched the wall slide past instead.

The cut was wider than it first appeared. Weeds and old rope made its banks seem close. A working ledge ran along the right side, broken here and there by steps to yards whose doors had been barred from within. The ledge was just wide enough for a person carrying nothing. Briska would not have wanted to walk it with her tool case while a boat went past.

Above the next bend, washing hung between upper windows. A saffron cloth lifted in the breeze and let the sun through it, staining the water beneath them gold. The water was filthy. For a few strokes it looked expensive.

Pello let the skiff drift while Briska put the pole down beside the ledge. Its iron tip met something soft, then stone. She marked the wet line with her thumbnail and drew it up.

“This isn’t a sounding,” she said. “I need several places, at the same tide, with a proper weight.”

“I know.”

“You may know. I’m saying it for him.” She nodded toward Ilaro.

“I wasn’t going to sail anything on the strength of your thumbnail,” he said.

She looked at him. The arch came into view beyond the washing. It carried an old cart crossing, low and broad, built of stone which had blackened in the middle where smoke from boat braziers touched it. Someone had cut scratches into the approach wall to show the water at different seasons. None was labeled.

Pello brought Mallow against the ledge. Briska climbed out with the pole. The stone under her boots was greasy. She put a hand to the wall and felt the cool grit bite into the cut below her bandage.

The cutter’s mast could not pass upright. That much required no measurement. Lowered, with the spar resting in its crutches, it might. The hull was the worse question. Briska laid the pole across the opening at the level of a projecting stone and sighted beneath it.

“What do you think?” Ilaro asked.

“I think I wish the man who made those marks had kept writing.”

At the far end of the arch the channel opened into a small receiving basin. The broad main channel entered from the opposite side. On the right bank stood a pair of heavy wooden gates. One was open. Men were moving cane poles through the gap into a covered yard, and the towboat they had seen the previous afternoon lay against the inner wall.

“Mulberry,” Pello said.

The name had been painted on a board above the gates. Briska could see no mulberry tree. She could see the men who had let the towboat in. Two carried ordinary loading hooks; one had a short sword and was keeping his coat clear of it. A woman on the upper walk was speaking to the boatman, who held up both hands and then lowered them when she pointed toward the waiting cargo.

“So they can open it from there,” Briska said.

“Someone has to move the dray first,” Pello answered.

She followed his gaze. A low wheeled frame had been left inside the working entrance. Its load of bundled poles projected across the path of the gate. The hinges were sound. The obstacle had legs and opinions.

Ilaro rose carefully in the skiff, holding the wall. “Who is the woman?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you know the boatman?”

“Enough to say good morning.”

“Then don’t say it yet.”

They stayed beneath the arch while Briska finished the rough measurements. No one in the yard looked down the old cut. The people there were busy with the poles, with the dray, with the small irritation of having to move something twice.

On the return, Pello stopped rowing at the bend and let Mallow turn slowly. Briska reached for the gunwale.

“What are you doing?”

“Seeing how she takes it.”

The bow swung toward the ledge before the stern followed. A little curl of current formed where the arch narrowed the flow. Pello dipped an oar and brought the skiff square again.

“That gets stronger on the ebb,” he said.

Briska looked back. The water had concealed the turn until the boat showed it.

She marked an arrow on the board beside her measurements.

Ivenne read the board twice. Then she made Briska show where each measurement had been taken.

“Enough water, perhaps,” she said. “With some ballast out. Not with all of it out, whatever idea you’re having.”

“I haven’t suggested—”

“You’ve looked at the ballast three times since you got back.”

Briska glanced down through the open hold. Iron pigs lay below the cargo floor, accessible through narrow lifted boards. They had been chosen for weight and shape, not ease of carrying. Removing enough to alter the draft would be unpleasant work.

“My parent used to tell me to sell a man the machine he needed before discussing the one he wanted,” she said.

Ivenne looked puzzled.

“I find,” Briska added, “that I’m beginning to understand the irritation of being that man.”

The pilot laughed once, without softening her inspection of the board. “Get me proper depths. I’ll tell you what can leave the hold.”

Briska put the board in her tool case, where it would remain dry. Across the bight, a child had begun throwing little pieces of bark into the water and running along the stones to see which one reached the bend first. Pello watched the child for several moments before returning to his mooring line.

Chapter 8 — An exceptionally generous offer

The room at Harrow was cool enough that Rosceline wanted her gloves back on.

She had removed them on the landing to wipe boat spray from her face. Vaska kept them waiting at the inner door while a man checked their names, then took them past a woman complaining about a delayed load of timber. Neither worker lowered their voice as Rosceline passed.

She put the gloves away instead of admitting the cold had reached her.

Daska walked beside her. The monk’s eyes had moved over the stair, the barred opening beside it and the door through which Vaska led them. She had not given Rosceline any reassuring sign about what she saw.

The meeting room stood low in the building, against the water. A high window admitted pale light which quivered on the opposite wall. Above it, workers were moving something heavy. The sound traveled through the stone, arriving as a distant rumble before the individual knocks became clear.

Lyra sat beside a table with a worn edge. A clean work coat hung from the peg behind her chair. A shallow dish stood near her elbow. Its water was dark, and something small shifted beneath a piece of slate.

“Sit,” she said.

Rosceline heard the word twice. The second sound was lower and seemed to come from nearer the wall. She kept her expression pleasant while she chose the chair that did not put the table between her and the door.

Daska took the other one.

Vaska stayed by the stair. Her hand lay loosely on the pommel of her knife, scarred skin pale against dark leather.

“I must say,” Rosceline began, “your invitation was delivered with considerably more grace than your instructions to the harbor.”

Lyra looked at her. “Which instructions?”

“A number of otherwise hospitable people have developed concerns about allowing us to stand still.”

“I asked my receiving agents not to unload my stolen silver for you.”

“And the others?”

“Which others?”

Rosceline paused. The woman had asked the question as if she expected names. Offering them would reveal which doors the crew had tried. Refusing would leave Rosceline defending a complaint she could not prove.

“People are easily frightened,” she said.

“So I’ve found.”

Daska’s gaze remained on Lyra’s face. Rosceline wished she would look away from it and give her something useful to work with.

Lyra moved the dish a little farther from the table’s edge. She used her left hand. Her right arm stayed close to her side, the shoulder held with a restraint Rosceline recognized from people who had learned which movement would hurt.

“The ship,” Lyra said. “The twelve chests. The repairs. Then six months’ work for me.”

“What kind of work?” Daska asked.

“Work you can do.”

“That isn’t a kind.”

Vaska smiled at the wall.

Lyra turned toward Daska. “Recoveries. Watching a load when I cannot send my own people openly. Finding out why somebody has stopped paying. I would not send the mechanic to climb a wall because I had run out of climbers.”

“The mechanic has a name,” Rosceline said.

“I know it.”

“And the rates?” Daska asked.

“Agreed for each job. You’ll be paid for waiting when I require you to wait. Expenses arranged beforehand.”

“Exclusive?” Rosceline said.

“For the six months.”

There it was. A shape with walls, apparently furnished, its door held open while someone told her how pleasant the light could be.

She put her hands on the table. The wood was cold. Beneath her fingers, a vibration traveled from the wall and stopped. Lyra had turned her head before it reached Rosceline’s hand.

A moment later, someone above shouted for the load to be lowered. The rumble became a scrape. Ordinary timber, ordinary men discovering they could not fit it through the space they had chosen.

Lyra’s gaze returned to her.

“You have objections.”

“I have an appreciation of what you are offering. But the proposed arrangement overlooks a service we have already performed.”

Daska’s eyes closed briefly.

“Your vessel was vulnerable,” Rosceline went on. “That vulnerability has now been established by people sufficiently competent to demonstrate it without losing the vessel or its cargo. An expense, certainly. But one might consider what an enemy with less interest in profit could have done.”

“Which part of returning my silver is the service?” Lyra asked.

“The demonstration.”

“You have made that.”

Vaska moved at the door. Rosceline did not look at her.

“There are merchants,” Rosceline said, “who pay very well to discover weaknesses before they become losses.”

“Then you should have stolen their ships.”

Daska made a small sound. When Rosceline turned, she saw no smile, only the monk’s mouth pressed tightly shut.

Lyra watched the exchange without joining it. Her shadow lay against the wall beneath the window. Rosceline thought it had spread instead, thickening along the joint between two stones. She looked directly at the place and saw the wavering light from the water.

“How is the man?” Daska asked.

“Which man?”

“The watchman at the basin.”

“Solren. His wrist is broken. He can swallow. They’re watching the swelling.”

Daska’s face changed when the name was supplied. She looked at her own bandaged hand, then back at Lyra.

“I wasn’t trying to kill him.”

“He was trying to keep you from taking the ship.”

“I know what he was doing.”

“Did you know his name?”

“No.”

“He’s being paid,” Lyra said. “You will pay for the treatment.”

“How much?” Rosceline asked.

“I don’t have the final account.”

“Then we cannot agree to an amount.”

“You can agree to what the treatment costs.”

Rosceline turned one glove inside out beneath the table. The silk lining caught on her nail. She released it carefully before it tore.

“What happens,” she asked, “when you require us to do something we decline?”

“You explain why.”

“And if you dislike the explanation?”

“I may.”

“That is the part I had anticipated.”

Lyra leaned back. The shadow settled with her, or seemed to. “You stole a vessel with a delivery booked and silver already promised. I have kept the people who need that delivery waiting while you tried to sell it to someone else. If you return it now, I can still make the departure. You are being offered wages because I can use you. Would you prefer that I offer gratitude?”

Rosceline thought of Bastienne’s closed landing. She could picture the merchant eating her ordinary dinner in her ordinary house, six hundred marks poorer and still able to sleep. Rosceline herself could have been eating dinner in a house with a dry stair. She was here because the ship had seemed both possible and magnificent.

Daska picked up an empty cup from beside her chair, looked into it and set it down again. Rosceline became aware of the cold in her teeth.

“We can return the cargo,” Rosceline said. “For an end to the matter. The repair costs can be examined.”

“That returns what you took. The six months remain.”

“Six months is a substantial addition to a morning.”

“Take the offer to the others.”

“We are not accepting it.”

Daska looked at her, then at Lyra. “We aren’t.”

Lyra looked from one woman to the other. There was no immediate movement toward the door, no change in Vaska’s posture. Rosceline had a sudden, unreasonable wish for someone to be theatrically offended.

“Then Vaska will take you back,” Lyra said.

“That was already promised.”

“It is still promised.”

Rosceline rose. Her knees felt stiff from the cold. She put the gloves on now, with deliberate care, and gathered her skirt clear of the chair’s foot. Daska waited until she was moving before following.

At the door Lyra spoke again.

“Do not open any more chests to make your next offer more convincing.”

Rosceline turned. “We know what silver looks like.”

“So does everyone else.”

Vaska led them upstairs. The warmth outside struck Rosceline’s face with such force that she almost stopped at the top step. The timber load was still suspended above the receiving floor. Two men were arguing over its angle. A woman in a stained work coat stood beside them with her hands on her hips.

“Lower it,” she said. “You cannot argue that joint stronger.”

The men lowered it. Work continued around the place where the load came to rest.

On the boat Daska sat opposite Rosceline rather than beside her. Vaska took the stern seat. No one bound their hands or asked for their weapons. The rowers pushed them away from the steps.

Rosceline waited until Harrow’s wall had passed behind them.

“You found that amusing.”

“The service,” Daska said. “I did.”

“It was worth trying.”

“Was it?”

Rosceline looked at the monk’s bandaged knuckles. “Would you have accepted?”

“No.”

Daska looked toward the water. “I wanted the fee. I still want the fee.”

Vaska turned in her seat. “That may be the most expensive thing anyone has said today.”

Rosceline held her gaze. “You must hear a great deal of competition.”

“Usually from my employer.”

This time Daska did smile. Rosceline hated that she felt herself answering it.

When they reached the cutter, Vaska let both women climb aboard before she spoke to Ivenne.

“They’re back.”

“I can see that.”

“The meeting is finished.”

Vaska’s boat backed away from the quay. One of her rowers turned it toward the main channel. She remained facing them until the stone shoulder hid her.

Chapter 9 — The name on the quay

Ors began moving the empty rope frames toward the berth while Rosceline was still describing the offer.

Ilaro watched the first one pass the office door. Its wheel had a flat spot. Each turn made a soft, ugly knock, and he found himself anticipating the sound instead of listening to the six months Rosceline was refusing for all of them.

“Paid work,” he said, bringing his attention back.

“Exclusive paid work.”

“At rates agreed each time?”

“So she says.”

Daska leaned against the mast. “She said it clearly. She also said we would explain ourselves if we refused a job.”

Briska looked from one to the other. “And then?”

“That was the less attractive part,” Rosceline answered.

The rope frame knocked over another uneven stone. Ilaro put both hands flat on the table they had made from the closed hatch.

He had wanted to hear what could be accepted. There was a room near the herb market, with birds whose noise ended, and six months of work could pay for it. Lyra would want useful answers. He could supply useful answers. He had once supplied them to men who wore the authority of an office so comfortably that he had forgotten it could be taken off at the end of the day.

“What did she call the work?” he asked.

“Recoveries. Watching loads. Finding people who had stopped paying.”

“Of course.”

Rosceline’s face changed. She heard judgment where he had meant recognition. Before she could defend the refusal, he shook his head.

“I would rather make an arrangement that lets us refuse the second job.”

“So would I,” Briska said.

Pello looked at the moving frames. “We have until noon to make any arrangement.”

Ivenne came forward. “You have until noon to leave the quay. You can float outside it. But if you expect me to sit here another night, I want to know what I’m waiting for.”

“We’re working that out,” Rosceline said.

“Then work it out before you hire the next night.”

She went aft to speak to Nescor. The deckhand had laid a spare oar beside the companionway. He was looking toward the main channel so often that Ilaro began looking too.

A shallow workboat lay just beyond the stone shoulder. He could see the upper body of one rower and the tip of an oar. It was enough to keep a watch without entering Ors’s water.

“They could wait for dark,” Briska said quietly.

Ilaro turned back to her. “They could.”

“Or for us to get tired.”

“They won’t have to wait very long for that.”

She studied him. “Do you need to go below?”

“Soon. First I need to talk to the towman.”

Pello went ashore with him. Ors was at the end of the quay, directing two workers to move a stack of tarred cloth. He saw them coming and sent the workers elsewhere before either could speak.

Pello looked at the cloth, then at the space where the cutter lay. “Another hour.”

Ors shook his head. “You’re standing like your father.” He pointed beyond the bend. “The rope boat is outside.”

Ilaro had taken the vessel waiting beyond the next bend for part of Vaska’s watch. Now he saw the bundles piled beneath its awning and the man ashore uncoiling a heavy line. “We can wait off the stones,” Pello said.

“You can. And no sending your friends across my quay after you.”

Ors spoke without anger. That made it harder to hear as the beginning of a negotiation.

“You said the anchorage was open.”

“It is. I haven’t told you otherwise.”

Pello’s shoulders drew back. “I came to you because I knew you’d say what you meant.”

“I did. Thirty until noon.”

They looked at one another. The old man wiped his hands on his waistcoat, leaving pale streaks where rope dust had collected in the seams.

“Your mother’s letter came last week,” he said. “I was going to send it along when someone went toward your rooms. It’s in the office.”

Pello glanced at Ilaro. “I’ll get it.”

“After you move the ship.”

For a moment the younger man looked as though he might say something he would enjoy saying. Then he went back along the quay to tell Ivenne.

Ilaro stayed where he was.

“Did she send word?” he asked Ors.

“A man asked who was using the berth. He could see who was using the berth. I told him it was paid until noon.”

“And after?”

“I told him after was booked.”

“That wasn’t what I meant.”

“I know.” Ors looked at the cutter. “I’ve let him have rope without money, Selci. Lent him a man when a load was too much for his cousin’s boat. I won’t do it while he thinks coming to me means I’m in whatever he does.”

“Have you told him that?”

“I’m telling you because you’re asking. He knows.”

Across the channel, the towman from Mulberry was making ready to leave the receiving basin with his boat empty. Ilaro lifted a hand. The man saw him, looked toward the cutter, and went on coiling.

Ilaro did not call louder. He walked to the public steps beyond Ors’s last post and waited where the towboat would have to pass.

It took long enough that he found a place to sit. A damp patch soaked through the back of his trousers. He discovered it too late to improve matters and stayed where he was, his hat shading his eyes.

The towman slowed.

“No tow,” Ilaro said before the man could speak. “I need to ask about a berth at Mulberry. Who takes the work there?”

The man rested his oar against the side and studied him. He had thick eyebrows which moved almost separately from the rest of his face. At last he pulled the oar once and brought his boat closer to the steps.

“The yard mistress. Upstairs before dinner, below after. Ask at the street door. Don’t come through her gate waving a paper unless she told you to.”

“Whose permission does she need for a ship?”

“Depends on the ship.”

“For the one behind me.”

The man looked past Ilaro’s shoulder and laughed without pleasure. “You know whose.”

“The Countess?”

“That’s a title. She’d want the woman.”

Ilaro nodded. The towman pushed away before he could ask anything else. The empty boat moved cleanly through the water. It passed Vaska’s watching craft without being stopped. One of the men aboard that craft raised his head; the towman ignored him and continued toward his next work.

Ilaro watched until the boat was beyond the bend. Then he stood, carefully, and went back to the cutter.

They moved off the quay under Ivenne’s direction. Daska and Nescor handled the lines while Pello guided Mallow clear of the fenders. The rope boat entered as soon as there was space. Its master called a greeting to Ors. The woman at the winding frame had brought out a jug for the arriving crew.

Pello returned for his mother’s letter in the skiff. He climbed the steps, went into the office and came back with a folded sheet inside his coat. Ors followed him to the door. Neither waved when Mallow left.

Outside the stone shoulder, Ivenne set the anchor where the cutter could swing without fouling the channel. It was a better anchorage than Ilaro had expected, sheltered from most of the traffic’s wake. He could also see how easily a boat approaching in darkness might put people against the hull before anyone on deck woke.

Rosceline was standing at the stern, watching the shore through a little gap in the awning. She had made the awning herself from a spare length of canvas, with more attention to its fall than Ivenne considered necessary.

“The Court yard is receiving,” Ilaro told her. “The towman has been in and out. They aren’t taking orders from Harrow.”

“Nor will they take orders from us.”

“No. They’ll want hers.”

Rosceline moved the canvas aside. “Must we?” she asked.

“We could accept Lyra’s offer. We could go out to sea and hope to find a buyer before she sends ahead. Or we can ask the Countess what she wants badly enough to receive the vessel.”

“You make it sound as if there are no independent merchants in Silkstrand.”

“There are. We found one yesterday.”

She looked away.

Below them, Pello was tying Mallow to a stern cleat with a knot which could be released from either boat. Briska sat on a coil beside the mast, her measurement board against her knee. Daska had taken the spare oar from Nescor and was showing him how to keep its blade from striking the rail when he turned. The workboat beyond the shoulder had changed position while they moved. It could still see them.

“We could tell her we took it for her,” Rosceline said.

Ilaro waited until she looked at him again.

“I know,” she said. “I know precisely how that would sound.”

“Then don’t spend the journey improving it.”

She breathed out through her nose. “You are becoming distressingly useful to me.”

“I thought that was why you kept asking.”

This time her smile was real, and very brief.

They put the choice to the others beside the mast. Briska wanted permission to bring the cutter inside the Court’s gate, not a promise that someone would discuss it later. Daska wanted their hired sailors covered by whatever arrangement was made. Pello wanted to know whether the Countess could buy the vessel herself.

“She could,” Rosceline said.

“Then ask.”

Pello brought Mallow alongside to take them to the public steps. He would return directly; Briska and Daska stayed with Ivenne and Nescor to watch the cutter. Rosceline gathered her skirt and took the hand he offered. Ilaro followed more slowly. From below, the cutter looked larger than it had while he stood on it, its weight hanging over the skiff’s low sides.

As they pulled toward the steps, Rosceline looked back at the twelve-chest ship they had stolen so successfully.

“Not begging,” she said.

Ilaro rested his hands on his knees. “Then bring her something she can buy.”

At the steps he let Pello steady the boat while he climbed. The sun had moved high enough to lay a white blade along the street. Rosceline stepped into it first, then turned back and waited for him in the shade of the archway. Behind them, Pello pushed off for the cutter.

Part III — A less excellent purchase

Chapter 10 — Work performed without instruction

Celise looked at Rosceline’s shoes before admitting her.

“Is it necessary to leave them outside?” Rosceline asked.

“It may be necessary to leave you outside. I am deciding.”

The left shoe had dried with a white rim above the sole. Rosceline had tried rubbing it off with her thumb on the way up from the landing. The attempt had spread the salt and given her a dirty thumb.

Ilaro waited behind her in the strip of shade cast by the door, beneath a hanging basket of yellow flowers. He had asked to stop twice on the climb from the public steps and now held one hand against his brow.

“We have come on a matter which may be useful to your employer,” Rosceline said.

“Does she owe you money?”

“Not for this.”

Celise took a little longer over her decision.

The house smelled of cut lemons and hot cloth. Someone was pressing curtains in a side room. They passed the open door and saw a man holding a great weight of blue silk away from the floor while a woman worked the iron along its hem. He looked almost prayerful. Rosceline had paid for curtains once that had required two men and a dispute with a stonecutter before they could be hung. She could still remember how pleased she had been when they finally fell correctly.

She wanted the money from the ship more than she had wanted those curtains. This did not improve her mood.

The Countess received them in a small room overlooking the inner garden. Her veil lay over the back of her chair. In the shaded light, the hollows beside her mouth were visible, as were the fine veins on the hands resting against her skirt. She wore an embroidered shawl although the room was warm. A woman with a tray of cut fruit withdrew through the garden door.

“Where are the others?” the Countess said.

“The others are maintaining a position,” Rosceline answered.

“Where?”

“On a ship.”

“Whose?”

There was a pause that Rosceline would have preferred to use differently.

“Lyra’s,” Ilaro said.

The Countess looked from him to Rosceline. She lifted a slice of peach from the plate and ate it without haste.

“Do sit down.”

Rosceline took the chair across from her. Its cushion was softer than she wanted. Her hip sank, making her feel as though she had been arranged for an interview with a physician. Ilaro chose the chair against the wall and put his hat on his knee.

“We have acquired the White Lark,” Rosceline began. “With a consignment which places us in an unusual position to affect certain movements of trade.”

“You have stolen it.”

“I was coming to the manner of acquisition.”

“Have you sold it?”

“We had a buyer.”

The Countess put the peach stone on the side of her plate. She had not smiled yet. Rosceline found this more offensive than the smile would have been.

“Our buyer has become excessively impressed by a visit,” she continued. “This is unfortunate, but the cargo remains valuable. There may be an opportunity for someone with greater resources and a more durable appetite.”

“Bastienne Corval,” the Countess said.

Ilaro sat forward a little.

“Did she come to you?”

“No. She refused an unrelated delivery this morning. I wondered what had frightened her.”

Rosceline had expected to enjoy the first telling of the theft. There had been several moments in the repair yard worth describing. She could still hear the foreman’s voice going high when he understood that the gangplank had come off the quay.

Instead she said, “She paid six hundred in advance.”

“Which she will not recover?”

“She accepted that risk in writing. The remaining fifty-four hundred was payable when we brought the vessel to her landing.”

“And you did.”

“We brought it.”

The Countess looked at Ilaro.

“Have you killed anyone?”

“No,” he said. “Daska broke a watchman’s wrist.”

“Was that necessary?”

“It kept him from stopping us.”

The Countess’s attention returned to Rosceline.

“What does Lyra want?”

“The ship, the silver, repairs and six months in which our talents are exclusively hers. At wages.”

“How civil.”

“She seemed to think so.”

“You refused.”

“We did not think the arrangement suited our existing relationships.”

“Do not put me among your reasons. You arranged this without asking me.”

Rosceline felt the heat rise beneath her collar. Ilaro was watching the Countess, giving Rosceline none of the assistance for which she might later be grateful.

“We arranged it privately,” she said. “It was a sound opportunity.”

“Until you had the ship.”

“The taking was extremely well done.”

“I do not doubt it.”

The Countess said it gently. Rosceline had an impulse to stand, to give the woman a precise account of what she could do with her comfortable garden and her peach. She stayed in the soft chair. She was tired of water coming over landings where people stood looking at her without taking a rope.

“The taking,” she said, “is complete. We would like to discuss the disposal.”

Ilaro opened his notebook.

“There are twelve chests. We checked the marks and the seals. Two were opened for the assay and secured again. The other ten seals are undisturbed. The silver is ordinary silver. No one is aboard under restraint. Captain Ivenne Rasc commands the vessel, with her own man. They know the work.”

“Where is it now?”

“Outside Ors Tregard’s landing.”

“Not at it?”

“Not since noon.”

The Countess turned her head toward the garden. A gardener was lifting a pot onto a low wall. He placed it carefully, stepped away, then returned to turn the cracked side inward.

“I require six unloadings at Blackglass next month,” she said. “At the ordinary rates.”

Rosceline waited.

“Lyra has found reasons to offer me dates on which my vessels will not be there. If I provide a date, she finds an obstruction. If I provide alternatives, she supplies new obstructions. My people can use other landings, but the carts must then cross two bridges, and certain goods do not improve through being dropped from a cart.”

“Six visits,” Ilaro said. “Not possession of the quay.”

“Scheduled unloadings. With crews supplied on the terms everyone else receives.”

“That would be a modest use for eighteen thousand marks in silver,” Rosceline said.

“It would be a very expensive price for silver I do not own.”

“You would have it.”

“And Lyra would know where. I have enough property that she could make a lasting disagreement tiresome.”

The Countess reached for her water. Rosceline noticed the slight care with which she closed her fingers around the glass. She had noticed it in other people and made use of it. Here, in a room with two doors and a house full of employed men, she felt only the unfairness of being allowed to notice something she could not exploit.

“Bring the vessel into the Mulberry yard,” the Countess said. “With the cargo intact. I will pay fifteen hundred marks and arrange its return in exchange for the six unloadings.”

Rosceline heard herself laugh. It was not a pleasing sound.

“That is the sum you declined to pay us eight weeks ago.”

“For an object you destroyed.”

“An exceptionally fruitful sum for you.”

“You are welcome to refuse it twice.”

Ilaro had stopped writing.

“And when you return the ship, what happens to us?”

“You will have been paid.”

The Countess set down the glass.

Rosceline sat upright, finding the edge of the cushion with the back of her knee.

“Our release must form part of the settlement,” she said. “From any claim for service or further payment arising from this theft. There will be repairs. We will meet properly established costs. But you cannot hand over the cargo, collect your six conveniences and leave us waiting outside the door.”

“The captain and her man as well,” Ilaro said.

The Countess looked toward him.

“They were hired by us. If we need them to bring the vessel in, they need to be included.”

“Do you propose to write everyone who touched a rope into my negotiation?”

“The two people we hired,” Ilaro answered. “And the five of us. It is a short list.”

Rosceline let the silence remain. The Countess could afford to dismiss them. She could not obtain the ship merely by wishing it across the harbor, and that was the first agreeable thought Rosceline had had since Bastienne’s men left her rope in the water.

“At a bounded cost for damage,” the Countess said. “Not an assurance that Lyra will welcome you into her premises hereafter.”

“Nobody is proposing a second visit for pleasure.”

“Nor immunity for anything you may decide to do next week.”

“Madam, we should like to be at liberty to reach next week.”

Celise came to the table with paper. She had been standing beside the garden door; Rosceline had not heard her enter.

“The Mulberry yard,” Ilaro said. “Who commands the gate?”

“Ullan. She will receive instructions.”

“Instructions to admit us. Will she send boats?”

“She will keep the yard open for you and the approaches within her walls secure. She will not conduct a war down the channel.”

Ilaro nodded and wrote that down. His pencil paused. He looked at the words, rubbed one out and started the line again.

Rosceline watched Celise draft the promise. It was brief enough to be read before anyone became accustomed to a bad condition. She asked for the two hired sailors to be named. She asked for the payment to be due on actual delivery rather than the completion of the Countess’s separate bargain. The Countess permitted the first change and considered the second long enough to make Rosceline sit very still.

“On delivery,” she agreed.

Celise made two copies. She sanded them, shook the grains into their little dish, and set one before each woman. Rosceline read hers through again. There was no mention of a later commission. No promise could reach into the drawer where she kept Sarem’s book.

She put the copy in the flat pocket inside her coat.

“I will take this to the others.”

“I thought you spoke for them.”

“I speak a great deal,” Rosceline said. “It does not always amount to the same thing.”

For the first time, the Countess smiled.

They found the White Lark lying at her anchor with the shade cloth over the afterdeck. Briska had taken off her boots. One foot rested on the tiller bench; the other was braced against a coil while she worked at a strip of iron. Pello brought them alongside in Mallow and held the skiff steady until Ilaro was aboard.

“Fifteen hundred,” Rosceline said.

Briska set down the iron.

“For what?”

Rosceline gave her the copy. She stayed standing while it was read. The ship smelled of the cold lunch they had not finished, pitch warming along a seam, and Nescor’s wet shirt spread over the rail.

Daska fetched the shirt down so everyone could see the main channel. A shallow workboat stood across it, close to the warehouses. No one aboard pretended to be fishing.

Pello read after Briska. He folded the paper once along its existing crease and handed it to Ilaro.

“The salt cut,” he said.

“Can she use it?” Rosceline asked.

Ivenne came from the companionway with a cup in her hand.

“Possibly. Which is the word you employ a captain to put between your idea and the water.”

“Can you make possibly more exact?”

“If you will stop sending my boat away while I need to inspect the channel.”

Pello took the rope from the cleat. He did not look at Rosceline.

“We can take you now.”

Briska stood and put her boots on. “I want the loaded depth before we move anything.”

Rosceline caught her sleeve before she passed.

“Do you want to take the job?”

Briska looked first at the hand and then at her.

“I wanted the first one.”

“So did I.”

“Then we should probably take this one before it becomes less.”

Daska said she would do it. Ilaro agreed, with his copy of the release terms already folded into his notebook. Pello looked out toward the workboat before he nodded.

Ivenne put her cup down.

“My man and I have earned the passage we agreed. This is another movement under pursuit. Eighty for me. Forty for him. The existing injury provision stands.”

Nescor had come up behind her. Rosceline could see him trying not to look relieved that someone had said it aloud.

“One hundred and twenty,” she said. “Agreed.”

“And I decide when we turn back.”

Rosceline glanced toward the watched channel. “Let us first discover somewhere you would care to turn forward.”

Ivenne went down into Mallow. Pello steadied her by the elbow, then moved his hand away as soon as her feet were set.

Chapter 11 — The depth that is there

Briska had disliked the salt cut from the safety of a small boat. With the White Lark’s loaded depth marked on her sounding pole, she liked it less.

The old channel ran between two banks of brickwork patched with stone. Salt had once been hauled along its ledge; pale scars on the walls showed where sacks or barrows had rubbed the corners. There were weeds in the joints now. A little fig tree grew high over the water with its roots thrust through a carved fish’s open mouth.

Pello kept Mallow in the middle while Ivenne sounded. Briska watched the marks on the pole. The captain lowered it vertically, found bottom, lifted, then repeated the sounding a little to one side. She did this wherever the wall bulged or the color of the water altered. A surface that looked flat from shore kept rising and falling beneath them as she found it.

At the low arch she asked Pello to hold position.

The underside was stained black. A line of mussels stopped below an older white mark, which had nothing to do with today’s water. Briska had already learned that a man on the adjoining quay was willing to describe that mark as the proper height for passage if it made a stranger leave him alone.

Ivenne pushed the pole through a gap between two stones and felt along the sill.

“Here.”

Briska moved closer. The skiff tipped and Ivenne caught her wrist without looking up.

“Not all of you on one side.”

Pello shifted his weight. Briska waited for the boat to settle, then read the wet mark.

“I have more beneath the keel than that.”

“You have what is beneath the keel where the keel is now.”

“I do understand the distinction.”

“Then let it offend you quietly.”

Beyond the arch, the cut opened into a short pool. The Mulberry yard’s water entrance lay along its right side, two broad wooden leaves set between dressed-stone piers. The main channel reached the same pool from the opposite direction. A workboat could travel from one approach to the other without going through the yard.

Briska studied that junction. “They can come round.”

“They can,” Ivenne said. “They have less under them than we do.”

“And they can get out of our way faster.”

“That depends whether they want to.”

The captain sounded again. She made no allowance in her face for how much Briska wanted a better result.

Pello laid a hand against the wall and pushed them slowly backward. The stone scraped his knuckles. He had taken Mallow through here in the morning as if the skiff and the water had agreed upon the matter years ago. Now he watched Ivenne’s pole as closely as Briska did.

“We could take weight out,” Briska said.

“You could.”

“How much?”

“Enough to matter. Not enough to make her tender when we need her to answer the helm.”

“The mast will be down.”

“And the silver will still be aboard. I will see the ballast before I promise you anything.”

Briska crouched with the pole across her knees. A loose strand from the bandage on her thumb had caught in the grain. She unwound it carefully rather than tear the cloth and reopen the cut. The wood smelled of mud and the bitter green stain that came off old water walls.

Pello pointed with his chin at a flat shelf just before the arch. “You can walk there.”

Ivenne looked along it.

“Your man with the notebook?”

“He can see the turn.”

“And get to the yard?”

“There is a stair past the arch.”

Briska looked up. “How do you pass the arch on foot?”

“Over it.”

Pello indicated footholds worn into the stone at the side. They led to the top of the narrow bridge. A person could use them, though Briska would have preferred not to carry tools while doing so. Ilaro might need to go around by the lane beforehand and stay on the farther side.

“We put him beyond it,” she said. “He does not climb anything while the ship is moving.”

Ivenne nodded. “He must see my bow and theirs. If he can only see ours, he may as well stay aboard and worry comfortably.”

A beetle clung to the end of the pole when she lifted it. Briska tipped it onto the ledge. It remained still, coated in gray water, then began to find its way beneath a leaf.

They returned by the same route. Pello rowed without being asked to hurry.

The ballast lay beneath boards on either side of the hold, squat iron pigs dark with moisture. Briska lifted one far enough to discover that a corner of her plan had been optimistic.

“Put that down,” Ivenne said.

“I have it.”

“Put it down while you still have your fingers.”

She lowered it. The thud made the chests answer against their lashings.

Nescor brought the short lifting bar. He and Daska worked the first pig onto a folded sack and took it up together. Daska had tied her coat round her waist. Sweat already darkened the linen between her shoulder blades.

“Where do you want it?” she called.

“Into the waiting punt,” Ivenne answered. “One on the left. The next on the right. Do not make a heap under one man’s feet.”

They had hired the punt from the anchorage’s keeper, who insisted on coming aboard it himself. He would hold the iron until they paid to have it moved or returned. He had declined to ask why they needed it moved in such a hurry. His boat rode low enough with the first few pigs to cure Nescor of trying to hurry the rest.

Briska made marks against the hull as the weight came out. Ivenne checked them. She stopped the work while there was still iron beneath the last board.

“One more pair,” Briska said.

“No.”

“We gain—”

“I know what we gain. I want weight where I can use it if she rolls into the turn. You may dislike the depth or the stability. Today you cannot improve both.”

Briska put her pencil back behind her ear. She wanted to argue, partly because the captain was right in a way she could not take apart and improve. The iron pigs sat in the hired punt like a collection of very dull judgments.

Pello crouched at the rail and looked down the hull’s wet side.

“Enough?” he asked.

“With the water we should get. If we use it when it is there.”

“What if it is late?” Nescor asked.

Ivenne turned toward him. The young man was bent over his knees, breathing hard after the last lift.

“Then we wait until the mark is wet. We do not sail on somebody’s promise about the hour.”

“And if it doesn’t reach it?”

“We do not take that cut.”

He looked toward the main channel. The workboat had moved closer to the warehouse corner.

“So we give them the ship.”

“We keep people aboard it long enough to consider the next thing. Yes.”

Nescor straightened. He wiped his face with his sleeve, leaving a black line across his cheek.

“I would rather know.”

“That is why you ask me.”

Briska left them and climbed to the mast heel. She could accomplish something there without requiring the sea’s cooperation.

The tabernacle was sound. Its pin had been driven home with a washer thick enough to have been made by someone who expected the mast to come down more than once. The damage was in the tackle. A sheave had worn against the inside of its block until the rope dragged on one edge. Someone had greased the exposed side and ignored the other. It would turn under a light pull. Under the mast’s weight it might lock.

She leaned into the temporary line and let a little strain come onto it. The block made a sound like a person drawing breath through his teeth.

“There,” she said.

Daska, still breathing heavily, came to look.

“What do I do?”

“Hold this. Do not decide to improve its position.”

“I was going to stand here.”

“Excellent. An underappreciated skill.”

Briska opened the block with her smallest punch. The pin would not move at first. She used a folded cloth to protect its head, struck again, then caught it before it could fall between the boards. Daska held the load while she dressed the fouled cheek and scraped away a curl of swollen wood.

Her parent would have replaced the block and sold the old one to somebody whose load weighed less. Briska could hear the explanation with infuriating clarity: the purchaser needed a block, and no one had claimed it would lift a mountain. She had once thought it a good answer. She had been young enough to admire how efficiently the world divided into things that worked and people who had failed to ask the right question.

She turned the damaged face inward, pared a little more away, and tested the fit. The sheave moved freely.

Daska’s hand had begun to tremble.

“How long?”

“Ten breaths.”

“Yours or mine?”

Briska looked at her, then took more of the strain with her shoulder. Daska let out a long breath and reset her grip.

The pin went through. Briska secured it, pulled the rope clear and tested the block again under load. The sheave turned without a catch.

“That will do,” she said.

Daska lowered the line carefully.

Briska touched the small flaw in her own first casting through the fabric of her pocket. The bronze was warm from her body. She did not ask the Breath to mend what she had overlooked. She went back and checked the retaining pin a second time.

Pello had Mallow alongside, with a light guide line coiled at his feet. The coil lay flat. His knife was on the thwart where either hand could reach it.

“You could use the hired punt,” Briska said.

“No.”

“It would save your boat.”

“It would turn slowly.”

She rested both hands on the rail. “You have seen how narrow it is.”

He looked up at her.

“I showed you.”

Briska felt a small, hot embarrassment. She had been talking to him as if he had acquired a boat yesterday and mistaken ownership for knowledge.

“I meant the space if they come alongside.”

“So did I.”

He took the guide line around a cleat and checked its lead. After a moment she knelt and passed down a pad of canvas for the rubbing place. He accepted it. It was the sound canvas he had bought at the Masque sale, cut and doubled for work. She recognized the weave because she had helped him find the holes.

She watched him lash it in place. He used less cord than she would have used, but each turn went where it was needed.

On the White Lark, Ivenne called them to show where they would stand when the mast came down.

Chapter 12 — Waiting for water

The man at the Mulberry yard did not care that Ilaro knew his employer.

“Most people who come here know her,” he said. “It does not make them a load of cloth.”

He stood in the shadow of the landward wicket with a small dog beside his boot. The dog’s expression suggested that it too had heard enough about people who knew the Countess. Behind them, men were rolling a cart beneath a covered gallery. Bundles of reed matting hung from the rafters, giving off a warm grassy smell.

“We are expected,” Rosceline said.

“Then I will have a name.”

“Rosceline.”

The man looked down at the dog. It offered no assistance.

“Ilaro Selci,” Ilaro supplied. “Ullan has instructions concerning a vessel.”

That produced movement. The gatekeeper called through the yard. A stocky woman in a sleeveless coat came from the gallery, drying her hands on a strip of cloth. Her nose had been broken and set crooked.

Ullan read Celise’s copy, which had arrived before them by a boy with unremarkable shoes. She looked at Rosceline’s copy afterward, comparing the last lines.

“Intact,” she said.

“That is our intention.”

“The cargo and the vessel.”

“Both,” Rosceline answered. “Do you require us to arrive intact as well, or is there scope for economy?”

Ullan folded the paper and put it into the pouch at her belt.

“I need to know which gate you are using.”

“The water gate.”

“Most ships do. Which approach?”

Ilaro showed her the little drawing Briska had made on the back of an old notice. It contained only the anchorage, the narrow cut, the arch and the yard. She had crossed out his first version because he had made the turn too wide. He disliked having a drawing that was less attractive than his own and more useful.

Ullan set one blunt finger on the arch.

“Long way round on foot from here. You want the steps behind the dye store.”

“Is the ledge continuous?”

“On the far side. There is a break before the arch. Don’t try it in the dark.”

“We need to check the vessel from behind as she approaches the arch. And I need somewhere I can see both approaches.”

“Use the post at the cut’s upper end for the line. If someone tells you the iron ring beside it is sound, make him put his boat on it. You can watch from the ledge beyond the arch.”

She took them through the yard.

The water gate stood open on a pool colored by the buildings around it. A blue warehouse made one strip dark; the saffron cloth hanging from an upper balcony shone in another. Where neither reflection held, the water was a flat, dirty green. The old arch lay to their left. The main channel opened opposite, around the warehouse corner where Vaska’s men had been waiting.

The yard itself contained two short landings and a broad working platform. A wheeled rack stood near the left gate leaf, loaded with empty wicker hampers. Its position left room for an ordinary lighter. The White Lark would need more of the opening.

“That has to move,” Ilaro said.

Ullan looked at the rack.

“It moves.”

“Before the ship reaches you.”

“I shall mention that to the men pushing it.”

Rosceline gave Ilaro a brief look. He could not tell whether she wanted him to stop irritating their host or continue until something had been settled. He had known her long enough to accept that she might want both.

“Who will be here?” she asked.

Ullan pointed to two workers at the farther landing. One was sharpening a pole’s iron shoe. The other had a crossbow laid across his knees and was rubbing wax into its string. Two more men rested beneath the gallery. Ilaro had taken one for a porter until he saw the sword hilt above the man’s folded coat.

“They stay inside,” Ullan said. “They keep the yard. If your captain comes near enough to throw a line, someone will take it. I am not sending them out to pull her out of a fight.”

“I understand.”

“Make certain she does.”

They went back to the landward wicket. Rosceline remained to arrange the gate and the people who would move the rack. Ilaro took the pale cloth Briska had given him and followed the lane to the dye store.

He found the steps by their smell before he saw them. Old dye had soaked the brickwork, a sharp mineral odor under the stink of exposed mud. He took the branch leading toward the anchorage and found the mooring post at the upper end of the cut. Its base was packed into stone. He pushed against it, felt no movement, then pushed again with his shoulder. His head objected to the effort.

Pello brought Mallow alongside with Nescor aboard. The deckhand held the long checking line coiled over both arms. He passed its standing end up. Ilaro laid it over the post, took the turns Nescor showed him and helped secure it.

“Not round your hand,” Nescor said as Ilaro gathered a loose part.

Ilaro released it. “Thank you.”

“People forget when it starts moving.”

They left the rest in a long, clear coil on the ledge for Nescor to pick up as the cutter passed. He would take it through the stern fairlead and pay it out around the after bitts, checking their forward drift whenever the captain required. The post would remain behind them.

Pello tested the lead from the skiff. Ilaro watched until Nescor was satisfied, then went back up the steps and round by the lane to the farther side of the arch. Here a ledge overlooked both the opening and the pool beyond it. He set his hat on the low wall and sat in its shade until the brightness settled.

It would have been useful to be frightened only of the things currently happening. The high glare on the water had begun bringing back the room with the swinging lamp, the face he had trusted, the peculiar knowledge that he was falling while there was still time to be astonished. He closed his eyes. The sound he could hear belonged to a woman beating a rug on a balcony. Each blow came with a little explosion of dust.

When he opened his eyes, the ledge remained where it had been.

Pello and Nescor came through the arch. They had tied the line’s free end where it could be reached from the cutter’s stern. Pello landed to see Ilaro’s position.

“Here?” he asked.

“Unless you see a better place.”

Pello stood beside the low wall. He crouched, looked through the arch, then moved two paces to his right.

“There. You can see their bow sooner.”

Ilaro moved. The difference was small until he imagined a boat coming out from behind the arch’s near pier. From his first place he would have seen its middle before he saw where it was going.

“Here, then.”

He raised the cloth.

Nescor nodded. “Hold.”

Ilaro lowered it and extended his arm toward the yard.

“Ease on.”

“If the captain says something different?”

“She is aboard. We listen to her.”

Pello took the cloth from him and tested how it showed against the stone. Ilaro had expected him to say that it was too small. He only turned it so the pale face was outward and handed it back.

A small boat came through while they were speaking. An old man stood at its stern with a basket of onions behind his feet. He used a pole to keep the boat off the arch’s right pier, thrust twice in quick succession, then swore when the current took the stern anyway. The hull tapped the stone. An onion rolled out and floated beneath the ledge.

Nescor went to catch the boat, but the old man waved him away and brought it straight.

“Does it always pull there?” Ilaro asked.

“Only when there is water in it.”

Pello watched the boat move into the pool. He pointed to the swirl beside the pier. Ilaro saw it now: water coming from the narrow cut struck the broader flow from the main channel and turned back along the stone. A deep hull would not answer as quickly as the onion boat. Two hulls together would have no room to answer at all.

He made himself look at the actual gap instead of the neat drawing in his pocket.

“Tell Ivenne I will stop her if the other boat enters beside her.”

“She’ll see.”

“She will see from her stern. Tell her where I am looking.”

Pello nodded and returned to Mallow.

When they were gone, Ilaro found a square of shade cast by the arch’s parapet. He moved a loose brick out of it and sat with his back against the wall. The brick had left a cool rectangle in the dirt. He put his palm there, then felt faintly foolish and left it anyway.

Rosceline arrived carrying bread wrapped in paper and a small bottle of water. She took in his position and the pale cloth in his hand.

“Have you occupied the entire ruin?”

“Only the part I require.”

She sat beside him. Her coat caught on a stone and she freed it with a short, bitter tug.

“The rack will be moved. Ullan has been persuaded that we might arrive while she is elsewhere.”

“Was that difficult?”

“She believes boats should arrive when expected.”

“A dangerous conviction.”

She gave him the bread. It held a little hard cheese and something green, which he smelled before biting. Fresh herbs. He had been expecting fish.

“I thought you would like that better.”

“I do.”

They ate without talking for a while. Above them a cart crossed the bridge. Dust sifted from the mortar onto the ledge near Rosceline’s knee. She shifted her skirt clear. Ilaro drank some water and handed her the bottle.

“Would you have taken the six months?” she asked.

He chewed the last of his bread before answering.

“I would rather not.”

“That is not quite what I asked.”

“I know what you asked. I have not yet been made sufficiently afraid to answer it.”

She turned the bottle between her hands. He expected a graceful account of why she had refused. She only drank.

Through the arch, the water had reached the mark Briska had scratched beside the sill. It wet the lower edge and withdrew. Came again, a little higher.

Ilaro stood too quickly. He waited for the ledge to stop seeming to move, then took the position Pello had chosen beside the wall.

At the main-channel mouth, the shallow workboat came away from the warehouse wall. Vaska stood near its bow. She had removed her coat.

Rosceline saw it too.

“I will go to the gate.”

He raised the cloth toward the approaching Mallow, holding it where Pello could see.

Pello looked past him at Vaska. Then he turned the skiff back toward the anchorage, pulling hard enough for the blades to leave little white scars on the water.

Chapter 13 — The mast comes down

Briska felt the mast’s weight before she saw it move.

The tackle tightened across her palm. Above her, the narrow strip of sky beside the spar widened. Ivenne had taken the cutter out of the anchorage under a scrap of sail; now the sail was furled, the boom stowed along the port rail, and Nescor was easing the forward stay. The mast began to turn on its iron heel.

“Slow,” Briska said.

“I have it,” Daska answered.

“Keep having it.”

Daska stood behind her with the hauling part across her hips. The repaired block turned. Briska watched the rope travel through it, watched the mast’s heel stay centered between the iron cheeks, watched the top descend toward its after crutch. It was the sort of work she enjoyed when nobody was trying to stop her.

Pello waited ahead in Mallow. His guide line ran back to the cutter’s bow, slack enough to give him room. He used an oar over the skiff’s stern to keep her straight. Farther out, beyond the mouth of the cut, the evening light had begun to flatten the harbor into bright sheets between dark hulls.

The mast settled into its padded supports with a noise Briska felt through the soles of her feet.

Nescor secured the forward lashing. She secured the after one herself, pulling the binding tight and pressing the knot with her thumb. The shallow cut stung beneath its bandage. She was pleased by the ordinary little pain. Everything she had expected to carry weight was carrying it.

“Down,” she told Ivenne.

The captain had the tiller under her right hand. She looked along the deck, making her own inspection, then gave Pello a short forward gesture.

They entered the cut.

The water changed its sound. Out in the anchorage it had slapped separate pieces of the hull; here the ship’s passage sent a long wash sliding along the brick walls. A wet line rose beneath the ledge on their right. Nets hung above it from a row of old hooks, dry and unused, like scraps of somebody’s shed skin.

Nescor picked up the waiting checking line as the stern passed its coil on the ledge. He brought it through the after fairlead and laid turns around the stern bitts. As the post fell behind them, he paid out line under control. Briska kept her feet clear of the working length.

“Hands inside,” Ivenne said.

Daska took her hand from the rail. She flexed the fingers instead, looking toward the low arch at the bend ahead.

Briska could not see Ilaro yet. She could see the pale cloth for an instant against the farther stone, then Pello’s head crossed her line of sight. The cloth lowered. A forearm extended toward the yard.

“Ease,” Nescor called.

Ivenne let the cutter gather a little movement. Pello drew Mallow to the left to lead the bow clear of a projecting stone. He seemed to be taking a private, infuriating amount of time.

Then he looked over his shoulder and pulled the guide line free of the skiff’s cleat.

Briska saw the other boat.

It had come up from the pool beyond the arch and turned while it still had room. Now it lay tight against the right-hand ledge, bow pointing back toward the arch, stern toward the approaching cutter. Vaska stood near its after thwart with a hooked pole in her hands. Behind her, three men had stopped rowing. A fourth held a short line, ready to cast it.

“Keep coming slowly,” Ivenne said. “No one runs.”

Daska had already moved to the cutter’s starboard side. She caught the hatch coaming to steady herself as the hull turned. Briska crouched beside the mast heel. She needed to stay where she could reach the lowered spar’s lashings without climbing over the checking line.

Vaska lifted her pole.

“That is a very low mast,” she called. “Did you find a bridge you wanted to steal?”

“Get clear of the bow,” Ivenne answered.

“We can help you turn.”

“I am turning exactly as I intend.”

Vaska smiled and thrust the hook toward the guide line.

Pello dropped his end. The rope struck the water and sank away from her pole. She withdrew it promptly, letting the Mallow slip past the workboat’s bow rather than reaching too far and giving him her weight. One of her rowers pushed them off the ledge. The shallow hull backed toward them as the White Lark continued forward. Its stern came alongside the cutter’s starboard bow.

“Off,” Daska said.

A man threw a loop over the cutter’s forward cleat. Daska stamped down on the line before it could draw tight and kicked it overboard. The man swore. Vaska put her hook through a rail opening farther aft and pulled.

The workboat struck them with a hollow bang.

Briska lost sight of the arch. A shock ran down the mast cradles. She caught the binding she had just tightened and felt the long spar shift against it.

“Do not let them turn us,” Ivenne said.

Nescor took strain on the checking line, arresting the cutter’s forward movement enough to keep the workboat from slewing her broadside. A little water came over the low starboard edge of the other boat. Vaska moved her feet without looking down.

Her hook stayed caught. She passed the pole back to a man and put one hand on the cutter’s rail.

Daska drove the heel of her palm at the fingers. Vaska let go before the blow landed. She came up again two paces farther aft, where the lowered mast narrowed the deck and Daska had to turn sideways to reach her.

Briska grabbed the short boat hook lying beside the hatch and thrust its blunt end toward Vaska’s chest.

The woman caught the shaft. She pulled hard enough to bring Briska onto her knees, then let it go when Daska struck at her wrist. The boat hook clattered into the bottom of the workboat.

Briska felt fury before fear. It had been an entirely serviceable hook.

A loop of the mast tackle fell across the starboard rail, caught between the hulls and drew taut. The block slammed sideways. Briska’s thumb slipped under the standing line, and the squeeze drove the breath out of her.

She tore her hand free. Blood showed through the loosened bandage.

“Daska. Knife.”

Daska had no hand to give her. Vaska had got a knee onto the rail, and the monk caught her at the elbow, trying to fold her arm inward before she found a foothold.

Briska reached for her own blade. The rope vibrated where it was trapped. If the workboat pulled away, it would drag the mast tackle after it; if she cut the wrong part, the spar would come loose over Ivenne’s head.

She found the hauling end beyond the secured lashing and pressed her knife against it. The first fibers parted. The line jumped, sawing across the wood beneath it. She cut again with the edge against her thumb’s side, felt the blade bite the last strands, and fell backward as the tension vanished.

The loose rope snapped into the workboat. A rower ducked. The mast stayed in its crutches.

For one second Briska could hear nothing but the hard, ugly sound of her own breathing.

Then Vaska’s boot came down on the cutter’s deck.

Chapter 14 — A boat of his own

Daska gave ground because there was nowhere useful to stand.

Vaska came over the rail low, knife held near her hip. She had no room for a sweeping cut and did not attempt one. The point went toward Daska’s belly. Daska turned it with the outside of her forearm, caught the knife wrist and drove her knee forward.

Vaska took the knee against her thigh instead of her stomach. She struck Daska beneath the ear with her free hand.

The sound was small. Daska’s head snapped sideways. Briska saw her eyes lose their fixed attention for an instant, then find it again.

Nescor started toward them.

“Line,” Ivenne said.

He stayed at the after bitts. The checking rope tightened, restraining the stern while the current carried the bow onward. The other boat pressed against the stern quarter, forcing the cutter toward the ledge.

Briska looked down at the mast binding. The cut tackle lay in a loose heap around her boots. She pulled it clear of the passage, rolled the useful length under the mast and shoved the knife back into its sheath. There was nothing in reach she could strike Vaska with without striking Daska first.

The woman’s scarred hand closed over Daska’s wrist. Briska had seen it laid easily on a boat’s gunwale that morning. Now the old burn drew white against the skin as Vaska pulled the monk off balance and tried to put her across the lowered mast.

Daska let her come close.

She drove her forehead into Vaska’s mouth.

Vaska recoiled. Blood struck the mast. Daska caught the knife wrist with both hands, stepped around its outside and drove it down against the rail. The knife stayed in Vaska’s grip. She made a harsh sound, half laugh and half pain, and brought her boot down toward Daska’s instep.

Briska seized the loose end of a binding and threw it over Vaska’s shoulder. It caught nothing. Vaska turned far enough to see her, and that gave Daska the space to push.

The smuggler went backward onto her own gunwale. A man caught her coat. She dropped into the workboat, taking Daska’s sleeve with her until the fabric tore.

“Very intimate,” Vaska said, spitting blood over the side. “Shall we do the rest standing up?”

Daska did not answer. She held her right hand against her jaw and looked as though she was deciding whether her teeth still belonged where she had left them.

The workboat’s forward man put his hook over the cutter’s after rail.

Ivenne moved the tiller. The cutter did not answer quickly enough. The workboat’s hook drew their after quarter toward the stone. The checking line kept them from escaping forward, and the gap beside the ledge narrowed.

Pello brought Mallow into that gap.

Briska saw his intention too late to call it anything. He cut across the workboat’s bow, rowing hard, his stern almost touching the cutter. He thrust an oar against the stone and forced the White Lark’s after quarter outward with Mallow’s padded side.

For a moment it worked.

The cutter turned a hand’s breadth away from the ledge. Ivenne eased the tiller. Nescor let the checking line run a little, allowing the stern to follow the bow.

Then the workboat’s bow caught Mallow at an angle.

The sound was nothing like the earlier contact between the larger hulls. It was a hard crack, followed by a splintering rush. The skiff’s forward boards folded inward. Water came through them in a dark sheet.

Pello dropped the oar and caught the ledge.

His left forearm slid along the rope he had thrown there. The skin stripped red. One boot stayed in the skiff, dragging him sideways as its bow sank.

Daska lunged across the narrow gap and caught his collar.

Nescor put the checking line into its prepared turns and came to help. Together they hauled Pello’s chest onto the stone. His trapped foot tore loose. He struck the ledge on one hip, rolled, and clung there coughing with his face against an old patch of lime.

Briska had stood without knowing it. She could see into Mallow. The folded canvas floated up beneath the thwart. Pello’s knife had gone into the water. The skiff’s stern remained clear, wedged against the lowest of three wash steps cut into the ledge.

“Pello,” she said.

He lifted his head.

Nescor threw him a short rope. He caught it, pushed himself to his knees and looped it around the step’s iron handhold. The sinking skiff drew against it and stopped.

A bang came from behind Briska.

The workboat had struck the cutter’s after quarter again. One of its men fell against an iron rowlock and came up gripping his arm. The lowered mast jumped in its rear crutch. The crutch’s side split where an old knot had weakened the wood; the spar rolled the few inches its tight lashings permitted.

Ivenne had been turning to look at Pello.

The spar hit her high on the left shoulder.

She dropped against the tiller bench. Her right hand missed the wood. The tiller swung free and the cutter’s stern began to turn.

Daska left the rail.

“Here,” Ivenne said through her teeth. “Get here.”

The monk stepped over the mast and took the tiller.

“Not that way. Toward me. A little. Stop.”

Daska held it. Ivenne’s left arm lay against her body at a wrong angle, though Briska could not see what was wrong with it. The captain’s face had lost its color.

“Can you breathe?” Briska asked.

“Later.”

Nescor returned to the after bitts. A man on the workboat threw his hook again. It skated along the cutter’s rail and struck Nescor above the right ear.

Blood ran into his eye.

He flinched, caught the rail, and remained on his feet. Briska snatched the cloth from around her neck and pushed it into his hand.

“Your head. Tie it.”

“I have the line.”

“It is on the bitts. You have a moment.”

He pressed the cloth to the cut. His fingers shook as he pulled its ends behind his skull. Briska tied the knot for him, harder than he would have liked. He swore once and went back to work.

Below them, something struck wood.

A second blow followed, heavier.

Briska looked through the open hatch. Chest nine had pulled one of its lashing staples from the timber along the hold’s side. It had slid across the wet floor and struck the foot of the ladder. The next movement would bring it against the lower row of chests, which were still secured.

“Nescor. When she steadies.”

He looked down. “I see it.”

The cutter rolled again. Briska went down the ladder before the chest could move. She dropped onto the floor with one knee beside it, braced her shoulder against the end and felt its weight come alive under her. Above, Nescor secured the checking line before coming down.

She could not hold it.

For a sick instant she imagined her leg caught between the silver and the hull. Then Nescor was beside her, pushing from the other corner. Together they moved the chest far enough for her to kick a loose wedge under its runner.

“Stay,” she told it.

Nescor barked a laugh. Blood dripped from the edge of his cloth onto the lid.

Briska pulled the spare binding through the sound ring behind the chest. Nescor hauled it tight while she drove the wedge home with the heel of her hand. The chest settled against it. She tested the knot, then tested it again, too frightened now to resent the repetition.

Above them, Ivenne gave an instruction in a voice that was scarcely audible.

“Daska,” Briska called. “What did she say?”

“Hold where we are.”

The checking line creaked.

Briska climbed halfway up the ladder. Through the hatch she saw the arch directly ahead, its dark mouth close enough for individual shells on the stone to show. The pale cloth stood above the farther ledge, held rigid in Ilaro’s hand.

Nescor pushed past her and went aft to the bitts. The line strained across his palms. He took another turn, keeping his hands clear.

The ship stopped moving forward.

The water did not.

Chapter 15 — Wait

Ilaro held the cloth up until his shoulder began to burn.

He could see why Ivenne wanted to keep coming. The yard stood open beyond the pool. Men waited at the landing with ropes. There was room for the White Lark inside, and almost no room where she was.

While the cutter slowed, Vaska’s rowers had carried their smaller boat forward along her right side. Its bow now projected far enough ahead to enter the arch beside her. The current that had turned the onion boat would catch both hulls there. The larger ship would press the smaller against the pier; the smaller would drive the larger’s stern sideways. Ilaro did not know which would break first. He knew that if they arrived together, the difference would not help anyone aboard.

Nescor had taken the line. The White Lark held short of the opening.

Vaska looked up at Ilaro.

“Let them through,” she called.

He kept the cloth raised.

She followed his raised cloth to Nescor at the cutter’s stern, then looked along the taut line leading back up the cut. Ilaro held her gaze when she turned toward him again.

“We can take them before your gate,” she said. “Your employer does not need to be troubled.”

“She will survive the inconvenience.”

“Will the captain?”

Ilaro looked past her.

Daska stood at the tiller. Ivenne sat beside her with her head lowered and her left arm cradled against her. Blood marked the front of Nescor’s shirt. Briska had come out of the hold and was fastening something beneath the mast.

He had not seen the blow that hurt the captain. He had heard the breaking boat and had seen Pello on the ledge afterward. There were gaps in what had happened within a few dozen paces of him. He wanted someone to tell him the order, the injuries, which things could still be relied upon. There was no time to obtain it.

He watched the bow of Vaska’s boat.

“Back her away,” he said. “Then they can come through.”

She grinned, blood dark on her lower lip.

“You have a very quiet idea of whose boat this is.”

At the yard entrance, Rosceline had gone behind the left gate leaf. Ilaro could no longer see her. The wheeled rack still stood near it, though the hampers had been taken down. It would catch the cutter’s broadest part if she entered at the angle the current gave her.

He turned far enough to call.

“The rack.”

No answer came. He faced the cut again before he lost the workboat’s position.

Vaska spoke to her rowers. Two men dug their oars against the water, trying to turn the bow across the cutter’s path. The workboat shifted. Its side struck the ledge with a thud, and one of the men shouted that they could not bring the after oar round.

The shallow boat was trapped between the ledge and the White Lark’s starboard bow. It could back out. It could not turn across without giving the current more of its side.

Vaska knew it. Ilaro saw her take the measure of the gap and the height of the cutter’s rail. She looked at Daska, who was holding the tiller while the injured captain spoke close to her ear. She looked at the crossbowman now standing inside the Mulberry gate.

Ilaro’s arm shook. He lowered his elbow against his ribs without lowering the cloth.

Pello came along the ledge behind the workboat. He moved with one hand against the wall. His sleeve was wet and red from elbow to wrist. At the break in the ledge before the arch, he stopped and looked down. He would have to use the worn footholds or go back to the lane.

“Go round,” Ilaro called.

Pello looked up at him.

“The lane. Don’t climb.”

Pello’s face tightened. He glanced at Mallow, wedged at the wash steps behind him, then turned away from the broken edge. He disappeared through a narrow opening beneath the net hooks.

The tide mark beside Ilaro’s knee stood clear of the water now.

He had been staring at the boats. He had missed the first fall.

“Captain,” he called.

Ivenne looked up with visible effort.

“We have lost water.”

“I know.”

He wanted to tell her that he would not hold them a moment longer than necessary. The words were useless. He kept the cloth raised and looked at Vaska.

“You can take your boat back out,” he said.

“And yours?”

“The captain can still bring her through.”

“If I wait?”

He did not answer. Vaska’s smile changed, losing its enjoyment. She turned to look at the White Lark’s hull where the water met it. At this distance the narrow dark band newly exposed along the side was plain.

For the first time, Ilaro understood that she might be more angry with the thieves for making her choose than for taking the ship.

A crash came from the yard. He glanced back.

Rosceline had found the man who owned the rack’s key. The man stood holding it while she and a porter dragged the emptied frame backward. One wheel refused to turn. She had taken off her coat and thrown it over the gate’s stone footing; her sleeve was caught above her elbow, showing the pale line of an old scar. Ullan came toward them from the gallery.

Rosceline called Ullan’s name with such force that Ilaro heard it clearly, though none of the words around it reached him.

Ullan stooped, put her shoulder beneath the rack’s jammed corner and lifted. The porter pulled. Rosceline kicked a loose wedge out of the wheel’s path. The frame lurched backward into the yard, taking a strip of skin from the porter’s hand as it went.

Ilaro turned back.

“Put it down.”

He stared at her.

“Down.”

She wiped her mouth with the back of her scarred hand and looked up at Ilaro.

“If you sink her after this, I will take it personally.”

He did not attempt a reply.

Another held his upper arm tucked close to his body; blood had run to his elbow, though Ilaro had not seen him wounded.

Ilaro lowered the cloth.

He extended his arm toward the yard.

Nescor eased the checking line. The White Lark moved.

Briska stood at the lowered mast, looking upward along its length. The spar’s top passed under the arch with so little room that Ilaro could not stop himself bending his own head.

Part IV — What they can keep

Chapter 16 — All twelve

The silver was prettier in daylight than any of the people waiting to claim it.

Rosceline stood beside the open hold and watched a man lift the corner of a cloth. Pale bars lay underneath, snug in their wooden divisions. A little water had reached the cloth from somebody’s sleeve. The metal itself was dry. It looked very much as it had when they had expected to sell it for six thousand marks.

She had slept in a chair in the Mulberry yard’s office. Her left cheek bore the pattern of a seam, and her hip had stiffened while she slept until getting out of the chair required a private negotiation conducted in language unsuitable for clerks. She had washed at the yard pump, put her hair up again, and borrowed a clean collar from a woman who had insisted on seeing the dirty one first. That woman now stood by the scale, waiting to finish the inventory so her own work could resume.

The Countess had brought a clerk, Celise and two guards. A further pair stood at the water entrance. Beyond their shoulders Rosceline could see the broad channel shining between warehouses. The little arch and the place where Mallow had been crushed lay out of sight, behind the salt cut’s bend.

Pello had gone there before dawn with the repair yard’s men. He was back now. His cuffs were wet. He did not look down at the silver.

Lyra arrived through the street gate with Vaska and a woman carrying a narrow inspection case. She wore a plain coat, darkened at one shoulder by the damp morning air. “Mavet will look at the damage,” Lyra told the Countess. The woman crossed to the ship without waiting for an answer.

“Has the loose chest been moved?” she asked.

“Wedged where it stopped,” Briska said. “The new line is sound. The old staple isn’t.”

“I’ll look at both.”

Lyra glanced at Vaska. Vaska went up the gangboard first. She used the rail lightly with her scarred hand and paused where Daska stood beside the hatch.

“You steer better when someone tells you how.”

“I had an excellent teacher.”

“Did she enjoy it?”

“You can ask her when she can sit up without swearing.”

Vaska looked toward the office. Ivenne had been carried upstairs there the previous night, and the surgeon had stayed long enough to make everybody who had been trying to help feel poorly qualified. Nescor sat on the stair beneath her room, his head bound in fresh white linen. He raised two fingers when he saw Vaska looking and then, apparently reconsidering the gesture, lowered one.

Vaska laughed once and descended into the hold.

The Countess waited until Lyra stood beside her.

“Good morning.”

“Is it?”

“The ship is afloat.”

“For which I am to congratulate you?”

“I was going to suggest we inspect it.”

Lyra’s gaze passed over Rosceline and stayed a moment on Pello’s wet cuffs. Then she took the gangboard.

In the hold, the air was close with worked timber and the sourness of clothes dried badly. They had opened the forward hatch for light. A blade of sun crossed the chest nearest the ladder and showed a fresh scrape in its lid. Rosceline remained at the hatch with Ilaro while the people below did the work. She wanted very much to lean against something. Everything within reach had recently acquired grease.

Vaska read the numbers aloud as the clerk checked them. The Countess’s woman watched the weighing. At the first disturbed seal, Ilaro passed down the cargo slip. Briska’s penciled marks accounted for that chest and number seven; the other ten still bore their original seals. Lyra opened the chests with the shipmaster’s keys and examined the packing, lifting samples into the light where she could see the stamps. She made no attempt to conceal the stiffness with which she used one arm. When a lid resisted, she told Vaska to hold it.

There was a small pause at number nine. Mavet put her fingers beside the pulled staple and looked at the timber around it.

“This will need a new fastening in sound wood.”

“I’ve left enough room,” Briska said from the hatch.

“I can see that.”

Briska closed her mouth.

The count finished at twelve. The weights agreed. There was no triumphant announcement. The clerk drew a line beneath the last figure and blew gently on the ink. Lyra stayed below long enough to examine the damaged fastening again. Then she climbed out.

A shaft of sun caught her as she reached the deck. For a moment Rosceline expected it to improve the morning. Instead she saw the edge of Lyra’s shadow spread across the hatch coaming and had the unpleasant impression that something below had moved toward her foot. She looked down. A corner of the wet cloth was sliding in the draught.

She moved her shoe anyway.

The shipmaster had been waiting on the quay. He was a thick-necked man with a very clean shirt, which Rosceline resented until she remembered why he had been able to change it. He went aboard at Lyra’s nod and stopped beside the lowered mast. His face grew tight.

“Who cut this?”

“Me,” Briska said.

He looked at the severed rope, then at her.

“It was fouled under load,” she added. “The heel pin and the crutches held. You’ll want the after crutch reset before you raise it.”

“I will want several things.”

He went below. His voice came back up the companionway.

“Where is my wine?”

Nobody answered quickly enough.

Rosceline looked at the Countess. A corner of the veil moved against her mouth.

“Among the damages,” Rosceline said.

They used the yard office because the shipmaster wanted his cabin back.

The window stood open over the basin. Men were moving a freight barge at the next landing; their calls reached the room in short bursts, interrupted by the scrape of its fenders against the stone. Celise set the written terms on the desk and weighted one corner with an ink bottle. The Countess sat in the chair Rosceline had slept in. Rosceline remained standing and hoped the seam would be equally unkind to everyone.

Lyra read the page. Vaska stood at her shoulder. The inspection case rested by Mavet’s boots, ready for the next place that required her attention.

“Six,” Lyra said.

“Six unloadings next month,” the Countess replied. “At the ordinary rate.”

“You wanted eight.”

“I wanted twelve. We have both had an opportunity to reconsider.”

“Named vessels. Named dates. Your carriers use the landing. They do not keep a watch there.”

“The carriers need room to work.”

“They will have it.”

“And the loads will not be held while somebody finds an old question about the sender.”

Lyra looked up. The room seemed cooler than it had before she entered, though the sun still lay warm on Rosceline’s right wrist.

“If your men bring what you have named, on the days you have named, it will go through. If you send armed men in place of carriers, they stay outside.”

The Countess drew the sheet toward her. “Put that in.”

Celise did so. Her pen scratched through a lull in the noise outside.

Rosceline could see Lyra looking past the desk at the ship. The silver was due elsewhere; its intended recipient had sent a messenger that morning, a damp, angry man whom the gatekeeper had made wait beneath the wrong awning. Rosceline had heard him asking whether someone proposed to reimburse three days of idle men. Vaska had taken him away before he reached the office. He remained in the yard now, conspicuously holding his hat.

“How soon can it move?” Lyra asked Mavet.

“Under tow, today. The mast needs a new line and the crutch shifted. I would have the shipwright look at the rail before putting her outside the harbor.”

“The original repair was complete,” Rosceline offered.

Lyra turned toward her.

Rosceline felt the beginning of a much longer account rise into her throat: the agreed berth movement, the sound chain, the generous care they had taken of a vessel whose owner had given them so little reason to oblige her. She swallowed it.

“The rudder works,” she finished.

“I have been told.”

The Countess waited. Rosceline had once admired how she could make a person fill a silence with something expensive. This morning she found the habit oppressive.

Lyra touched the lower section of the page.

“Who is Nescor?”

“The deckhand,” Ilaro said. “On the stair.”

“I know where he is. Who hired him?”

“We did. Captain Rasc agreed his terms with us.”

“He is included in this?”

“So is she.”

Lyra read the line again. The agreement named the five and their two hires. Return of the ship and cargo, with the stated repairs and treatment, ended Lyra’s demands for payment or service arising from their theft. Her people would not detain them for it or collect the same demand through another landing. There was no promise concerning the next thing Rosceline might do.

Vaska’s eyes moved from the page to Ilaro. “You were busy with that cloth.”

“I had a good view.”

“You had one view.”

He considered her for a moment. “It was the one we needed.”

Lyra put her finger beside the repair sum. “The guard’s treatment stays.”

“It is there,” Celise said.

“His wrist is broken.”

Daska had been standing by the window. She turned from the barge below.

“I broke it.”

Lyra looked at her. Daska’s scraped knuckles rested against the sill. She did not put them behind her back.

“Solren will be paid while he cannot work,” Lyra said. “You are paying the treatment. That does not buy a visit to him.”

“I didn’t ask for one.”

“Then we understand each other.”

Rosceline disliked the relief she felt when Lyra returned to the page. She had been afraid Daska would apologize, and equally afraid that she would not.

The Countess took off one glove to sign. Her hand was narrow and older than the veil allowed strangers to expect. She held the paper flat until the ink had settled. Lyra signed after her, with no title beneath her name.

Celise passed the copies across the desk. Lyra took hers; Ilaro received the company’s and put it inside the stiff back of his notebook, where the edges would not fold. Outside, Vaska called to the men waiting by the street gate. They moved toward the ship without drawing their weapons. The Court’s guards stepped aside at the gangboard.

The Countess did not speak until the shipmaster had accepted his keys.

“The delivery is complete,” she said.

Celise set a leather bag on the desk. Fifteen hundred marks, the weight of an entire lost afternoon made inconvenient to carry.

Rosceline watched Lyra watch the money.

“You could have had six months’ use of us,” Rosceline said.

Lyra’s mouth changed very slightly. “I have seen two days.”

Vaska laughed outside the door. Rosceline could not decide whether she had waited there for that purpose.

Lyra took her copy of the agreement and left. The Countess remained by the window, watching the disputed ship become an ordinary ship surrounded by people who knew what to do with it. Mavet was already telling someone to put a support beneath the damaged rail. Ullan waved the anchorage keeper’s punt through the gate, and the removed ballast went back into the hold under the shipmaster’s direction. The messenger followed the master to the hatch, shaking his wet hat at him.

Rosceline would have liked a few words acknowledging the difficulty of bringing the vessel here. She had them ready herself, should the Countess require assistance.

Instead Serafine said, “The six dates will be useful.”

“They cost us a boat.”

“The boat can be repaired.”

“Yes,” Rosceline said. “I have been assured of that by people who own other boats.”

The Countess looked at her, then toward Pello. He stood in the doorway with his left forearm loosely dressed and his eyes on the basin.

“Take your money,” she said, more quietly.

Rosceline took the bag. She needed both hands.

Chapter 17 — The cost of wanting it

Mallow lay on trestles in a yard that smelled of hot pitch and split oak. Without water beneath it, the boat seemed indecently small.

Briska had seen the bow fold in the cut. She had not seen what it looked like after men finished hauling it clear. The cracked planks spread from the stem like ribs prized apart. Mud had dried inside, with a little bed of broken reeds trapped beneath the forward seat. One of Pello’s oars lay along the grass beside it. The blade was sound. She was absurdly glad of that.

The boatwright waited while Pello looked.

She was a broad woman in a man’s old coat with its cuffs cut off, and her fingers were stained yellow around the nails. She had no particular interest in how the damage had happened. A boat had been delivered into her yard, the salvage men had been paid, and it occupied the place where she had intended to put a fisherman’s skiff. She wanted to know what work she was being asked to do.

Briska crouched by the broken stem.

“You could bring the new fastening farther aft,” she said. “Spread the load over this frame instead of carrying it into the end grain. There’s room if you cut the—”

“Briska,” Pello said.

She stopped.

He had not raised his voice. She looked up at him and then back into the boat. The little hook he had put under the thwart for his coat was still there. He had made it from a bent nail and filed the point away. Beside it, his knife had worn a shallow place in the wood where he had cut bread.

“What do you want kept?” she asked.

Pello lowered himself onto the bench beside the trestles. It took him a moment to arrange his left arm without pulling the dressing.

“The run of her. The seats where they are. I don’t want a different boat.”

“The bow has to be new,” the boatwright said.

“I can see that.”

“I ask because I have built a new boat round a man’s favorite plank before. He was pleased. I was poor.”

Pello put his good hand on the sound side.

“This can stay?”

She stepped closer and pressed the plank with her thumb. Then she took a small hammer from her pocket and tapped along it. The sound changed beneath the after seat. She tapped again.

“That fastening comes out. The plank can stay if it hasn’t split inside. I’ll tell you when I lift it. I won’t promise from here.”

Briska liked her better for that, though she would have preferred the answer to be yes.

They went over the boat together. Pello spoke more than he had at any point since the cut, describing the tendency to fall away when lightly loaded and the place where the forward seat caught his knee if he turned too quickly. The boatwright listened without offering to improve every defect. Briska held the sound oar against the side while they checked the remaining clearance.

A boy passed carrying a pot of black compound with both hands. He stopped to watch them.

“What hit it?”

“Another boat,” Pello said.

“Was it bigger?”

Pello looked at the ruin of his bow.

“Yes.”

The boy went on, satisfied with the explanation.

Briska found a strip of torn canvas beneath the after seat. It was part of the cover Pello had bought at the sale after the Masque. The cloth had been sound then, although he had argued over its three little holes as if they were wounds in a hired horse. She drew it free and shook out the mud.

“It can still cover the stern,” she said.

He took it. “I’ll wash it.”

The boatwright named the repair price. Briska knew enough about what lay opened before them to find it reasonable. She wished the woman had left some foolish extra in it, something they could refuse together.

“Four hundred and eighty,” Pello repeated.

“Assuming the plank holds when I lift it. The salvage is separate.”

“Paid,” Briska said.

“Then I need half for timber and a place to work. You’ll owe the rest when you take her. Three weeks if the drying goes well. Four if it doesn’t.”

Pello stared at the boat. Briska could hear a plane working on the other side of the yard, a long soft rasp followed by the short click of its return. Somebody was singing badly in time with it.

“I’ll pay it,” he said.

“We’ll pay it,” Briska told him.

He turned. “I know where the money comes from.”

She felt the correction in her face. “I meant now. Ilaro brought the bag.”

“I know.”

She took the scrap of canvas while he stood. This time she waited to be asked before folding it.

Ivenne had chosen a room above a sailmaker’s house, where a woman she knew could keep the bed made and bring food without requiring a report on how she felt about it. The surgeon had inspected the sling again that morning. Her left arm lay against her body; the collar of her shirt had been opened and folded back where it rubbed.

Briska came with Daska and the money. Rosceline had proposed visiting as well, but Ivenne had sent word that two visitors were enough, and she would rather choose which two.

The room was warm with the smell of clean linen. A square of sun lay on the coverlet beyond Ivenne’s feet. On the floor beside the bed stood one sea boot, upright. The other lay on its side. Briska picked it up before noticing Ivenne watching her.

“You can leave them like that.”

“They were in the way.”

“Of my walking?”

Briska set the second boot beside the first. “Of mine.”

Ivenne looked pleased by the answer. Daska moved a stool near the bed and sat.

“I brought your coat,” she said.

“Did you find my pipe?”

“In the pocket.”

“Good. I thought I’d been robbed.”

Daska unfolded the coat carefully. There was a black smear along its shoulder and a tear where the lining had caught. She laid it over the chair within Ivenne’s reach and put the pipe on the little table.

Briska took out the money. The agreed hire, the additional time, the allowance for the weeks she could not work. Treatment had been paid separately. Ivenne counted with her good hand, sliding the coins against the edge of the table. When one rolled onto the bed, she told Daska to leave it where it was until she had finished the others.

“No deduction for the rudder,” she said.

“You kept it off the wall,” Briska answered.

“Your monk kept it off the wall. I told her which wall.”

Daska looked down at her hands. The knuckles of her right hand were still scratched. A little dirt had worked beneath the dressing.

“I thought you were going over,” Daska said.

“I nearly did.”

“I couldn’t reach you and keep hold.”

“You kept hold.”

For a while neither spoke. Ivenne pushed the coin on the bed toward the edge with two fingers. Daska caught it and put it with the others.

“Will you take another pilot’s work?” Ivenne asked her.

“Only if they let me choose the channel.”

“That isn’t always available.”

“No. I’ve noticed.”

The captain leaned back. The movement pulled at her shoulder and she stopped before she found a comfortable position. Briska could see the effort she made to keep her breathing level. Daska stood and offered to shift the pillow.

“Under the elbow,” Ivenne said. “Not the shoulder. There. Leave it.”

Daska left it. She sat again without asking whether that was better.

Ivenne closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, she looked directly at Briska.

“Do you intend to spend the afternoon telling me the tackle would have held if people had stood somewhere else?”

“No.”

“Good. I would rather have the window opened.”

Briska got up and did it. Below, someone was beating a sail with a flat stick. The blows came slowly, then quickened as the man found a part that offended him.

“The mast stayed on its crutches,” she said, before she could help herself.

Ivenne looked at her.

“I wasn’t going to tell you why.”

Daska bent over her knees laughing. Ivenne shook her head, began to smile, and held her ribs still while the smile became something that hurt too much to complete.

“Go,” she said. “Both of you. Let a woman take her medicine in peace.”

They left the money within her reach. At the door Daska touched the pipe once, making certain it would not roll when the window wind reached it.

Nescor waited downstairs on the sailmaker’s bench. His bandage sat over his right temple, giving him the appearance of a man who had dressed for a medical appointment and decided to make a day of it. He was eating a pear with a small knife.

“Did she take the money?”

“She counted it,” Briska said.

“She does that.”

They paid him as well. He wiped his fingers on the clean side of a cloth before taking the coins, then set aside enough for the room where he had slept. The remainder went into a purse inside his shirt.

“I said I would stay until the vessel was delivered,” he told them.

“You did,” Daska said.

“I’d like the next delivery made to the first place.”

“So would I.”

He cut another piece from the pear and considered them while he ate it.

“Are you doing another one?”

“Not today,” Briska said.

“Then I’ll finish this.”

He returned his attention to the fruit. They went out into the lane, and Daska began laughing again. Briska did not ask her to explain.

The five met at a covered stall where the cook would permit a table to be occupied if somebody kept buying something. Ilaro had ordered a pot of tea. Rosceline had ordered enough sweet bread to establish that they were not merely using the place. Pello sat with the rolled scrap of canvas beside his cup.

Briska showed Ilaro the boatwright’s undertaking. He put it with the other paid bills and laid a hand over the pile to stop a breeze scattering them.

“Four weeks?” Rosceline said.

“If it needs four,” Pello answered.

“There are boats we can hire in the meantime.”

“I know there are boats.”

Rosceline touched the handle of her cup. She appeared about to say something, changed her mind, and took a drink instead.

Briska sat down. Her left thumb throbbed beneath the dressing. It had done so all morning, faithfully reminding her of the first piece of the job she had thought finished.

Ilaro waited until the cook had brought the second plate. Then he laid five small heaps on a folded cloth.

“Ninety-one each.”

Rosceline stared at hers.

“That is a disappointing number to pronounce.”

“The preparation and the rest of the hire have been paid. The treatment, the damage, the salvage. I’ve put aside the second half for the boat.”

“Ninety-one.”

“You may count it.”

“I don’t doubt your arithmetic, Ilaro. I’m objecting to what it has achieved.”

Daska took her share and tipped it into her purse. She left one coin on the cloth for the tea and bread.

Pello did not touch his yet. “The second half stays separate?”

“With your name on it,” Ilaro said. “The yard gets it when you accept the work.”

“Give it to me.”

Ilaro brought out the second purse and passed it over. Pello put it inside his coat, apart from the ninety-one marks he now gathered into his usual purse.

Briska could feel the place in her own workshop where the lifting bench would have stood. She had cleared nothing to make room for it. She had merely moved things there in her mind until it had begun to seem an inconvenience that the bench was absent. Ninety-one marks would buy the metal for a better screw. The rest would still be her old trestles and her knee under the load.

She took her share.

Rosceline broke a piece from the sweet bread. There was orange peel in it and enough butter to make the fingers shine. She ate it with visible resentment, then took another piece.

“The wine was good,” Daska said.

“It was excellent,” Rosceline replied.

Pello looked at them. He shook his head, but he accepted the bread when Daska passed it.

The cook came to collect the empty plate and watched Ilaro tying the papers together.

“Successful business?”

Rosceline looked up with her mouth full. Briska waited, expecting a description that would make the silver seem almost theirs again.

“Busy,” Rosceline said, after she swallowed.

The cook looked at the five purses, nodded, and asked whether they wanted more bread.

Chapter 18 — Other passengers

The public ferry took its own time coming back.

Rosceline could see it making for the opposite steps, a low boat with a patch of white paint at the stern and more people aboard than seemed desirable. One of those people carried a chair. Another carried a birdcage covered in blue cloth. The ferryman had arranged them so that nobody fell into the water, but apparently felt no corresponding duty to make them arrive quickly.

Three days had passed since the settlement. Mallow remained on the trestles, its damaged bow stripped back far enough to make Pello stop speaking when he saw it. The boatwright had let him test the sound plank himself before she put it aside to be reused. It lay under cover now with his seats and the small hook for his coat.

They had gone together that afternoon to approve the first fitting. Rosceline had stayed outside the boat while Briska and the boatwright discussed a hole whose position appeared to invite strongly held opinions. Daska had helped Pello lift one of the seats. Ilaro had found shade and sat with a yard cat that wanted his hand kept exactly where it had put it. It had been a tolerable afternoon until they discovered that the ferry had just left.

“I could have hired a boat,” Rosceline said.

Pello watched the far landing.

“We would have been home by now.”

“Then hire one next time.”

His left sleeve was rolled above the dressing. The rope burn had begun to itch; Rosceline had seen him reaching for it, stopping himself, then finding something else for that hand to do. He tucked the rolled canvas beneath his sound arm.

She looked away first. Beside the ferry steps, a woman was selling hot chickpeas from a pot wrapped in sacking. Rosceline bought a paper twist and gave it to Pello. He looked inside, then at her.

“I wanted some,” she said.

He held it out. She took a few. The pepper caught her at the back of the throat, and she had to turn away to cough while Daska laughed at something a child had said about the chair on the approaching ferry.

The woman with the pot offered water. Rosceline declined too quickly, reconsidered, and accepted the ladle.

By the time she had recovered, the ferry was at the steps and nobody aboard seemed willing to decide who should get out first. The chair’s owner moved it across the landing, put it down and returned for a bundle he had left beneath the seat. He blocked the steps doing both. The ferryman waited with the pole planted, looking over the man’s head at a point far out in the harbor.

“Have you brought anything else?” he asked when the bundle appeared.

“My sister has the cushion.”

“Of course she has.”

The sister came down carrying the cushion and a sleeping child. Daska reached for the cushion; the woman gave her the child instead. For a moment Daska stood completely still with it against her chest. Then she adjusted her hold and carried it ashore while the woman wrestled the cushion past the chair.

Rosceline watched the child’s damp curls rest against Daska’s shoulder. The monk said something quietly to it. It slept through the remark and through being handed back. Daska returned with a line of drool on her sleeve.

“Your reputation is ruined,” Rosceline told her.

“That one won’t remember.”

They boarded with a woman taking two baskets of washing to the western steps and an old man whose covered cage smelled sharply of feathers. A boy carried a pail between his knees. Rosceline wanted the seat clear of the washing, but it faced into the full sun. She let Ilaro take the shade beneath the ferryman’s little awning and sat beside the baskets.

The boat pushed out. Water slapped the boards under her shoes. It was a small, irregular sound, and she found herself checking where it came in before remembering that the vessel had a person paid to worry about that.

Pello sat near the bow. His knees were too close together for his comfort, with the canvas upright between them. He glanced at the ferryman’s grip on the pole and then looked firmly away.

Rosceline ate another chickpea. She had not intended to share the whole twist, but Pello had passed it to Briska while she was watching the pole, and Briska had passed it to Daska. Now it contained more pepper than food.

At the next landing, a man came aboard with a long wrapped parcel. The ferryman made him turn it twice before finding a place where it obstructed only one passenger. That passenger was Rosceline.

“What is it?” she asked.

“A glass.”

“A remarkable ambition for a parcel of that size.”

“Looking glass. For my daughter.”

He held it carefully when the ferry moved again. His hands were clean except for one little crescent of blue paint beside a nail. Rosceline shifted her foot so the lower corner could rest on the board instead of his boot.

The wide channel opened beyond the warehouses. Sails moved out there in the afternoon haze, pale triangles stained gold along their seams. Silkstrand’s roofs rose behind them, with laundry suspended between houses and a new red awning over a landing that had been gray timber when Rosceline last looked. A girl on the landing shook crumbs out of a cloth. Gulls descended as if she had promised them money.

“Busy today,” the glass carrier said.

“We have had a rather more eventful week,” Rosceline told him. “There are times when a successful acquisition presents problems which ordinary merchants have the luxury of leaving to their—”

“Could you hold that corner?”

He was pointing at the glass. The ferry had turned across the wash of a passing lighter. Rosceline caught the wrapped corner before it knocked against the basket.

“Thank you,” he said. “She has wanted one for years.”

“What will she use it for?”

He looked puzzled. “Looking at herself.”

“An occupation I recommend. There are very few people one can count on to pay adequate attention.”

He smiled politely and checked the wrapping.

Across the channel, the White Lark lay beside a chandlery with her mast raised. The new line looked pale against the dark tackle. Her own crew moved about the deck. One man tested a repaired length of rail by putting his weight against it; another was carrying the master’s belongings down the companionway. A small boat waited beside the stern with fresh water jars.

Daska saw it first. She put a foot lightly against Pello’s boot.

He turned.

For a while all five watched. The shipmaster came out of the cabin holding an empty bottle. He said something to a man on the quay and pointed at it. Even at that distance Rosceline recognized the particular motion of a grievance which had found a new listener.

Daska’s shoulders began to move. She pressed her lips together and looked down.

“It was a very good bottle,” Rosceline said.

Briska leaned past the washing to see the mast heel. She frowned, satisfied herself about something and sat back. Ilaro watched the stern boat.

“Water,” he said.

Pello nodded.

“They’ve moved the boarding watch forward,” Pello said.

Rosceline looked again. A man stood by the hatch with his belt knife conspicuous and his hands free. There was another at the gangboard. Both were watching the quay while the water carrier passed jars up from his boat at the stern.

Pello rubbed a little mud from the rolled canvas with his thumb.

“Could have gone with the water,” he said. “They bring it aboard before the master comes back. No yard gate.”

Daska made a noise that began as a laugh and stopped before it had decided what else to be.

Rosceline felt the answer assemble itself: the man in the water boat, the jars, the minute when both watchers looked toward the quay. It was a much less elegant entrance than hers. It had several advantages.

“Pello,” Briska said.

He turned from the ship. His face changed when he saw them looking at him. Then he laughed, briefly and with genuine surprise. Daska lost the effort to contain herself. Ilaro smiled down at his hands.

The ferryman drove his pole into the shallows and brought them against the next set of steps. The washing woman rose before anyone else could block her. Rosceline gathered the empty paper twist and reached to keep the glass from falling as its owner stood.

“Would one of you take this end?” he asked.

Pello put the canvas under his arm, took the weight, and helped him ashore.
