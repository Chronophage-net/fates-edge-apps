The Last Blade

Prologue: The Mist Walker

On the third day of fog, Riarhin brought two rabbits to the Tulkani fire and was asked to dance.

She had expected that. The elder had watched her come out of the white without a footfall he could hear; now he wanted to see her feet beside the feet of his people. His hands were open, his welcome properly given. Behind him, a woman stopped cutting onions.

“I don’t know your dances,” Riarhin said in Tulkani.

The elder glanced at the rabbits. One was still bleeding from its nose.

“Then you may learn.”

“I’d rather eat.”

That earned her a little laughter, and the woman with the onions put down her knife.

Riarhin held out the rabbits by their bound hind legs. The woman took them without touching her hand and went behind a wagon to gut them. Only then did the elder move aside.

“Water and wine. Take whichever pleases you.”

“Water first.”

He liked that. Or liked that she knew to say it. Either would do.

The wagons stood on a rise above the Queen’s Highway, their wheels chocked against the wet turf. In the last of the daylight she could make out red flowers painted round a green door, a blue hand beside a latch, the pale rectangle where somebody had replaced a rotten board and not yet painted it. Washing hung beneath one axle. A little boy sat in the dry place under the wagon, feeding a wooden horse through the spokes.

These people had spent three days moving their entire household through weather which allowed them to see no farther than the next pair of oxen. Still they had found time to wash a child’s shirt.

She put her pack down where she could lean against it. The elder watched her lay the travelling bundle beside her: bow and sheathed sword wrapped together, the cloth worn pale at the corners.

“Far from home?” he asked.

“Tonight?”

He considered that, then nodded as if it were an answer.

No one asked her name. The young man who had first spotted her called her Mist Walker, and within a few minutes the child under the wagon had improved it to Shadow Lady. He made his wooden horse step round her boots.

She had been keeping pace with the caravan since noon. Earlier, from the ditch, she had watched two of their men repair a broken trace while the rest waited. No shouting, no suggestion that the man whose leather had failed ought to be left. She had meant to pass them before dark.

The rabbits had changed that. Or the fire. She had no need to determine which.

The stew was hot enough to hurt the split in her lower lip. There was barley in it, onion, something sour she could not place. She ate slowly. Across the fire, the elder waited until she had swallowed before taking his own bowl.

They sang while they ate. A road song to Kuva first, the verses passed round without anybody appearing to lead them. One woman sang a name differently from the others. No one corrected her. When the verses became familiar, Riarhin kept her mouth on the rim of her cup.

A raven called somewhere above the road.

The elder looked up. A drop fell from the end of his nose into his beard. He wiped it away and called for more wood.

By the time the rabbits came off the spit, the camp had ceased watching Riarhin. The boy had fallen asleep under his wagon. Somebody carried him indoors by the armpits, his heels making two tracks through the wet grass. Two women were arguing over the tune of a wedding song; a third demonstrated that they were both wrong, to their evident annoyance.

Then the lute player began The Golden Locks of Claire in appalling Viterran.

Riarhin laughed before she could help it.

He heard her. He pointed at her with his whole hand, lost a chord, recovered, and sang the next verse directly to her. His Claire had apparently hidden a cooper beneath her skirts instead of a copper. It improved the song.

When the music quickened, the woman who had corrected the wedding tune stood. She gathered her skirts above her boots and danced with the onion cutter, then the young man, then alone. She was short and broad through the hips, with a silver tooth that flashed when she laughed. Her black hair had come loose at one temple. When a partner tried to draw her back, she flicked his fingers away and came to sit beside Riarhin.

“You know our dances,” she said.

“Do I?”

“Your heel does.”

Riarhin stopped tapping it.

The woman grinned. Up close she was older than she had looked across the fire. There were small burn scars on her wrist and flour in the seam of one thumbnail.

“You’re spotted.”

“Most people call them freckles.”

“Most people haven’t seen your back.”

Riarhin looked at her.

“Your shoulders,” the woman said, laughing. “The rain has gone through your cloak in spots. You’ve been sitting in a wet thing all evening.”

“I hadn’t noticed.”

“A terrible lie.”

The woman pulled a folded blanket from behind her and offered one end. Riarhin took off the cloak. Cold travelled straight through the linen beneath it. She had noticed; she had simply been attending to other things.

The blanket smelt of smoke and clean wool. The woman settled closer, leaving a hand’s breadth between them.

“Better?”

“Yes.”

Another raven called, farther off. The woman tipped her head towards it.

“They’ve been following since the ford.”

“They follow cooking.”

“Is that what brought you?”

“Two rabbits brought me. You ate one.”

“Then I’m in your debt.”

“No.”

The word came more sharply than she intended. The woman looked at her, then handed her the cup.

“All right.”

They listened to the end of the song. Riarhin drank. The cup had warmed where the other woman had held it.

After a while the woman said, “My grandmother had a word for people who arrived like you. Kethra.”

Riarhin knew enough Tulkani to quarrel in it, to buy a horse and discover afterwards precisely how she had been cheated. She did not know that word.

“What did it mean?”

“I was nine. I thought it meant someone she wanted to keep away from the hens.”

“And now?”

“Now I wish I’d asked while she was alive.”

The woman took the cup back. She had stopped smiling, but there was no solemnity in it. She drank, wiped her mouth with her thumb, and asked Riarhin to dance.

“Not tonight.”

“All right,” she said again.

She made no move to leave. Presently her knee rested against Riarhin’s. Riarhin could have shifted. Instead she let her hand lie open on the blanket between them.

The woman’s fingers found it.

Later they went to the green door with the red flowers. The woman lifted the latch, then waited on the step until Riarhin followed. Inside, the roof was low enough that Riarhin had to bow her head. There was a copper pan, a pair of boots drying heel upwards, a curtain separating the bed from a disorder of baskets and folded clothes.

“Your sword will scratch the paint,” the woman said.

Riarhin set it by the door.

She woke once in the night with a warm arm across her ribs and the wagon rocking slightly as somebody outside climbed past the wheel. She knew exactly how long it had been since she had slept without her back against something solid. She left the reckoning alone and settled against the woman.

Then the woman drew her closer, half asleep, and she stopped trying.

By morning the fog had thinned enough to reveal leaves.

Riarhin washed at the roadside ditch, returned the blanket, and left before the oxen were yoked. The woman watched from the green doorway, fastening her hair. Neither asked where the other was going.

The highway bent south around a stand of alder. Water lay between its paving stones, but the old surface held. The lesser roads were mud. She had seen a cart sunk to its hubs on one of them the day before, the driver sitting on the load with his boots in his lap.

She heard four horses coming from the south.

One rider was singing. Another kept attempting the refrain a line too soon. The sound reached her well before the riders did. She stepped off the road and waited among the alders.

The first was young, freckled, in a blue coat with gold foxes at the collar. A large man in mail rode behind him, carrying a second sword badly strapped across his saddle. The two men at the rear wore matching caps and the patient expressions of men being paid to listen.

They passed within six feet of her.

“Another silver for the song,” the large man said.

“Then two for yours, Keading. One to begin and one to stop.”

The younger man laughed at his own joke. The others joined him.

Riarhin waited until the last horse had passed. South was clear. She could be well beyond Harwick by noon.

Behind her, the caravan’s first wheel sounded on the bridge.

She stood a moment longer, then went back.

The bridge crossed the Whiterun where it spread shallow over gravel before descending towards the Belworth. Spring water filled it from bank to bank. The leading wagon was already across. The second stood on the crown of the bridge, its oxen tossing their heads while the four riders blocked the southern end.

The elder held his staff across his body. Not as a weapon. As something to do with his hands.

“A silver,” the young man said. “Each wagon.”

“The Queen’s road.”

“My father’s bridge.”

Riarhin walked between the second wagon and the parapet. The woman from the green wagon saw her first. Her expression changed, but she did not call out.

“We have paid at the river,” the elder said. He reached inside his coat for the folded pass.

Keading pushed the paper down with the end of his riding crop.

“Different river.”

The elder looked at the men behind him. One of them had put his horse across the leading wagon’s path. Beyond the bridge lay a mile of open highway. There was nowhere for the caravan to turn.

Riarhin stopped beside the oxen.

“Move the horse,” she said.

The young man looked her over. His gaze paused at her ears, then the sword at her hip.

“Whose is that?”

“Mine.”

“Did he know you were taking it?”

Keading laughed. The two paid men did not. One was watching her hands.

“Cedric,” the elder said carefully, reading the name from something he had been told or something he remembered. “My lord. We have children waiting in the rain.”

“Then pay promptly.”

The elder lowered his staff and reached for his purse.

Riarhin took the staff before it touched the stone.

The man watching her hands began to say something. She drove the butt into his thigh as he dismounted. His knee folded; the horse jerked sideways, drawing its bridle through his fingers. She stepped past it and struck Keading’s wrist against his saddle before he could clear his sword.

Cedric’s horse reared at the noise. He clung to the reins and came down badly, one foot still caught in the stirrup. The remaining man had drawn. Riarhin caught his blade against the parapet with the staff, thrust the other end into his face, and felt his nose break.

Keading got his hand round her sleeve.

She hit the side of his knee. He went down holding it and shouting for the horse to stand, though it was already running.

Cedric freed his foot. He staggered upright, drew, and cut at the space she had just left. She struck his sword hand. The weapon fell into the runoff beside the parapet. Her next thrust folded him over the staff.

It was finished then.

The man with the broken nose had both hands to his face. The first man stayed on the ground, looking at the sword he had not drawn. Keading rocked over his knee. Cedric was trying to breathe.

Riarhin could hear the harness rings clicking as the oxen shook their heads.

She gave the staff back.

“Take them through.”

The elder took it, but did not move. He was looking at her other hand.

She had drawn Toothpick from her boot.

Cedric saw the knife and tried to crawl behind his fallen sword. She put a knee between his shoulder blades, caught his hair, and pulled his head back.

“No,” he said. It came out wet. “No, please.”

He smelt of good soap and horse sweat. There was a little gold thread caught in the seam of his collar. Someone had sewn each fox by hand.

She cut him across the bridge of the nose and onto the cheek: the old brigand’s mark, where anyone facing him would have to see it.

His cry carried over the water.

She released him. He curled round his face. Blood ran through his fingers, much more than the depth of the cut deserved.

At his belt hung a purse too heavy for a morning’s ride. She cut the thong and stood.

“The silver goes back,” she told the elder.

He did not take the purse.

“Which silver?”

She looked down at it.

“Whose?” he asked.

The wagons behind him were still. In the green doorway the woman held the little boy against her skirt, one hand covering his ear.

“You should go,” Riarhin said.

“Yes.”

The elder lifted his staff and called to the drivers. The first wagon moved. The second followed, its wheel passing through the water beside Cedric’s sword.

Riarhin stood clear as they went by. Some of the younger faces turned towards her; one man raised a hand before the elder said something she could not hear. The hand dropped.

The woman in the doorway looked at Cedric, then at Riarhin. She drew the child inside and closed the green door.

Riarhin wiped Toothpick on the wet grass. Then she found the broken-nosed man’s horse, which had caught its reins in the alder, and brought it back for them.

He would not meet her eyes when she gave him the bridle.

Act I

Chapter One: The Summer Knight

The messenger from Harwick had borrowed a horse for the last stage. Elyas knew because the man was trying to explain whose horse it was while the Duke asked him who had sent him.

“Edran Aeryl, Your Grace. But the gelding belongs to the miller at—”

“Tell my stable clerk. He will see it returned.”

“It has a sore place under the girth.”

“Then tell him that as well.”

The messenger bowed, relieved out of all proportion to the kindness, and backed into Elyas. Elyas caught his elbow before he struck the doorframe.

“Forgive me, Highness.”

“Go and see to the horse.”

The man had answered the Duke’s questions without lifting his eyes. He looked up now, at the permission rather than the prince. Elyas moved out of the doorway before he could ask it a second time. Tall and broad like his father, he had learned how easily standing still could oblige someone else to retreat.

Duke Braedyn waited until the door had shut. He was shorter than Elyas and broader still, his coat fitting shoulders that had kept their strength through the campaigns. His left arm rested where he had placed it on the chair; the wound at Lence had left it useless. Lately he took a moment before rising, even when there was no one to keep waiting. His quill lay across the open letter. There was mud on the floor, a damp patch on the chair, and a smell of hard riding in the room which the river air from the window did nothing to dispel.

“Cedric Aeryl has been collecting tolls at the Whiterun,” he said.

“Again?”

“First reported. There is a distinction.”

Elyas came to the desk. Behind the Duke, the roofs of Tarlington descended towards the Belworth in uneven terraces: slate above tile above thatch, interrupted by the tower of a guildhall and the high arm of the river crane. Floodwater had submerged the bottom three steps of the ducal landing. A boatman was standing on the fourth, holding a horse by its bridle while a ferry nosed against the quay.

“Aeryl has no grant for a toll there.”

“No.”

“Then he must repay it.”

“He is presently more concerned with his face.”

Braedyn slid the letter across with his right hand. Elyas read it standing. Edran wrote a small, cramped hand, with corrections crowded between the lines. His account did not try to make his brother appear innocent. It did spend half a page explaining why a father might be angry when someone cut his son.

“Riarhin,” Elyas said.

“You remember her.”

“Sir Seniro pointed her out once.”

“That will have pleased her.”

The Duke smiled, then rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger. There was a red mark above the bridge of his nose where his spectacles had rested. Elyas was so used to seeing him at this desk that the small evidence of discomfort seemed an intrusion.

“Is she being sought?”

“Aeryl has men out. Edran says he has kept them close to the hall. His father thinks they’re searching the back roads.”

“He’s disobeying his father.”

“He is trying to keep his father’s men alive.”

Elyas read the last lines again.

“Does he know what he is asking of you?”

“Enough to send by the mill road.”

The Canray board stood on its own table beside the shuttered window. Elyas had been allowed to study it when he first came to Tarlington, but never to play from the position. He had once asked who had the next move. Braedyn had said he was waiting, and that had been the end of it.

There was dust on the velvet beneath the pieces. Nothing else in the study was allowed to gather dust.

“Will you protect her?” Elyas asked.

“From Aeryl?”

“From judgment.”

Braedyn looked at him for longer than was comfortable.

“You have read one letter, Elyas.”

“The mark is not disputed.”

“No. Neither is the toll. Unfortunately they are not the same offence, and settling one will not settle the other.”

He took a clean sheet from the drawer and began to write.

“Go to Harwick. The road is to remain open. Whatever Cedric collected is to be returned, and there will be no levy on his father’s tenants to replace it. Make that last part understood.”

“And Riarhin?”

“Stop the hunt. Aeryl may bring his complaint here.”

“Will she attend?”

“Find her and ask.”

Braedyn sanded the page. Outside, the horse at the landing finally stepped aboard the ferry, and several men shouted at once.

“Why me?” Elyas asked.

The Duke looked up.

Elyas wished he had not said it. He had been waiting years to be given work that mattered; now it was in front of him, and he sounded like a boy asked to carry wood.

“Because you can speak for me without sounding like my steward. Because Aeryl will hear your father’s name before he hears your words. Because I would like you to discover what happens after you have said something sensible.”

He folded the order.

“Take Rekedim. Orvain as well.”

“Orvain will enjoy this.”

“See that he doesn’t enjoy it too much.”

The Duke pressed his seal into the wax and held it there until it had cooled. When he gave Elyas the letter, his fingertips were stained black.

“And tell Riarhin I would like to see her.”

“Is there a reason I should give?”

Braedyn’s hand rested on the desk.

“If she asks, say that I asked.”

“A prince to console a baron,” Orvain said. “I suppose a duke would have been excessive.”

He was leaning against the stable door, speaking Istovari with the accent he used when he wanted Elyas to remember home. His saddle lay on the mounting block. He had cleaned every buckle but left a smear of last week’s mud on his boot. His black hair was still wet from washing, making his skin look paler. He had the shoulders of a knight and the ease of a man who knew exactly how much of a doorway he could occupy before someone objected.

“Have you finished?” Elyas asked.

“With the saddle? Nearly.”

Orvain reached across and picked a dirty-blond hair from Elyas’s collar. He had been doing such things almost since arriving in Tarlington, late in Elyas’s wardship: a minor Istovari lord’s son sent from the western border with Ethor to be his companion. In those first weeks, familiar names for roads and villages had been enough to make them friends.

“With the rest.”

“I could go on.”

“Viterran, please,” Rekedim said from inside the stall.

Orvain changed languages. “I said that His Highness’s talents deserve a larger audience.”

“I heard the titles. The rest was probably unnecessary.”

Rekedim brought the horse out and passed the reins to a groom. He had shortened the stirrup leathers on Elyas’s saddle by a hole without asking. Elyas put a hand to them.

“Road’s wet,” Rekedim said. “You’ll want your weight off the seat.”

“We’ll take the mill road.”

“It will be wetter.”

“It’s quicker.”

“Without carts.”

“Then we shall hope for no carts.”

Rekedim went back for the saddle bags. He was a little past thirty, though men meeting him tended to add years for each of the things he did without asking help. His coat pulled across a mass of old scars at his neck. Above them were high cheekbones, dark grey skin and almond-shaped eyes. A Viterran lady had once called him handsome in Elyas’s hearing, then added “so exotic,” as though he required further explanation. Rekedim had asked whether she would like the bread passed. He had given no sign of hearing the rest. Elyas had seen men study those scars and decide they understood what Rekedim was for. He had also seen Rekedim keep a hundred horses fed through a week in which the quartermaster could not feed their riders. The Duke had told him to listen. He intended to.

Mara came down the stable passage with a bundle under one arm and Elyas’s travelling cloak over the other. She handed him the cloak and put the bundle on her own saddle.

“You’re coming?”

“You asked me yesterday.”

“To ride out.”

“And now you’ve found somewhere to ride.”

Orvain opened his mouth.

“Finish saddling,” she said.

He laughed and did it.

Mara took Elyas’s arm and turned him slightly, inspecting the cuff where the lining had come loose.

“If you pull at that all the way to Harwick, there’ll be no sleeve left.”

He took his hand away from it.

Her thumb remained on his wrist for a moment. He wanted to ask whether she thought he would do well, which was a reason not to ask. She smoothed the lining back into place and let go.

They left through the lower yard, where the farrier’s hammer rang under the covered walk. Tarlington’s keep rose behind them in pale courses laid so close that Elyas had never found a place to put the point of a knife between them. Its later additions were less exact. Rain had blackened the joins beneath the ducal windows; a mason on a hanging stage was scraping them out while two men above argued over the rope.

Beyond the gate, the highway ran through a market still setting out for the day. A woman moved her baskets for the riders. Rekedim thanked her. Orvain bought an apple without dismounting and passed it to Mara, who divided it with her knife and gave half to Elyas.

For a little while the errand seemed almost an outing.

The first blocked cart stood where the mill road narrowed between a drainage ditch and a bank of hazel. Its left wheel had sunk past the hub. Two more carts waited behind it. There was no room to pass.

Elyas looked back at Rekedim.

The sergeant dismounted without comment.

“Keep the oxen still,” he called. “Don’t make them pull again.”

A woman beside the lead cart had a sack split across her knees. Beans lay in the mud. She closed her hands over the tear when she saw the riders.

“We’ll clear it, my lord.”

“How long has it been down?” Elyas asked.

“Since the morning.”

“Why didn’t you take the highway?”

She looked at his cloak. The man holding the oxen’s heads looked at her.

“The toll,” he said.

“There is no toll.”

Nobody answered.

Elyas heard himself as they must have heard him: clean, mounted, explaining the condition of a road he had not travelled.

“There should be no toll,” he said. “How much?”

“A silver at the bridge. Another if he disliked the look of you.”

“Who?”

“The young lord.”

“And you paid?”

“Yesterday. We couldn’t pay again.”

Elyas got down. His boot went into water deep enough to enter at the seam.

“We’ll help you out.”

Rekedim had already put Orvain and the two drivers to work unloading the cart. Mara spread a cloak on the bank and lifted the woman’s torn sack onto it. Elyas crouched by the wheel and reached beneath the bed.

“Not there,” Rekedim said.

Elyas withdrew his hand.

“If it drops, the axle takes your fingers. Bring that fence rail.”

The rail belonged to a man in the next field, who arrived to say so. Orvain went to speak with him. By the time the cart was unloaded, they had the rail, a second rail, and the use of the man’s son. Orvain would not say what he had paid.

They levered the axle high enough to pack brush and stones under the wheel. Elyas held the rail while Rekedim worked beneath it. The wood bit through his glove. He could feel each small movement of the man whose hand he must not let the wagon crush.

“Now,” Rekedim said.

The oxen leaned into their yoke. The wheel came out with a sucking noise and rolled onto the stones.

The woman shut her eyes.

There was still the reloading to do, and the ruined beans to gather, and the second cart to get round the broken place. Elyas stayed. By the time they remounted, the half-day he had hoped to save was gone.

“Go by the highway,” he told the drivers. “The Duke has forbidden the collection.”

The woman looked at the order he held out. She did not try to read it.

“Will you be there?”

“At Harwick.”

She nodded politely.

Elyas put the letter away. It had begun to rain again.

Chapter Two: The Lesson

Harwick’s wharf was busy when they reached it. Men were carrying sacks uphill on their backs because the hoist had lost a tooth; on the river, two ferries waited for a third to clear the landing. The village smelled of damp grain, forge smoke, and the Belworth mud left drying under the quay.

Elyas had expected evidence of disorder. Instead he found people working hard enough to continue despite it.

The hall stood on the lower bluff, its western wall falling almost sheer towards the water. The Aeler stone was sound. The wooden gallery beneath it was not. Somebody had braced one corner with a fresh oak trunk, bark still on it. Above the gate, House Aeryl’s fox had been repainted so recently that its gold was brighter than the blue around it.

The captain admitted them promptly. He sent a boy for the horses and a younger boy for the Baron, then seemed to regret having given both orders in their hearing.

“We were expected,” Orvain observed.

“Yes, sir.”

“How disappointing.”

The captain did not smile.

At the hall door they were met by a herald scarcely old enough to shave. His sash kept slipping from one shoulder. He corrected it before he bowed.

“Berent Ailsean, Your Highness. It is my honour to receive you on behalf of Baron Lorran Aeryl.”

“You have the advantage of me, Berent. I have been riding all day and you appear freshly brushed.”

The boy’s mouth twitched.

“My aunt has a brush, Highness.”

“A formidable office.”

“Yes, Highness.”

The boy had answered the joke only after a glance towards the steward. He knew where he was allowed to be amusing. Elyas liked him at once. When the boy turned to announce them, he leaned towards Mara.

“He’d make a good squire.”

“He may prefer being a herald.”

“I only meant—”

But Berent had begun, clearly and correctly, and Elyas had to enter.

Lorran Aeryl stood beside his chair at the far end of the hall. He was a broad man with a weathered face and a splint on the smallest finger of his left hand. Beside him stood Edran, narrow where his father was broad, his expression much less guarded than his letter.

Cedric was not present.

“Highness,” the Baron said. “Harwick is honoured.”

He made it sound like a fact he regretted but could not dispute.

“Your son wrote well of you.”

Edran looked at Elyas. The Baron’s eyes shifted towards him, then back.

“Which son?”

“The one who asked for my help.”

The Baron left his hand on the back of his chair. Edran remained standing beside him, though there was a place set for him too. Elyas thanked Edran for the road directions before addressing his father again. The younger man could accept the thanks without being made to explain why he had sent them.

There was bread on the table, cheese, a jug of ale, four clean cups. Elyas thanked his host and waited until they had sat before bringing out the Duke’s order. The Baron read it. Then he read it again, moving his lips at the last sentence.

“You forbid me to seek the woman who cut my boy.”

“The Duke requires you to bring your complaint to Tarlington.”

“My son is upstairs with a fever.”

For the first time Elyas looked towards the stair.

“Has he a physician?”

“He has my physician. Shall I send that man to Tarlington as well, to explain what a knife does?”

“I am sorry he is ill.”

“No doubt.”

The Baron’s splinted finger caught against the cup. He adjusted his grip with visible care.

“Do you know what they call that mark?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Then you know what she has called my son. Before traders. Before servants. Before anyone he will ever meet who knows where to look.”

“He was taking money on the Queen’s Highway.”

“The bridge is mine to maintain.”

“The grant pays for that.”

“The grant pays at midsummer. The water takes the piers in spring.”

“That did not authorise him to collect.”

“No.”

The admission stopped Elyas more effectively than an argument would have.

Aeryl put the order down.

“He is a fool. I have told him so. It does not give every passing bastard the right to put a knife in his face.”

Behind Elyas, a bench creaked. Rekedim had sat down.

“The complaint will be heard,” Elyas said. “The collection ends now. Those who paid must be repaid.”

“With what?”

“Your son took the money.”

“And your Duke’s friend took it from him.”

Elyas had not known that. Edran looked down at the table.

“How much?”

“That depends which account you believe. Cedric says twelve silver. Keading says eighteen. My tenants say what they think I want to hear.”

“Then hear them somewhere else.”

The Baron sat back.

“At the bridge,” Elyas continued. “With your steward and a witness from Tarlington. Let them say what was taken without having to enter this hall.”

“And if each claims a purse of gold?”

“Then your steward can question them.”

“Before the same witness?”

“Yes.”

Aeryl turned the cup in his good hand. Elyas could almost see the settlement becoming possible: an amount, a place, a procedure sufficiently public to satisfy the Duke and sufficiently local to leave the Baron in command of his own people.

Then someone laughed behind the door at the stair.

Cedric stood there in a loose shirt, his face swollen round a strip of linen. The dressing had slipped, showing a dark crust at one nostril. He looked younger without his coat.

“Shall we ask the elf to judge the claims?” he said.

His father closed his eyes briefly.

“Go upstairs.”

“She’ll know. She counted it.”

“Cedric.”

The young man came forward. A servant reached towards his elbow and thought better of it.

“You come here to order us about for her. Why doesn’t she come herself?”

“I am here about the road,” Elyas said.

“Then open it. She’s the brigand on it.”

“The road will be opened when your men come home.”

Cedric looked towards Rekedim, then back to Elyas. There were people watching from every bench. Elyas could see the moment in which the young man remembered them.

“Perhaps her friends would answer for her.”

Elyas should have stood and asked the Baron to remove his son. He knew that later. At the time he saw a different conclusion: anger given a form, honour satisfied, the pursuit ended before somebody died in a ditch.

“What answer would satisfy you?” he asked.

Mara moved beside him. It was only the scrape of her shoe, but he heard it.

Cedric looked towards the men by the stair. His shoulders settled when one of them nodded. Elyas saw it and mistook the relief for a willingness to accept a form they would all recognise.

Cedric heard his own opportunity.

“Steel.”

“To first blood,” Elyas said.

Edran put his cup down too hard.

“No.”

They looked at him.

“He has a fever,” Edran said. “And the road is not a wager.”

“The road is settled,” Elyas replied. “The order stands whatever happens in the yard. This concerns the pursuit of Riarhin. If your brother accepts satisfaction from me, he will not seek it from her.”

“You do not have her word.”

“You have mine.”

Aeryl looked from the prince to his younger son. For a moment Elyas thought he would refuse. Instead the Baron laid his palm on the order and asked, “No prosecution for the blood lawfully drawn?”

“None.”

“First blood, and no farther.”

“Agreed.”

“Tomorrow, if the physician permits it.”

Cedric began to object. His father turned on him.

“If you insist on having terms, you will hear them.”

Outside, the courtyard had emptied except for a man rubbing down their horses and two women drawing water. The noise of the wharf came up the bluff.

Edran followed them to the guest stair.

“I sent for the Duke because my father would not hear me,” he said. “Now he’ll say I brought a prince to fight my brother.”

“The terms protect your house.”

“I know what the terms say.”

Elyas looked at him. Edran’s controlled manner had worn thin; beneath it was a young man who had spent several days between people he could not persuade and could not leave.

“Will Cedric be well enough?”

“Ask the physician. He may tell you something different from what he tells my father.”

He went back inside.

Mara took Elyas’s wet cloak from his shoulders.

“You had him agreeing about the bridge,” she said.

“He has agreed.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Will finish it.”

She wrung the hem of the cloak over the drain. Water struck stone between them.

By morning Cedric’s fever had broken. The physician said he might stand, provided nobody struck his face. He said this while looking directly at Elyas, who gave his word.

They fought in the lower yard, away from the horses. Aeryl had ordered the household to attend. Berent stood beside a steward with the written terms in his hands. He read them aloud without stumbling.

The tolls were to cease. Claims were to be heard at the Whiterun. The private pursuit was to end. The Duke’s hearing remained open to both parties.

Last came the duel: first blood, no prosecution, no second demand arising from the same quarrel.

Cedric listened with his sword pointed at the ground. Overnight the dressing had been replaced by two narrow strips which left most of the cut visible. It was red and raised and ugly. It was going to remain ugly.

Elyas saluted him.

The first attack came too quickly. Cedric’s feet had not caught up with his hand. Elyas turned the blade and could have struck him then, but stepped away instead.

He had made a plan in the night. A clean opening, offered late enough to appear earned. A little blood. A face preserved where another had been cut. He had heard such stories all his life. He had admired the men in them.

Cedric came again. Better this time. Elyas gave ground and felt the uneven yard beneath his heel. He had chosen silk for the ease with which it would part. Now, absurdly, he was thinking of what Mara would say about the sleeve.

On the fourth exchange he let his guard drift high.

Cedric did not take the opening. He thrust at Elyas’s throat.

Elyas knocked the point aside hard enough to numb his own fingers. Someone in the crowd made a sound. Cedric stumbled, recovered, and came at him with two short cuts that were no part of any drill Elyas recognised.

There was fear in the boy’s face. Not shame, not understanding. Fear of being struck in the place already wounded.

Elyas stopped trying to teach him.

He caught the next blow, turned Cedric towards the empty side of the yard, and opened his left arm. This time the blade found it.

The cut was deeper than he had intended.

“Blood,” the steward called.

Elyas stepped back at once. Cedric’s sword remained raised. For a moment nobody moved. Then Berent repeated the word, very loudly, and Cedric lowered his hand.

The blood had reached Elyas’s wrist.

Aeryl came across the yard and took his son’s shoulder.

“Satisfied?” he asked.

Cedric looked at Elyas. There was something searching in his expression, but Elyas could not tell what he sought.

“Yes.”

“Say it so they hear.”

“I am satisfied.”

A man near the gate began to clap. Another joined him. The Baron did not stop them.

Elyas inclined his head, sheathed his sword, and went to the physician.

Mara held the basin while the wound was washed. Rekedim stood at the door, keeping the curious away. Orvain had disappeared into the yard.

“Did you mean him to cut you?” Mara asked.

“Yes.”

“Did you mean this much?”

“No.”

She passed the physician a clean strip of linen.

Orvain came back as the bandage was being tied. He had lost his easy smile.

“What are they saying?” Elyas asked.

Orvain glanced at Mara.

“Tell me.”

“That Cedric Aeryl drew blood from a prince.”

Elyas looked at the physician’s hands. The knot was small and competent. He had wanted a lesson no one would have to explain.

“The men have been recalled,” Rekedim said. “Edran sent the order before the swords were put away.”

“Good.”

“And the first claims will be heard tomorrow.”

“Good.”

Mara emptied the basin through the window into the drain below. The water was pink. Elyas watched until she brought it back.

Chapter Three: Two Pebbles

They found Riarhin after Elyas stopped looking for her.

There had been a boot print beside a ford, a report from a shepherd, a woman at a bakehouse who said the red-haired traveller had gone east and a boy who said west. Orvain bought the boy a bun. He then said north.

By late afternoon they were half a day’s ride from Tarlington and had spent two days describing a rough circle through its outlying farms. The leaves were fully out in the Fenwood. Rain shook from them long after the sky had cleared.

“We go home tomorrow,” Elyas said.

They had stopped in a clearing used by charcoal burners. A stack of cut wood stood under a sloping roof; beside it a ring of old stones offered a place for their fire. Rekedim inspected the roof before putting the saddles beneath it.

“Shall I leave a message at the next house?” Mara asked.

“What message?”

“That the Duke would like to see her.”

Elyas looked at her.

“That is what we came to say.”

He almost replied that Riarhin knew perfectly well they were looking for her. He could not have said how he knew.

“Leave one,” he said.

Mara went down the track to the burners’ cottage. When she came back she had four eggs and the message had been given to a woman who promised nothing.

They ate the eggs with the last of the soft bread. There was enough hard biscuit and salt meat for another two days, but no one wanted either. Orvain cracked an egg badly and spent some time removing shell from his share while Rekedim watched with satisfaction.

“I shall remember this,” Orvain told him.

“You’ll remember it better if you chew.”

The fire settled. Elyas’s arm throbbed when he held it still and hurt when he moved it. He loosened the sling Mara had made him, put his rolled cloak beneath his head, and tried to think of the exact words he would use at Tarlington.

The road is open. Aeryl’s men have returned. I have not found your friend.

Those were the facts. It should have been possible to say them without apology.

Orvain took the first watch. Rekedim the second. Elyas was to take the last, over his own objection that he did not need special consideration. The argument ended when Mara asked whether he meant to sleep at all or merely discuss it until morning.

He slept.

A small stone struck the buckle beside his head.

He woke with his right hand on his sword. A second stone landed on his chest.

“Your turn,” a woman said.

Riarhin sat beyond the fire, holding a stick between finger and thumb. Her deep auburn hair was tied back, the fire catching red along the loose strands. Her skin was grey-brown, more brown than grey, like bark warmed by the light. The cloak over her knees was green and country-cut, a hill woman’s cloth in a Duke’s party, and she wore it without any sign of having considered the question. She had unwrapped her bow and sword and laid them within reach. A cloth lay across her knees, with which she was cleaning the sword.

Elyas sat up too quickly. Pain went through his arm.

“Where is Rekedim?”

“Down the track. He heard me before I came in.”

“You might have spoken.”

“I did. You said you were awake.”

A little distance off, Rekedim coughed. It sounded suspiciously like laughter.

Elyas put the sword down.

She leaned forward to take the cloth off her knees. Freckles crossed her nose, giving her a girlish look that he had missed in the dark. Her exposed ears tapered like leaves; he could imagine men calling them knife-shaped, depending on how she had received them. She was watching his sword hand.

Across the fire, the cloth moved away from Riarhin’s blade. Narrow and straight, with two edges: an arming sword, Elyas would have called it, except that it seemed to have no hilt. Then she turned it and he saw the small block above her fingers, worked with ornament too fine to read from where he sat. There were no spreading quillons. The metal was neither bright nor dull. Moving it through the firelight did not make it look like any steel he knew.

“Remorse,” she said, catching him looking.

“Who made it?”

“I found it.”

She wiped the last of the dirt away. There was not a chip along either edge. It looked fit to receive an oath from a kneeling lord. She used the cloth to rub something off her thumb, inspected it, and flicked it into the fire.

“A rock?”

“Two. Pebbles.”

“I can see what they are.”

“Then why ask?”

He drew his feet beneath him and sat. There were things he had rehearsed saying to her. None belonged at this hour, in this posture, with one of the pebbles still in his lap.

“The Duke would like to see you.”

“Yes. The charcoal woman said.”

“He sent me to find you.”

“She said that too.”

“You could have made it easier.”

“I wasn’t ready to be found.”

The answer irritated him partly because it was so nearly an answer he respected. He looked at the bundle. Beside it lay a leather purse with its thong cut through.

“Cedric’s?”

“Some of it.”

“You took his money.”

“I took the purse. Whether the money was his seems to have been the difficulty.”

“Claims are being heard at the bridge. His father is repaying them.”

“With his own silver?”

“With Cedric’s allowance first.”

She looked up. It was the first news that had appeared to interest her.

“And after?”

“The Baron has agreed not to recover it from his tenants.”

“Agreed to whom?”

“The Duke.”

Riarhin rolled the stick between her fingers. Then she put it on the fire and pushed the purse towards him.

“There were sixteen silver and some copper. I changed one silver. The bread and eggs came out of it.”

“The eggs?”

“The ones you ate last night.”

He looked towards the track.

“She wouldn’t take payment twice,” Riarhin said. “Your friend was very insistent. The woman will give it back when you pass.”

Mara had opened her eyes. She lay still a moment, then sat up and reached for her boots.

“So you knew where we were.”

“You were asking everybody where I was.”

“That wasn’t what I said.”

“No.”

Riarhin untied the bundle. There was a loaf, a little cheese, four wrinkled winter pears. She cut the loaf and held it out to Mara, who took it.

Elyas looked down at the purse.

“You are returning less than you took.”

“Yes.”

“That makes the accounting harder.”

“Then put my name beside the difference.”

“Which name?”

She gave him a quick look, and for the first time he saw amusement without feeling that it was wholly at his expense.

“Riarhin will do.”

Rekedim came back from the track and crouched beside the fire. He took bread only after Mara had passed it to Elyas.

“Will you come?” Elyas asked her.

“What did he say?”

“That he would like to see you.”

“Those words?”

“Yes.”

Her voice had lost the small resistance with which she had answered everything about the money. Elyas put the purse down. Whatever she had come to settle, this question was not part of the bargaining.

She watched the fire. A small beetle crawled out of the end of the stick she had put in it. She lifted the stick with two others and laid it on the bare earth beyond the stones.

“Is he well?”

“He was when we left.”

“How well?”

Elyas remembered the fingers pressed against the bridge of the Duke’s nose. Nothing unusual. A tired man, writing late. Yet he found himself reviewing it for her.

“He complains about the light when he reads. He complains about the cook. He still works longer than his clerks.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

She nodded once.

Orvain had been awake for some time. Now he sat up, combing his fingers through his hair.

“Do we ask which of these is poisoned, or is that discourteous?”

“The pears,” Riarhin said.

He examined one. Its skin had puckered round the stem.

“I thought so.”

“You’ll have to eat all four. It takes years.”

Rekedim held out his hand. Orvain gave him a pear.

The sky had begun to lighten through the leaves. Mara put water on. For a little while they were simply five people waiting for it to boil.

Then Riarhin looked at Elyas’s arm.

“Did he do that?”

“Yes.”

“With a sword?”

“What else?”

She did not answer. He could feel Rekedim attending to the conversation without looking at either of them.

“The pursuit is over,” Elyas said. “He accepted satisfaction.”

“For my part in it.”

“For the injury.”

“Did you ask him what he wanted before you offered?”

“He asked for steel.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Elyas set his bread on his knee.

“I kept him from following you. I kept his father’s men from following you. The people who paid him are getting their money back. What would you have done?”

He heard the question after he had said it.

Riarhin looked at him, then lowered her eyes to the purse between them.

“You saw what I did.”

The water stirred in the pot. Mara moved it off the strongest heat.

“He was beaten,” Elyas said. “Before you cut him.”

“Yes.”

“There were children there.”

“I know.”

He had expected her to defend it. He had prepared himself for a defence, for a hard account of the road and the things a prince did not understand. Her agreement left him nowhere useful to go.

“Why?”

She rubbed a thumb along the heel of the loaf, collecting crumbs.

“I wanted him to carry it home.”

Elyas looked at his bandage. Beside him, Mara poured the water. She handed Riarhin the first cup, and Riarhin took it with both hands.

“I will go to Tarlington,” she said.

No one thanked her for agreeing. Rekedim got up to see to the horses.

They reached the rise above the city before noon. The Belworth filled its bends below them, brown with rain from farther upstream. Tarlington’s lower roofs shone where the sun had dried them. High above the stables a mason’s stage hung empty against the keep.

Riarhin stopped where the road divided. Elyas reined in beside her.

“The east gate is quicker,” he said.

“I know.”

She was looking at the keep. He followed her gaze but could see nothing there to account for the delay.

A bell began in the lower city. A ferry bell, answered from across the water. Riarhin settled the bundle holding her sword and bow against her shoulder and took the eastern road.

At the gate the porter recognised Elyas and raised his hand. Orvain rode ahead to ask whether the Duke was receiving.

“Since breakfast, sir. He’s had the bridge men in.”

“Then we shall give him a change of subject.”

Orvain turned in his saddle and looked back at Elyas.

“Shall I tell him you won?”

“Tell him the road is open.”

For once Orvain made nothing of the answer. He touched two fingers to his brow and went on ahead.

Chapter Four: The Corridor

By the time the Duke sent for them, the courtyard lamps were being lit.

Elyas had washed, changed his coat, and had his arm dressed again. The physician had asked what he had been doing to it. Riding, he had said. It was the answer least likely to prolong the visit.

His report took less time than he expected. Braedyn asked about the restitution, the witnesses, the wording of the duel’s terms. He asked nothing about Elyas’s cut until the end.

“Was that necessary?”

“I thought so.”

“And now?”

Elyas hesitated.

“The men have been recalled.”

“Yes. You have told me.”

Then Orvain came in with a folded paper and asked to speak privately. The Duke read the first few lines, put it beside his lamp, and asked Elyas to wait outside.

Riarhin was in the corridor. She had been offered a chair and had chosen the broad sill beneath the nearest window instead. Her pack stood between her boots. Beyond her, the last light lay on the upper roofs of Tarlington.

“Have you seen him?” Elyas asked.

“Briefly.”

“He’s occupied now.”

“I gathered.”

Elyas walked to the turn of the passage and back. At Harwick he had been able to say what was required and have men attend to it. Here he was waiting to be told whether he had done well, and Riarhin had only to sit there to make him conscious of the difference.

On his third turn she said, “Sit down.”

“I’m quite comfortable.”

“So are the stones. Leave them alone.”

The door opened. Orvain came out, closing it behind him.

“Well?” Elyas asked.

“Not tonight.”

His gloves were in his hand. He pulled one on as he went down the passage, working each finger into place. Elyas watched until he turned towards the outer gallery.

“What did he want?” Riarhin asked.

“I don’t know.”

A few minutes later the Duke opened the door himself. He had taken off his spectacles and was holding them by one arm.

“Go to bed, Elyas. I can hear you from the desk.”

“I was waiting.”

“I know. Tomorrow.”

Riarhin got down from the sill.

“You too,” Braedyn told her.

“I haven’t asked for anything.”

“An agreeable change. Preserve it until morning.”

There was affection in the words. Elyas heard it before he saw the small movement at the corner of Riarhin’s mouth. He had wondered, in Harwick, what sort of protection passed between these two. Here it sounded like an old permission to be rude.

Boots sounded in the gallery.

Orvain came round the corner wearing his travelling cloak. He was walking quickly enough that Elyas thought he had remembered something urgent.

“My lord.”

“Your Grace,” Braedyn said. “And whatever it is can—”

Orvain’s hand came out from beneath the cloak. He caught the Duke by the front of his robe and drove a knife into his side.

Elyas saw Braedyn’s spectacles fall. One lens broke on the stone.

“Orvain!”

The name stopped him for an instant. He turned towards Elyas with the knife still in his hand.

“Mor auf despères!”

Elyas knew the sounds were Vhasian. He had no time to fit them to a meaning. Orvain was already retreating towards the gallery.

Riarhin threw.

The knife struck high in Orvain’s shoulder. He hit the wall, caught himself with his free hand, and ran. Elyas reached the corner in time to see him disappear down the servants’ stair.

Behind him the Duke fell.

He turned back.

Riarhin was on her knees beside Braedyn, pressing a fold of torn linen against his side. The Duke’s hand moved over hers, trying to find a grip.

Elyas had no memory of her tearing the linen. She had been on the window sill when the door opened. She was on the floor with her weight down through both arms before he had finished saying Orvain’s name, and the strip in the wound was off her own sleeve, and the rest of the sleeve lay on the stone beside her knee.

Braedyn found her wrist and stopped trying to take the work from her. He put his right palm flat over the back of her hand instead, and held it there, the way a man holds down a thing he has been told to hold down.

“Help,” she said. “Get somebody.”

Elyas shouted down the passage. No answer came. He ran to the gallery and shouted again, this time for the guard by name.

A door opened below. He could hear boots on the steps, then the seneschal demanding to know what had happened. Elyas told him too quickly. He had to say Orvain’s name twice.

When he returned, Riarhin had eased Braedyn onto his uninjured side. Blood was coming through the linen. She looked up as the seneschal knelt beside her.

“Keep pressure here. Don’t lift it to look.”

She said it the way a man gives a number. Elyas had been instructed all his life — masters-at-arms, physicians, his father’s marshal — and none of them had ever sounded like that, because all of them had been teaching. This was not teaching. It was a sentence that had been said before, to people who had not required it explained, somewhere there had not been time to explain it.

“Has the physician been sent for?”

“He’s coming,” Elyas said.

“Then give him room.”

The seneschal sent one guard down for a stretcher and another to the gate captain. The wounded man on the floor was still Braedyn Fenwood; people had to be told what to do before they would step around him.

The Duke opened his eyes.

“Gone?”

“Down the little stair,” Elyas said.

Braedyn tried to raise his head. Riarhin put her forearm across his collarbone and told him to lie still, in a voice Elyas had not heard her use before and did not hear again that year.

He stopped. He did not argue and he did not ask what she was doing, and Elyas noticed the not-asking, because the Duke argued with his physician about broth.

“Don’t let them lift it,” Braedyn said.

“No.”

“They always lift it.”

“I know what they do.”

Neither of them looked up. Elyas stood in the doorway with his hand on the frame and understood that a thing had been agreed in front of him in words he had all of and none of.

Rekedim came up from the gallery with Mara behind him. He looked once at the blood and turned to the seneschal.

“Gates and landings.”

“I’ve sent to the captain.”

“Send to the ferries separately. The captain’s men will do the gates first.”

The seneschal caught the page at his elbow and repeated the instruction. Mara went for more linen.

A guard came back from the stair. Orvain had crossed the lower yard and used the postern. The keeper had opened it for him. He knew the knight and had been told there was urgent business in the city.

“And the blood?” Elyas asked.

“The keeper thought it was his hand, Highness. He said he was holding it under his cloak.”

The physician arrived with an assistant. Only when the assistant took over the pressure did Riarhin get to her feet. She left a bloody print on the wall where she steadied herself.

Elyas had expected her to tell the man something. She did not. She watched his hands until she was satisfied with them and then looked away, and that was the whole of it, and it had taken perhaps four breaths.

He looked down at his own hands.

“He’s wounded,” she told the seneschal. “High in the shoulder. A knife still in it when he went down.”

“You saw it strike?”

“I threw it.”

Elyas looked towards the stair. Orvain was somewhere below them, taking a turn, finding a door, becoming harder to find with every question asked here.

“I’ll go,” he said.

The seneschal looked at the sling.

“You will stay with the Duke.”

The physician had begun asking for light. Elyas wanted to help him, wanted to see what he was doing, wanted most of all to make the last few minutes into something that had not happened.

Then he remembered the painted lamp beneath the weighhouse. Orvain at the door. A little packet passed into his hand.

“He has somewhere to go,” Elyas said. “I know a place.”

Riarhin turned towards him.

“Show me.”

“Two guards,” the seneschal said. “Bring him back if you can. And send word here. I want to know where you are.”

Rekedim touched Elyas’s good shoulder.

“I’ll see to the river. Send to the lower landing.”

Elyas nodded. Through the open door of the Duke’s room, he could see Mara moving a lamp closer to the couch. Braedyn lay still while the physician cut his robe away.

Riarhin had gone to the stair. He followed her.

Chapter Five: The Lower City

The blood led them out of the postern and down through the merchant quarter.

At the first crossing Riarhin stopped. Elyas saw a dark drop near the gutter and pointed left. She took the other lane.

“Here,” she said.

There was a smear on a doorpost, shoulder high. He had been looking at the ground.

They went down another flight of steps. A woman was bringing her washing in. When the guards asked whether she had seen a wounded man, she said yes, and held out a strip of linen he had offered to pay for. She had refused the coin and fetched a longer piece.

“He said he fell,” she told them.

Riarhin asked which hand he had used to take it. His left. She asked whether he had been alone. He had. Then she thanked the woman and went on.

At the next turn the trail disappeared among the wet tracks of a handcart. Elyas could smell fish waste and river mud. Somewhere below, the watch was calling for the landing to be cleared.

“The weighhouse is there,” he said.

Riarhin looked where he pointed.

“The door with the lamp.”

She waited for a pair of porters to pass, then crossed the lane. He went with her.

Elyas had passed it twice with Orvain. Once they had been looking for a bootmaker; once Orvain had bought a small packet of something from the man who came to the door. He had said it was salve for a saddle sore, and Elyas had made some joke about the horse deserving it more.

He remembered the joke very clearly now.

At the weighhouse a cart stood across the lane. They squeezed between its load and the wall. Beyond it, the painted door was open.

Riarhin put a hand against the door and pushed it farther open. Elyas entered behind her, with the two guards.

There was a counter, an empty stool, jars of oil on shelves. Behind the counter a man stood holding a candle. Wax had run over his knuckles. When he saw the guards, he put it down too quickly and had to catch it.

“I gave him the key,” he said. “He has a right to it.”

“Sir Orvain?” Elyas asked.

“Out the back. Just now.”

Riarhin went through the rear room. Elyas heard the court door open. One of the guards followed her. The other stayed beside him.

The keeper recognised Elyas and attempted a bow. He put a hand to the bench to steady himself.

“Sit down,” Elyas said.

The man sat.

“I’ve done nothing,” the keeper said.

“Did he come here today?”

“Sir Orvain? Yes.”

“Did you know he was coming?”

“He has a room.”

That stopped Elyas.

“Since when?”

“Before the rains. I don’t remember the date.”

“Where?”

The keeper pointed upwards.

On the floor above, a narrow bed had been stripped except for its mattress. There was a basin of cloudy water, a wet cloth, and Orvain’s riding coat turned inside out on a peg.

Elyas touched the cuff. He knew the little tear beside the button. He had watched Orvain pick at it through supper three nights before.

There was blood on the lining. On the washstand lay a knife with a narrow strip of flesh caught beneath its guard. Riarhin’s. Orvain had pulled it out here. Beside it, the longer piece of linen had been cut into bandages.

Below the bed stood Orvain’s riding boots, one fallen on its side. The mud on them had dried pale. He had come here to change. How long had the other clothes been waiting?

Elyas put a hand on the bedpost.

The guard waited in the doorway.

“Highness?”

Elyas turned away. Orvain had not made these arrangements in the minutes since he left the keep. He had been sleeping beside them on the road, joking over supper, while this room waited for him.

From below came a call. The other guard had come back from the court. Riarhin had sent him to say they were to follow the retaining wall towards the lower quay.

Elyas went down.

“Who pays for the room?”

“The Commission.”

“Which commission?”

The keeper stared at him.

“The Continuing Commission. The house belongs to them. They pay me to keep it.”

“What does it do?”

“Road business. Petitions. Old grants. People who say they’ve been charged twice at a crossing. Sir Orvain had papers to collect.”

“From whom?”

“Whoever left them. I don’t open his packets.”

The guard beside the door shifted. Elyas held up a hand before he could speak.

The keeper’s mouth had begun to shake. He pressed his lips together and tried again.

“He said a horse would be brought. He asked me to keep the back court clear.”

“When?”

“Before he went away with you.”

Elyas left one guard with the keeper and followed the other into the court.

The back court opened onto a narrower lane running along the retaining wall of the terrace. The paving was torn where a horse had slipped on the turn. One iron shoe lay by the gutter.

They found Riarhin two streets farther down, beside a man sitting in a puddle.

He held his left forearm against his chest. Blood had soaked through the sleeve. A bay horse stood with its reins beneath Riarhin’s boot, trembling hard enough to shake the buckles on its bridle.

Toothpick was still in her hand.

For a moment Elyas thought the man in the puddle was Orvain. Then he turned his face and Elyas saw the beard.

“Where?” Elyas asked.

“Quay stairs. On foot.”

“Which landing?”

“Lower chain. He saw me at the turn and got off.”

She put Toothpick away and gave the reins to the guard.

“The man?” Elyas asked.

“Held the horse for him. Drew when I came round the corner.”

The man in the puddle looked up.

“She didn’t say who she was.”

“Nor did you,” Riarhin said.

She went down the next flight of steps. Elyas followed. Behind him the guard called to the nearest house for someone to hold the horse and someone else to fetch a surgeon.

The steps were shallow, worn in the middle. Elyas could hear the river before he saw it. At the bottom, porters were holding back a little crowd while a loaded barge worked away from the quay. A loose line trailed in the water.

Orvain stood in the stern with his right arm held against his chest.

His hair was wet and combed flat. He wore a labourer’s coat and had no sword. Elyas might have failed to recognise him if he had not lifted his head at the sound of their feet.

Riarhin took her bow from her shoulder.

Silence was short enough to turn with her among the mooring posts, its limbs curving back on themselves beyond her hand. Fine carving followed the silvery wood without covering it. Elyas had seen steppe bows of horn and sinew; the joined layers of this one had a stranger look, held by some Lethai substance he could not name. He had tried its pull once on the road and scarcely moved it. She drew without looking at the string.

Between her and Orvain, a bargeman stood at the steering oar. Two more men were crouched beneath the rail. The boat was already swinging in the current, presenting one body after another where Orvain had been.

“Down!” Elyas shouted.

The man at the oar looked towards him. Orvain caught his shoulder with his left hand and pulled him backwards. The barge turned farther out.

Riarhin moved along the quay, arrow drawn, seeking an opening. Elyas ran beside her until the mooring posts stopped him. Orvain was crouching now. Only the top of his head showed beyond a stack of wicker hampers.

A horn sounded upstream. The gate watch had reached the higher landing. Too late to stop the barge leaving; perhaps not too late to stop it crossing.

Orvain rose long enough to look back.

“Elyas,” he called.

Riarhin released. Elyas heard no string, no creak of the limbs.

The arrow struck the edge of a hamper as Orvain dropped. Wicker burst outwards. One of the crouching bargemen shouted and scrambled away, unhurt as far as Elyas could see.

Riarhin had another arrow ready. She did not loose it.

The barge passed behind the stone pier. When it emerged, Orvain was no longer visible.

Elyas stood with one hand on a mooring post. He had drawn his sword without remembering it. He put it away.

“Can they stop him?”

“If they stop that boat,” Riarhin said.

She was watching the water between the quay and the pier.

“Has he gone over?”

“I didn’t see.”

She lowered the bow.

It was the first time she had admitted to him that there was something she had missed.

Rekedim found them at the quay. He sent men along both banks and a rider upstream. Riarhin gave them the colour of the borrowed coat, the missing shoe of the horse, the places Orvain could have reached if he had dropped into the water behind the pier. She did not say which she believed he had chosen.

The horse holder had been carried to a cooper’s shop. Elyas went there before returning to the house.

The man was pale, angry, and much less willing to talk now that his arm had been bound. He said he had been paid to bring the bay. He had been paid half in advance. He did not know where his customer had gone and wanted no part of whatever had happened at the keep.

“Who told you something happened at the keep?” Elyas asked.

The man’s eyes moved to Riarhin.

She stood at the door, saying nothing.

He would give them no more.

At the house beneath the weighhouse, the keeper had finally remembered a name: Sir Gyllan. An old man, he said, sometimes sent people there. No, he had not seen him with Orvain. No, he did not know whether they knew each other. Yes, he was certain of the name; Sir Gyllan always complained that the front step was loose.

Elyas had met Gyllan at the Duke’s table. He remembered an argument about whether a pear should be eaten with the skin on. The old knight had refused to concede the point long after everyone else ceased caring.

He asked for paper and wrote a summons.

Riarhin watched him sand it.

“What?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

“You think he will run.”

“I don’t know him.”

Elyas looked down at the address. Then he folded the paper and gave it to Rekedim.

“Bring him yourself. Ask him not to discuss it on the way.”

Chapter Six: The Lamp Beneath the Arch

Gyllan arrived carrying the summons in his hand.

He had read it closely enough to dislike it.

“Am I accused of something?”

“Not yet,” Riarhin said.

Elyas wished she had left him to answer. They were in a small room off the keep’s lower gallery. He had chosen it because the upper passage was being cleaned and he could not bear the sound of the water being wrung from the cloths.

“Please sit, sir.”

Gyllan looked from Riarhin to Rekedim, then at Elyas’s bandage. His hair had gone well into silver, and his short beard was as carefully kept as Elyas remembered. The northern cast of his complexion softened its Amaranthine bronze; now the skin round his mouth had paled.

“Has the Duke been hurt?”

Elyas did not answer at once.

“Your messenger asked me whether I knew Orvain,” Gyllan said. “Half the city is being searched. And you have blood above a bandage which was put on before today. I would rather you told me.”

“Yes.”

“How badly?”

“We don’t know.”

Gyllan sat. He laid the summons on the table and smoothed it, although it was not creased.

“By Orvain?”

“Yes.”

The old knight drew breath through his nose. He looked towards the door as though he might ask to see Braedyn, then evidently thought better of it.

“What do you need?”

The seneschal had brought the paper Orvain left on the Duke’s desk. Elyas had read it twice while they waited: a warrant for armed escorts to pass the ducal crossings, issued by the Continuing Commission. Its first line declared that the Crown was vacant.

Elyas put it before Gyllan. The lamp beneath the arch had been pressed into the wax below the signature.

“Who gave him this?”

Gyllan read it. At the second paragraph he brought it closer to the lamp. His lips moved at a name below the text.

“I don’t know who gave it to him.”

“Do you know what it is?”

“A warrant no one has the authority to issue.”

“The Commission?”

“They preserve claims. They don’t command escorts.”

“Who does?”

“The Crown. The lords to whom it delegates that power. You know that.”

“Then why does this exist?”

Gyllan read the first line again.

“Because there are people who consider the present Crown an interruption.”

The room was quiet. From the yard came the rattle of a bucket lowered into the well. Someone was drawing more water for the upper rooms.

“You mean the Vigil,” he said.

He repeated the words Orvain had shouted in the corridor. Gyllan’s face changed.

“You know them?”

“I’ve heard them at meetings. And in taverns from men who never attended one. The words alone would tell you very little.”

Elyas knew the name Vigil. At Tarlington it had belonged to old quarrels, to men who could agree upon what an emperor had meant three hundred years ago and scarcely anything done since. He had heard it at supper without attending to who fell silent.

Gyllan touched the warrant again.

Riarhin leaned forward. “And you?”

“I belonged to it.”

“Belonged?”

“I still send people to that house when I think it can help them. I have never sent a man there to hire a horse for a killing.”

Elyas watched his face. Gyllan looked angry, but his attention kept returning to the warrant, not to the people questioning him. He straightened a corner of it without seeming to know he had done so. Elyas stopped preparing the accusation he had meant to put next. The old man was recognising something.

“What are they trying to restore?”

“Utar. Depending on whom you ask, its law, its roads, or its throne.”

“Together?”

“Usually in opposition.”

Gyllan pushed the warrant back.

“The people who keep that house believe an old right can survive the government that granted it. It is why they help a miller find a charter proving he does not owe a second toll. Some of the same people believe a royal line can survive its own extinction, provided they want it badly enough.”

“And Orvain?”

“I don’t know what he believes.”

“He used to dine with those young men. The ones petitioning over Lence.”

“From both sides,” Gyllan said. “Houses that lost land, or sons, or their place at someone else’s table. The Vigil gave them somewhere to be heard.”

“And someone to blame.”

“Some arrived knowing whom they blamed. It gave them something to do about it.”

Orvain had grown up with war on the Ethorian border. He could tell a story about a burned stable and find something in it to laugh at. Never Lence. Elyas had thought his silence on that subject a kindness to Braedyn’s household.

“He tried to kill the Duke.”

“Yes. I heard you.”

The sharpness embarrassed Elyas. He looked away first.

Rekedim placed a second sheet beside the warrant. He had brought it from the room below the weighhouse: a receipt for the advance on the bay horse, with the keeper’s own entry for a bale of straw on the reverse.

“The same account paid for the room and the horse,” he said. “The keeper showed me where to ask.”

Gyllan compared the two documents.

“This name,” he said, touching the warrant. “It is one of theirs. I have seen it on appeals for money. Restoring the succession. Endowing a court before there is anyone to sit in it.”

“Is the signature his?” Elyas asked.

“I can’t swear to that.”

“Will you find someone who can?”

“Yes.”

There was no hesitation. Elyas distrusted himself for taking comfort from it.

Riarhin asked, “Why Braedyn?”

Gyllan took his hand off the page.

“You should ask him.”

“The physician says he is not to talk.”

“Then wait.”

For a moment Elyas thought Riarhin would stand. Instead she laid both hands flat on the table.

“He put a knife under his ribs. Someone gave him a bed and a horse waiting below the keep. We aren’t asking whether the Duke offended a friend.”

“No,” Gyllan said. “You are asking how much of this is something I once helped to build.”

He looked at Elyas then, not at her.

“The Duke belonged too.”

Elyas heard a cart cross the yard. Its wheel squealed once at each turn. He waited for Gyllan to qualify the statement.

“He thought the law could be recovered without putting a claimant on a throne. We argued about it. He spent money on it. That house would not have stayed open through its first winter without him.”

The room with the stripped bed. Orvain’s coat on the peg. A key hanging where a trusted visitor could take it.

Elyas had imagined finding a place outside the Duke’s protection from which the attack had come. Now he had found something the Duke had protected.

“Can you get us into their records?”

“Some.”

“Without telling them why?”

“Fewer.”

“Start with those.”

Gyllan nodded. When he stood, he took the summons but left the two sheets on the table.

“I would like to see him when I can.”

“I’ll ask.”

“Tell him Gyllan came. He’ll know which one.”

There was no joke in his voice, though Elyas thought there might once have been.

After midnight the river watch sent word that they had stopped the barge at a landing downstream. The crew said a man had forced them to cast off and gone over the side behind the pier. They had not seen where he came ashore. Their accounts differed about the knife, about what he said, about how long he had been aboard.

Orvain was not with them.

Elyas read the report to Riarhin in the passage outside the Duke’s room. She took it when he finished, although there was nothing in it he had left out.

“I should have kept him in the house,” he said.

“Which house?”

“The corridor. I called his name.”

She looked at him.

“You turned back when Braedyn fell.”

“Yes.”

“So did I.”

“I know.”

She gave the report back. For once she did not ask why he had said what he already knew.

Inside, the physician was explaining something in a low voice. Braedyn interrupted. Elyas could not hear the words, but he recognised the tone in which the Duke disliked being advised to rest.

“Will you go after him again?” Elyas asked.

“Yes.”

“Take me.”

Riarhin looked towards the door. He expected her to refuse, or to look at his arm, or to say that he had already seen what happened when he tried to stop Orvain.

“What did Gyllan mean,” she asked, “when he said there were records he could get into without telling them why?”

“He meant somebody keeps account of who asks.”

“Then we need to know who that somebody is.”

He waited.

“When the Duke lets you leave,” she said.

From inside the room, Braedyn called his name.

Chapter Seven: Leave to Go

Braedyn wanted the window open.

The physician said the room was warm enough. Braedyn said he had not asked about the temperature. Elyas crossed to the casement and unlatched it. Air moved the edge of the bed curtain; from the dark below came the creak of the ferry chains.

“Sit where I can see you.”

The chair was close to the bed. Elyas had to move a basin to make room for his feet. Someone had taken away the Duke’s boots and put slippers beneath it, their toes pointing towards the floor as if he might rise in the morning and find them ready.

“Gyllan came,” Elyas said.

“I know.”

“He told us about the Commission.”

“He would.”

“Why didn’t you?”

Braedyn looked at him. In the lamplight his face had the colour of old linen. Elyas waited for the rebuke.

“What would you have asked me yesterday?”

“Whether you belonged to the Vigil.”

“No. You would not.”

Elyas looked down at the basin. There was a scrap of thread floating in the water.

“Did Orvain ask you to honour the warrant?”

“He asked me to recognise the authority that issued it.”

“And you refused.”

“Yes.”

“Was that why he—”

“I don’t know.”

The admission was quiet. Elyas had spent much of his life in this house believing that the Duke knew more than he chose to say. It was difficult to hear the limit of that knowledge spoken from the bed.

Braedyn drew a slow breath. The sheet moved under his hand.

“He had a horse waiting. He brought the knife. Whatever my answer was to be, he had considered the other work before he came.”

“Then let me find him.”

“You have already tried.”

“Let me find who paid.”

The Duke closed his eyes. For a little while Elyas thought he had fallen asleep.

“Gyllan has a man at Pont-de-Tithe,” Braedyn said. “Ask him whether the man still has his desk.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means Gyllan will remember whom I mean.”

Elyas leaned forward.

“Are you sending me away?”

“Yes.”

The answer hurt more for being given without disguise.

“Because I brought him back?”

Braedyn opened his eyes.

“You brought back four people I told you to take or find. One of them chose to hurt me. Do not claim his part because you dislike your own.”

The physician moved at the far end of the room. Braedyn heard him and raised one finger, asking for another moment.

“Take Rekedim. Listen when he tells you that you cannot do a thing with the men or the time you have. Send what you learn by the river factors. Not through my correspondence clerk.”

Elyas glanced towards the door.

“Which clerk?”

“I don’t know that either.”

He had begun to sweat. Elyas reached for the cloth beside the water jug and held it out. Braedyn took it but seemed unable to decide what to do with it. Elyas folded it and wiped his forehead.

It was a small service. He had seen servants perform it all his life. His hand shook.

“Seniro will go north,” Braedyn said. “The Younger with him. The appointments stand.”

“What will they be told?”

“That I am ill.”

“And if they ask how ill?”

“Tell them you opened the window and I complained of nothing for almost a minute.”

The Duke tried to smile. Pain stopped it. The physician came forward then and put his hand on the back of Elyas’s chair.

Outside, Riarhin was no longer in the passage.

Gyllan’s house stood above a cooper’s yard. Its stairs were narrow, and the old knight insisted that the boy carrying his books take them down in two loads.

“I can manage.”

“I know. I would like you to manage with a hand on the rail.”

The boy divided the stack. He had the pallor of someone who spent the daylight over books, and ink on both wrists and a piece of cloth tied round the toe of one shoe where the leather had split.

“Alric,” Gyllan told Elyas. “He has been correcting my dates.”

“Only the impossible ones,” Alric said.

“Then I shall be young again by winter.”

A woman emerged from the upper room with Gyllan’s saddle bags. She was perhaps a few years younger than Elyas, a dark Hyro woman with a straight nose and strong brows. Her hair was braided tightly away from her face, leaving a small scar through one eyebrow plainly visible. She set the bags down and began checking the straps without joining the joke.

“Lyra,” Gyllan said. “Who believes I ought to have left half of what I own behind.”

“You own three whetstones.”

“One is for the kitchen knives.”

“We aren’t bringing the kitchen.”

She removed a stone from the bag and put it on the stair. Gyllan waited until she turned away, then moved it to his coat pocket. Alric saw him and said nothing.

Elyas gave the Duke’s message.

“The man with the desk,” Gyllan repeated.

“Yes.”

For a moment the old knight held the buckled bag without lifting it.

“Sir Edras Venn. We haven’t called him sir for years. He handles petitions at the Commission registry in Pont-de-Tithe. He could authenticate that signature, if anybody could.”

“Does he know Orvain?”

“I’ll ask.”

“Before we arrive?”

Gyllan looked at him.

“No. When I can see his face.”

Lyra passed Gyllan his staff. He tested the ferrule against the stair and asked whether she had packed his spare stockings.

“In the bag you haven’t opened again.”

He looked at Elyas.

“We can go.”

They did not leave that day.

The river watch brought in a man who had bought Orvain’s horse six months earlier and knew nothing about him now. A second witness remembered a fair-haired stranger washing at a landing below the city. When Elyas said that Orvain had black hair, the man became less certain of the colour, and then of whether he had seen an injured arm at all. The wounded horse holder admitted he had taken instructions from a woman who collected rents for the Commission. By the time they found her lodging, she had gone.

Riarhin went out twice and returned without Orvain. Once there was mud to her knees. The second time she brought the knife Elyas had seen on the washstand and spent a long while cleaning beneath its guard.

At noon, the Duke’s physician allowed him broth. By evening he had kept some of it down. These became the measures by which the household judged whether there was hope.

Mara found Elyas in the stable with his travelling bags open at his feet.

“Rekedim has packed food,” she said.

“I was checking the letters.”

She looked at the piece of hard biscuit in his hand. He put it back.

“Will you come?”

“I thought you needed someone here.”

“I do.”

“Then ask me that.”

He sat on the edge of the mounting block.

“I need to know what happens after we leave. Not what the household agrees has happened. What the physician says. Who comes to the door. Whether the Duke asks for us.”

“All of that?”

“What you can tell me.”

She took the bag from him and pulled the strap through its buckle.

“I’ll write through the river factors, as he told you. But I won’t be the only person you hear from. Ask the steward for his account as well.”

“Why?”

“Because I won’t see everything either.”

He wanted to tell her that he trusted her. After Orvain it seemed both necessary and insufficient. She finished the buckle, set the bag down, and came close enough for him to take her hand.

“Come back with that arm mended,” she said.

“I’ll try.”

She kissed him before he could turn the answer into a promise.

Riarhin was waiting in the lower gallery when he came down at dawn. Gyllan and the others had gone ahead to the bridge. She had asked to see Braedyn before they left and had been refused by the physician.

“Will you wait?” Elyas asked.

“No.”

They passed the study. The door was open. Fresh rushes lay on the floor, and the curtains had been pulled back from both windows. The board stood in its accustomed corner.

Riarhin did not look in.

At the head of the stairs she stopped and reached inside her coat. Elyas thought she meant to give him something. Instead she took out a strap, tightened it round her pack, and went down.

From the courtyard he could see the window he had opened for the Duke. Someone had shut it again.

Chapter Eight: Pont-de-Tithe

Beyond the bridge from Tarlington, Pont-de-Tithe spread along the rise above the Belworth. The Commission’s receiving houses stood beside a small tributary on the town’s outer edge. Its stone bridge was being repaired; a ferry took the traffic across one wagon at a time.

They joined a convoy carrying finished cloth and came away from the ferry behind two wagons of iron pots. The Scar road and the passage into Istovar still lay ahead of them.

On the eastern bank, while the animals were being loaded, a man in a plain travelling coat had stood beside the landing clerk. He had a healed split beneath his lower lip and a narrow hand which he kept on the head of his cane. The clerk asked him something, but the man answered too quietly for Elyas to hear.

He moved aside for Lyra’s horse. She thanked him. He inclined his head, then watched Rekedim bring the pack mare down the slope.

Elyas would not have remembered him except that everyone else at the landing wanted something: a place aboard, an explanation of the delay, a toll reduced, a lost sack found. The man waited until the ferry filled, then walked back up the bank without asking to cross.

Orvain had been making Elyas notice such things. He disliked how little use noticing was without knowing what to make of it.

He mentioned the man to Rekedim after they landed.

“What did he do?”

“Nothing.”

“Then remember his face.”

“Do you think—”

“I think you have a face and nothing else. Keep it until there’s something to put beside it.”

Before they entered the town they passed an abandoned posting station. Its roof had fallen across the yard. A fig tree grew through a gap in the stable wall, its roots lifting the old paving in slow, thick folds. On the road outside, a family had made a stall from the station’s doors and was selling onions from it.

Gyllan bought six. Lyra asked what they intended to do with six onions before nightfall.

“Eat some,” he said.

“And the rest?”

“Possess them.”

He hung them from his saddle and rode on. At the next village he left four with a woman who had no vegetables to put in the pot. He did it while she was showing Alric where to fetch water, and returned to the horses with the air of a man who had merely been inconvenienced by the delay.

Riarhin had gone ahead along the receiving yards. When she returned, she asked Rekedim to use a different watering place. The sergeant changed their halt without explaining why.

Elyas asked her what she had seen.

“A dead sheep above the pool.”

“Why not tell us?”

“I told him.”

After that he tried to ask questions which had not already been answered by somebody doing their work.

That evening Alric asked Rekedim for the steppe name of a position he had copied from the manual. The sergeant said it, listened to Alric repeat it, and corrected the ending.

“Does it change with the number of pieces?” Alric asked.

“With who is doing the enclosing.”

Elyas had heard those names all his life. He had thought of them as part of the board, like the colours, rather than words somebody could put into an ordinary sentence.

“Your father taught you that one?”

“Among other things.”

Rekedim moved a piece. Alric bent to study the position, and Elyas let the answer pass.

Beyond the wall, a mare began stamping. Riarhin got up. Elyas heard the scrape of a halter, and beneath it a soft whisper, too low to make out. Through the doorway he saw the mare turn her head towards Riarhin. She freed a fold of cloth caught beneath a strap and came back to her seat.

They took rooms near the Pont-de-Tithe market. While Rekedim bought fodder, Riarhin went into a second-hand clothes shop. Elyas saw her through the open door holding a length of blue silk against the light, inspecting the seams rather than the price ticket.

At supper she wore the skirt with a borrowed blouse.

It was a darker blue indoors, gathered in loose folds beneath the table; she turned back the narrow sleeves of the blouse before reaching for the bread. She had unbound her hair and fastened it again with a comb. When the innkeeper brought another candle, she moved her cup to let the light fall on the silk.

“You bought it,” Elyas said.

“Yes.”

“For tonight?”

“Among other nights.”

He had been preparing to ask whether there was someone they were to meet. Now he could see no purpose in the purchase beyond the pleasure with which she smoothed a crease from the skirt. He was embarrassed by how long it took him to find that sufficient.

He pulled out the chair beside him, his hand turned on its back in the invitation used at his father’s table: a place offered under his protection. He made the gesture without thinking she would recognise it.

She touched two fingers to his wrist, paused for exactly the required interval, and took the chair. The acceptance was correct, though the turn of her hand belonged to an older court form he had only seen at formal receptions.

Elyas sat down.

“Where did you learn that?”

“My mother.”

“She was at court?”

“She was a courtesan.”

He reached for an answer and found only several ways to make the question worse.

“Is it still done?” Riarhin asked. “She taught me fifty years ago, in a courtyard in Silkstrand, with the shutters open because the shutters there are thin.”

“The first part is.”

She nodded and broke her bread. He could not decide whether she had answered with the old form because it was all she knew, or because he had offered a courtesy too formal for an inn.

He did the arithmetic twice before he allowed himself to look at her across the candle.

“Fifty years ago.”

“Yes.”

“You don’t look—”

“No.” She turned back the other sleeve. “My father’s mother was Lethai-thora. His father was Lethai-al, and was styled a prince by people who wanted something from him. My mother was a Rocke. She told me the rest. I never met him. It makes the counting untidy.”

Elyas had been taught a word for what that made her. He could not think of a way to use it that was not an inspection.

“It seems to have stopped about twenty-six,” Riarhin said. “I notice when somebody I knew as a child gets old in front of me. The rest of the time it is a thing about me, like the freckles.”

“How old are you?” Alric asked.

“Sixty-seven. Near enough.”

“And will it begin again?”

“It may be going on very slowly. Ask me in fifty years.”

“Do you expect to be here?”

“People like me generally have two centuries. Some a good deal more. Unless something kills them.”

Lyra was looking at a faint white line above Riarhin’s wrist.

“You heal well.”

“They don’t fester.”

“Everything?”

“No. They still have to close. Enough blood out and there’s no one left to mend.”

“Do you forget things?” Alric asked.

“Nothing I have looked at.”

“That is not the same as nothing.”

“No.” She turned her cup a quarter turn. “What I attend to, I keep. All of it, in order, for as long as I have been alive. What I do not attend to was never there to be kept. People hear it and think of a room with a lamp in it.” She set the cup down. “It is the lamp.”

“Then you can be surprised.”

“I am surprised most days,” Riarhin said. “Usually by the thing I decided not to look at.”

Alric did not write it down, which Elyas noticed, because Alric wrote down everything.

She reached past him for the salt. He found he had nothing to set beside that, and that she had not expected him to.

She left the chair only when a carter sat down between her and the stair. Elyas noticed Gyllan watching her. The old knight moved his own chair without comment, making room where she could see both.

“You know her?” Elyas asked him later.

“I know people who have been unable to sleep in rooms.”

“From the wars?”

“Among other things.”

He took an onion from his pocket and began to peel it with a small eating knife.

“She served with the Duke,” Elyas said.

“Under the black banners. Both of them. Baldvyn too, before he was king.”

“He has never told me anything.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Gyllan turned the onion and took off a strip.

“I was Braedyn’s squire. Younger than you are now. He had come home from Theperygos with a university education and a quarrel ready for his father.”

“And went north.”

“Baldvyn was going. Wymund had married Elianor. There was very little left at court which Baldvyn wished to look at.”

“Braedyn went with him?”

“Where else would he have gone?”

Gyllan said it as though the answer had been obvious to everyone at the time. He cut another strip from the onion.

“Baldvyn had the generals. Braedyn had the pay and the wagons. She went forward. I knew her face better than I knew her.”

“Were they friends?”

“Those two?” Gyllan looked towards the stair where Riarhin had gone. “Yes, in time. The other two had been long before. You could always tell when Baldvyn had written. Braedyn stopped correcting my work for an hour.”

Elyas waited for more. The old man kept peeling.

Elyas thought of the corridor: the sleeve on the stone, the instruction given in a voice with no teaching in it, the Duke putting his palm flat over the back of her hand and holding it there.

“She knew what to do.”

“She did. So did he.”

“He was the one on the floor.”

“Yes,” Gyllan said. “It is not a thing you forget because it is your turn.”

He ate the strip of onion, which Elyas had not expected, and did not go on.

Elyas looked at the staff leaning against Gyllan’s chair. The wood was polished where he held it, scored near the lower end.

“You don’t carry a sword?”

“Not now.”

“May I ask why?”

“I laid it down. This does for the road.”

He turned the onion in his hand and cut away a bruised place. Elyas decided to leave the question there.

The old knight finished peeling the onion.

“Do you want half?”

“Not raw.”

“Neither do I. We must persuade the kitchen.”

He stood and took the bowl away. Elyas was grateful to him for having made the refusal so easy to receive.

The next morning they went to the Commission registry.

It occupied a former posting house at the division of three roads. The original building had been enlarged with a timber counting room and a covered court where carriers could have their loads weighed. The old imperial eagle above the door had lost its head. Somebody had fitted a lamp beneath it, making the broken carving serve as a hood against the rain.

Edras Venn received them behind a desk.

He was thinner than Gyllan and perhaps a little younger, with the stiff shoulder of a man who had once carried injuries more openly. He stood when he saw his old companion, hesitated, then came round the desk and took him by both arms.

“You should have sent word.”

“We might have missed you.”

“I haven’t been anywhere in eleven years.”

“Then I judged it well.”

Venn laughed. He had a good laugh, warm and unguarded. Elyas felt himself relax at the sound and immediately wished he had not.

Chapter Nine: The Coal Door

The signature was genuine.

Venn said it without touching the warrant. Then he put on his spectacles, bent over the page, and said it again.

“His hand. His seal. Whether he understood what he was signing is a different question.”

“Whose hand?” Elyas asked.

“Lord Merrow’s. He has a fund for the restoration of the succession. What he considers the succession changes with his dinner guests.”

“Did he pay Orvain?”

“I don’t keep his private accounts.”

“Do you know Orvain?”

“Yes.”

Venn looked at Gyllan.

“What has he done?”

“Stabbed Braedyn.”

For a moment the only sound was Alric setting down his satchel too carefully. Venn took off his spectacles.

“Alive?”

“When we left.”

“He was always a fool,” Venn said.

Elyas could not tell which man he meant until Venn added, “I told Orvain not to go to Tarlington with that paper.”

“You saw it before?”

“I prepared the copy.”

Gyllan drew a chair closer to the desk.

“Tell us what you thought it was for.”

Venn rubbed the bridge of his nose. He had begun to look tired.

“A recognition. That the Commission might issue safe-conducts to its own escorts. Merrow wanted Fenwood’s assent because without it the eastern crossings were closed to any authority we claimed. Orvain thought he could get it.”

“By asking?” Elyas said.

“Yes, by asking. He had been in the Duke’s service. He thought the Duke liked him.”

“He did.”

Venn looked at him then.

“I am sorry.”

Elyas did not know whether to accept it. He asked where Orvain had gone after the attack.

Venn said he had not seen him.

Riarhin, who had remained by the window, asked whether anyone had paid for a wounded rider travelling west.

“That is a question for the carrier accounts.”

“Let us see them.”

“I can ask for an extract.”

“How long?”

“A day. Perhaps two.”

Elyas looked towards Gyllan. The old knight had removed his gloves and laid them on his knee. His expression told Elyas nothing.

“Ask,” Gyllan said.

They were given rooms above the old stables. Alric discovered that the shelves in one contained registers and asked whether they were to sleep beside them. Venn said they were obsolete weighings and nobody would need them before morning.

Lyra opened one while they unpacked.

“Why keep them if they’re obsolete?”

“Because it costs more to decide which can be thrown away,” Venn said.

He sent a boy up with bread and cold meat. He had work to finish, he told Gyllan, but would join them after supper.

Rekedim went downstairs before eating. He returned with coal dust on one hand.

“What were you looking for?” Elyas asked.

“The place the coal comes in.”

“Why?”

“The front door is for petitioners. Carriers use the back. I wanted to know who signs when a load arrives.”

“And?”

“The same man signs for coal and iron. He’s here until dusk.”

Gyllan put down his cup.

“Will he let us see the receipts?”

“He thinks we’re checking a disputed charge.”

“Are we?” Elyas asked.

“We will be when we find it.”

They went down together. Riarhin stayed in the yard, looking at the buildings beyond it. Lyra brought the obsolete register. Alric brought paper.

The receiving clerk worked in a room beside the coal store, with a rag beneath his wrist to keep his sleeve clean. He looked at the warrant and said he knew nothing about commissions. If there was a charge to contest, he wanted the load number.

Rekedim gave him the horse receipt from Tarlington.

The clerk found the account. There were three payments in the previous month: the horse, the room, and an allowance for a travelling officer. The officer’s allowance had been entered against a carrier bond.

“What carrier?” Elyas asked.

“That doesn’t say.”

“Where would it say?”

“In the book the bond was opened in.”

“Can we see that?”

“You can ask upstairs.”

Lyra turned her register round.

“Is this the right series?”

The clerk looked irritated, then interested.

“Where did you get that?”

“They’re in our room.”

“Then that’s where they put them.”

He stood, wiped his hands, and came round the table. The obsolete entries were the daily weighings; the open bonds belonged to another series; neither referred to the other by a name a stranger would have known to ask for. Alric began writing the references down. In a few minutes the clerk was dictating and scarcely attending to who was listening.

Elyas watched the columns fill. No lock had been opened. No man had been bribed. They had simply found someone whose work made it possible to ask the next question.

The account ran west.

By evening the table in their room was covered with copied entries.

Iron fittings from Aeler. Finished shafts. Hardened points packed in greased cloth. The consignments passed through Vhas under bridge and road contracts, then divided among carriers bound for the Acasian marches.

Some of it was precisely what it claimed to be. Rekedim tapped an entry for chain.

“A sluice would take that. So would a ferry.”

“And these?” Elyas asked.

“Enough spare axles for a town with no wagons.”

Alric had found the destination in a gazetteer. It was an old military station, abandoned twice and reoccupied by whoever could hold its well.

“Could they be rebuilding it?”

“Yes,” Rekedim said. “With all this iron, they could rebuild it several times.”

Lyra was comparing two sheets. She had eaten nothing since midday. Gyllan put bread beside her elbow and she moved it to keep grease off the paper.

“There are six wagons,” she said.

“On which road?”

“On this one. The next weighing has five.”

“Loads get divided.”

“The weight doesn’t.”

She showed Rekedim the figures. He drew his chair beside hers.

Six wagons left the registry. Five were weighed at the next post with all the original cargo aboard. The sixth had gone somewhere without its load, but its driver and two passengers were still being fed from the carrier allowance until the day before the attack.

“An escort?” Elyas asked.

“Perhaps,” Rekedim said.

“An empty wagon,” Lyra said. “Coming back. Why pay for it to come back empty?”

Nobody answered immediately.

Venn knocked and came in with a lamp. He said he had finished his work. He looked over the table, asked whether they had found what they wanted, and apologised that the extracts had not yet been delivered.

Gyllan told him what they had found instead.

Lyra looked up sharply. Elyas saw it, but Venn was already drawing a page towards the light.

“That carrier,” he said. “Yes. I know where their driver puts up. I can have him brought in.”

“When?”

“In the morning.”

“The empty wagon,” Lyra said. “Ask him about that one.”

Venn nodded. He did not write it down.

After he left, she asked Gyllan why he had told him.

“Because we came for his help.”

“He knows we’re looking for the allowance. Now he knows where we’ve found it.”

Gyllan took the spectacles from his pocket and polished them although he had not been wearing them.

“He helped me once when he had a great deal to lose.”

“How long ago?”

The old knight put the spectacles away.

“Long enough that you are right to ask.”

He rose and went to the door. Elyas followed him into the passage.

“Should we leave?”

“Not without the name on the western receipt. But you should have the horses ready.”

Below them, a cart was being brought into the covered court. One wheel squealed. A man called for a lantern. It sounded like ordinary work at the end of an ordinary day.

Chapter Ten: The Receiving Room

The driver did not come in the morning.

Venn came instead. He looked as if he had slept badly; the skin beneath his eyes was swollen and the collar of his shirt sat crookedly against his neck.

“He’s willing to speak. He won’t come here.”

“Where?” Gyllan asked.

“The carrier’s receiving room. Across the yard.”

Rekedim went to the window. Riarhin was already below, bringing the pack mare out of the stable.

“Have him come to the door,” Elyas said. “We’ll speak there.”

“He is frightened of the Commission.”

“You are the Commission.”

“I’m a clerk with a seal. It is a different thing from the people who pay the account.”

Venn looked at Gyllan.

“He has the western receipt. The original. Not an extract. I thought you wanted it.”

Lyra began gathering the loose papers. Alric helped her. Elyas could hear their quick, careful movements behind him.

“We’ll speak to him,” Gyllan said. “At the door. If he will not do that, he can keep his receipt.”

Venn nodded too quickly.

The receiving room occupied the far corner of the court. Carts backed beneath its raised loading door; a smaller door beside it admitted men on foot. Both were open. Elyas could see a table, a stool, a man in a patched coat waiting beside a stove that had gone cold.

Riarhin took one look and went round the side of the building.

Rekedim remained with the horses. He handed Elyas his reins and said, quietly, “Keep these.”

Gyllan stopped at the threshold.

“Your name?”

“Perrin.”

“Bring the receipt here, Perrin.”

The man hesitated, then came forward with a folded sheet. There was no weapon in his hands. Elyas looked past him. The room smelled of coal ash and old leather. Against the far wall stood a high-sided wagon with its shafts raised.

Six wagons out. Five with all the weight.

Lyra said, “That’s it.”

The man turned his head towards her.

She had seen the painted number on the wagon’s side. Elyas followed her glance and saw the number too, black over an older red mark.

Gyllan reached for the receipt.

Perrin let it go and stepped backwards.

From behind the wagon a man raised a crossbow.

Elyas dropped the reins. He hit Gyllan’s shoulder with his good hand, trying to push him clear, and the bolt struck the doorframe where his own chest had been. Splinters cut his cheek.

Gyllan stumbled into the room.

There were men behind the open loading door. Two, then a third. One came across the threshold at Lyra, who had drawn a short sword but had Alric too close behind her to step back.

Gyllan drove the end of his staff into the attacker’s wrist, then caught Lyra by the shoulder and pushed her backwards through the doorway, into Alric. His foot slipped from the loading step. The three of them went down together.

Elyas had his sword clear. He turned the first man’s cut, felt the shock in his bad arm, and gave ground. Gyllan was trying to rise. There was no room for him between Elyas and the wall.

“Stay down.”

The man behind the wagon dropped his crossbow and reached for a spear. Elyas saw the point come round the raised shafts.

Rekedim struck the loading door with his shoulder. It swung inwards, catching the spearman against the wagon. The spear fell. Elyas used the moment to get his back to the wall and move clear of Gyllan.

Riarhin came through the rear door with her straight sword in her hand.

The spearman had recovered his weapon. She struck him as he turned towards her. Elyas saw the sword withdraw and the man drop against the wheel. Another attacker ran for the loading door and met Rekedim there. The man facing Elyas threw his knife down.

“Hands clear,” Elyas said.

His voice sounded steady. The point of his sword did not.

Behind him, Gyllan had managed to sit up. Blood was running from a split above his ear. The old knight put his fingers to it, looked at them, and asked whether anyone had seen where Lyra went.

“Here.”

She was kneeling beside Alric. His sleeve had been cut from wrist to elbow, and he was holding the edges together as if that might repair what lay beneath.

“Let me see,” she said.

He took his hand away. Blood filled the cut again.

Gyllan came down beside her. He did not touch the wound. Lyra set her fingers to either side of it and began something under her breath which Elyas could not distinguish from the noise in the yard.

The blood slowed. Alric cried out once and then went still, staring at his arm.

“Don’t watch,” Gyllan told him.

“Is she—”

“Look at me.”

By the time Lyra lifted her fingers, the edges had drawn together. She sat back on her heels, breathing hard.

“Too quickly,” Gyllan said.

“It was bleeding.”

“Yes. And now he has to use it.”

She took Alric’s hand and made him open and close it. On the third attempt he could move all his fingers. She closed her eyes briefly before asking him to do it again.

Elyas put his sword away and fetched Gyllan’s staff from beneath the loading step. The old knight used it to get up.

Venn was in the office.

He had not run. Elyas found him standing beside the desk with a small leather case open in front of him. Inside were money, seals, folded papers, a clean shirt.

“You told them.”

Venn looked at the blood on Elyas’s sleeve.

“Is Gyllan—”

“Alive. Did you expect him to be?”

The clerk sat. He pushed the open case away from him.

“I said to stop you leaving with the accounts.”

“Who did you tell?”

“The people who own them.”

“Names.”

Venn gave him two. Elyas knew neither. He made him say them again while Alric wrote them down. The boy had followed him into the room without his satchel. His paper was torn from the blank back of one of their copies.

“You were helping us,” Elyas said.

“Yes.”

“And telling them.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“They paid for the registry. They pay the carriers. You were looking into their business. I could not keep it from them.”

“You could have told us.”

Venn put both hands over his face. He stayed that way until Rekedim arrived with the local watch captain.

The captain wanted to know under whose authority he was to arrest a Commission officer. Elyas gave him his name, the Duke’s letter, the dead men in the receiving room, and the two surviving attackers. It was still not enough until the captain’s own clerk read the warrant that purported to displace the Crown.

Then the captain became very busy.

Elyas left him to it. On the way out he saw that Venn’s clean shirt had fallen from the case. One sleeve lay in the dust beneath the desk.

The receipt Perrin had carried was real.

They found it beneath the loading step where Gyllan had dropped it. It bore an Aeler receiving mark beside three of the consignments they had copied, but the edge carrying the factor’s name had been torn away. Beneath it another hand had authorised the transfer of the empty wagon and its travelling allowance to Merrow’s account.

The driver admitted carrying a wounded man from a landing below Tarlington to Pont-de-Tithe the previous day. He had waited with the wagon while someone took the passenger through a courtyard. The doors had closed before he saw which house stood beyond it.

“A name?” Elyas asked.

“They paid through the office.”

“Which office?”

The driver looked at Venn’s empty doorway.

Rekedim asked about the yard, the colour of the doors, the time of day. Alric wrote with pauses to ease his injured fingers. There were eleven yards in Pont-de-Tithe that might have been the one described.

Gyllan waited until the driver had been taken away before speaking.

“I should not have told Venn what we found.”

Lyra sat on the edge of the table. She had been looking at the floor.

“You knew him.”

“I knew him a long time ago.”

“He helped you.”

“Yes.”

She looked up.

“What am I meant to do with that?”

Gyllan touched the dressing above his ear. He had taken some trouble over the bandage and none over the blood on his collar.

“Ask me again the next time I think knowing a man settles the question.”

“And if you don’t listen?”

“Then ask somebody else.”

Elyas looked down at the copied accounts. He had waited for Gyllan to resolve the matter as he resolved his questions on the road. The old knight was still there, hurt and tired, and had given Lyra something she would have to use against him if he was wrong again.

“Will you stay with us?” Elyas asked.

Gyllan looked at Lyra, then Alric.

“I mean to.”

“Then I will send the depositions to Tarlington.”

They spent the rest of the afternoon preparing copies. Riarhin came in with the recovered receipt and stayed beside the window, looking out at the yard while Alric copied its endorsements.

At supper he brought the bowls in a stack against his chest. His grip failed and the top one fell, breaking on the stone. The boy stopped as though the noise had hurt him.

Elyas took the rest of the stack. He put six bowls on the table, swept up the broken pieces himself, and paid the cook for the seventh before she had finished telling him what it cost.

When he returned, Riarhin moved her pack off the bench beside her.

He sat down without having to ask.

Chapter Eleven: The Deputy

The man waiting outside their room stood up when the cook came past with a bucket. He had chosen the chair nearest the stair, although there were better ones in the passage. His coat was brown, brushed thin at the elbows. The only thing in his hands was a folded invitation.

Elyas would have taken him for a messenger if the cook had not lowered her voice to ask whether he wanted anything.

“No, thank you,” the man said. “They have had a difficult day.”

He looked at Alric’s bandage, then away before the boy needed to do anything with being looked at.

“Kaeden Fayd. I work for Baron Seld.”

Gyllan stopped behind Elyas.

“In what capacity?”

“Deputy.”

“To which office?”

“The one you’ve been asking questions of.”

Elyas opened the door. Fayd waited for Gyllan to enter before following. He did not take the chair at the table’s head. When Rekedim moved the papers, however, he waited until a particular sheet had been put face down before sitting.

Elyas saw it. So did Riarhin. She remained by the window.

“The people in the receiving room were not sent by me,” Fayd said.

“Your superior?”

“Men whose allowances he authorised. I would prefer to distinguish what I know from what would be convenient to say.”

“Where is Orvain?” Riarhin asked.

Fayd put the invitation on the table.

“Alive.”

Elyas felt his hand close on the back of the chair. The clerk noticed without looking down.

“Here?”

“In Pont-de-Tithe.”

“Under whose protection?”

“Mine.”

Gyllan sat down heavily. Fayd moved his own chair enough to leave space for the old man’s knee.

“You know what he did,” Elyas said.

“Yes.”

“And you’ve kept him.”

“I kept him in Tarlington until the searches became inconvenient. He was brought here yesterday. He will not be moved again.”

He said it with the ordinary certainty of someone stating when a shop closed. Elyas looked at the man’s hands. They rested on either side of the invitation, unhidden and quite still.

“You’ve come into our room to tell us this?”

“I thought you would prefer it to another message through Venn.”

Riarhin came away from the window.

“Tell us what you want.”

“The Baron’s private ledger.”

Fayd described a small book with a worn green cover and brass corners. Baron Seld kept it in a locked drawer in his study. He carried the key on a chain beneath his waistcoat, but the lock was an ordinary one. The difficulty was the room, the clerk outside it and the fact that Seld took his visitors there himself.

“What does the book contain?” Rekedim asked.

“Payments he does not enter in the Commission’s account. Promises to Lord Merrow’s fund which contradict promises to the Road committee. Some of the same securities pledged to both.”

“You have the other accounts.”

“Yes.”

“Then you know what he’s done.”

“I know where the difference is. I require his record of it.”

Gyllan had been looking at the invitation. “You could bring a complaint.”

“To him?”

“To the members.”

“They would ask him for the book. He would furnish an account.”

“Not this one,” Lyra said.

“No.” Fayd turned towards her. “Not this one.”

Elyas watched the small adjustment. Fayd had not patronised her or praised her for understanding. He had simply begun addressing the part of the answer she could use. It made Elyas more uncomfortable than flattery would have done.

“What happens to the Baron?” he asked.

“That depends what I find.”

“And you take his office.”

“If I can secure the votes.”

The answer was so plain that Elyas almost thanked him for it.

“Would that restore Utar?” Gyllan asked.

“No. Leaving him in place would make restoring it more difficult.”

He said Utar without the slight change of voice Elyas had heard from men speaking of lost things. For Fayd it appeared to be a place with unfinished business waiting in it.

Riarhin drew the invitation towards her.

“A dance.”

“The Baron agreed to give one. Several members are already here. They need an occasion to speak without attending a meeting.”

“When did he agree?”

“Three days ago.”

Elyas looked up.

“The household had not announced the attack.”

“No.”

“How did you know?”

“Orvain came to a house I pay for with a knife wound in his shoulder. I had reason to inquire.”

Outside, the innkeeper called for someone to bring more water. Fayd waited for the footsteps to pass.

“I suggested an evening at which the Baron could reassure his subscribers. He found the idea useful.”

“You were arranging this while we were searching Tarlington,” Elyas said.

“I was arranging access. I did not yet know whom I could send through it.”

He glanced at Riarhin, briefly. She was holding the invitation by its unsealed edge.

“You could steal it yourself.”

“I have entered that room daily for eleven years. If its lock is opened, he will first ask what I knew about it.”

“He’ll ask that anyway.”

“Yes.”

Fayd met her eyes. Elyas had seen men hold Riarhin’s attention by force of rank or offence. This man did neither. He sat while she considered the risk he was accepting and made no attempt to reduce it for her.

“What do we get besides an address?” she asked.

“The name behind the receiving mark on your damaged receipt. A current account number. The house in Acasia where inquiries will reach her.”

“You could write any name.”

“I can show you an earlier payment in Seld’s public account. You can compare it before doing anything for me.”

Rekedim pulled a clean sheet towards him.

“Show us.”

Fayd left before dusk. The invitation remained on the table with the comparison copied beneath it.

Alric had asked for the account number twice. Fayd had waited through both readings without correcting the speed at which the boy wrote. When he reached the stair, the cook stood aside for him again.

“He hasn’t a guard,” Elyas said.

“Not in the passage,” Riarhin answered.

“Would you do it?”

“Yes.”

“Because you believe him?”

“Because I believe he wants the book.”

Gyllan rubbed the bridge of his nose. “There are men in the Vigil who would give us help without this.”

“Can you find one before tomorrow night?”

The old knight did not answer.

Elyas read the invitation. He and a companion were invited under their own names. Seld apparently meant to display the Duke of Istovar’s son among his guests.

“He thinks I’ll come after what happened in his receiving yard.”

“He thinks you’ll want an explanation,” Rekedim said.

“I do.”

“Then he knows something about you.”

Riarhin took the invitation back and read the description of the evening.

“There will be dancing.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him.

“You can do that?”

Elyas put his hand flat on the table.

“Rather better than you appear to expect.”

“Good. I’ll need a skirt.”

“You bought one.”

“So I did.”

The innkeeper moved two tables for them. Elyas paid for the room until supper and asked Alric to take the chairs to the wall. Riarhin came down in the blue silk with her hair loosely pinned. She had left the sword upstairs.

He offered his hand. She took it with the old pause, exactly measured, and stepped to his left.

“The other side.”

“For this one?”

“For any of the dances we’re likely to be given.”

“My mother taught it this way.”

“How long ago?”

“You’ve done the sum.”

He began again. Her weight was always where she intended it. She could have followed a correction before he finished speaking; twice she did, then returned to the old form when they began the figure afresh.

He stopped.

“You aren’t having difficulty.”

“No.”

“You’re having difficulty being taught.”

“That sounds worse.”

From the window Gyllan made a noise over his book. Elyas declined to look at him.

“At the turn you let me pass, then give your hand to the next man.”

“How long does he keep it?”

“Until the next turn.”

“And if I don’t like him?”

“The figure is the same.”

“An excellent arrangement for him.”

He set the pace with his heel. She followed him through half the figure, then stopped in a place where he had to go round her. It was graceful. It was also wrong.

“At least Cedric let you finish,” she said.

Elyas let go of her hand.

The amusement left her face, but she did not take the remark back.

“He agreed to terms,” Elyas said.

“He did.”

“I knew he was frightened.”

“I know.”

“He kept looking at the men by the gate. Every time he got near enough to hurt me.”

“And you thought he’d want them to see him generous.”

Elyas looked at the space they had cleared. He had wanted that very much. He had seen the looks and the fear and supplied the part that ought to follow.

“What should I have done?”

“Listen to Edran.”

He looked up at her.

“Afterwards?”

“You did listen afterwards.” She offered her hand. “The other side, was it?”

They began again. This time she made the turn correctly, smiled at him with such unexpected warmth that he nearly missed his own step, and passed him without waiting to see whether he had caught up.

At supper he asked Rekedim to go through the arrangements once more.

The sergeant did, without making him explain why.

Chapter Twelve: The Blue Silk

Before they left the inn, Elyas found Riarhin leaning towards the little mirror over the washstand. A thin scar crossed her cheek, so faint he had taken it for a mark left by sleep. She touched colour over it with the end of a brush and turned her face to the light.

“Is it straight?” she asked.

“The scar?”

“The clasp. Help me.”

He fastened it at the back of her neck. In the mirror the line on her cheek had disappeared. She inspected his work, lifted the blue skirt clear of the floor and went to find Lyra.

Baron Seld received them beneath a portrait of a man whose armour had cost more than the room.

He held Elyas’s hand between both of his. The gesture was too familiar for a first meeting and sufficiently public that Elyas could not shorten it without appearing cold. Seld had chosen his place well. The guests waiting behind them could see his concern.

“A most unhappy business. I have instructed the captain to pursue it without consideration for office.”

“The captain is already pursuing it.”

“Then we are in agreement.”

Seld released one hand and used it to introduce his wife. She had been watching his face during the exchange. Now she looked at Riarhin, taking in the silk and the unadorned throat, and inclined her head a fraction less than she had to Elyas.

Riarhin answered the exact inclination. No more, no less.

“Your house is beautiful,” she said.

“The old part is difficult to heat.”

“The candles must help.”

Lady Seld laughed. It was the first sound she had made which was not required of her. She turned to point out the new gallery, and Elyas felt Riarhin’s fingers settle on his sleeve as if they had spent years entering rooms together.

He had known she could perform the old courtesies. He had not expected the ease with which she gave another woman a subject her husband could not correct.

They passed into the hall.

The musicians were waiting for a man beside the hearth to stop speaking. He knew it. Each time the leader raised his bow, the man began another sentence, and the people round him laughed a little before there had been anything to laugh at.

“Merrow?” Riarhin murmured.

“His agent. The one with the amber pin.”

“Do you know him?”

“I’ve met him. He doesn’t remember.”

“How fortunate.”

She lifted her hand from his sleeve as Seld’s steward approached. Elyas saw how the absence invited the steward to offer his own arm, and how the slight movement of her body made it impossible without appearing to separate her from Elyas. The man settled for asking whether she wished for wine.

“Only if my companion may have some too. He has been made to discuss roads all day.”

“I have very little else to offer,” Elyas said.

“You promised dancing.”

The steward smiled as though they had included him in a confidence. He sent for two cups and went away.

“You’re enjoying this,” Elyas said softly.

“I like the skirt.”

“That wasn’t what—”

The musicians began. Riarhin turned towards them before he could finish, her face changing with the first phrase. Elyas knew the dance. It was the one he had abandoned trying to teach her.

She gave him her hand on the correct side.

On the second figure he stopped wondering whether she would make a mistake.

On the third he found himself watching the other men watching her and disliked them for it, which was absurd and precisely what her hand on his arm had been designed to accomplish.

She did not claim the whole room. She gave each partner enough attention to make him believe he had recovered her from it. An older man with a stiff hip received a shorter step before he needed to ask for it. A young one who squeezed her fingers found her already turning towards the next figure. She laughed once at something Elyas had said and made him wish he had meant it to be funny.

Across the floor, Alric carried a tray beneath a stack of folded napkins. His borrowed page’s coat was too long at the wrist. The injured arm was held close, and his mouth had the set it acquired over a difficult passage of text.

A woman beside the service door raised a hand to stop him.

Elyas turned his next movement slightly, bringing Riarhin into her line of sight.

“Lady Seld was telling me about the gallery,” he said as the figure brought him within speaking distance. “Will it be opened tonight?”

The woman was a relative. He had watched the Baron submit to an adjustment of his collar from her. She could not resist knowing more about the house than its hostess had told him.

“The north gallery? It has never been closed.”

“Then I misunderstood.”

She began explaining where guests were permitted to walk. Alric passed behind her.

Riarhin’s fingers pressed Elyas’s once. He kept his eyes on the woman and asked a second question.

At the far door Lyra appeared with a linen basket on her hip. She wore a plain cap and looked irritated by the weight, an expression she had not needed to rehearse. A servant directed her down the passage. She went without looking at the dancers.

The gear had entered the house.

They drank the wine standing, because taking a chair would have made leaving it an event.

Baron Seld came to them with Merrow’s agent. His smile had become easier since the dance. Elyas could feel the conclusion being reached: a young man whose wardship had given him an exaggerated opinion of his authority, comfortably distracted now.

“Your father’s health?” Seld asked.

“Good enough to make inquiries when his letters go unanswered.”

“I must look to my correspondence.”

“You must.”

Riarhin lowered her eyes to her cup. The tiny smile might have been admiration for Elyas or amusement at him. Seld chose the explanation he preferred.

“And the Duke of Fenwood? We hear he has taken a fever.”

Elyas watched the agent, whose attention had gone quite still.

“He is receiving his physician.”

“A cautious answer.”

“It is the one I have.”

The agent touched the amber pin, then took his hand away. Seld had supplied a question the man did not wish asked quite so directly. Elyas gave him no relief by turning towards him.

“I have reports to send home tomorrow,” he continued. “The captain will wish to know that he has your assistance.”

“He has every assistance.”

Riarhin leaned a little closer to Elyas.

“Must you conduct all your business before supper?”

“Would you prefer after?”

“I had hoped you might forget it.”

Seld laughed and put a hand on the agent’s arm. “We have detained you.”

Elyas bowed. Riarhin’s fingers returned to his sleeve, warm through the thin cloth.

They walked towards the gallery. Her step slowed where people could see it. At the turn she looked back once, not towards the Baron but towards the floor they had left, and let Elyas draw her out of view.

He opened the little withdrawing room. She went in ahead of him.

The door closed.

The catch was new. Riarhin lifted it twice, listening, then dropped the small bolt beneath it.

“Chair.”

Elyas moved one. She set its back under the handle and knelt by the linen chest.

Lyra had left the bundle beneath the second layer. Good. Alric’s parcel was behind the screen, wrapped in napkins which would have to be returned. The boy had tied it with his left hand; the knot was a nuisance. She cut the cord.

“Turn round.”

Elyas turned towards the window.

She undid the skirt. The clasp had rubbed above her hip through the last two dances. She laid the silk over the screen instead of letting it fall and pulled on her trousers.

“You knew the dance,” he said.

“Yes.”

“All of it.”

“The woman with the keys was going to stop Alric. You did well.”

He said nothing. She put a hand inside the bundle for the soft shoes.

“Have I offended you?”

“No.”

“Then help with this.”

He turned at her word and held the strap while she secured the short roll of tools beneath her coat. His hands were steady now. The dancing had helped more than the rehearsal.

“Lady Seld gave you the better answer,” he said.

“About the gallery?”

“About her husband.”

Riarhin looked up.

“She waited until he was speaking to me before she called the old rooms cold. He would have explained the cost otherwise.”

“Yes.”

“You noticed.”

“So did you.”

She took Toothpick from the napkins and slid it into her boot. The sword would stay here. There was no useful way to conceal its length in a corridor meant for clerks.

Beyond the door a couple walked past, laughing too loudly. Riarhin waited for the woman’s uneven steps to reach the gallery before shifting the chair.

“Two knocks, then one. Only me. If it’s someone else, you’re waiting for your companion to come back from the privy.”

“For how long?”

“Until I do.”

She saw the answer arrive on his face and relented.

“If the musicians stop twice, take Lyra and Alric out. Rekedim knows where to wait.”

“And you?”

“I know too.”

She opened the door just far enough to look through it.

Chapter Thirteen: The Green Book

The study was one floor above the music. Fayd had given her four doors between it and the service stair. There were five.

The fifth stood open. Inside, two men were setting glasses on a table. One wore the Baron’s colours, the other the narrow blue band she had seen on the Commission’s petition clerks. A meeting could require wine without becoming a dance.

Riarhin continued past them carrying a folded cloth. Lyra had put three into the bundle. She had brought the cleanest.

At the foot of the stair a maid held out a hand for it.

“I’ll take that.”

“Above.”

“They have their own.”

Riarhin let her have the cloth. The maid looked at the sleeve beneath her coat, then at her face. Riarhin lowered her voice.

“The gentleman said there was a room.”

For a moment the maid did not move. Then she looked back towards the music, her mouth tightening at something that was evidently not new.

“Not up there.”

Riarhin allowed herself to look dismayed.

“Where?”

“Wait here.”

The maid went to the two men. Riarhin took the stairs while all three were looking towards the hall.

She had disliked the exchange. Her mother would have disliked how little use she had made of it. Neither opinion altered the distance to the landing.

A clerk sat outside the study with a writing board on his knees. He looked up as she approached.

“His lordship is below.”

“He asked for the narrow account. Green, with corners.”

The clerk’s glance went to the study door before he asked who had sent her. Fayd had been right about the book, then.

“Master Fayd. He said you would bring it.”

“I’ll speak to him.”

“Very well.”

She moved aside. The clerk rose, put his board on the chair and tried the study handle. Locked. He left it so and went down the stair, taking the ring of office keys from his belt as he walked.

She counted his steps until the music covered them.

The study door’s lock was better than the drawer’s would be. Fayd had not promised otherwise. She crouched close enough to keep her hands in the shadow of the jamb and felt for the wards.

The first tool was too broad. She returned it to the roll. On the second the lock gave a little and stopped.

Somebody laughed downstairs. The clerk answered. Too near.

She eased the tool back, turned it a fraction and tried again. The bolt withdrew.

Inside, a lamp stood on the desk. The shutters were closed. There was a second door behind the book press which Fayd had not mentioned, and the mat beneath it had been kicked back into the room.

She went to it first. Voices on the other side. The two men carrying wine had been preparing the outer room of this one.

She laid her palm against the latch to feel whether it moved, waited through a sentence about Merrow’s guarantee, then crossed to the desk.

There were three locked drawers. The centre lock had a pale scrape beside it where Seld’s key had missed. The others were dusty along the lower edge.

This one was ordinary. She opened it quickly.

The green book lay under a bundle of loose letters. She lifted both together and set the letters back. Beneath them was another small book, red, which she left. She had not been hired to steal the contents of a life.

On the open page of the green ledger, Merrow’s name appeared above a sum charged to the Road committee. The sum was familiar from Alric’s sheet. A line beneath it was crossed out so lightly that she could read the account number through it.

She read the facing page too. Then she put the book inside her coat.

The other door opened.

Riarhin was already moving towards the space between the shutter and the book press. The man who came in carried a tray and was watching the glasses on it. She reached the shutter before he looked up.

“Who—”

She caught the tray with one hand and his coat with the other, driving him backwards against the door. Glass clicked but did not break. His breath went out in a grunt.

“Quiet.”

He tried to shout. She put the edge of her hand beneath his jaw and pressed until the sound became a swallow.

The other room went silent.

“Jev?”

Riarhin looked at the man she held. Young, broad in the shoulders, frightened now. She moved her hand enough to let him speak.

“Dropped one,” he managed.

“Pick it up, then.”

She waited. A chair scraped beyond the door. No one came.

She took him with her to the desk, his own weight kept a little ahead of his feet, and set the tray down. The green book pressed against her ribs.

“You will sit here until the music stops.”

He shook his head.

“They’ll blame me.”

They would. She looked at the glasses, then at the empty drawer.

“Tell them a woman put a knife to you.”

His eyes went to her hands. She had drawn no knife.

“You can describe me.”

“You’re allowing me?”

“No. I’m telling you not to make something up.”

She left through the corridor door. Behind her the young man drew a breath too large to be quiet. She could not leave him safe and leave with the book. The door closed on him before he decided whether to call.

The clerk was on the stair coming up with Fayd.

Riarhin stepped into the open room at the landing. A bed stood beneath a coverlet with its tassels combed straight. No other door. The window looked over the stable yard.

She heard the clerk say, “She used your name.”

“I shall speak to his lordship.”

Fayd did not hurry. The quiet steps passed the door, followed by the clerk’s quicker ones. Riarhin took off her coat, turned it inside out to hide the dark facing and folded it over her arm with the book enclosed.

Downstairs the music had ended. There was applause, then the sound of instruments being tuned.

She reached the withdrawing room and knocked twice, then once.

Elyas opened the door before the last knock had finished.

“You’re early.”

“Someone else was earlier.”

She put the book in his hands and went to the screen. He had hung the skirt higher, away from a wet mark on the floor. She was unreasonably glad of it.

“Can we leave?”

“In a moment.”

She changed with him facing the window. The pins were harder to put back into her hair than they had been to remove. Her hand wanted to hurry. She made it stop.

When she turned, he was still holding the book.

“Inside your coat. The other side from the invitation.”

He obeyed.

“Don’t carry it like a confession.”

“What does a confession weigh?”

“More than that.”

He looked at her and laughed once, without pleasure. She took his arm.

“Not yet,” she said.

“What?”

“The face. We’re pleased with ourselves.”

They went out together.

Fayd waited in the gatehouse after the servants had brought their horses.

Alric and Lyra were already beyond the arch with Rekedim and Gyllan. Riarhin could see the boy carrying her sword in its cloth, held like a parcel he had been told not to put down.

Fayd’s collar had been pulled loose at one side. There was a red mark on his neck.

“He was displeased,” he said when Elyas looked at it.

“You went in.”

“He rang for me.”

“And?”

“He is now occupied with a difficulty in the other account.”

Elyas took the book out. Fayd put a folded paper on the mounting block but kept his hand over it while he checked the brass corners and turned to a marked page.

His expression changed very slightly. He read the line twice.

“Enough?” Riarhin asked.

“Yes.”

He took his hand from the paper. An address, an instruction to the keeper, a second account number. Veyra Keld, written plainly beneath the receiving mark which had survived on their torn receipt. A house in the southeastern Acasian marches.

“Orvain is at the disused mill above the salt stair,” he said. “On this side of the Scar road. The keeper has been paid through the week. Tell him I have withdrawn the protection.”

“Will he resist?” Elyas asked.

“The keeper will not. I cannot answer for Orvain.”

Riarhin read the instruction. Fayd had signed it in his own name.

“What happens to the man with the tray?”

“The Baron has decided he was overpowered.”

“He was.”

“Then we agree about something.”

Fayd put the ledger inside his coat. It changed the hang of the cloth less than it had changed Elyas’s.

“The road to Acasia is long,” he said. “You should buy fodder before leaving Istovar.”

“Is that concern?” Riarhin asked.

“Advice.”

She let him pass beneath the gate. No one stopped him. Elyas watched until the brown coat was lost among the servants crossing the yard.

“He’ll have the office.”

“Probably,” Rekedim said.

“We’ve helped him.”

“Yes.”

Elyas looked at the paper again. “Bring a lamp. I want Orvain to see who’s asking.”

Chapter Fourteen: The Last Refuge

The disused mill above the salt stair had two doors and an opening where the wheel had been.

Riarhin took the opening. Rekedim went to the rear yard. Elyas approached the front with Gyllan, who had insisted on coming when they told him whom they expected to find. Lyra followed with Alric and their horses. The keeper had read Fayd’s instruction, collected his own coat and left by the river stair. A lamp burned beside the wheel opening.

Inside, sacks had been stacked against one wall. A narrow stair led to a loft. There was a cold hearth, a table, and a saddle whose girth had been laid out for repair.

Orvain stood beside the table. His right arm was out of its sling but he held it close to his body. He had a short blade in his left hand.

He saw Elyas first.

“I wondered when you’d come.”

“Put that down.”

“I will speak to you.”

“Then put it down.”

Orvain looked towards the rear door. Rekedim stood beyond it. Riarhin remained beside the empty wheel housing, where the light behind her made the room hard to read.

“I wasn’t paid to hate him,” Orvain said.

Elyas stopped inside the threshold.

“No one asked whether you hated him.”

“He was given a chance to withdraw his protection. Merrow’s people had terms the houses would accept. Your duke kept the crossings closed to them.”

“So you stabbed him.”

“He would have kept the old arrangement alive until everyone who could change it was dead.”

“Who told you that?”

“Men who had been trying to change it for years.”

“And paid for your passage afterwards.”

Orvain’s mouth tightened. He glanced at Gyllan.

“You know what the Commission has been doing.”

“I know what it says it is doing,” the old man answered. “Put the blade on the table.”

Outside, a horse stamped. Lyra said something quietly to Alric. Orvain looked past Elyas towards the sound.

Riarhin moved far enough for him to see her.

He recognised her before he looked at her hands. She had Toothpick out, held low beside her leg.

“The woman from the corridor.”

“Yes.”

“You had your chance then.”

“I was helping him.”

Elyas took another step. Gyllan put his staff across the space in front of him.

“No closer.”

Orvain shifted towards the wheel opening. Riarhin moved with him. There was nowhere he could go without passing one of them, and enough room to lay down the blade.

“Come back with us,” Elyas said. “Tell it where he can answer you.”

“Answer me.” Orvain laughed once. “In his house. Before his people.”

“You chose his house the first time.”

For a moment Orvain looked as though he might put the knife down simply to have both hands free of the argument. His injured shoulder moved. He winced and brought it back against his chest.

Then he went for the opening.

Riarhin caught his left forearm. The point passed her hip and struck stone. He tried to pull free, turning into her grip. She drove Toothpick beneath his ribs and held it there until his hand opened.

It took less time than the last thing Elyas had said.

She lowered him beside the wheel housing. His eyes remained on her. He tried to draw a breath, failed, and caught at her sleeve with his good hand.

She waited for the hand to fall before taking the knife out.

Gyllan reached them first. He knelt awkwardly, felt for a pulse, and then looked up at her.

“He was getting past,” she said.

The old man looked at the narrow opening behind her. He did not answer.

Lyra stood in the doorway while they searched the room.

She had seen the end of it. Riarhin could tell from where she was looking: the blood beside the wall, the space Orvain had tried to cross, the knife now lying out of his reach.

“Did he tell you anything?” the girl asked.

“Not enough.”

Riarhin meant it as an answer. Afterwards she wished she had used different words.

They found a warrant, a note authorising a change of horse, and money sewn inside the lining of his coat. The warrant bore the same receiving mark Fayd had identified as Keld’s. The note promised onward passage to an Acasian military station. There would be no passenger now. Someone had begun a letter on a scrap of good paper, addressed to an agent of Merrow’s house. A separate note named Baron Seld as the person to whom the completed account should be returned.

Elyas read it standing beside the table. Then he folded it carefully and gave it to Alric.

“Keep that with the account of what happened here.”

“What account?”

“The one we’re going to write.”

He looked at Riarhin. She nodded.

They buried Orvain next morning on the rise behind the mill, with the keeper’s permission.

Gyllan said a few words. Elyas remained until the earth was level. No one said the pursuit had been worth it.

That evening Riarhin found him washing his hands. There was earth beneath his nails. He kept working at the same place with his thumb.

“He thought I hadn’t understood.”

“Yes.”

“Would you have let him pass?”

“No.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

She leaned against the wall. She could still feel the turning of Orvain’s arm in her hand. There had been a choice in it, brief and familiar. She had made it without needing to give the choice a name.

“I wasn’t willing to lose him again.”

Elyas put down the cloth.

“I asked him to come back.”

“I heard you.”

He went inside. She stayed until the water stopped moving in the basin.

Act II

Chapter Fifteen: His Father’s Hand

They left Pont-de-Tithe with two copies of the account of Orvain’s death.

One went back across the bridge towards Tarlington in a factor’s locked case. Alric carried the other, wrapped with the papers found at the mill. Elyas had made Riarhin read it before he signed. She had altered three words which made what happened sound less deliberate than it had been.

At the posting house a new name had been nailed beneath the Commission’s lamp. Kaeden Fayd, acting deputy with authority to receive accounts. The office above his was vacant.

Gyllan stopped long enough to read it.

“He hasn’t waited for the hearing.”

“It’s his office that receives the complaint,” Rekedim said.

An ostler hurried out to ask whether they were the travellers with the six horses and the pack mare. Their fodder allowance had been withdrawn. No reason had been entered.

Elyas paid. He asked for a receipt naming whoever had withdrawn it, and the ostler’s face closed.

“Just the receipt, then,” he said.

Outside the town they turned south along the Belworth before taking the westward throat of the Scar. The river had dropped since the attack, leaving black water in the roots of the willows. Riarhin looked back once at the rise carrying Pont-de-Tithe and the bridge to Tarlington. The road they were taking lay below it, between the tributary banks. From here the high route and the low one could not be mistaken for each other.

On the far side of the water, another valley opened eastward into Viterra. The two cuts nearly met across the Belworth. A horseman could descend one, cross and climb the other without going over the high ground. So could carts, if someone held the approaches long enough.

Elyas watched the western valley. He had stopped discussing the receipt.

The first riders wore no colours.

Riarhin saw them above the road where the stone rose in shallow shelves. Three horses, one man dismounted. She signalled Rekedim to keep the others below the bend and went ahead with Elyas.

“Whose?” she asked.

He shaded his eyes.

“The saddles could be Adrian.”

“Could be.”

“That’s all I have.”

She nodded. They waited. The man on foot took something from the ground and remounted. The three went west without coming down to question them.

“They’re counting the fords,” Elyas said.

“Or the roads to them.”

“My father will have seen them.”

Riarhin looked at him. He had said it as a fact, not a hope.

“He’ll let them through?”

“Yes.”

A cart came round the bend behind them, its driver clicking at two small oxen. Elyas moved his horse out of its way. He stayed there after it passed.

“Then you knew,” she said.

“Yes.”

She waited until he looked at her.

“I thought seeing it might make it different.”

Farther up the valley, a bird rose from an orchard. Riarhin had been watching the wall beneath it. A man stood where the trees left a gap, his horse held low behind him. He raised one hand when Elyas turned towards him.

These riders she knew how to read. They had let the Adrians pass and remained to watch the road behind them.

Elyas took his hand from the pommel and returned the greeting.

The scout met them at an inn after dark. His name was Harl, and he had ridden for Oswyn long enough to call Elyas young sir before remembering the title.

Elyas did not correct him.

“The Duke is well?”

“He was at the south camp yesterday.”

“With Harald?”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

Harl glanced towards the kitchen. Elyas drew him farther from the door before repeating the question. Riarhin stayed at the table with the tack she was mending. She could hear the low reply, not all the number, but enough to know it was no ceremonial watch.

“And Valdais?” Elyas asked.

“At Sennet now, waiting on the water. More grain bought in Ethor. Less coming down for sale.”

“Do you expect trouble here?”

“We’re told to report it if there is.”

Elyas nodded. Harl waited for an order. When none came, the man asked whether he should send word ahead to Charel.

“We aren’t going to Charel.”

“Your father would—”

“I know. I’ll write.”

After Harl went to eat, Gyllan came down the stair with a stocking in his hand. He had been trying to dry it too near the fire and had scorched the heel.

“Old Harl?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“He carried your brother’s saddle home.”

Elyas put down the scrap of paper he had been turning over.

“I remember.”

Gyllan looked at him, then at the paper. “I’m sorry.”

“No. It was his horse I remembered.”

The old knight sat. For a time neither spoke.

At the other table two carriers were arguing about whether the Adrian duke could be held west of the valley. One glanced at Elyas, lowered his voice and used a sentence Riarhin had heard from Braedyn years before.

“Oswyn’s coin was spent at Lence.”

Gyllan reached for the burned stocking. He turned the heel inside out and began considering how much could be cut away.

Elyas wrote after the others had gone upstairs. Riarhin came down for the awl she had left beside the lamp and found him with one sheet finished and a second beginning.

“Is there room on the road for a letter that long?”

“The first is for Braedyn.”

“And that one?”

“My father.”

She picked up the awl. “Harl leaves early.”

“He said.”

At the stair she looked back. Elyas was staring at the empty part of the sheet.

“He would like to see you,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You don’t want to go.”

“Twelve years. I’ve spent more evenings in Gyllan’s house this week than at Charel in the last two years.”

“He’s still your father.”

“I know.”

He had not sounded angry until then. Riarhin put the awl away carefully.

“He would have the rooms opened. Harald would come back from wherever he’s watching the roads. They would be glad. Then I would have to explain why I couldn’t stay.”

“Write that you’re well.”

“Am I?”

“Better than some.”

Elyas looked down at the first sheet.

“That won’t do.”

“No,” she said. “It won’t.”

She went upstairs. In the morning Harl had the letter beneath his coat, and Elyas had ink on the side of his hand where he had laid it over the last line too soon.

They spent eight days crossing Istovar.

On the third, the road to Charel went north between two stone posts. Elyas identified it for Alric without slowing. On the fifth, a household offered them beds before examining the Duke’s letter, then became much more formal about the offer after examining it. Elyas spoke to the woman who kept the keys and obtained a room on the ground floor for Gyllan. Riarhin had been prepared to carry the old man’s bag upstairs. She set it down where he could reach it.

They kept turning northwest. Behind them the Scar ran back towards the Belworth; ahead the larger roads began to divide into the tracks serving the borders of three cantons.

Oswyn’s rider found them while they were watering the horses.

She wore a coat white with dust down one side and had a second horse on a lead. She asked for Elyas by his full name, waited while he answered, and gave him a packet sealed in green wax.

“Your father asks that I see you receive it.”

“You’ve seen.”

“I’ll water them, then.”

He moved aside for her. Riarhin held his reins while he opened the seal.

The letter was longer than the one he had sent. He read most of it quickly, then stopped near the bottom. His mouth moved without sound.

“Bad?” Riarhin asked.

“No.”

He began again at the top.

The rider returned to ask about a loose strap on the borrowed horse. Elyas answered her, asked whether his father’s knee still troubled him in the mornings, and listened to the reply with the letter held against his leg.

When she had gone, he passed it to Rekedim.

“We should change the second stop.”

The sergeant read, nodded and took out their list of houses. Oswyn had sent ahead to the barons along the Thin Road, friendly or otherwise, naming his son as a diplomatic envoy travelling on his behalf. Beneath that assurance were particulars: a widow whose hospitality was safe but whose steward repeated every conversation; a younger baron who needed witnesses when giving his word; a house to avoid even if the alternative meant an extra night outside.

“His own hand?” Rekedim asked.

“The last part.”

He gave it back. Riarhin saw where the even clerk’s script ended and the larger, wavering letters began.

Elyas held the paper where she could read them.

Perform your duty. Stay Safe. I am ever proud of my Summer Knight.

The capital in Safe had been gone over twice. The last downstroke of Knight trailed away as though the hand had tired before the word was finished.

Riarhin returned his reins.

“Keep that dry.”

“Yes,” he said.

He folded the letter along its existing creases and put it inside his coat, apart from the papers they had come west to find.

Chapter Sixteen: The Near Side

Rekedim led the pack mare from the near side. Riarhin had been watching him do it for three days.

“The ditch is there,” she said.

“I can see it.”

“She puts you in it if she shies.”

“Then don’t frighten her.”

Riarhin went round the mare and took the other rein. Rekedim kept his. For six paces they walked on either side of the animal, who appeared pleased to have so much assistance.

“How far are you coming?” he asked.

“To the end of the ditch.”

“It goes to the village.”

She let go. He made room for her on the dry part of the track.

Behind them Gyllan was disputing a date with Alric. His voice was as obstinate as it had been before the receiving room, which Riarhin preferred to gratitude. He had thanked her once. She had told him where to put his boot so she could inspect his knee, and that had usefully ended it.

The knee was stiff. He rode with his stirrup lengthened and disliked being helped down. Lyra had offered to work on it. He had asked her to wait until the swelling subsided.

Riarhin had seen the receiving room was wrong. The table too near the door, the wagon left inside with its shafts raised, the driver standing where he could move behind it. She had gone round to find another entrance. There had been a bolt across the back door.

She had lost time lifting it through a split in the wood. Then the crossbow had sounded, and she had stopped trying to keep quiet.

She could still feel the resistance of the bolt against her knife. Another few moments and there would have been no old man behind her complaining about dates. She had begun checking back doors before she inspected the fronts.

The road dipped into a shallow valley. Ahead, Elyas rode with his left arm out of its sling. He had folded the strip of cloth and put it away that morning without asking anyone whether he might. It would hurt by noon. He had begun to recognise which hurts were his own business.

Rekedim stopped them beside a stream and went up to inspect the crossing.

Riarhin dismounted. Her right boot had rubbed the heel raw. She had ignored it since dawn, which was foolish. She could hear Ghere saying so in the brisk tone he used for preventable damage to useful things. Fifteen years he had spent trying to make a Sentinel of the Valewood out of her, two seasons in five, and what he had got for it was a woman who knew exactly how to look after a boot and had not. People had told her his name meant prince. People had told her grandfather the same thing about his. She had never been able to hold either word still long enough to check. She sat on a flat stone, pulled the boot off, and found that the blood had soaked through the stocking.

Elyas looked over.

“What happened?”

“My foot has been in a boot.”

He opened his bag.

“I have clean linen.”

“So have I.”

He held it out anyway. She took it.

He was changing, but unevenly. Sometimes he could hand over a piece of cloth without making a speech. Sometimes he watched her with the solemn expectancy of a boy waiting for a statue to confirm its inscription. She preferred him irritated. It made fewer demands on her.

He sat on the other end of the stone, leaving her room.

“The captain at Pont-de-Tithe sent word with the factor. Venn has given him the names of two more account holders.”

“Good.”

“Does that help?”

“It may help whoever asks those people next.”

“He says he didn’t know they meant to kill.”

She wound the linen round her heel. There was a small sharp stone in the boot. She shook it out into her palm, looked at it, and threw it into the stream.

“He may be telling the truth.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I believe he wanted you stopped and hoped not to think about how.”

Elyas looked towards Lyra. She was helping Alric lift their paper bundle clear of the wet ground. His part of it was heavier; she exchanged the bundles without comment.

“I keep thinking there was a point at which I could have made him tell us.”

Riarhin put the boot on.

“Yes. I do that too.”

She regretted saying it. He turned towards her, and for a moment she thought he would ask for a history she had no intention of giving him.

Instead he stood.

“Will that linen be enough?”

“Until tonight.”

“I have more.”

She watched him go back to his horse. Then she tightened the laces and got up.

Gyllan paid the innkeeper for two rooms, inspected both, and came downstairs to request different mattresses.

Riarhin had taken the place beside the kitchen door. She could see the yard and smell the pan on the stove. Eggs. They had been left too long.

“Do you ever stop examining rooms?” Elyas asked.

“When somebody brings the food.”

He sat opposite her. He had finished writing to Tarlington and was still wearing the expression with which he composed formal sentences.

“Have you heard from Harwick?” she asked.

“Three more claims paid. The steward sent copies.”

“Will they name the bridge after you?”

He looked up.

“What?”

“A modest plaque. Paid for out of Cedric’s allowance.”

For a moment he appeared to consider explaining why that would be improper. Then his mouth moved.

“There is already a name on it.”

“Have them write smaller.”

He laughed. She found she did not mind the sound.

The eggs arrived, firm at the edges and grey underneath. Rekedim came in as she lifted one to inspect it.

“Too late?” he asked.

“By years.”

He exchanged his bread for her eggs. She had not asked him to. He ate them while she fetched butter from the kitchen, and neither thanked the other.

The kitchen boy brought the butter with a dish of windfall plums. He began taking one away when his thumb went through its skin.

“Leave it,” Riarhin said.

She ate it before he could fetch another dish. Alric had come down in time to see the pale grub on the broken flesh disappear with it.

“There was something in that.”

“Yes.”

“Alive.”

“Not now.”

He put his own plum down.

Riarhin reached for it. “Are you finished?”

“Does nothing make you ill?” Lyra asked.

“If it is alive, or ever was, I can eat it.”

“That one wasn’t yours.”

Riarhin looked at Alric. He pushed the dish towards her without touching the fruit. She took it, though the overcooked eggs had smelled better.

Later, Lyra changed the dressing on Alric’s arm. Riarhin watched from where she was cleaning the sword. The cut had held, but the skin round it was swollen.

“I could take that down,” Lyra said.

Gyllan looked without touching.

“Is it worse?”

“No.”

“Can he move the fingers?”

Alric demonstrated.

“Then let it rest.”

Lyra put the clean linen down. “You always say that when I’ve nearly done it.”

“Nearly done what?”

“Made it right.”

The old man drew a stool beside her.

“It has to go on being right after you take your hands away.”

She was silent for a while. Then she asked him to look again at the way the skin pulled when Alric bent his wrist, and he did.

“The good physician leaves the patient something to mend,” Gyllan said.

Riarhin looked up from her sword.

“Builder. Something to finish. Fourth discourse, seventh section.”

Gyllan considered that.

“Was it a builder?”

“A householder complaining that the plasterer had left before the walls were dry. The next section concerns the unpaid balance.”

“There we are, then.”

“No. The plasterer came back.”

Lyra looked from one to the other. Gyllan set the basin on the floor and turned Alric’s hand palm upwards.

“If you do more tonight, will his hand be stronger tomorrow, or will you be less troubled by having stopped?”

“I don’t know,” Lyra said.

“Then that is what we need to find out.”

Riarhin drew the cloth along her blade. The author had been discussing the completion of a contract. She could remember the heading and the short, irritated note beneath it. The book had belonged to one of her mother’s clients, who kept it with its gilt spine towards his guests. Gyllan had quoted neither correctly.

Alric reached for his cup with the injured hand. Lyra watched him lift it, then folded the clean linen for morning.

Riarhin cleaned a dark deposit from beside the sword’s block guard. The edge needed nothing. There were professions in which stopping a little sooner was the hard part. She had not been trained for one of those.

Chapter Seventeen: The Smaller Table

They left the border road for a house a mile inside Ethor. Elyas had sent ahead to ask for beds; the answer had found them at the last watering place.

The hall smelled of beeswax over wet wool.

Two men were carrying a trestle through the door when the party arrived. They had turned it on its side and still could not get it past the iron latch. Riarhin stepped back to let them try again. One leg had been repaired with a strip of fresh wood. The other left a pale scrape on the doorpost.

Their host apologised for receiving them in the smaller room.

“It is quieter.”

Through the open hall door Riarhin could see three more tables, a heap of bedding and a woman counting cups into a basket. The woman stopped counting whenever someone spoke to her. No one appeared to have noticed this except the boy waiting to take the basket away.

Elyas thanked the Baron for receiving them at all.

“Your father’s son,” the man said. “Of course.”

He looked behind Elyas at the others, then asked his steward whether the horses had been taken round. The steward said they had. The Baron asked him to make certain there was water.

There were seven places at the smaller table. One had a different chair.

They were still in Ethor by less than a mile, the Baron told Alric. The road above the orchard belonged to the other jurisdiction. The orchard itself had been disputed since his grandfather’s time, though no one disputed who should prune it.

“Adria collects from the upper gate,” he said. “We mend the road to it.”

“Has it always been so?” Elyas asked.

“No. It has always been temporary.”

The steward brought soup. He set the first bowl before Elyas, paused, and moved it to the place at the head of the table. The Baron pretended to be occupied with his napkin.

Riarhin watched the steward’s hands. There was a burn across one knuckle. He kept it away from the bowls as he served, turning his wrist farther than the work required.

“Your arm is better?” the Baron asked Elyas.

“It is.”

“And the Duke?”

“Still unwell when we left.”

“We had heard.”

He took up his spoon. He had heard more than he intended to repeat over soup.

“Which road brought you here?”

Elyas named their last night’s lodging. The Baron nodded before he had finished.

“The lower way, then. Did you use the ford at the willows?”

“The cart bridge,” Riarhin said. “The ford was cut up.”

“Both banks?”

“The nearer one worse. Someone had laid brush in it. The water had taken most of that out.”

The Baron ate a little soup.

“I sent brush.”

“The bridge held,” she said.

“Yes. I thought it would.”

Gyllan reached for the bread. The Baron passed it and asked whether the well at their lodging was still drawing clear.

“They were using the upper one,” Rekedim said.

“Ah.”

“The lower rope had broken.”

“I sent rope too.”

The steward came back for the empty dishes. His master asked him for more bread. There was enough on the table, but the man went without looking at it.

The joint was smaller than the dish.

The Baron carved it thinly, with care. Lyra accepted the first slice offered and declined the second. Gyllan asked for the heel of the loaf. Riarhin took a little of everything and left room on her plate for the onions when they came round.

“Were there many carts still on the road?” their host asked.

“Fewer after the turn,” Rekedim said.

“Coming back?”

“Some. Two carrying empty barrels.”

“Marked?”

“One had a split stave.”

The Baron looked at him. Rekedim took a mouthful of bread.

“I didn’t inspect their owner’s mark.”

“No. Why should you?”

A board creaked in the hall. The woman counting cups said something sharply, followed by the sound of several being set down together.

“His Grace was here,” the Baron said.

Elyas lowered his fork.

“Adria?”

“For a night. His officers stayed longer.”

Riarhin looked at the different chair. Its arms were worn smooth by hands, but a fresh dent cut across the right one near the end.

“He preferred this room,” the Baron said. “We brought his chair through from the hall. Then there was no room for the carver to pass.”

“You could have moved the table,” Alric said.

“We did.”

The boy looked down at his plate.

“It was a sensible suggestion,” the Baron added. “It took us longer to arrive at it.”

He served the last slice of meat to the empty place where the steward would sit when he had finished carrying things.

“Nine thousand,” he said. “Not here, of course. In the fields and all along the road. The household was here.”

“Did they pay?” Elyas asked.

“For some things.”

“And the others?”

“They have not finished requiring them.”

The onions had been cooked with a little honey. Riarhin took another before the dish went away.

“The well beside the willows,” the Baron said. “Did you see that one?”

“A broken cover. A trough on the low side.”

“Stone?”

“Yes.”

“They asked to have the trough moved. The teams couldn’t turn.”

“The stones were still there.”

“But the trough?”

“On the low side.”

He nodded. She could have told him which end had settled into the mud. He did not ask.

“His Grace is at Sennet now,” he said. “Or was when the last carts came back. He has been waiting on the water.”

“Since Floralis?” Gyllan asked.

“Late in the month. We have had two requests for fodder since. Each was to be the last before he moved.”

Elyas turned his cup by its stem.

“Did he ask you to declare for him?”

The Baron looked towards the door, though no one was there.

“He asked how much hay I had.”

“I meant whether you signed an undertaking.”

“I understood.”

He put his knife across his plate.

“The upper gate takes a toll from every cart I send through it. When Adria sends carts here, I am asked to remember that we are neighbours. I have remembered it through three repairs to the culvert.”

Elyas waited.

“If I had signed, would your father have sent someone to keep the hay here?”

“I can’t speak for him in that.”

“No. Nor can I make the road go round my fields. His men were thirsty. Mine had buckets. We drew water.”

“I’m not asking you to justify—”

“I know.”

The Baron picked up his knife again. There was nothing left on his plate that needed cutting.

“I should like the barrels back. There were twelve.”

Elyas set his cup down. Riarhin had seen him making notes after every house that received them. That evening he left the folded paper in his coat.

They had pears afterwards, halved and kept in a dark syrup. The steward finally sat. He ate his meat cold while the Baron served the fruit.

“Madilyn would have objected to these,” their host said.

Gyllan looked up.

“Of Ethor?”

“My cousin. She said we spoiled them. She used to take hers from the kitchen before they went into the pan.”

He pressed a pear with the edge of his spoon.

“We still cook them too long.”

“Valdais’s first wife,” Elyas said.

“Yes.”

“Did she grow up here?”

“Some of the summers. Her own house was farther down. Her father sent her here when he wanted his dogs exercised. She would take them out in any weather.”

The Baron turned his spoon over. The silver bore a crest almost rubbed away by polishing.

“Her father could have served every guest from silver. He used to say he needn’t send to a king for a plate.”

“A good marriage,” Gyllan said.

“For a Duke of Adria. Certainly.”

“Lady Elianor might have had an opinion.”

“She generally does.”

“She knows both houses.”

“Yes. Though people here sometimes speak as if she had been born in Ethor.”

The Baron set the spoon down.

“Madilyn brought her own pear trees. They did badly in Adria. She kept asking for another rootstock.”

Gyllan waited until the steward had gone for the wine.

“Does Lady Elianor still have the boy with her?”

The Baron’s eyes went to the open door.

“I haven’t counted her household.”

“They call him the last Blood—” Alric began.

“Not at that volume.”

The boy stopped. Their host had spoken mildly; Riarhin saw that his hand had closed round the stem of his glass.

“Has Adria asked?” Elyas said.

“His Grace said a question would upset his mother. He couldn’t see what an answer would alter.”

“And you?”

“I should like my larders full, and to cross my own passage without jumping at shadows.”

The steward returned. The Baron held out his glass and asked whether there was another jar of pears in the kitchen.

The steward reached for the salt, remembered what he was eating now, and withdrew his hand.

“Did the daughter come with him?” Gyllan asked.

“Vaelrie. Yes. They call her something else.”

“Paget.”

“That’s it.”

The Baron looked at the different chair.

“She asked why there was a notch in the stair rail. I told her her mother had done it, trying to bring a long stick round the turn without putting it down. She went out to look. Came back and asked how old she’d been.”

“What did you tell her?”

“I couldn’t remember. I told her that.”

He finished his pear.

“She wasn’t two when Madilyn died. There are people who tell her she must remember things.”

“Does she?” Lyra asked.

“She didn’t say she did.”

For a while the only sound was the steward’s spoon against his dish. Then Alric asked whether the fruit came from the disputed orchard.

“No,” the Baron said. “Those are apples. Adria is welcome to them this year. They’ve canker.”

He smiled at the boy, and Alric smiled back.

Their rooms had been aired in haste.

Riarhin opened her window wider. A shaving brush lay behind the washstand, missed by whoever had cleared it. She put it where it would be found and took her boots off.

Below, in the yard, the Baron was asking whether the horses had enough water. The steward answered him a second time.

She heard Elyas thank their host again. Neither mentioned the question at dinner.

On the stair the next morning she found the notch. It was lower than she had expected, no higher than her knee, with dark polish worn into the cut. Someone had rubbed at it recently; there was a pale edge where the wax had come away.

She put her pack on the other shoulder and went down.

The tables were back in the hall. At the door a boy gave Gyllan bread wrapped in a cloth and asked him to return the cloth if anyone came this way again.

Their host had gone to the granary. They left their thanks with the steward.

Chapter Eighteen: The Thin Road

Gyllan gave his lessons in the saddle, where half of what he said blew away.

Every morning he put a question to Lyra, rode out of hearing while she answered, and then accused her of leaving out the important part. By the fourth day she made him repeat her answer before she would listen to his objection.

Alric kept count. He scratched marks into the margin of a discarded page until Gyllan confiscated it on grounds of disrespect.

“That was a draft of your letter,” Alric said.

“All the more reason.”

Elyas rode beside them between breakfast and the first watering place. His arm was improving; he had stopped cradling it against his body when the horse changed pace. Riarhin heard him ask Gyllan to repeat a question lost to the wind. Gyllan’s questions concerned difficult, ordinary things: a tenant who had repaired a mill he did not own; two brothers who had borrowed against the same harvest; a woman whose husband had pledged her tools while she was away.

Lyra usually gave the rule without hesitation. Gyllan always asked what she intended to do with it.

“The tools go back to her,” she said one morning.

“And the lender?”

“He can pursue the husband.”

“He has gone.”

“Then he can pursue him farther.”

Gyllan laughed. Lyra did not.

“She cannot work without them,” she said. “You keep adding things to make it impossible.”

“No. The people add things. I have the advantage of knowing when to stop.”

He let the question rest until evening. At the inn he paid for a room with a table, and after supper Lyra sat at it working through the case on the back of Alric’s spoiled copy sheets. Riarhin found them still there when she went down for water. Gyllan was asleep in the chair beside her, his mouth open. She had put his folded coat behind his head.

She did not wake him to hear her answer.

At the northwestern corner of Istovar, the road lost its ditches.

The country south of it belonged to Ethor. To the north, beyond low workings and dark seams of spoil, lay Adria. Alric had drawn the cantons on a sheet and made Adria too large. Elyas turned the paper towards him and moved its boundary with a blunt finger.

“Ethor is much the same size as Istovar. Adria fits above it.”

“All that iron from there?”

“And silver. The fields aren’t what the dukes count.”

Adria lay in the rainward shadow of the Aelerian mountains. Beyond the northern slopes they held a bank of weather which never seemed to come any nearer. The wagon road threaded between the cantons, taking one side of the boundary when the other became difficult. At some posts two men collected a toll; at others one man had two purses.

Oswyn’s warning about the younger baron proved sound.

The man welcomed Elyas, promised feed, lodging and an escort, and waited for him to sit before adding that the undertaking required a small bond. His secretary had already put the bond on the table.

Elyas thanked him and remained standing.

“The escort is kind. We don’t require it.”

“These are unsettled roads.”

“So my father wrote.”

“He knows this house will do its duty.”

“Shall we have the steward in? He’ll need to know what you’ve offered.”

The baron looked at his secretary. The secretary looked at the bond.

Riarhin watched Elyas choose a chair only after the steward entered. He put it where the older man could hear every word without having to turn his head towards his master.

They left next morning with feed paid for at the ordinary rate, beds recorded as hospitality and no escort. Alric asked how much the bond would have been.

“Enough to make us wait while he found a reason to release it,” Elyas said.

At the next inn, two men by the fire were discussing the price of a tutor. One had heard that a young gentleman in Ethor was being taught the old court forms.

“Whose young gentleman?” the other asked.

The first man looked at Gyllan’s coat, then at Elyas. “An expensive one.”

“The forms cost the same. It’s the pedigree you’re paying for.”

The innkeeper set their jug down hard enough to spill it. Neither complained. By the time Riarhin had found a chair, they were discussing a horse.

“You knew.”

“He watched the paper when he offered the horses. He wasn’t interested in the horses.”

Riarhin rode beside him until the gate was out of sight.

“Will you let him cut your arm for the trouble?”

He looked at her, then laughed reluctantly.

“No.”

“Good. You’re nearly out of sleeves.”

The men at the next road station had their barrier across before the party reached them.

There were four in matching coats and two who might have been carters. The leader carried a narrow case with the Commission’s lamp painted on it. Behind him, a wagon stood with its cover drawn. No horses were harnessed to it.

“Documents for inspection.”

Elyas dismounted. Riarhin kept her horse a little behind his, where she could see the wagon’s far wheel.

“By whose authority?”

The man displayed a seal.

“That’s an authority to examine freight,” Elyas said. “We have no freight for sale.”

“All papers conveyed on the western road may be—”

“Whose order extends it to private correspondence?”

The leader went on reading. He was trying to draw Elyas closer to the case. One of the carters had begun moving towards the pack mare.

Rekedim handed the mare’s lead to Gyllan.

Riarhin dismounted. The other carter watched her boots. He knew something about them, then.

“Your scholar can bring the records inside,” the leader said. “We need not detain you.”

“He will stay here.”

“Highness, the clerks only require—”

Alric cried out behind them.

The covered wagon’s flap lifted. A man jumped down on the far side of the mare and took the boy’s satchel with both hands. Alric held the strap. The man dragged him against the wheel.

Riarhin drew the sword as she moved. The carter stepped between her and the wagon with a hooked bill, turning its point towards her stomach. She could not pass without giving him the side of her body.

She struck the shaft down. He let it go with one hand and drew a knife with the other. Trained enough. She drove her shoulder into his arm and forced him towards the barrier.

Behind her the official voice had become a shout.

“Stand clear! This is an inspection!”

Elyas caught the man’s wrist before he could reach inside the case. Something small and metal fell out. A whistle.

The bill struck the barrier. Riarhin cut the carter’s forearm where it lay exposed, took the weapon under her boot and turned him into the path of the man coming from the wagon. That one stopped rather than cut his own companion. The hesitation gave Rekedim time to seize him from behind.

Alric was on his knees. The satchel strap had broken. Lyra stood over it with her short blade out, facing two men who had abruptly found the road too narrow for their number.

Gyllan backed the pack mare into them.

“Mind the feet,” he said, to nobody in particular.

The animal kicked the wagon with a noise like a dropped chest. One of the men ran. The other caught at Lyra’s arm and received the butt of Gyllan’s staff in his throat. He went down coughing.

The leader shouted Elyas’s title again. Elyas had bent his wrist over the edge of the case and was making him kneel.

“Now tell them to stop.”

They stopped.

The station keeper emerged from his own grain bin when Rekedim opened it.

He had been shut in since dawn. There was blood in his beard where one of the men had struck him. His wife and daughter were at the next farm; he kept asking whether anyone had gone after them.

Elyas sent a farm boy to bring them home with witnesses. Riarhin searched the case. The seal was genuine. The page beneath it ordered the recovery of copied accounts and named an office in Pont-de-Tithe.

It did not name Fayd.

“His enemies or his friends?” Elyas asked.

“Ask him when you see him.”

“I don’t intend to.”

“Then keep the paper.”

Two of the men had escaped. The rest were bound to the posts while the station keeper sent for his lord’s bailiff. Riarhin washed her sword. The carter watched with his arm held against his chest.

Lyra dressed the cut. She made him hold the cloth himself when she had finished.

Alric had lost the skin from one knuckle. He said his arm was all right. Gyllan made him move the fingers anyway.

The satchel could be repaired; the strap could not. Rekedim divided the papers among three bags. They had meant to stop at a house beyond the next rise. Its name appeared on the inspection order as the place to which recovered documents should be sent.

“We won’t go there,” Elyas said.

“No,” Rekedim answered. “We won’t.”

They slept beside a charcoal burn with the keeper’s borrowed spade across the fire pit and took the watches in pairs. Riarhin woke twice to the sound of Gyllan coughing. Each time he said he had only swallowed smoke.

In the morning the keeper’s daughter came with bread. Her father had no money to pay the bailiff’s men for staying, she said. They had eaten the food laid aside for market.

Elyas opened his purse. He counted what remained before handing anything over.

For eleven more days the road kept them between the mines and the broader fields of Ethor, then drew west beyond the last reliable posts.

A letter reached them from Mara by a factor going the other way. Braedyn had been moved to a room overlooking the garden. He could sit for a little while. Seniro and Braedyn the Younger had gone north. Veralyn’s host was on the northern road too, and the steward had complained that every decent courier now carried military orders.

Rekedim listened while Elyas read that part.

“Had you expected her to go?” Riarhin asked him afterwards.

“Yes. When I left her service, the orders were being copied.”

“Will she miss you?”

“She’ll have someone who tells her the oats are late.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

He retied the sack he had been inspecting.

“I know.”

At the end of the letter Mara had added that Cedric’s first allowance had paid six claimants at the Whiterun. Three more were waiting. Elyas read it twice and put it beside Oswyn’s letter, then changed his mind and kept it with his own unfinished reply.

The next morning they entered Acasia by a road whose milestones had been recut so often the oldest letters appeared inside the newest ones.

At the first fork they passed a cart with a broken yellow wheel. The driver was asleep beneath it. At noon they passed it again.

Riarhin stopped.

She knew the missing spoke, the square patch on the cover, the fly walking along the driver’s lower lip. Behind them the road ran straight between two banks. They had taken no turning.

Rekedim dismounted. He looked at the marks of their horses in the mud beside the cart.

“Wake him,” he said.

The driver heard them out, took a drink and asked whether anyone had promised to return this way.

Alric began to answer. Gyllan put a hand on his arm.

“No promises,” the old knight said. “We’re asking the road.”

The man pointed across the field to a line of dead pollards. They paid him to walk with them until the yellow wheel was no longer in sight.

Chapter Nineteen: The Man Under the Bridge

The bridge was closed by a rope with three bells hanging from it.

A woman in a cart had ignored the rope and was now halfway onto the span, arguing with someone Riarhin could not see. Her pony stood between the shafts. A boy sat on a sack behind her, eating the end of a carrot.

“You said after noon.”

“I said I would know after noon.”

“It is after noon.”

“Now I know. You cannot cross.”

The voice came from beneath the nearest arch. A short, broad man climbed into sight on a ladder. His beard was tucked into his belt. He carried a plumb line which he coiled as he came.

The woman pointed at the sun. While the man looked at her, the pony took a step.

Riarhin heard the stone move.

It was a low grinding sound, almost lost beneath the water. The man dropped the plumb line and ran to the pony’s head. The cart settled on one side. The boy caught at his mother’s coat.

“Get him off,” the man said.

The woman turned in the seat. The wheel went through.

Riarhin was over the rope before the first bell stopped ringing. The cart tipped towards the parapet, its shaft dragging the pony sideways. The boy vanished behind the sacks.

Elyas caught the woman as she fell. They went down together against the stones. Riarhin climbed onto the cart’s high wheel and looked into the bed. The boy lay between two sacks, his mouth open, one leg caught beneath the seat.

“Look at me.”

He did. She reached for his coat. Something shifted under her foot.

“Don’t pull,” the mason said.

The pony screamed. Its hind feet were sliding towards the broken stones. Rekedim came through the bells with a rope in his hands.

“The axle,” the mason told him. “Far end. There.”

Riarhin put a knee against the side of the cart and held it while Rekedim passed the rope beneath. She could feel the whole load leaning into her. This would not last.

Gyllan had reached the boy’s mother. He took her from Elyas and turned her face away from the gap.

“What’s his name?”

“Jori.”

“Tell Jori where you are.”

She called to him. The boy tried to turn his head.

“Stay looking at me,” Riarhin said. “You can hear her.”

Lyra climbed onto the sound side and began pulling sacks off him. Alric caught them where they slid. The first split, scattering grain over the stones. The second nearly took him through the gap. He let it go and reached for the next.

The mason cut the near trace. The pony surged against his hold. Elyas caught the bridle with his good hand and took the second knife the man held out.

“Other trace. Then back him.”

The animal came free, hitting Elyas hard enough to send him against the parapet. He kept the bridle. Riarhin put her hand beneath the boy’s knee.

“Can you move your foot?”

He moved it. Not far.

Lyra found the bent slat that held his boot and pulled until her face went pale. The wood would not give. Riarhin passed her Toothpick handle first.

“Cut the boot.”

Three cuts. The boy’s leg came free.

Riarhin lifted him against her and turned. The cart shifted again. She stepped from the wheel to the parapet, felt the top stone roll beneath her sole and jumped towards the road.

She landed badly. Pain struck her hip. Gyllan took the boy before she could get her knees under her.

Behind them the rope tightened. Rekedim and the mason let it run together, trying to ease the cart down. The axle caught the broken pier. The bed tore away from it and fell into the water with the remaining grain.

Alric sat on the road, coughing dust. Lyra climbed down beside him carrying the small, ruined boot.

The woman held her son with his bare foot trapped between her knees. She kept telling him he was wet. He was not wet.

The mason’s name was Teren Vaul.

He inspected the sound approach before allowing anyone to fetch what had fallen from the cart. Riarhin sat on the bank while Lyra looked at her hip. A scrape had opened through the cloth; beneath it a bruise was already rising.

“Can you stand?”

“Yes.”

“Then let me clean it before you do.”

The girl worked carefully. Her eye paused over an older line above Riarhin’s belt, thin and almost colourless, then returned to the fresh blood.

Vaul came down carrying the plumb line. He had retrieved it from the water.

“True Mason?” Riarhin asked.

“Yes.”

“Your rope did well.”

“It saved the axle.”

He looked at the pony, which Elyas was walking slowly along the bank. “That too.”

The woman had begun gathering the scattered grain by hand. Gyllan brought a sheet from their packs and knelt to help her.

They stayed the night.

After supper the woman asked Vaul what it would cost to replace the broken span.

“More than you can pay.”

“The village can pay.”

“The village has already pledged two harvests.”

She looked towards the water. Without the crossing, her market was on the other side of a day’s detour. Riarhin had seen the pony’s ribs beneath its wet coat. An extra day’s haul would not be a small matter.

“Then what can we give?”

Vaul opened his hands.

“A longer term. The holds can advance the stone and the work. Their portion of the toll continues until the account is discharged.”

“How long?”

He gave her a number. She looked down at her son, asleep with his foot wrapped in Gyllan’s spare stocking.

“He’d be collecting it.”

“Possibly.”

“You won’t tell me less?”

“No.”

She went to speak with the other villagers. Elyas watched her go.

“At least she knows what she’s agreeing to.”

Lyra put her cup down.

“She knows what happens if she doesn’t.”

“She knows both,” Vaul said.

“Can she refuse?”

“Yes.”

“And take the long road until the pony dies.”

“I cannot shorten that road by changing the terms.”

Lyra began to speak, stopped, and looked at the woman beyond the fire. Vaul waited for her to continue. When she did not, he took his soup and ate it before it grew cold.

Riarhin liked that he had not pretended the question was foolish. She did not much like his answer.

In the morning Elyas held the lantern beneath the sound arch while Vaul examined it. A local man helped them bring the ladder back. His father had worked on the last repair, he said, and he had kept the tools.

“Then you know the courses,” Vaul said.

“I know what he showed me.”

“You can begin the facing while the cut stone comes.”

“Could we do the rest?”

Vaul looked towards the broken span.

“Not with those tools.”

“If we had them.”

“If someone taught you to use them.”

“Will you?”

“I can take one apprentice. He would have to travel.”

The man had three children and a mother who could no longer leave her bed. He said so without asking Vaul to alter his conditions.

“Send someone younger,” Vaul said.

They stood for a little while looking at the water. Then the man went to begin the facing.

Gyllan had heard the exchange. He came down with the lantern’s oil jar.

“What if nobody can go?”

“Then I shall have to come back.”

“You don’t mind that?”

“The bridge was built to outlast the people who commissioned it. We accepted work that does not end with their lives.”

“Or yours.”

“No.”

Gyllan handed him the oil.

“I used to think keeping a promise was a matter of remembering it.”

“What do you think now?”

“That I should have asked who would be left to keep it.”

Vaul looked at him properly. “Then you have learned something useful.”

“Too late for several people.”

The mason held the jar without opening it. Above them Jori called to his mother that his foot did not hurt. She told him to stop proving it.

They crossed by a cattle ford upstream after two days. The villagers showed them the sound bottom. One led Gyllan’s horse through, and another carried the documents above his head.

Vaul was taking a parallel road northwest to inspect another crossing. He came with them as far as the next market, leaving the village man working on the facing with measurements cut into a strip of wood.

“Do you know Veyra Keld?” Riarhin asked him on the road.

“Four of her receiving houses.”

“Fayd called her an Edgewalker.”

“She holds the licence.”

“To do what?” Elyas asked.

“To find opportunities beyond the holds.”

“And does she?”

“Yes.”

“Do they ask how?”

Vaul shifted his tool roll to the other shoulder. “They ask whether the account is profitable.”

Riarhin watched him settle the strap.

“What do they ask you?”

“Whether the work will stand.”

He stopped to let Gyllan’s horse come past on the firmer ground. The old man was telling Lyra how to mend the little boot they had brought away from the bridge. Jori’s mother had asked whether it could be saved. Neither of them had thought to ask her why he had only the one pair.

Chapter Twenty: What She Would Defend

Veyra Keld had left the first receiving house twelve days before they arrived.

Its keeper told them so while standing in the doorway. He was polite to Vaul, wary of Rekedim, and uninterested in Elyas’s name. Riarhin was beginning to enjoy the road for that last small kindness.

“When is she expected back?” Elyas asked.

“When she has finished elsewhere.”

“Where elsewhere?”

The keeper smiled. It was not a hostile smile. He was declining to do his employer’s enemies’ work before he knew whether these people were her enemies.

Riarhin drew the copied receipt from her coat. She kept the original elsewhere.

“Who signed here?”

The keeper glanced at it.

“I can send an inquiry.”

“To her?”

“To the account office.”

“Do that.”

She let him copy the number and gave him the name of the inn where they intended to stay. It was a busy house in the same village, with a yard where they could keep their own watch.

On the walk back, Elyas said they might have shown him the Duke’s warrant.

“He knows you have one.”

“How?”

“You reached for it.”

He looked down at his coat, then put his hand elsewhere.

The innkeeper spoke Acasi. Gyllan began negotiating for rooms while Riarhin inspected the stair. The man’s vowels had the broad, rounded finish she had heard through thin shutters above a Silkstrand courtyard. She went up another step.

“He says the front rooms are quieter,” Gyllan told her.

“Back.”

“He hasn’t told me the price yet.”

The innkeeper spoke again. Gyllan listened, nodded, and began to explain that supper was included.

“And mend the latch,” Riarhin said.

Gyllan stopped.

“I hadn’t got to that.”

She tried the door. The latch lifted without resistance.

“It doesn’t catch.”

Later she asked for hot water in Viterran. The innkeeper shook his head. She asked in Vhasian, and he shook it again. Gyllan rose to help, but she had already made the request in Acasi, specifying a clean jug and no rose oil. The innkeeper went to fetch it.

“Your pronunciation is better than mine,” Gyllan said.

“Yes.”

“Would you like to arrange breakfast?”

“No.”

He sat down again. Rekedim moved his cup so she could reach the bread.

They received no answer that day. Nor the next. On the third morning a clerk came to the inn and explained that the receipt concerned a subcontractor whose accounts were kept at another house, two days farther west.

Riarhin asked him to sit. He said he had other calls to make.

After he left, she found Rekedim in the stable yard checking the pack mare’s girth.

“They’ll send us from house to house until winter.”

“Yes.”

“Can we afford it?”

“No.”

“Can she?”

The sergeant looked up.

“That depends what else she has to pay for.”

They took the room above the kitchen because the cook’s chopping made conversation difficult to hear from the passage.

Lyra spread the copied shipping schedules on the bed. Alric found the names of the western settlements in his gazetteer. Vaul listened from a chair too high for him, his boots resting on the rung.

Elyas stood aside to let her choose a place before taking his own. He had been careful about invitations since the blue silk. Her mother would have told her to reward the care; Riarhin sat down and moved the lamp so he could read.

She knew the current form as well as the old one. Fifty years had not made people stop wanting the best chair. The older answer had merely left him something to think about while she ate.

Her mother would have approved of the blue silk at the dance. She would have asked who had paid for it, and then why Riarhin had paid for it herself. Riarhin remembered being made to leave her hand still while a man finished complimenting it. Even now she sometimes caught herself counting the rings on a hand held out to her, and how easily each would come off.

Riarhin disliked meetings round papers. They encouraged people to believe a thing was contained because its edges could be seen. But men had tried to kill them for these particular pages. She owed them attention, if nothing else.

Rekedim pointed to three destinations.

“These bought the same kinds of goods. This one pays in grain, this in silver. The third buys on credit.”

“Who holds the credit?” Elyas asked.

“Keld. Or the account behind her.”

“Then the third matters most.”

“Perhaps. Or she would be glad to stop lending to it.”

Lyra had put her finger on the dates.

“The grain buyer received a shipment after the roads flooded. The others waited.”

“Because the roads to him stayed open,” Alric said.

“Did they?” Rekedim asked Vaul.

The mason leaned forward. He studied the dates, then the place-name.

“No. His road lost a span. They would have had to use pack animals for the last twenty miles.”

Riarhin looked at the listed weight. Even without knowing the cost, she knew what it meant to take iron off wagons and put it onto backs.

“Why do that for grain?”

“We don’t know,” Rekedim said.

He turned to Elyas.

“How much could you undertake to buy without writing to Tarlington?”

The prince was still looking at the entry.

“Buy what?”

“The next deliveries.”

“We don’t need them.”

“Somebody does. We offer a good price, payable promptly, for material that would otherwise go to these three places. Then we watch which promise she keeps.”

Lyra straightened.

“We would be paying her.”

“If she sells.”

“With the Duke’s money.”

“Yes.”

“She arranged passage for the man who stabbed him.”

“Her account may have paid without her knowing. We need to learn what she knowingly protects.”

Riarhin watched Lyra’s hands. They had gone still on the papers. That was worse than anger; the girl had heard the answer and found no mistake in it, only something she could not accept.

“There are limits,” Elyas said.

“Tell me,” Rekedim replied.

He did. The Duke’s letter gave him authority to redeem certain household bonds through the river factors. It would buy less iron than Rekedim had hoped, but enough to make an offer worth reporting. He could not command the carriers. He could not pledge the northern revenues. He would have to sign his own name beneath the purchase.

Rekedim listened through the whole of it.

“We only need one delivery to move. The rest can be offers.”

“And if she accepts all three?”

“We make all three conditional on inspection before payment. Which is ordinary practice. We have enough for the deposits.”

Riarhin looked at Vaul.

“Is it?”

“Yes.”

“Would it trouble you to inspect?”

“I leave tomorrow. Ask the woman who keeps the receiving books. She knows what comes out of a hold and what has only been stamped to resemble it.”

“Will she charge?”

“She should.”

He went to help Gyllan with the clasp of his writing case. The old man had been forcing it closed over a page.

They spent four days becoming buyers.

It required a kind of patience the hunt for Orvain had never permitted. Elyas met a factor. Rekedim visited a carrier yard. Alric copied specifications until he put the pen down and began rubbing his hand. Lyra took over. While he went to fetch more ink, she shook her own fingers loose beneath the table. The receiving clerk corrected the specifications where they would have bought goods no experienced purchaser wanted. She charged them for the work.

Riarhin went to the market and listened.

The marches had begun to arm before anyone called it a war. Men bought the things that would let them deny it for another month: fittings for carts, good nails, stores that kept, horses described as breeding stock and ridden away in matched pairs. A woman selling needles had doubled her order for heavy sail thread because the soldiers’ wives were repairing tents.

At the end of the fourth day, Riarhin saw Keld.

She knew her from the receiving keeper’s sudden change of posture. The Aeler woman had entered through the side gate in a coat cut for riding, with no ornament except a clasp shaped from dull green stone. Two people followed her. She handed one her gloves without looking and stopped to hear something from the keeper.

Riarhin stood across the lane beside a basket seller. She could reach the gate before it closed. There were no guards between them. Toothpick lay against her ankle; Remorse hung at her hip.

She could have a hand on the woman’s coat in ten breaths.

Then what? A knife, a demand, the keeper shouting for help. The same receiving houses, the same accounts, but every man in them warned and every paper moved.

She stayed beside the baskets.

Keld went inside. The gate closed.

Only afterwards did Riarhin notice that her hand had tightened round the strap of her pack until the old cut across her palm hurt.

That evening a carrier came to reject Elyas’s offer for the grain buyer’s consignment.

The price had not been refused. The goods had been withdrawn from sale.

“On whose instruction?” Elyas asked.

“The factor’s.”

“Can she supply an equivalent quantity later?”

“Not on terms I am authorised to discuss.”

Riarhin watched the carrier while Rekedim asked which yard held the withdrawn wagons. He would not tell them that. He did ask whether the inspecting clerk had heard if the north crossing would bear the load before dawn. She went down to her office and returned with Vaul’s posted limit. It would not.

After the man left, Rekedim folded the unsigned offer.

“The road that lost its span.”

Lyra found the entry before he asked. Alric had already opened the gazetteer. Gyllan moved the lamp nearer them.

The place had changed hands since his book was written. The clerk knew the present occupier by report: a Ykrul exile with no recognised grazing claim, buying provisions in quantities that his small camp could not consume.

“His name?” Riarhin asked.

“I don’t know it.”

She looked at Rekedim. His face told her nothing useful.

“Can we follow the wagons?” Elyas asked.

“Yes,” she said.

For the first time since the receiving room, she wanted the morning to come.

Chapter Twenty-One: The Weight Allowed

The wagons left without the iron.

Riarhin watched the yard hands lift the last bundles onto pack saddles. There were twenty-seven mules, some with old sores under their straps. A driver walked down the line slipping two fingers beneath each girth. At the gate the receiving keeper counted animals instead of loads and entered the smaller number in his book.

She went back to the others.

“They’ve divided it.”

Rekedim looked at the posted limit they had copied. “One at a time, then.”

“They’ll be slow.”

“So will we.”

For three days the loads went north and west through a country more cultivated than it first appeared. What looked like waste above the road was common grazing. What looked like an abandoned ditch carried the water that kept six small fields alive. Riarhin crossed it on a plank and found a child waiting with a hoe, furious at the mud her horse had knocked into his work.

She dismounted and cleared it with her hands.

By the third afternoon, the iron had acquired sacks of meal and two carts of leather. At the next village, half the mules disappeared behind a mill. The others continued along the road in plain sight.

Riarhin found the missing half again after dark. They had crossed a pasture on folded hurdles, lifting the rear ones forward to keep the hooves out of the wet ground. The pasture belonged to someone who expected to use it afterwards. She approved of the method and disliked having to spend half the night discovering it.

She came back with a burr inside her sleeve and a useful route.

Gyllan had saved her supper beneath an inverted bowl. He had also saved her a place by the fire, which required moving his bad leg when she sat down.

“You could have eaten it,” she said.

“I did eat it. Lyra made me replace it.”

Across the fire, Lyra looked up from sewing a split in Alric’s bag. “I made him replace the meat.”

“The barley was indistinguishable.”

Riarhin lifted the bowl. Two pieces of meat, a great deal of barley. She ate while Rekedim asked about the hurdles, the mill, and the depth of the ditch beyond it. He asked nothing about the night’s difficulty. He knew what the mud on her shoulders cost.

When she had finished, Gyllan held out his staff.

“Would you look at the ferrule?”

It had worked loose. She turned it, felt the worn wood beneath, and asked Alric for a strip from his ruined bag. Gyllan watched her wind it under the metal.

“A wedge would split it,” she said before he could ask.

“I was going to ask whether you wanted another bowl.”

“I do.”

He got up to fetch it. She finished the repair and laid the staff where his hand would find it.

When he came back he sat down more carefully than he had stood up.

“I knew a man in Viterra with the Lethai-thora turn of mind,” he said. “He could give you the contents of a ledger he had read at nineteen.”

“Was he any use?”

“He was insufferable.”

“Then it wasn’t the ledger.”

Gyllan laughed, which cost him something below the ribs. He did not go on to ask her anything else. Riarhin noticed the not-asking and found she liked him more than was convenient.

The old military station stood above a dry wash. Its outer wall had been robbed of stone to build pens; the gate still had enough iron to be worth guarding. Riarhin counted eleven men in sight and allowed for twice that number behind the buildings. Too many for an abandoned station. Too few to consume the meal arriving at its gate.

Beyond it, cart tracks went out across the grass.

She spent the morning on her stomach among stones, waiting for someone to make a mistake. The sun warmed the back of her neck. A beetle took a long time to climb over her wrist.

At noon a woman rode out with four attendants.

The green clasp was visible even at that distance.

Keld stopped below Riarhin’s slope and looked towards the road. One of her attendants carried a white cloth folded over his wrist. He did not raise it.

Riarhin waited.

“Your horses need water,” Keld called in Vhasian. “There is a trough at the lower house. You may use it whether you speak to me or not.”

The beetle dropped from Riarhin’s hand.

She had concealed herself well enough. The horses were farther back, with Elyas and Rekedim, where an observer above the station could have seen them arrive. She had looked for watchers along the walls and neglected the roof of the ruined granary.

It was a small error. She disliked it sufficiently to remember it.

Keld received them beneath an awning at the lower house. Riarhin chose the end from which she could see the granary. Rekedim remained standing. Elyas accepted water and waited until the attendant had poured for everyone before speaking.

“Your account paid for Orvain’s passage from the river.”

“My account paid for a wounded man under a valid warrant.”

“He had stabbed Duke Braedyn.”

“So your letters say.”

“You knew before the letters.”

Keld considered him. Her skin had the fine, dry texture of old paper where the shade failed to reach it.

“I knew he would be pursued. Conveyance is not an endorsement of every act that makes it necessary.”

“He died at Pont-de-Tithe,” Riarhin said.

“I have been told.”

“Who told you?”

“The office that cancelled his onward passage. You have changed its deputy.”

“We didn’t appoint him.”

“No. You merely made the appointment possible.”

Elyas set his cup down carefully.

“The money that bought his passage came through Lord Merrow’s fund.”

“The fund bought many passages.”

“And weapons,” Rekedim said.

“And flour. Do you object to the flour?”

“Depends who is eating it.”

For the first time, Keld looked at him for longer than courtesy required.

Riarhin watched the attendants. Three stood far enough apart to come at them separately. The fourth had gone indoors. It would have been useful to know what he was doing. It would also have been useful to have brought Gyllan, who knew how to talk to people who believed a distinction remained respectable after it had been used to conceal a body.

Keld leaned back.

“The marches are arming. Some of their lords will burn one another’s stores. Some will call that loyalty to a distant court. My arrangements allow the houses most likely to survive to obtain supplies without destroying the roads they require. A damaged bridge serves no one.”

“A sound one serves whoever gets there first,” Elyas said.

“Yes. You do understand.”

“Then you understand why I can’t leave it with you.”

He said it without raising his voice. Keld’s cup paused short of her mouth.

“The duke’s authority does not extend here.”

“His credit does,” Rekedim said.

Riarhin wished he had not told her that. Then she saw the attendant in the doorway turn his head, and understood that Rekedim had wanted someone besides Keld to hear it.

They were allowed to leave. No one offered a second cup.

At the gate a light carriage stood beside the baggage wagons. Its passenger was arguing with Keld’s steward through the open window. An Aeler man, dark-haired, with a travelling rug folded over one knee. Riarhin had seen his seal on the house’s guarantee, beside Keld’s and written smaller.

“The two drivers,” he said. “Before I go.”

“Their employer has not—”

“I have their employer’s money. They have done their employer’s work. Bring them.”

The steward went back between the wagons. The man noticed Riarhin, looked past her at Elyas and Rekedim, and made a small sound of recognition.

“She’s so messy.”

“Keld?” Elyas asked.

“She leaves people wondering which promise she means to keep. Then she pays men with crossbows to stand about while she explains herself.”

The steward returned with two carters. The passenger counted money into each man’s hand and waited while they counted it again. One disputed the amount. The Aeler unfolded a paper and showed him a line. The carter read it, swore, and took the money.

“You could have told me that at the first station.”

“Yes,” the man said. “I could.”

He handed the paper back through the window. The steward moved aside and the carriage started. Elyas had turned towards Rekedim to ask about the seal; by the time he stepped forward, its rear wheels were through the gate.

“Another Edgewalker?” he asked.

Rekedim nodded.

The carter spat beside the wheel rut. His companion was still counting.

They found Vaul again three days later, at the crossing named in the freight guarantee. He listened beside a bridge too small to have a name in Alric’s book.

He had taken off his boots to examine the lower course. Water darkened his trousers to the thigh. A basket of pins lay beside him; each old one he removed he set apart from the others.

“The carriers won’t wait through another closure,” Rekedim said. “Their advances are secured on her delivery dates.”

Vaul looked at the paper the receiving clerk had sent with them. “And you want me to make the delay certain.”

Vaul fitted a pin between finger and thumb.

“The north bridge requires a new bearing before harvest. I was to certify it for another season when the work was done.”

“Has it been?”

“No.”

“Will you certify it?”

“No.”

Riarhin looked from one man to the other. “She knows that. Hence the mules.”

“The mules are permitted under its present limit,” Vaul said. “The bonded loads are not. Her lenders have accepted the limit as temporary because I have always performed the work.”

“Can you give them a date?” Rekedim asked.

Vaul laid the pin down.

For a while there was only water moving beneath the planks and Gyllan’s staff tapping as he came down the bank.

“I can bring a crew here instead of completing the downstream work. Then two villages wait with a failing sluice.”

“Will you?”

“No.”

“Then tell the lenders.”

Vaul put his wet boots on without answering. When he stood, he looked at Elyas.

“Before you witness it, meet the people who use this crossing.”

They spent the next two days on that question.

There were three villages above the crossing. One could reach a mill by another road. Two could not. There was a woman who took cheese to market twice each week, a lime-burner whose fuel came over the bridge, and a carter who had already borrowed against the harvest haul. None of them appeared in the accounts they had bought.

Elyas visited the carter. Riarhin went because she wanted to see whether he would offer money he did not possess.

He offered paid work carrying grain to the smaller mill instead. The man asked who would make up the difference in distance. Elyas said he would.

“For how long?”

“Until the bearing is replaced.”

“When is that?”

Elyas looked at her. She could not help him.

They went back to Vaul and reduced the purchase of iron to pay for cart hire. Rekedim complained about the price, argued it down, and made a copy of the agreement for the carter’s wife, who could read.

The cheese could cross under the existing limit. The fuel could not. There was no arrangement that left everyone where they had been.

Vaul wrote the notice in a hand so steady that Riarhin found herself watching his face for evidence of the effort.

No renewal of the heavy-load certificate. No promise of repair within the current season. The existing restrictions remained in force.

He gave Elyas the pen to witness it.

“You could say the work is delayed,” Elyas said.

“I am declining to bring the work forward. That is what they have been borrowing against.”

“You’ll lose the contract.”

“And the men employed on it.”

Gyllan shifted on the bench. “I have letters I can write.”

“Then write them. They will need employment.”

He did not thank Gyllan. The old man fetched his writing case.

Chapter Twenty-Two: What Still Moved

The first refusal reached Keld’s receiving house five days after Vaul’s notice.

Riarhin was buying thread when the carrier arrived. He took a folded paper inside and came out with it still in his hand. At the next yard he watered his horses, unharnessed them, and removed the receiving house’s chalk marks from his wagon.

By evening, Rekedim had heard the terms. Payment before loading. No acceptance of a promise secured on goods beyond the north crossing.

By the end of the week there were wagons in three yards with nothing to do.

The grain buyer sent a representative to Elyas. The withdrawn iron could now be made available, on favourable terms, if the duke’s young gentleman still required it.

He did not.

Riarhin sat through the discussion. The representative had brought a gift of preserved apricots. It remained between them throughout, growing more conspicuous each time Elyas declined an offer.

When he had gone, Alric opened the box.

“Should we return these?”

“Yes,” Elyas said.

Gyllan, who had already taken one, put it back.

“Not that one,” Lyra told him.

He ate it with an expression of having been unfairly singled out.

The small losses took longer to arrive.

Vaul’s apprentice came to collect tools. He had found work two days away and had to take his wife and their child because the room they occupied belonged to the bridge office. Vaul helped him strap a level onto a mule.

“You said the other work came first,” the apprentice said.

“It does.”

“My room goes with this contract.”

“I know.”

“Would you have signed if they hadn’t come?”

“No.”

“Would you have put it in writing this week?”

Vaul stopped tightening the strap.

“I don’t know.”

The apprentice looked at his wife, waiting with their child beside the gate.

“I told her you’d find something.”

“I can offer work downstream. Not a house.”

“We can’t live on the road.”

Vaul took the level off the mule and packed it between two folded sacks so it would not bruise the animal. The apprentice let him finish. Then he led the mule to his family.

At the mill, a sack split while being transferred to one of Elyas’s hired carts. Riarhin helped sweep meal from the boards into a pan. The miller’s wife would sell what they saved cheaply, without saying why it was grey.

Keld’s iron stopped moving.

The meal continued.

It went in smaller lots, from different yards, paid for before it was loaded. Riarhin followed two of them as far as the road above the old station. Both turned east before the gate. The tracks joined a much older route, marked by low cairns rather than milestones.

She rode back fast enough to lather her horse and regretted it before she arrived.

Rekedim came out when he heard her. He took the bridle while she loosened the girth.

“Still buying,” she said.

“How much?”

“More. Different sellers.”

“Coin?”

“One took cut silver. The other weighed a sealed bag and didn’t open it.”

He ran a hand down the horse’s neck.

“Someone has been using her roads without needing her money.”

Riarhin went to fetch water. She gave the horse a little at a time and stood with it until its breathing settled.

Mara’s letter arrived folded inside a factor’s demand for confirmation of the cart hire.

Elyas took it outside. Riarhin saw him read it twice, standing beside the stable wall with his thumb caught beneath the seal. He did not smile until the second reading, and then only briefly.

At supper he told them the duke was receiving visitors again. Short visits. Seniro’s last dispatch had come from the northern road. Mara had put in three lines about a broken gutter outside his old room, which he repeated as though it were important that they know.

Afterwards Riarhin found him adding figures to the cart agreement.

“Bad news?” she asked.

“She says he tires.”

“She would say if there were more.”

“Would she?”

Riarhin had no right to answer that. She pulled out the other chair.

He turned the paper towards her. “I’ve spent more than I thought.”

They went over it together. The amounts were small enough separately. Added together, they would buy several good horses, or maintain one bad road for less than a month.

At the bottom he had begun a letter to the duke. There were three lines describing what they had achieved. Nothing yet about what it cost.

“Start there,” she said, touching the figures.

“He’ll ask why I didn’t find a cheaper way.”

“Then he’ll know you tried.”

Elyas looked at her for a moment, then drew another sheet towards him.

They left the towns behind when the eastern carriers stopped returning to the same yards.

Vaul rode with them as far as the last maintained crossing. He had a bag of food from Gyllan, two letters of introduction for his displaced men, and no promise that either would be honoured.

At the bridge he dismounted.

“I shall replace the bearing when I can afford it,” he told Elyas. “Your carter should know that the contract may last longer than you intended.”

“I’ll write to him.”

“Send the payment with the letter.”

He shook Rekedim’s hand. Gyllan complained that he had refused to stay for the meal after accepting the food. Vaul took a piece of bread from the bag and ate it while they finished saying goodbye.

When he reached Riarhin, he glanced at the spare thong securing her pack.

“That will wear through against the buckle.”

She moved it. He nodded and went back across the bridge.

Chapter Twenty-Three: The Empty Houses

The hamlet had a well, roofs and no smoke.

Riarhin counted nine houses from the ridge. Two had lost their thatch. The others stood close around a common yard with a stone trough and an elm whose lower branches had been cut for fodder. A cart lay on its side beside the nearest house. There was nothing particularly old about the broken harness beneath it.

They had lost the northward road in rain. The guide at the last market had told them of a ford; by the time they reached it the water covered the stone he had described. Rekedim would not put the pack mare in. They had followed the bank upstream until the light began to fail, and now the hamlet lay between them and another night without shelter.

“Look first,” Riarhin said.

Elyas and Rekedim stayed with the others above the trees. She went down on foot.

The first house smelled of damp flour. A stool lay beside the hearth, one leg burned short. There were bowls on the shelf and a cradle with a folded cloth in it. She touched neither.

Outside, the earth had been rooted up along the walls. Hoofprints, split and deep. Hogs. A great many, from the way the track crossed itself at the trough.

Something moved in the last house.

She heard a pot roll, then settle. No voice followed. She drew Remorse from her hip and went around the trough to look through the open door.

A sow stood inside with her head lowered. Straw hung from one tusk. There were smaller bodies behind her, crowded against the back wall, and a smell of old blood under the stronger animal stink.

Riarhin backed away.

The sow came to the door but did not charge. She stood on the threshold, watching. Her ears were torn, one almost gone.

“No shelter,” Riarhin called towards the ridge. “Bring them round the outside. Keep the dogs away.”

They had no dog. The words had come from an older yard, an older sound of hooves. She bit the inside of her cheek and looked back at the sow.

There was a shadow behind it. Nothing wrong in that. The house had no lamp and the light was nearly gone.

Then the shadow moved across the open door without touching the pig.

Riarhin stepped back again.

The first charge came from the house behind her.

She heard the clatter of a broken pot and turned as a young boar struck the trough with its shoulder. It had misjudged her movement, not the obstacle; it came round the stone at once, head swinging low.

She climbed onto the trough. Its back scraped her boot. A second animal hit the first and drove it sideways, neither making the angry sounds she expected. They were breathing hard, almost choking.

At the head of the lane Rekedim shouted for the others to turn.

The pack mare reared. Gyllan lost the lead. Alric caught it and was pulled forward until Lyra caught the back of his coat.

Riarhin jumped down on the far side of the trough, putting stone between herself and the boars. The sow stood in the same doorway. Behind her the darkness had reached the lintel where the last light should have fallen.

“Up the bank,” Riarhin called. “Leave the lane.”

Elyas brought his horse down far enough to put himself between the nearest boar and the others. He had the lance unhooked. The horse flattened its ears and sidestepped, but held when he asked it.

The boar came at them. Elyas set the point and took the blow without trying to lift the animal. The shaft bent, held for a moment, then split.

His horse wheeled as another struck its hind leg.

Riarhin reached the animal before it could strike again. She cut behind its shoulder with the straight sword. It turned on her, dragging one foreleg. She stepped aside and finished it when it passed. Its weight took the blade nearly out of her hand.

Above the yard Lyra had got Gyllan onto the bank. The old man turned back for Alric.

“Keep moving,” Rekedim said.

“The mare—”

“I have her.”

He did not. The pack mare was pulling the lead through his hands while two hogs came up the lane. Alric had let go and was trying to unbuckle the bag which held the papers.

“Knife,” Riarhin called.

He looked at her.

“Cut it off.”

He cut the strap. The bag fell against his chest. A hog hit the mare’s front leg, and she struck down with both feet. One struck the animal. The other struck the stones.

Rekedim cut the remaining load free. Grain burst from a sack under the mare’s hooves. Relieved of the weight, she turned uphill and dragged him with her.

Riarhin followed, keeping the sword low. The injured boar beneath Elyas’s broken lance had stopped moving. Another climbed over it without pausing.

She could have killed that one. There were more behind it.

The bank rose above a dry ditch, too steep for the horses until it reached the ruined orchard wall.

Gyllan stood at the gap, directing the animals through. Lyra took Alric’s bag and threw it over the wall, then went back for him when he tried to recover the other one.

“The ink,” he said.

“Leave it.”

Elyas’s horse would not put weight on the struck leg. He had dismounted and was leading it sideways, speaking into its ear. Riarhin came up behind him.

“Go through. I’ll hold here.”

“You won’t hold them all.”

“Then be quick.”

He did not argue again. Rekedim caught the bridle on the far side, and together they brought the horse over the broken stones.

Riarhin backed into the gap. The hogs below had stopped.

They stood with their heads pointing towards her. Five that she could see, perhaps others in the lane. Their sides heaved. One tried to turn away. Its forefeet moved sideways, scraping earth, and then its head came round again towards the gap.

Beyond them, between the houses, the shadow rose higher than a person.

There was still light above the roofs. She could see every stick of the ruined thatch through the space where a head should have been. The dark thing bent, and all five animals moved together.

She went over the wall.

“Don’t wait,” she said.

They ran the horses between the trees. Gyllan stumbled and Alric caught him. Riarhin heard bodies striking the gap behind them, stone falling, a high squeal cut short. She did not look back until they reached the open slope.

Nothing came out of the orchard.

They stopped on ground bare enough to see anything approaching.

Rekedim counted the animals. Elyas’s horse had a shallow wound above the hock, ugly but without the swelling Riarhin feared. Lyra cleaned it while Elyas held the bridle and watched the dark orchard below.

“Did you see it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What was it?”

“I don’t know.”

“The pigs were running from it.”

“Towards us.”

He looked at her. She could remember the darkness passing across the sunlit lintel. She could have drawn its edge. That did not give her a name to offer him.

Gyllan divided the food that had remained on the riding horses. Most of their grain and half the dry blankets were in the village. Alric’s second bag held ink, spare shoes and two books. He had one bottle in his coat. Lyra made him show her before she would stop asking.

“Tomorrow?” Elyas said.

“No,” Rekedim answered.

“We could go in daylight.”

“We could lose more in daylight.”

They ate without a fire. The grass was wet and the blankets insufficient. Gyllan put his coat over Lyra’s shoulders; she waited until he slept, then laid half of it back over him.

Riarhin cleaned the sword with grass before using the last dry rag. A pale smear clung where she had struck bone. Beneath it the edge was unmarked, as it always was. Her hands were steady. She disliked them for that, briefly, then folded the rag to a clean part.

At dawn she could see the hamlet again. The well, the roofs, the bare elm. A pig stood beside the trough with its head down, eating spilled grain.

They went northwest without descending.

Chapter Twenty-Four: The Other Everblood

They came to the water station on the third day after the hamlet, walking beside the horses.

Elyas’s gelding could bear him for short stretches, but Lyra made him get down at every rise. The grain they had bought from a shepherd was mostly husk. Gyllan insisted the horses seemed pleased with it. Rekedim said the horses had not been asked to pay.

The station stood where two roads met beneath a long outcrop. A low wall enclosed a cistern, a stable and the remains of a roofed market. Beyond it the northwestern road climbed a slope with no other water before dark.

Eleven armed people waited among the rocks short of the gate. Their horses were standing nose to tail in the narrow shade. One rider had a bloody cloth tied above the elbow.

The woman in charge watched Elyas until he came near enough to recognise her.

“Another one,” she said.

“Aryka.”

He said the name without a title. Riarhin saw the woman register it and decide to be amused.

“Has Veralyn begun sending them separately?”

“We’re travelling.”

“So were we.”

Aryka Everblood wore a patched riding coat and a sword whose grip had been renewed more carefully than the rest of her equipment. There was nothing provisional in the way her people waited for her. Whatever title she had lost, she had found men willing to hear what she said next.

Riarhin looked at the wounded rider.

“What happened?”

“The price changed after we watered the first two horses,” Aryka said. “Apparently we are an armed company, and the permission we purchased was for travellers.”

“You are an armed company.”

“Today, yes.”

She looked at Rekedim, then Gyllan, then at the documents beneath Alric’s coat. Riarhin shifted half a step. Aryka’s eyes returned to her face.

“You have someone worth keeping dry.”

“We all are,” Gyllan said.

The woman almost smiled.

The station keeper wanted their horses.

He explained it from the wall while his men sat with bows across their knees. Passage could be granted against a deposit. Two good mounts would suffice for Aryka’s following; one for the six new travellers. Returned, of course, when they came back with the countersignature of the lord whose toll this was.

“Where is the lord?” Elyas asked.

“North.”

“How far?”

“Depends where he has ridden.”

The men on the wall laughed. Elyas let them finish.

“The deposit receipt. Will you sign it?”

The keeper looked to his left. The man there wore no better clothes than the rest but was the only one who had not laughed.

“He wants you to refuse,” Elyas said quietly when he returned to the rocks.

“I had noticed,” Aryka answered.

“The keeper might still take money. The man beside him won’t let him.”

“Very perceptive. Can your father burn this place before supper?”

“No.”

“Then we require another inducement.”

Riarhin went behind the rocks with her. The station’s outer wall followed the slope badly. At its western end runoff had opened a space beneath an old drain. Big enough for a person who did not mind the mud, and hidden from the men watching the road.

Aryka pointed to it.

“Two of mine could get through. Then the stable gate opens. We take back the horse they kept and draw the rest west.”

“Leaving us on the road,” Rekedim said.

“You could follow.”

“Through their bowmen.”

“If you’re quick.”

“No.”

Aryka studied him. A little of the amusement left her face.

“What do you propose?”

“The outer gate opens first. Both parties come through together. Then the stable.”

“They’ll put their men at the outer gate.”

“Elyas keeps them looking at him until it opens.”

Riarhin watched Aryka consider the arrangement. She was not looking for a reason it would fail. She was looking for the part of it that would leave her depending on someone else’s nerve.

“And you?” Aryka asked her.

“The drain.”

“That is where I’d have put you.”

“Then we’re fortunate to agree.”

They settled who would move first, who would hold the horses and what signal meant withdrawal. Aryka repeated the last condition to her own people, then made Rekedim repeat his. Riarhin liked her better for that and no more for anything else.

The drain smelled of stagnant water and old leather.

Riarhin crawled through with one of Aryka’s riders behind her. He had taken off his coat and wrapped his knife to keep it from striking stone. Halfway along he stopped when something brushed his face.

“Root,” she whispered.

“I know.”

He moved again. At the far end she waited for a man carrying a bucket to pass, then came out on her elbows beneath a broken rack.

Elyas’s voice carried from the gate. He was asking for the keeper’s name and the name to be entered above it. Each question required an answer the keeper had to obtain from the silent man.

Riarhin went along the inside wall. The gate bar rested in two stone brackets. A guard stood beside it with his bow unstrung and a cup in his hand.

He saw her when she reached the second bracket.

She caught the cup before it fell, drove him against the wall and struck him beneath the ear with the hilt of Toothpick. He slid down. She put the cup beside him because breaking it would be louder than setting it down.

The other rider lifted the bar with her.

They pushed.

The gate caught on the body. Riarhin hauled the guard’s legs clear. Outside, Elyas had stopped talking.

“Now,” she called.

Rekedim came through leading the pack mare. Lyra and Alric followed with Gyllan between them. Aryka’s first riders entered at a walk, then spread enough to give those behind them room.

The man on the wall drew his bow.

Aryka shot him before he loosed. He fell backwards out of sight. Someone shouted from the stable, and the whole yard seemed to begin moving at once.

A man with a hooked pole ran at the pack mare. Riarhin caught the pole near its head and was pulled across his path. She let it go, stepped inside the second swing and drove Toothpick into his upper arm. He dropped the weapon. She kicked it beneath the water trough.

Elyas had put his horse between a bowman and Gyllan. He reached down and took the man’s bow before the string came back, wrenching it against the saddle. The bowman fell. Elyas’s horse stumbled on the injured leg and nearly followed him.

“Get down,” Lyra shouted.

He did, landing beside the man he had knocked down. Riarhin turned away when she saw him keep the sword between the two of them. He knew what to do there.

Aryka’s people reached the stable. The man with the bloody arm brought out a bay horse without a saddle and shouted that it was theirs. A second rider began unfastening another stall.

“Leave that,” Aryka said.

“It’s sounder.”

“It’s not the one we came for.”

He hesitated. She turned her horse towards him.

He left it.

The keeper had crouched beneath the wall with his hands on his head. Rekedim took him by the collar and brought him to the cistern.

“Who has the key?”

“I do.”

“Then open it.”

They watered under guard and left before the dead man’s companions could decide how many of them were still prepared to fight.

Aryka paid the original toll into the keeper’s shaking hands. He stared at it. She made him say before two witnesses what it was for.

Outside the station they separated the parties again. One of Aryka’s riders had a cut across his scalp. Lyra bound it while Riarhin watched the road. Gyllan helped the wounded man hold his hair out of the blood.

Elyas stood beside his gelding, examining the leg.

“You were right about the keeper,” Aryka told him.

“He wasn’t the one deciding.”

“He is now.”

“He may prefer to be somewhere else.”

“So may we all.”

She looked at the horse. “Don’t ride him up the next slope.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

Riarhin heard herself in the reply and looked away before he saw her smile.

Aryka mounted. “Give my regard to whichever of them is receiving it.”

“Veralyn?”

“If you like.”

“I’ll tell her where I saw you.”

“Yes,” Aryka said. “I expect you will.”

Her following took the western fork. The party waited until the last horse had cleared the narrow ground, then turned northwest towards the Ecktorian corner.

Gyllan had been given a packet of salt for binding the rider’s head. He put it in their food bag without comment. They had none left before that.

Chapter Twenty-Five: The Invitation

Three days later, a woman brought them Keld’s invitation.

She found them at a watering place where an old drovers’ shelter still had most of its roof. Riarhin had chosen it because the road could be watched from either side and because Gyllan’s knee had swollen again. He said it was the weather. Lyra said it was the saddle. They had been disagreeing about it since noon.

The messenger waited until Riarhin came out from the horses. She wore a dusty riding coat and carried no visible weapon. Riarhin had seen her at the lower house.

“Tomorrow. At the lime pits. Mistress Keld will have three people with her. She asks that you bring no more.”

“Why should I come?”

“She says you have noticed which loads are still moving.”

“Tell her midday.”

“Before midday. She has a long road afterwards.”

“We all have.”

The woman left without asking for water.

Riarhin went back through the shelter and out the other side. She walked a wide circuit, looking for a place from which the messenger could have been watched. There was a scatter of dung on the hard ground above the spring, dry enough to belong to any of the previous days. A narrow scrape marked one stone. She could make nothing certain of either.

Below her, Alric held a pot while Gyllan tried to persuade a strip of saddle leather to serve as a handle. Lyra had gone to the spring with their washing. Elyas was rubbing down his horse. Rekedim looked up once, saw her on the rise, and returned to his work.

She completed the circuit before going down.

Chapter Twenty-Six: The Lime Pits

Gyllan was sharpening Lyra’s knife when Riarhin woke.

From the ridge beyond the shelter she had seen Casta Ferrum’s outer towers the evening before. The road reached them by a long turn through the lime workings. A gate clerk had sent back their first inquiry: passage for the living, weapons declared, loads weighed. They would present themselves when they knew what they were taking through.

He sat with his bad leg stretched towards the cold hearth. The blade was short, suited to food and cord and the hundred tasks for which a sword was an expensive nuisance. He tested its edge against a shaving of wood.

“She lets it go too long,” he said.

“She has you.”

“For the moment. I intend to become idle.”

“You’ve left it late.”

“I wanted to be certain I’d earned it.”

She put water on the fire. His staff stood against the wall beside him, the ferrule still firm.

At breakfast they agreed the arrangements. Riarhin, Rekedim and Elyas would meet Keld. The others would stay at the shelter with the packs. If the three had not returned by sunset, Gyllan would take Lyra and Alric southeast to the last occupied village and wait there one day before sending word to the factor who held their copies.

“Not come looking,” Riarhin said.

“I heard you the first time.”

“You don’t always behave as though you have.”

“Nor do you.”

He held out a heel of bread. She took it.

Keld had chosen the pits for their approaches. No one could ride close without being seen. The white workings opened in the hillside like empty rooms; thin grass grew along their edges. Riarhin left the horses below the eastern bank and went up on foot with the others.

The Edgewalker waited beneath the surviving wall of a kiln. Three attendants stood apart from her. One was the messenger. One carried a crossbow pointed at the ground. The third looked ill and kept wiping his palms on his coat.

Riarhin stopped where she could see all four.

“You have made my affairs inconvenient,” Keld said.

“You asked us here.”

“I did.”

Elyas waited. It was becoming one of his more useful accomplishments.

Keld removed a folded account from inside her coat.

“The station was leased in my name. Provisions were purchased against my guarantees. I was told the occupier had claims which could be advanced once the southern houses had committed themselves.”

“Claims to what?” Rekedim asked.

“Men. Territory. An inheritance.”

“Whose?”

“Tulgoth’s.”

Wind moved loose lime across the floor. Riarhin felt it settle against her teeth.

Rekedim spoke before she did.

“What is the son’s name?”

“Kargath.”

The name did not give her a face. She searched for one anyway: boys beside fires, a crowded doorway, blood blackening a cuff. Too many years, too many people who had not introduced themselves before trying to kill her.

“How certain?” she asked.

Keld looked at her.

“The man who arranged the lease had authority to pledge collateral held under that name. Three witnesses accepted the lineage. One was old enough to have known the father.”

“That’s certainty about credit.”

“It was what I required.”

“And now?” Elyas asked.

“Now my guarantees have become a convenient explanation for transactions I did not approve.”

She held out the paper. Elyas took it only after Riarhin had looked at the attendant with the crossbow.

The account showed an advance against salt, silver, and payments due from three houses whose names Riarhin had seen in Merrow’s papers. Several amounts had been copied twice, first as receipts and then as expected receipts. The distinction appeared to trouble Rekedim.

“He’s spending promises from men who haven’t committed,” he said.

“He is paying their men before they do.”

“Where?”

“I don’t know. That is why I asked you here.”

Elyas unfolded the second page. “Orvain was expected here.”

“At the station. From Pont-de-Tithe.”

“To do what?”

“Appear before the houses’ agents. Explain the duke’s refusal in person.”

“He stabbed him.”

“He was to describe the refusal. I do not know what version he intended to give.”

“Did you pay for the attack?”

“No.”

Riarhin watched the crossbowman’s hands. He had raised the weapon slightly when Elyas spoke and had not lowered it again.

“And you would have carried him after it,” Elyas said.

“The passage had been purchased. It was cancelled. You already know why.”

“What does he mean to do?” Riarhin asked.

“Gather men whose lords have left them unpaid. Offer a settlement those lords cannot afford to refuse. There will be violence before there is agreement. There usually is.”

“And the duke?” Elyas said.

“Was an obstacle to several agreements.”

“Did you know he would be attacked?”

“No.”

She answered promptly. Riarhin could not tell whether that made it more credible.

“Who did?”

“I have given you the account.”

For a moment Riarhin thought Elyas would demand more. Instead he folded the paper, protecting its worn corners with his hand.

Keld looked at Rekedim.

“If you find the money, you will find the undertaking. I would have preferred to do that myself.”

“You still could,” he said.

“My name is already on enough of it.”

She called to her attendants in Aeler and walked out between them. The crossbowman went last. Riarhin watched until they were below the bank, then went to the rim and watched them mount.

No one pursued them.

They were late because Rekedim wanted to see the eastern track from the high ground.

Riarhin took them along the edge of the workings. Below, the road divided around a marshy hollow. The northern branch could take wagons in dry weather; the southern one passed a well and a ruined sheepfold. She pointed out the slopes that would require an extra team.

Rekedim studied them, then asked to go back.

She saw the horse first.

It was Alric’s, running loose below the ridge with one stirrup striking its flank. For an instant she tried to fit it into something ordinary: a slipped knot, a fright at the spring.

Then she heard metal.

She drove her heels in. Her horse stumbled on the descent, recovered, and came out above the shelter at a run.

Gyllan stood in the gap between the wall and the fire pit. He had a sword in his hand. Lyra was behind him, on one knee, trying to pull him backwards by the coat.

A man with two pale braid-rings caught Gyllan’s blade against his own. Another came from the side. Riarhin saw the short point go in below the old man’s ribs.

Gyllan struck the first man across the face with the guard. His knees bent. The second man pulled his weapon free.

Riarhin had the bow off her shoulder before her horse stopped.

The first arrow took the man beside Gyllan through the upper back. He turned with it, one hand reaching for something he could not grasp.

She had to move for the other. Lyra was between them. Riarhin dropped from the saddle, ran three strides, and drew again as he stepped over Gyllan’s legs.

This arrow entered beneath his jaw.

He fell against the wall, stood for a moment with its help, and slid down.

Someone shouted from beyond the shelter. Ykrul. Riarhin nocked a third arrow and swung towards the voice.

An older man stood beside Alric, one arm across the young man’s chest. He had a knife in his other hand. Elyas rode at him with his sword drawn. Rekedim shouted something in the same language, sharp enough to cut through the noise of the horses.

The knife fell.

Riarhin kept the arrow on the man’s throat while Elyas pulled Alric clear. Rekedim dismounted, kicked the knife away, and drove the man to his knees.

Only then did she look back.

Lyra had both hands pressed against Gyllan’s side. She was repeating a short phrase, setting each word against the pressure of her fingers. His sword lay across his shin. It had a wrapped grip and a nick halfway down the edge. One of the dead men wore the empty scabbard.

Riarhin put down the bow and knelt.

There was another wound beneath his breastbone. Lyra had not seen it, or could not leave the first one. Riarhin pressed her folded glove against it.

“Here as well,” she said.

Lyra made a sound of impatience, almost anger. “I know.”

Gyllan’s eyes moved towards Riarhin. She could not tell whether he saw her. His lips parted. Breath came wetly through his nose.

“Keep him still,” Lyra said.

Riarhin held him. The torn edges beneath Lyra’s fingers drew towards each other. Blood welled through the narrowing gap; farther down his side, his coat continued to darken.

“Get cloth.”

Alric brought a shirt. He had torn it badly, losing time to a seam that would not part. Riarhin took it and gave him the glove without thinking. He stood holding it.

Gyllan’s heel scraped once against the ground.

Lyra bent over him, her hair falling into the blood on his coat. She pushed it away with her wrist and tried again. Her mouth was moving. Riarhin knew no words that would help and listened because the girl was saying them.

The edges joined in one place and pulled apart in another. Lyra changed the phrase. A thin stream of blood ran from her nose onto her lip; she wiped it away and began again.

“No,” Lyra said.

Riarhin kept her hand where it was.

“No. I haven’t—”

Lyra took a breath that caught halfway in. She changed the position of her hands, one at his chest, one at his side, as though the fault lay in the arrangement.

Alric knelt beside her.

She pushed him away without looking. He stayed.

Riarhin felt the old man’s weight settle against her arm. Nothing else changed. There was sunlight on the pot beside the fire, and a fly walking over the cut edge of the bread they had left that morning.

Lyra began to weep. She was still trying when Rekedim came to stand behind them.

Act III

Chapter Twenty-Seven: Under His Ring

They moved Gyllan into the shelter because Lyra would not leave him outside.

Riarhin wanted to wash the blood from her hands first. She could not make herself ask for the time. She took his shoulders while Elyas lifted his feet, and they carried him between them beneath the broken roof.

His staff had fallen behind a water jar. Alric picked it up and laid it beside him.

For a while the boy stood looking at the repaired ferrule.

“The sword was on the ground,” he said. “He took it when the man dropped it. He told us to get behind the wall.”

Riarhin waited.

“I couldn’t get past the other one.”

“Show me.”

He pointed towards the spring. There were marks in the dust where his heels had dragged. The older scout had caught him there. He had been trying to reach the horses.

“What did he say?”

“I don’t know. I couldn’t understand him.”

“Did he cut you?”

Alric shook his head. Then he pulled his sleeve down over his wrist, where the man’s fingers had left bruises.

She checked him anyway. No wound. He submitted to it with an absence that frightened her more than an objection would have.

“Sit with her,” she said.

He went inside.

The captive sat with his back against a post. Rekedim had bound his hands in front, passed another rope behind the post, and tied his ankles separately. Elyas watched from beyond kicking distance.

Riarhin stopped beside the dropped knife. It was a working blade, sharpened often, with a new thong through the handle. She put it on the wall out of reach.

Rekedim asked the man his name in Ykrul.

“Serekh.”

“Whose ring?”

“My own.”

“Whose pay?”

“Kargath’s.”

The answers came in a low voice roughened by thirst. Riarhin understood them clearly enough. Elyas looked from the captive to Rekedim.

“You speak—”

“Yes,” Rekedim said. “Water.”

Elyas fetched it. Rekedim held the cup while Serekh drank, then waited until he had swallowed before continuing.

“How long?”

“Since the receiving market in southeastern Acasia.”

Riarhin knew the place. They had bought oats there while Rekedim arranged the first offers. Gyllan had argued with a woman about the price and then paid what she asked because she had made him laugh.

That far back.

“How many?” she asked.

Serekh looked at her, then at Rekedim.

“Three.”

“Remounts?”

“Two. Left north of the lime road.”

“Who reported for you?”

“A wool carrier. A man taking empty baskets east. We didn’t use them twice.”

She remembered the baskets. She had never looked at the man’s face. There had been a broken hoop, a strip of pale willow showing through the old weave. She had been watching another wagon.

“What were your orders?”

“Find what you were following. Watch the man from the Fens. Report whom he met.”

“Me,” Rekedim said.

“Yes.”

Elyas set the cup down. “And kill the people we left behind?”

Rekedim translated. Riarhin was glad he did. She did not want to help the man choose his answer.

“No.”

Serekh’s gaze moved towards the shelter and stopped at its entrance.

“I meant to speak to you. I told them to wait above the spring. When you left, they wanted the papers. They said six people had kept us crawling in ditches for half a season.”

“You let them go?” Riarhin asked.

“I forbade it.”

“Did you stop them?”

“No.”

The word had no room inside it for the things he might have tried.

“Under my ring,” he said. “Their zeal is my weight.”

Riarhin looked towards the bodies. One was older than she had first thought, with grey already mixed into his beard. Apprentices to this man, then, rather than boys. Good enough to follow her for weeks. Good enough to have reached the shelter unseen.

Serekh was watching her look.

“They knew better,” he said.

She believed that more readily than anything else he had told them.

Lyra came out while they were searching the dead.

Riarhin had taken the bowman’s quiver, a packet of dried meat, and two tally slips wrapped against the wet. There were braid-rings on both men. She left those until Rekedim said they should be kept to identify them.

“Identify them to whom?” Lyra asked.

“To their people.”

She looked at Serekh. “He can do that.”

“He can.”

“Then why are you taking them?”

Rekedim gave her an answer about kin and names and the things a body could no longer carry home. Riarhin scarcely heard it. She was looking at a neat repair on one man’s saddlebag. The stitch was the same as the thread caught on a thorn three days before, on the wrong side of the road for the meal carts.

She had seen it. She had left it there.

Lyra turned on her.

“You were looking for them.”

“I was looking for Keld’s people.”

“You said no one had followed us.”

She had said it beside the fire, after coming down from the rise. Gyllan had been holding the pot. Rekedim had looked up. She could recover every word; none would bear a kinder meaning.

“I was wrong.”

Lyra waited as though there ought to be something after that.

Riarhin had nothing.

Serekh made his offer after sunset.

He spoke to Rekedim first, then repeated it in halting Vhasian when Elyas asked. Beyond Casta Ferrum, on the grass, there was a camp north of the broken watercourses. Three leaders had gone there with their households. Others were expected. They were discussing what would happen when Kargath began asking for more than money.

“Opposition?” Elyas asked.

“Not yet.”

“Friends of yours?”

“People who know my name.”

“Will they admit us?”

“They will hear why I brought you.”

Riarhin sat on the low wall. Her hands had dried stiff. She had washed them twice.

“Why do you want him stopped?” she asked.

Serekh bent his bound wrists, testing where the rope pressed.

“He promises a country. He buys riders. The country has to live where the riders have eaten.”

“Where?” Rekedim asked.

“Acasia first. While the Viterrans and Vhasians fight each other. Then Ecktoria.”

Elyas looked up. “Through the fortress?”

“Past a man who has let our raiders through for years. Kargath thinks he has learned what the gate will bear.”

“And you?”

“A purse pays for a raid. An army makes the Prefect explain himself.”

“You took his money.”

“Yes.”

“And now you think he can’t win.”

“I think he can win enough to make it worse.”

From the doorway, Lyra said, “How useful.”

No one answered her.

“He kills Gyllan and happens to know exactly where we need to go.”

“I didn’t—” Serekh began.

“Don’t.”

He stopped.

She looked at Rekedim, then Elyas. Neither looked away. Riarhin wished one of them would find something that made this less naked.

“We can check the camp before we enter it,” she said.

“Orvain might have known a road.”

Riarhin went still.

“He had a knife,” Elyas said.

“So did this one. Against Alric.”

“He put it down.”

“Yes,” Lyra said. “And Orvain struck at her. And he had stabbed the duke himself, whereas this man says he told the others not to. I know. I was here.”

Her voice had grown more precise with each sentence. Gyllan would have stopped her there and asked a question. Riarhin could not find one that would not sound like an examination.

“It’s an unfair comparison,” Lyra said. “You don’t have to explain that to me.”

She looked at Riarhin.

“It’s still yours who gets killed for, and mine who gets explained.”

Riarhin got down from the wall.

“I didn’t save Gyllan.”

“I didn’t ask you to say that.”

“No.”

“Are you taking him?”

“Yes.”

“Then take him.”

Riarhin wanted to put a hand on the girl’s shoulder. Lyra drew her arms across herself before she could try.

“He may be telling the truth,” the girl said. “That is the worst part. I can see why you believe him.”

She went back inside. Riarhin stayed where she was, with the answer she had prepared about guards and verification no longer useful to anyone.

They buried Gyllan above the spring in the morning.

The ground was stony. Elyas worked with the little spade from the packs until his palms opened, then Rekedim took it. Riarhin loosened the stones with a length of iron from the shelter. They dug long enough for the sun to reach the bottom.

Alric read from a book whose pages Gyllan had folded at the corners. He lost his place once. Lyra found it for him with a finger and kept her hand there until he finished.

They put the staff beside Gyllan. After a moment Lyra took it out again.

“He’d say it was wasteful.”

Her voice broke on the last word. She held the staff with both hands and did not look at any of them.

Riarhin placed the first earth carefully. After that there was work to do.

They buried the scouts lower down, beyond the track. Rekedim spoke their names as Serekh had given them. Lyra stayed above the spring.

When the packs were ready, Riarhin found Gyllan’s small whetstone in the place where he had sat that morning. She took it to Lyra.

“For your knife.”

Lyra closed her hand around it.

“Thank you.”

It was worse, somehow, that she could still be polite.

Chapter Twenty-Eight: Casta Ferrum

The fortress was visible before the road to it.

Its towers stood above the broken country in courses of dark stone. Below them, walls had been added and abandoned as the town grew down the slope. Smoke rose from houses outside the newest wall. A wagon was being winched round a turn too narrow for its load.

Ecktoria began at a pillar half buried in the verge. Someone had scraped the old border inscription clear and cut a newer toll beside it.

Lyra stopped to read the figures.

“How did Serekh get past Casta Ferrum? Maybe we can do the same.”

“He paid the toll,” Riarhin said. “You always pay the toll.”

Lyra looked towards the bound man on the other horse.

“How much?”

“Ask him.”

She did. Serekh answered in Vhasian, giving the price for a rider, a remount, a bale of wool and a weapon carried openly.

He had entered as a drover’s guard. His apprentices had travelled separately.

“You weren’t hiding,” Lyra said.

“Not from them.”

Beyond the queue, men were unloading wine beside the garrison kitchen. A cook counted joints of meat as they came off another cart. He rejected one and sent the carrier back for a larger piece.

“Does the Prefect entertain often?” Elyas asked.

The carter ahead of them gave a short laugh.

“Last night was busy.”

He looked up at the wall and turned back to his team.

Lyra waited until they had moved beyond him.

“What happened last night?”

Serekh answered quietly. “They go at night. Forty, fifty Ykrul, in the dark. The Prefect feasts his men the next evening. Always. Each crossing.”

“Into Acasia?”

“Yes.”

“And they pay,” Riarhin said.

Serekh glanced at the wine. “They pay.”

“The Prefect has a proclamation about raiding,” Elyas said.

“Several.”

Lyra looked back at the kitchen door. “Why let them through?”

“Fewer complications than stopping them.”

“Until someone tells Ecktoria,” Rekedim said.

The carter had heard that. His shoulders rose. The woman behind them found something to adjust on her pack, and the gate clerk called loudly for the next traveller.

Riarhin looked up through the gate passage. The bar slid into a recess beside the stair. Two men stood below it; the bowman above could shoot anyone approaching, but not men already beneath his opening.

Forty or fifty would be enough. Let them come through as they always did, and one night they need only keep the gate open. The rest could follow before the Prefect had finished deciding whether it was a misunderstanding.

She nudged her horse forward. “Best have our papers ready.”

The gate clerk counted five free travellers and one prisoner.

“Whose prisoner?”

“Ours,” Elyas said.

The clerk dipped his pen and waited.

“Taken after an attack in Acasia. We have an account.”

“Was the attack here?”

“No.”

“Then the captain will read it.”

The gate passage smelled of hot animals and iron. Riarhin kept her horse close to the wall while a cart of hides passed. Above the arch, a man watched through an opening no wider than his face. The bow beside him was already strung.

Serekh’s horse tried to follow the cart. Rekedim shortened the lead.

“Do they know you?” he asked in Ykrul.

“The clerk does.”

“He hasn’t said.”

“He’s waiting to see who I belong to.”

The captain received them in a room over the scales. She wore her sword belt without the sword; it hung on a hook within reach. A tally of passing wagons lay under a stone to keep the draught from lifting it.

She read the death account, asked Alric whether he had written it and made him identify his own corrections.

Then she looked at Lyra.

“You ask passage with this man alive.”

“Yes.”

“For judgment on the grass.”

“For a hearing,” Lyra said. “We haven’t been promised a judgment.”

The captain put the page down.

“That’s a better answer.”

“What would it cost to keep him here?” Elyas asked.

“The guard, the room, his food, and the person you leave to answer when his people inquire.”

“Would you hear the charge?”

“I would hear why you were asking an Ecktorian officer to judge a killing in Acasia. Then I would send for someone who had time to argue the rest.”

No one spoke.

“You may take him through,” she said. “He remains bound. No weapons returned inside the walls. You pay for him as a traveller and lodge beyond the north gate.”

Lyra’s hand tightened on Gyllan’s staff. The captain saw the movement and waited, but the girl did not ask for another answer.

The ordinary toll was not the whole price.

Two clerks came while they were preparing to leave. One carried a copy of their route certificate from Pont-de-Tithe. The other had a request for the copied Commission accounts to be detained pending an inquiry.

The captain compared the seals. She looked more interested than troubled.

“Your roads have been busy.”

“The men carrying that order attacked us,” Elyas said.

“Here?”

“On the Thin Road.”

“Then you have had a long time to acquire enemies.”

Rekedim laid their damaged freight receipt beside the request. “This account still secures iron through your northern weighhouse.”

One of the clerks leaned nearer.

“Should it not?”

“The guarantor has withdrawn part of it. These loads were pledged twice. Your men can check the weight against the public books without taking our copies.”

The captain looked at the clerk who had leaned forward.

“Can they?”

“Yes.”

“Today?”

He considered the question before answering. “If the yard stays open.”

“It can stay open.”

She turned back to Rekedim.

“What else?”

Riarhin felt Elyas shift beside her. Rekedim did not begin offering everything they knew. He named the house in Keld’s account, the date of the refused certificate and three loads whose descriptions had changed between stations. Enough for the captain to inspect her own risk.

“And you want your passage cleared,” she said.

“We’ve paid the toll.”

“You have.”

She picked up the detention request and wrote across it. The first clerk looked unhappy. The second took the freight numbers down carefully.

“The accounts leave with you,” the captain said. “Those three loads wait here. If you have supplied false information, the north gate will have your descriptions by sunset.”

“How will you decide?” Lyra asked.

“By weighing what is in my yard.”

They were held below the north gate until late afternoon.

Alric used the time to buy ink. Elyas bought grain and two replacement blankets. The gelding was sound enough to ride now, but the garrison farrier made him walk it twice along the yard before agreeing, and charged for both the advice and the shoe he replaced.

Riarhin went to fetch Serekh water. He drank without raising his eyes to her.

“You knew there would be a letter,” she said.

“No.”

“You knew the toll wouldn’t end it.”

“Yes.”

She took the cup back.

“Next time tell us which part you know.”

He looked at the staff tied beside Lyra’s saddle.

“I am trying.”

Riarhin left him with Rekedim. At the arch the gate clerk counted them again, then counted the animals.

The gate opened. Beyond it the road climbed through the last of the stone workings towards open country.

Lyra rode beside her until the walls were behind them.

“Was that the toll too?”

“Yes.”

“The captain got the account.”

“And we got through.”

Lyra considered it. “She’ll keep taking it.”

Riarhin looked at the girl, at the staff and the newly mended pack strap.

“Probably.”

They went on. No one at Casta Ferrum called them back.

Chapter Twenty-Nine: The Water Account

Serekh rode with his hands tied to the front of his saddle and a lead on his horse. They took the first watch in pairs and changed the order each night. Riarhin scouted ahead, returned by another line, then went back to check the ground behind them. She rode more of each day than any of them. By evening she wanted sleep and would not let herself take it until she had checked the approaches once more.

On the second evening Rekedim caught her tightening a girth she had already checked.

“You’ll sore the horse.”

She let it out a hole.

“They were good,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You don’t have to make them poor scouts for this to be your fault.”

She looked at him.

“That came out badly,” he said.

“No. I understood it.”

Neither of them had improved matters. He handed her the reins and went to see about the water.

On the fourth day beyond the fortress they reached the place Serekh had described. There were hearth circles, dung and a channel whose banks had been trampled by animals. The people were gone.

“You said here,” Lyra told him.

“When the lower water held.”

“It doesn’t hold.”

“No.”

Rekedim got down and walked the channel. He returned with mud on the end of a stick and asked Serekh which grazing lay above the next tributary.

The answer gave them another two days. Riarhin saw Alric counting the grain bags. She had already counted them.

They bought food from a herding household that evening. Elyas offered coin; the woman wanted a needle fine enough for the coat she was mending. Riarhin had one. The exchange took longer than the purchase would have done because the woman wanted to show her the tear first.

At the next watering place, Serekh asked to have one hand freed so he could mark the bank. Rekedim refused and gave him a stick between his bound hands instead. He drew where the camp would have gone, then struck out one approach when he saw how high the water had been.

On the sixth evening they saw cooking smoke beyond a shallow ridge. Riarhin did not take Serekh’s word for the approaches.

She left the others in a shallow fold of land and went on foot. From the ridge she could see three groups of tents separated by livestock pens and a broad strip kept clear for wagons. Water ran through a cut channel along the southern edge. There were children driving geese away from it, a woman mending a harness, and enough armed riders to make retreat worth planning before arrival.

She counted for half an hour. Riders came and went. The numbers would not hold still.

An old woman below the ridge stopped sorting dung for the fuel stack and looked straight at her.

Riarhin remained still.

The woman raised one hand and beckoned impatiently.

There was no useful way to pretend she had not been seen.

By the time she reached the bottom, two riders had come out from behind the nearest pens. One held a bow across his saddle.

The woman pointed at the channel.

“If you cross it there, you’ll break the lip.”

Riarhin went around.

They brought Serekh into camp under guard. His bonds were inspected and left in place.

The woman who received them was called Iserin. She had a burn scar along one forearm and a strip of leather between her teeth while she repaired the fastening on a bridle. She put the bridle down when she saw Serekh.

“Where are the others?”

Rekedim answered in Ykrul. He gave the names, the attack, Gyllan’s death, and Riarhin’s two arrows. He did not begin with Serekh’s defence.

Iserin listened without interrupting. Then she asked Serekh whether the account was true.

“Yes.”

“Were they under your ring?”

“Yes.”

She looked at Lyra. Rekedim repeated the question and answer for her and Alric before Iserin continued.

“We will hear this before he leaves,” Iserin said in Vhasian. “It will not disappear because he knows a road.”

Lyra’s fingers tightened on the staff. She gave one short nod.

It was not trust. Riarhin was grateful that Iserin did not ask for it.

Velkan refused an audience until the water account had been settled.

Riarhin first took this for a way of keeping them waiting. Then a man came into the tent behind them carrying two empty skins and began shouting. Another followed with a split tally-stick. Neither appeared to care who Elyas was.

Velkan was sitting on a low stool with a board across his knees. He had a broad face, cropped hair, and an ink stain on the pad of his thumb. He listened until the first man repeated himself, then took the stick from the second.

“You were charged twice,” he said.

“Three times.”

“The third was storage.”

“They stored empty skins.”

“That is what I am trying to discover.”

Rekedim held out a hand for the stick. Velkan did not give it to him.

“You know this account?”

“I know an empty skin weighs less than a full one. Show me what you weighed.”

By noon there were eleven sticks on the board and a boy had been sent twice to the lower well.

Riarhin stayed near the entrance, where she could watch the people who came to listen. No one had announced an inquiry. Each new arrival seemed to know precisely what was being disputed.

The rents had been collected in water at the well and entered as water delivered to camp. The difference between the two had been charged as loss in transit. Three deliveries had no corresponding arrival mark at all.

“Leakage,” the collector said.

“On the same days?” Rekedim asked.

“Bad skins.”

“Whose?”

The man named a trader. Someone went to find him.

While they waited, Lyra took one of the supposedly defective skins from the pile. She filled it at the channel, hung it from a pole, and put a bowl beneath it.

The collector watched the bowl remain empty.

When the trader arrived, he identified his mark and said the skins had been sold to riders bearing Kargath’s authority. He had been paid. He had no complaint.

The men whose water had filled them did.

Velkan scraped an entry from his board.

“You may speak after the evening meal,” he told Elyas.

“About Kargath?”

“About what you came to say. I know about Kargath.”

He sent for the collector’s other accounts.

The crossing into Iserin’s horse enclosure had shifted during the spring floods. Two of its planks had been laid back badly. A mare put her foot through one while Riarhin was helping bring their mounts in.

They got her out with ropes. The mare shook and tried to bite anyone who touched her leg. Riarhin approved of that too. It did not make holding her head easier.

Iserin arrived with four people and a lifting bar. She asked Rekedim whether he intended to watch or take the other end.

He took it.

The timber available for repairs was green. Iserin would not let them cut the living trees beside the channel; the bank was held together by their roots. The spare seasoned lengths were under a grain wagon too heavily loaded to move.

Elyas began unloading it.

No one thanked him. Two children joined him, then Alric, then a woman who told the children where to stack the sacks properly. By dusk they had the timber free and the wagon back on its wheels.

Riarhin worked in the channel, guiding the rope beneath the beam. Mud came over the tops of her boots. Her shoulders ached from Gyllan’s grave. When she straightened, she found Lyra on the bank holding the other rope.

“I have it,” the girl said.

Riarhin passed her the slack.

They worked until the planks sat level. Iserin led her own horse over first. Halfway across, she stopped and shifted her weight hard in the saddle.

The bridge held.

At supper she put meat into their bowls herself.

“Fenwood knew your father,” Iserin said to Rekedim.

“Yes.”

“Before he took you south?”

“Before I was born. My father’s people carried for the Black Banners after Tulgoth drove them out.”

She looked across at Riarhin, then back to him. “How long after?”

“Ten years after Tulgoth died, give or take a winter. My mother counted better than my father.”

Iserin put another piece into his bowl. “They generally do.”

Amru’s tent stood nearest the water, where the ground had been raised with gravel brought from somewhere else.

He was smaller than Riarhin expected. One eye had clouded; the other followed her hands when she placed the scouts’ braid-rings on the mat between them. Serekh knelt beside the entrance, still bound, with one of Iserin’s guards behind him.

Rekedim had told her the words to use. She knew the language. She did not know which promises the form might bind her to, and had asked twice.

She named the dead men. She named Serekh’s command. She said where their bodies lay and how they had died.

Amru touched neither ring.

“And the man they killed?”

“Gyllan.”

“His people?”

Lyra gave his name more fully, with a place and a former service Riarhin had not known. Amru listened to all of it.

“No debt is settled here,” he said. “These have come home. He has not.”

Lyra sat back on her heels.

Serekh spoke. Amru let him finish, then asked three questions about the order at the spring. He asked Alric where he had stood and whether Serekh’s knife had touched him. He asked Lyra whether she had heard the older man call to the others.

“Yes,” she said.

“Before or after the first wound?”

“I don’t know.”

He accepted that. Riarhin saw how much it cost Lyra to leave it there.

“Serekh stays under guard,” Amru said. “You may use his knowledge. You may not call that a judgment.”

Outside, darkness had covered the gravel. Lyra went past Riarhin without speaking, but she did not pull away when Riarhin steadied the tent flap above her head.

Chapter Thirty: The Red Dog

The pack mare turned her head when Rekedim brought the saddle out.

He put it down again.

There was a hot place beneath the girth, no wider than two fingers. Riarhin had felt it the evening before. It was cooler now, but the mare still tightened her belly when a hand came near it. Elyas’s gelding had begun resting the injured leg whenever he stood.

“A day,” Rekedim said.

Riarhin looked towards the ridge. She could have walked to the top and seen what lay beyond it before breakfast.

“The horses,” he added.

“I heard you.”

He carried the saddle back. She stayed to comb the dried mud from the mare’s mane. At the third tangle the animal lowered her head and began leaning against the brush.

Beyond the pens a man was calling for a red dog.

The dog lay beneath a cart six paces from him. Riarhin could see its paws and one pale eyebrow. Each time he called, the eyebrow moved.

By the time she found breakfast, two people were arguing about it.

The man said the dog belonged to his sister. A woman in a yellow cap said his sister had given it to her. The sister was away visiting someone with a new baby. Neither disputant appeared to consider this a reason to wait.

A little girl held a length of rope. At every pause she asked which of them wanted it.

“It follows my flock,” the man said.

“It follows anything with a backside.”

“It knows the work.”

“It ate my work yesterday.”

“That was dough.”

“What did you think I was making?”

Riarhin sat on an upturned bucket beside the cooking fire. The woman tending it handed her a bowl without asking whether she wanted one. The contents were thick, white and faintly sour, with a spoonful of dark fruit pressed into the middle.

“Is it her dog?” Riarhin asked.

“Depends what it’s done.”

The cook took the spoon back to stir the pot. Riarhin found her own.

The woman in the yellow cap had flour on one side of her nose. Her coat was fastened with three different buttons and a loop of blue cord. She spoke with her hands held flat, pushing each assertion towards the man. He kept taking a breath to answer while she was still speaking. It gave his interruptions a wheezing sound which offended him more than anything she said.

The girl offered the rope again.

“Not now,” both adults said.

She wound it round her waist and went to the cart. The red dog came out at once. It was smaller than its paws had promised, with a white chest and a tail which curled so far forward it touched its back. The girl led it away without using the rope.

Neither adult noticed until she was gone.

Riarhin scraped the fruit from the bottom of the bowl.

Elyas had taken off his coat.

She found him beyond the drying lines, standing opposite a broad young man in a sleeveless green tunic. A woman was showing Elyas where to put his hands on the man’s belt. She had a narrow plait bound with little brass tubes; they clicked together when she bent.

“Lower,” she said in Vhasian.

Elyas shifted his grip.

“Your body. Not your hands.”

He bent his knees. The young man moved his foot, turned, and laid him on the grass with a gentleness which made the fall look worse.

Three children applauded. One was eating a raw onion like an apple.

Riarhin leaned against a post.

Elyas saw her and remained on his back for a moment.

“Good morning.”

“It was.”

The woman gave him a hand up. “Again.”

“What did I do?”

“You helped him.”

“I meant to.”

“Then you are learning something else.”

The young man laughed. He had a gap between his front teeth and a patch sewn over the seat of his trousers. Elyas laughed too, shook grass out of his sleeve and took the belt again.

This time he stayed on his feet longer. The man tried a different turn. Elyas followed too quickly, checked himself and brought them both down on their knees.

The child with the onion made a disappointed noise.

“That was better,” the woman said.

“For whom?”

“For you. He bruised his knee.”

The young man rubbed it, smiling, and said something Riarhin caught only at the end. It concerned the woman’s own last fall. She hit him on the shoulder and told him to stand up.

Elyas offered Riarhin his place.

“No.”

“You might learn something.”

“I am.”

He looked towards the children, who had begun imitating the way he had first bent his knees. His expression changed. He went over and corrected the smallest one’s grip, carefully repeating the woman’s words.

The child threw the one with the onion. The onion rolled into the dust.

Riarhin left before they decided who should replace it.

Lyra had borrowed a board to put across her knees.

She sat outside a tent striped with faded red, beside a woman wearing a man’s coat over a much finer dress. The coat’s sleeves were rolled almost to her elbows. A boy slept with his head in her lap. She held a cup above him, moving it out of reach whenever his hand wandered upwards in his sleep.

“Again,” Lyra said.

The woman repeated a word. Lyra wrote it, crossed out part, and tried it herself.

“No. You’re closing it too soon.”

“Here?”

“Listen.”

Riarhin stopped to let two men carry a rolled carpet past. When they had gone, the woman looked up.

“You speak it.”

“Yes.”

“Tell her.”

Riarhin said the word.

The woman tipped her head. “You do it too.”

“Do what?”

“That old ending. Where did you learn?”

“A long way north.”

“A long time north, I’d say.”

Lyra put another mark beside the word. She did not look at Riarhin.

The woman was called Nera. She translated for wool buyers during the trading season, and for her husband’s mother whenever the older woman wanted to pretend she could not understand him. She said this in Vhasian. A voice from inside the tent answered in the same language that he spoke very badly.

Lyra asked permission to write that down as well.

“His name?”

“The sentence.”

Nera laughed hard enough to wake the boy. He sat up, took her cup and drank from it before she remembered she had meant to stop him.

Alric emerged from behind the tent with two stools. He offered one to Riarhin. She shook her head and took the other end of the board while Lyra settled more comfortably.

There were three columns on the page: what she had heard, what it meant, and something about who had said it. Several entries had been squeezed between earlier ones. At the bottom she had drawn a little mouth with an arrow beside it.

Nera saw her looking.

“She wanted a word for that rope.”

“The short one?”

“I told her what I called it. My aunt told her I was wrong. Now she wants everyone’s name.”

“So I can ask again,” Lyra said.

“You won’t get the same answer.”

“I’d like to know that too.”

Riarhin lowered the board onto her knees and went on. Behind her, Nera was asking Alric whether he wrote as quickly. He said he wrote more legibly, and Lyra told him to fetch his pages.

Near midday the wind dropped.

The tent covers hung slack. Flies settled on the damp side of a water skin. Someone had draped a child’s red stockings over the horns of a tethered goat; it stood motionless beneath them, chewing with the injured dignity of an animal used to such treatment.

Rekedim was sitting with two carters in the shade of a wagon. One had taken a wheel pin out to show him. The other was explaining why neither of them knew anything about packing felt.

“It swells,” Rekedim said.

“Everything swells if you leave it where he leaves it.”

The man with the pin pointed it at his companion. “Tell him how many axles you broke.”

“One.”

“In a week.”

“One is still one at the end of the week.”

There was a jug between them and a fourth cup set upside down. Riarhin lifted it. Rekedim moved his knee so she could sit on the folded hide beside him.

She stayed for half a cup. The pin passed from hand to hand. No one had asked where she was going by the time she left.

At the edge of the shade, a grandmother was teaching a child to count with knucklebones. The child could count to eight but refused to allow that a small bone should be worth as much as a large one. His grandmother spread them farther apart. This satisfied him until she moved them back.

Riarhin watched him begin again.

A white thread clung to the old woman’s sleeve. Each time she reached for a bone it lifted and caught the light. She had tied her hair with a strip cut from the same yellow cloth as the cap at breakfast. Sisters, perhaps. Riarhin looked at the set of her mouth, then at the tent behind her. There was no one there to compare it with.

The child pocketed the smallest bone while his grandmother was looking away.

“Eight,” he said firmly.

She held out her hand without turning back.

In the afternoon Riarhin slept.

She had meant to mend a fastening. She woke with it still in her hand and an impression of the seam of her coat across one cheek. Outside, someone was singing the same line repeatedly, never reaching the end at the same height.

She put her boots on and went to find the mare.

The animal was asleep too. A fly walked beneath her eye. Her skin twitched, but she did not lift her head.

Riarhin checked the place beneath the girth. Cool. She left it alone.

The singing came from two old women spreading combed wool across a cloth. One sang; the other interrupted. Between verses they worked quickly, pulling apart the little clumps and laying them in the same direction.

“No river,” said the second woman.

“There has always been a river.”

“You put it there because you couldn’t remember the hill.”

“My mother sang a river.”

“Your mother couldn’t carry a tune in a bucket.”

“She carried you home often enough.”

The second woman laughed, then began the line herself. Riarhin knew neither version. In one a lover waited beside water; in the other he had gone over a hill, apparently with someone else’s horse. Both women preferred the horse, but the one who remembered the river insisted there was another verse which accounted for it.

Elyas came past holding his coat against his ribs.

“Again tomorrow?” Riarhin asked.

“They’ve asked.”

“Will you?”

He considered before answering. “If I can bend.”

She made room for him on the low fence. They listened until the woman with the river forgot her words and accused her companion of doing it deliberately.

A tethered goat stretched towards a tuft of wool beside her boot. Riarhin whispered without turning her head. It stopped chewing, considered her, and tried the other side of the peg. She moved her foot.

At dusk the red dog came back.

It walked beneath the fence with something wrapped in cloth between its teeth. The woman in the yellow cap followed at a run. Behind her came the man from breakfast, shouting that it was her dog now.

Riarhin lifted her feet to let it pass.

Chapter Thirty-One: No Aid Given

In the morning they crossed to the lower grazing ground with Amru.

The ford had silted over since his last inspection. He wanted the channel cleared before more households arrived. He also wanted to know whether the strangers could follow a person who knew where he was going without improving his instructions.

He said this while looking at Riarhin.

Three knotted cords lay on the bank. One for the guide, one for the person finding the stones, one for the person keeping the line. Amru gave each of them a strip of cloth.

“The wind-knot,” Rekedim said quietly. “You can refuse.”

“What happens if we do?”

“He hears us anyway. He remembers.”

Elyas took the first cloth. “In the romances there is usually a monster.”

“There’s mud,” Riarhin said.

Amru permitted three spoken words for the crossing. The cords were to carry the rest. Rekedim explained the signals, and Riarhin made him repeat the one that distinguished slack from trouble.

She went first, blindfolded, finding the ford-stones with her feet. Elyas held the guide cord from the bank they were leaving. Lyra followed at the interval Amru had measured with his staff. The water was cold enough to hurt her toes inside her wet boots.

The first stones were firm. The fourth rocked. Riarhin eased her weight away and tugged the warning.

Behind her, the cord tightened.

She could have taken off the cloth. The knowledge made the exercise faintly irritating. Amru was trusting them with a shallow ford, not a revelation.

Then the gravel beneath the next stone slid.

Her foot went down to the ankle. She shifted towards what felt like the bank. The line at her waist pulled sideways, drawing Lyra after her.

“Hold,” Lyra said.

Riarhin stopped.

Water pushed against her shin. She waited for another word, forgot there might not be one, and made herself attend to the cord instead. Two short pulls. Weight back. A long easing that asked her to lift her foot rather than drag it.

Lyra’s hand found her elbow.

It was shaking. It stayed there until Riarhin had a sound stone beneath her.

On the far bank, Amru removed the cloths. Riarhin looked back. A narrow runnel crossed the path she had been about to take, deep enough to put her on her face and pull Lyra in after her.

“You saw that?” she asked.

“I felt you going.”

“Thank you.”

Lyra untied the cord from her waist.

“It wasn’t a decision.”

Riarhin stood with the cloth in her hand while the girl went to help Alric carry the poles for marking the unsafe ground.

They met after sunset in a tent whose seams had been repaired with cloth from at least three others.

Velkan brought his account board. Iserin brought no papers. Amru sat beside the entrance, where he could put his leg out straight. There were nine others, some leaders, some people whose presence evidently required no title. Riarhin had not learnt all their names.

Rekedim sat beside Elyas. Riarhin chose a place where she could see the entrance without turning. Lyra and Alric sat together across from her. Serekh remained outside with his guard.

Elyas laid Keld’s account on the mat.

He spoke of the duke’s wound, the money through Merrow’s fund, the displaced loads, and the arrangements for Orvain’s passage beyond Pont-de-Tithe. He did not call it a Ykrul conspiracy. Riarhin had warned him against the phrase, and he had looked offended that she thought he required the warning.

When he finished, Velkan asked what he wanted.

“Help finding the money. Men enough to stop it reaching the people it is meant to buy.”

“And afterwards?”

“The accounts can show which houses promised payment. Which men arranged the attack.”

“Afterwards for us.”

Elyas looked at the faces around the tent.

“I can’t promise you land in another ruler’s name.”

“Can you promise it in your own?”

“No.”

Velkan leaned back.

Rekedim spoke in Ykrul. He did not translate each sentence. Riarhin heard him distinguish the taking of the train from the ownership of its contents, and ask who would still be owed wages when the coin was gone.

A man beside Iserin interrupted.

“You were paid to teach their sons.”

“Yes.”

“To bring them here?”

“To bring this one home.”

“And the others?”

“They came without a wage.”

The man looked at Lyra. His next words were quieter.

Kargath had settled a blood dispute among his mother’s people. No one else had come when asked. That mattered to him. The water mattered too. He did not see why he must pretend one cancelled the other in order to choose.

“I haven’t asked you to pretend,” Rekedim said.

“You have asked for men.”

“Yes.”

The discussion continued long enough for the lamp to require trimming.

“He will get us all killed,” Iserin said.

Oskel looked at her. “Some of our sons are with him.”

“I know whose sons are with him. Mine will die here when the Ecktorian soldiers come looking for theirs.”

“Acasia is divided,” Elyas said.

“So are we. He thinks the Viterrans and Vhasians will keep each other busy while he takes it. And then he means to turn on Ecktoria.”

Velkan rubbed his thumb across the rim of his cup. “The Prefect lets a raiding party pay its way through. He sends it somewhere it will not trouble his own villages. Kargath proposes to make him choose between his purse and his neck.”

“He isn’t Tulgoth,” Iserin said. “He has men who remember being afraid of Tulgoth. He thinks that is the same thing.”

Riarhin listened. No one disputed the danger. It did not follow that they would entrust their people to Elyas. A woman with a child asleep against her knee had two sons riding with him. She wanted to know how Elyas proposed to distinguish them from men who had stabbed a duke they had never seen.

Elyas did not have an answer that satisfied her. He did not claim to.

At last Iserin drew Keld’s account towards her and turned it over.

“This paper is yours. Our stores are ours. Our riders have obligations already. This gathering will furnish you with neither soldiers nor intelligence against Kargath.”

Velkan nodded. Others did the same, some slowly.

Elyas began to speak. Rekedim touched his wrist.

Amru looked towards the entrance.

“You may stay tonight. You will have water for the road. Serekh remains here until the witnesses can be heard again.”

Lyra spoke then.

“Will they be?”

“Yes.”

“How will I know?”

Amru called the scribe forward and dictated the undertaking. Lyra made him include the place to which a copy should be sent. Her voice was level. She had chosen Tarlington.

The scribe repeated the wording. She listened, corrected Gyllan’s name, and agreed.

Outside, Elyas walked far enough from the tent before he spoke.

“We repaired their crossing. Found the theft. Brought back their dead.”

Riarhin could hear the effort he was making to keep his voice down.

“The mare was hurt,” she said.

He stopped.

“And the water was stolen from people who needed it. We didn’t buy them.”

“I know that.”

She waited.

“I thought it would matter.”

“It did. They heard you.”

He looked back at the tent. People were still coming out in twos and threes, speaking without haste. One of the women paused to take a sleeping child from another’s arms.

Riarhin could not tell him that being heard was enough. It plainly wasn’t.

Rekedim came up beside them with the folded account.

“We’ll leave after the morning watering,” he said. “Get some sleep.”

He did not sound defeated. That was not new. Riarhin watched him go to their tent and wondered what she had missed this time.

Chapter Thirty-Two: The Forgotten Watch

The guard outside the records tent had a cough.

Riarhin had heard it during the afternoon while she waited for Lyra’s statement to be copied. Two dry barks, a pause, then a third that seemed to hurt. He wore a felt cap with a patch above the ear and warmed his hands under his arms though the day was mild.

After the evening gathering, she saw him carrying his bedroll towards the healers’ tents.

The relief did not arrive.

She noticed because she was sitting outside their own tent, rubbing grease into boots that had been wet for most of the day. From there she could see the records tent between two grain wagons. A lamp burned inside. Once, a clerk came out with a bundle, looked at the empty stool beside the door, and went on without calling anyone.

Riarhin finished one boot.

Rekedim sat down beside her.

“Is there a back entrance?” he asked.

“There’s a flap for passing chests through. Pegged from inside.”

“Good.”

“Subtle people.”

“They have families riding under his ring.”

She looked again at the empty stool. It was easy to make a joke of the gap from here. Less easy if a son might pay for it.

“How long?” she asked.

“Until the dawn watch, I think.”

“You think.”

“Yes.”

She put the second boot on without greasing it.

They walked separately. Riarhin went first, taking a water jar past the wagons. No one challenged her. At the channel she filled it slowly and watched the reflections while Rekedim crossed the open strip.

He entered through the front.

She waited until a woman driving two goats had passed, then left the jar beside their tent and followed him.

The records were not laid out for them.

There were leather cases, rolled maps, bundles of split sticks, hides with charcoal marks, and three chests with different locks. The lamp had been filled. Its wick had not been trimmed properly and smoked at one side.

Rekedim was examining the labels on a stack of folded hides.

“You could search for his name,” Riarhin said.

“They’re all his name.”

He showed her. Reports from wells. Lists of horses. Men absent from winter camps. Payments claimed and payments witnessed. Half the material concerned rumours that had come to nothing. A sketch of a fortified yard bore corrections from three different hands; one had moved the gate, another had crossed out the entire wall.

Accumulated knowledge. No agreement about what it meant.

Riarhin went to the chests.

“Leave the locks,” Rekedim said.

“That one is already open.”

“Then that one.”

Inside were receipts tied by locality. She brought the first bundle to him and took the next herself.

They worked on the floor to keep their shadows below the lamp.

The first route was too easy.

A sketch showed a convoy moving towards a northern ford. Beside it a scout had drawn covered wagons and a pair of outriders with long pennons. The report was recent. The number of wagons matched a tally from a salt yard.

Riarhin laid the two together.

“Here.”

Rekedim read them. Then he searched another bundle, found what he wanted, and shook his head.

“The ford is passable. The approach isn’t.”

“Your source?”

“A dispute about six drowned goats.”

She took the paper. A bank had slipped above the ford. The goats belonged to a household claiming compensation because Kargath’s surveyors had told them it was safe.

“Horses could still use it,” she said.

“Yes. Empty wagons might. Loaded ones won’t.”

He set the convoy sketch aside without discarding it.

The second route followed the wells. It had purchases of fodder, replacement wheels, and guards seen at three successive stops. Riarhin traced it with a finger.

“More convincing.”

Rekedim grunted.

“What?”

“The wheels.”

They had been bought in two sizes. Neither matched the axle measure taken by a scout who had helped repair one of the money wagons a fortnight before.

“That could be wrong.”

“It could.”

He left the route open.

Riarhin heard footsteps outside and covered the lamp with her palm. Light showed red through her fingers. For a moment she was back at the shelter, unable to see the wound beneath Lyra’s hands.

The footsteps passed.

She moved her hand away. Rekedim had stopped reading.

“Go on,” she said.

He did.

Towards midnight they found the oats.

There was nothing remarkable about the quantity. What interested Rekedim was the place: a disused fuller’s yard beside a narrow watercourse south of both reported routes. The purchaser had also hired eleven draught animals for two days and returned nine. The loss was being disputed.

Riarhin turned up a lamp-wick receipt from the same yard. Then a payment for mending harness, entered under another man’s name but witnessed by the same hand.

“Resting heavy teams,” Rekedim said.

“Or preparing them.”

“Yes.”

He asked her for every report from the southern watercourses.

They found a ferry rope renewed where no ferry toll had been collected in months. A salt seller had been told to keep three days’ stock out of the market. A drover complained that armed men had taken the dry wash below his grazing ground, forcing his cattle to water farther downstream.

Rekedim arranged these around the map.

Riarhin studied the ground it claimed to describe. The southern way looked longer. On horseback it would be. With laden wagons it avoided two steep climbs and brought them to water without crossing the broken bank.

“Here,” she said, touching the narrow reach beyond the fuller’s yard. “If this slope is right, they need the extra teams here.”

He placed the hire receipt beside her finger.

“And give them back here.”

“The missing two?”

“Taken onward. Or dead. We don’t need that answer yet.”

He returned to Keld’s account. The payment dates had looked untidy when they first read them. Now he put them beside the salt-yard entries and counted backwards on his fingers.

“Three days,” he said.

“Until the wagons?”

“Until the payments fall due. The wagons must reach the lower exchange the evening before. They can’t stop at the wells to be seen.”

Riarhin looked at the convoy sketch with its pennons.

“Those can.”

“Those should.”

He drew the route on their own paper, leaving gaps where the reports did not join.

“The southern way brings them through a cutting here,” he said. “A weighing house used to stand above it. Can you find it?”

“If the map is honest.”

“Find out before we put men in it.”

“We haven’t got men.”

He did not answer immediately. He was reading an escort report, his finger moving down its margin.

“No,” he said. “We haven’t.”

They found three reports of the escort. The oldest counted twenty-four riders. The newest counted thirty-nine mounted men and eleven wagon guards, with relief horses sufficient for more. Several guards had been seen carrying bows. One report mentioned shields hung beneath the wagon beds.

Riarhin copied the largest number and added a question mark.

Even surprised, even confined, they were not men she could make disappear by choosing a clever place to stand.

Rekedim took a fresh sheet and listed what an attack would require. People to hold the road behind the train. People to stop the lead team without slaughtering the animals and blocking access to the wagons. Enough bows to keep the escort from dismounting into a formed line. Riders beyond the cutting to prevent a courier getting away before the accounts were secured.

“And someone who knows which chest matters,” Riarhin said.

“Me.”

She looked at him. He kept writing.

They returned every report to its bundle. Riarhin had copied nothing. Somewhere west of the river Rekedim had stopped asking her to, having satisfied himself that a page she had read was a page they still had.

Riarhin trimmed the lamp’s smoking wick before she remembered that she should leave things as she had found them.

Too late. She put the trimming in her pocket.

Outside, the camp was beginning to stir. A man carrying a spear approached the records tent from the far end of the wagon strip. He stopped to adjust his sandal.

Riarhin and Rekedim went the other way.

Chapter Thirty-Three: Those Who Rode

Iserin met them at the horse enclosure after the morning watering.

She had a grey mare on a lead and a pack rolled behind her own saddle. Beside her stood a woman Riarhin had seen reseating the bridge planks. Several other riders were checking girths nearby.

“You are leaving?” Iserin asked.

“Yes,” Rekedim said.

“Which way?”

“South.”

She ran her hand beneath the mare’s cheek strap.

“The road is poor.”

“We’ve heard.”

“My niece takes horses to the lower grazing. Some of her people have kin there.”

The woman beside her looked at Rekedim without offering a smile.

“My name is Dareth,” she said in Vhasian. “How many wagons?”

She kept her right hand closed while she waited for the answer. There was an old bowstring cut across the palm, healed white, of the kind a woman gets once and then never again.

Iserin handed over the grey mare’s lead and walked away before he answered.

They did not depart as one company.

Dareth’s people rode first: eighteen with bows, spare strings in waxed leather, and remounts led in pairs. Later, Riarhin saw another party leaving by the eastern track, spears wrapped for travel. At their head was the man who had said Kargath settled his mother’s blood dispute.

“He is coming?” she asked Rekedim.

“We’ll find out.”

Elyas had been speaking to Velkan. He returned carrying a receipt for water and fodder, paid from what remained of their travelling money. No gift. No camp seal on an agreement to furnish troops.

“Don’t lose that,” Rekedim said.

Elyas put it inside his coat.

Amru came to see Lyra before they left. He gave her the copy of his undertaking and told her Serekh would remain under guard. Two people whose names she could send for had witnessed it. He pointed them out separately, so that she would know their faces.

She thanked him.

Riarhin waited until he had gone.

“You can stay for the hearing,” she said. “I’ll leave money. We can arrange your onward passage afterwards.”

Lyra looked at her.

“You need someone who can mend what you do.”

“You don’t have to be that person.”

“No. I don’t.”

She put the document away and tightened the strap on Gyllan’s staff, which rode along her saddle beneath her knee.

Then she mounted.

By late afternoon there were sixty-three Ykrul at the abandoned sheepfold.

Riarhin counted them twice, then asked Dareth. Sixty-three was right. Twenty-seven carried bows. Most had a second weapon. Six would be needed to hold the remounts and the pack animals once the fighting began. Two knew the southern cutting because they had driven loaded wagons through it. A broad man with a crushed ear had repaired wheels at the lower exchange until Kargath’s quartermaster stopped paying him.

None wore the marks of the gathering. Some had marks of their own.

Rekedim asked who commanded them.

Three people stepped forward.

It took an hour to turn that into an answer they could use.

Dareth would command the mounted archers.

The man whose mother owed Kargath was named Oskel. He had brought thirty spear fighters and would place them himself, and he did not point with his hand at any time that day; he carried a short spear reversed and used the butt of it, on the ground, on the drawing, once against the sole of his own boot to knock the dirt off while he thought.

The remaining riders accepted a grey-haired woman called Sava. She had a length of knotted cord looped twice round her saddle horn, and before she would agree to anything else she wanted to know where the wounded would be taken.

Rekedim drew the cutting in the dirt and kept the gaps in his information visible.

“We haven’t seen the ground yet,” he said. “These are reports, not distances I can swear to.”

“Then why should we agree?” Oskel asked.

“Because we won’t attack until we have seen it.”

Oskel squatted beside the drawing and put the spear butt on the place Rekedim had left blank.

The first argument was about the money.

Some of the riders had wages owing from service under Kargath. Others had kin whose animals had been requisitioned. One man wanted the silver divided on the ground as soon as the wagons were stopped. Elyas said the money might be evidence, and received a look Riarhin thought he deserved.

Rekedim put a pebble where the lead wagon would stand.

“You take that chest and pay yourselves tonight. What happens at the lower exchange tomorrow?”

“No payment.”

“And the riders waiting there?”

“They go home.”

“Some. Others take food from the nearest household. Some join whoever pays first. If we break the pay train, we need the wage rolls with it. We need to know who is owed, and send word before the hungry men arrive.”

Oskel rubbed his mouth.

“You propose to keep paying his army?”

“I propose to offer men their arrears for leaving it. With their names struck from his roll in front of witnesses.”

“With his silver.”

“Yes.”

Riarhin watched the faces round the drawing. That they could imagine. That had a shape larger than a share of plunder and small enough to believe.

It did not settle the argument. They worked through claims, witnesses, what would happen if the chests held less than expected. Elyas offered to have copies of the accounts carried to the river factors. No promise of land. No claim that his title settled what the riders were owed.

At last Dareth laid her bow beside the drawing.

“We take no households. We burn no grazing. A man who drops his weapon lives unless he takes it up again. Any of mine who forgets that answers to me.”

Oskel looked at the pebble for the lead wagon.

“And any of yours?” he asked Elyas.

“To me.”

Riarhin looked at Lyra, sitting beyond the circle with Alric. The girl had heard. Her face gave nothing away.

Riarhin went to inspect the cutting with Dareth and one of the former wagon drivers.

They left the horses below the last rise. The driver crawled less neatly than the two women but knew where the ground rang hollow over an old drain, and stopped Riarhin before she put her weight on it.

From the rim she could see the remains of the weighing house. One wall stood. The road entered beneath it, curved between banks high enough to conceal a dismounted man, and emerged beside a dry stone enclosure. Beyond that lay open ground.

No narrow miracle in which six people could defeat an army. There were places for the escort to rally. There was a second track a horse could climb if its rider accepted the risk. A scout sent ahead would see a careless ambush from the ruined wall.

She showed Dareth all three.

Dareth nodded at the second track. “Sava.”

“The wall needs to stay empty until their advance riders pass.”

“Mine can wait below the lip.”

“The rear guard may stop here.”

“Oskel won’t like that.”

“He doesn’t have to like it.”

The driver pointed to a rut below the far bend.

“Loaded wagons stop there to put the drag on. If they don’t, the rear wheels run into the teams.”

Riarhin studied the slope again. It was steeper than the records had shown, but not too steep for the hired animals. Rekedim had been right about the road.

Dust rose far to the south. Too thin for the train yet. A rider, perhaps two, travelling towards the weighing house.

They drew back from the rim.

At the horses, Dareth checked her bowstring and looked at Riarhin.

“Can your people hold until mine are placed?”

“Yes.”

“Even the young lord?”

“I’ll tell him it’s discourteous to begin without you.”

Dareth considered that, then gave a brief, unwilling smile.

They rode back by the longer way. At the sheepfold, Rekedim had found flat ground for the injured and put the water there already. Elyas was listening to Oskel explain why his thirty men would not stand where the first drawing had put them. Alric was making copies of the revised plan, large enough to read without passing them from hand to hand.

Lyra sat beside the water, sharpening her knife on Gyllan’s stone.

Riarhin dismounted and carried her own skin over. She knelt to add it to the others.

“Is there anything else you need?”

“Clean linen.”

“I’ll find it.”

“More than you think.”

Riarhin stayed a moment, waiting for something she had no right to expect. Then she went to ask among the riders.

Chapter Thirty-Four: The Shape of the Ground

Oskel had moved the wounded before Riarhin returned with the linen.

There were none yet. That did not make the argument less serious. Sava had put two blankets and the water in the lee of the sheepfold. Oskel had carried the blankets to the other side of the wall. Sava brought them back while he watched.

“An arrow from the rise,” he said.

“A horse from the cutting,” she answered.

They both looked at the empty space between the wall and the pack animals. Riarhin followed their gaze. From there the blankets could be reached without crossing the route by which the reserve horses would leave. The rise was screened by the broken end of the fold. She had seen each of those advantages separately. Neither Ykrul had pointed to them.

Sava picked up one blanket. Oskel took the other. They moved them together and went on arguing about the people needed to carry a wounded rider.

Lyra watched from beside the water.

“Have they agreed?” she asked.

“About where.”

“They haven’t said so.”

Riarhin put the linen down. “Leave it there. They’ll tell us if we’re still wrong.”

That night Dareth asked to see the approach again.

Riarhin was tired enough to resent the request. She went because Dareth would have to put her people beneath the bank Riarhin had chosen. Oskel sent a rider with them; Sava sent another. Neither rider behaved as though accompanying them was a vote of confidence.

At the cutting, Dareth stood still for a long time.

“Too close,” she said.

“To what?”

She opened her hands, one towards the weighing house, the other towards the far track.

Riarhin considered the distance. “They can’t support each other from farther apart.”

“They can be reached from the same turn.”

The rider Oskel had sent moved downhill. Sava’s rider moved in the opposite direction. Dareth watched until their positions satisfied her, then looked back at Riarhin.

A guard who broke through the first line would now have to choose. He could not ride through one party and carry his speed into the next. Riarhin had intended to bring the lines closer so that either could help the other. Dareth had made their separation the useful thing.

“The back of the wall is blind,” Riarhin said.

Dareth shifted three paces and crouched. From there she could see beneath its broken course.

“Not if I stay here.”

“And if you fall?”

The other woman looked at her sharply. Then she called her second rider forward and showed her the place.

They returned after midnight. Rekedim had left a cup upside down beside the pot to show which portion was hers. There was a skin of fat over it. She ate standing up and went straight to her blankets.

Before dawn the former wagon driver brought news of a second camp.

He had gone to inspect the renewed ferry rope downstream. There were horses in the fuller’s yard. More than the receipt accounted for, and saddle horses among the draught teams. A man had been sweeping the space outside the old counting room, pushing the dust into a crack with unnecessary care.

“Someone important,” he said.

“Or someone who hates dust,” Riarhin answered.

“He left the dung.”

She got her bow.

The fuller’s yard smelled of old urine whenever the wind shifted.

Riarhin approached along the abandoned outflow, where a crawl beneath a leaning sluice brought her into a trough screened from the stable by a waist-high wall. Dry residue cracked beneath her hands. She had left Dareth at the last bend with an account of where she meant to go and how long she intended to take.

There was no good way out if someone looked down into the trough. She spent longer finding a bad one than she had spent coming in.

Beyond the wall, a groom was walking a restless grey horse. An open door led into the counting room. Through it Riarhin could see a table, one chair leg, and a narrow hand resting on a cane.

“Again,” a man said in Ykrul.

Another voice recited the crossings. Riarhin kept still.

The speaker came into view while he was naming the last well: a young officer with a strip of red cloth beneath his belt. He carried a sheet that had been folded and unfolded until the corners curled. He was trying to read it and watch the yard at the same time.

“The nearer road is watched,” he said. “We should send the second escort back.”

“And leave what between the train and the exchange?”

“They will not risk an attack so close to—”

The cane lifted. Not far. The young officer stopped speaking.

“Show me.”

He put the sheet on the table. Riarhin could no longer see his hands, but she heard the scrape of objects moved across wood.

“You have left a hole,” the older man said.

“Only for an afternoon.”

“Yes.”

Nothing followed. The young man shifted his feet. At last he moved something on the table again.

The cane returned to the floor.

“You think I am asking because I doubt your courage.”

“No, my lord.”

“You keep giving me courage when I ask for distance.”

The officer bent over the table again.

“The Acasian stores first,” Kargath said. “The eastern armies will be at the Belworth. We need the mills held before the next levies come in.”

“And the Prefect?”

“Will take his toll until he is told to stop.”

“He has let riders through. These are companies.”

“Then give him time to count the money.”

The young man did not answer.

“After Acasia we turn on Ecktoria. We will need the grain for that. Do not spend it winning the places that must feed us.”

The groom led the grey across Riarhin’s view. She waited for it to pass. When it did, the man with the cane had risen.

He wore a plain coat. A healed split marked the flesh beneath his lower lip. She had seen him at the ferry, standing behind the clerk while Elyas’s horse was loaded. She had noted his hands, the cane, the absence of baggage, and judged him to be waiting for someone who did not arrive.

Kargath.

The name fit the face without changing it. He was not large. One shoulder sat lower than the other. He rested on the cane between movements but did not favour either leg when he crossed to the door.

“Those people at the gathering,” the young officer said. “They have been speaking with the Fenwood man.”

“The one who took the offer.”

“Should I bring him?”

“No.”

Kargath looked out across the yard. Riarhin lowered her head until a broken edge of stone stood between them.

“Have you read his book?”

The officer said he had read parts.

“Read the rest. His account of a convoy dividing at water is exact. He has put the drivers in it. Most men forget the drivers.”

He went back inside. A book landed on the table with the soft weight of worn leather.

“Canray,” the officer said.

“For his readers. A sound choice.”

There was a pause. Riarhin heard the grey horse snort, then the slap of the groom’s hand against its neck.

“My mother kept a board sent to my father from Ecktoria,” Kargath said. “Very fine. Our game, our name in the dedicatory leaf, our riders carved in costly wood. A gift from a man whose soldiers had closed our winter crossing.”

The officer murmured something she could not hear.

“They knew where it came from. Everyone knew. They taught it to their children. They praised us for it. We had given them a way to think, and they could not give us a road wide enough to bring our animals through the snow.”

The young man was looking towards the door again. A carrier had entered the yard and stood holding his cap. He would have seen Riarhin if he had come to the trough to piss.

Kargath continued more quietly.

“I will not send another beautiful thing for them to thank us for. I will have a place they must leave open when they draw their borders.”

For a time there was only the carrier shifting his feet in the yard. Riarhin heard a page turned.

“The Oshiiran correspondent,” Kargath said.

“Nothing further, my lord.”

“That is the fourth man who has had nothing further.”

“They send the same account each time.”

“They send the same paragraph. Green glass in a brass frame, out of the Himadri, and a wish with a clause folded into it. I can recite it in three languages. I have paid for it in four.” The cane touched the floor once. “Kalrantha. A word, a description, and nobody who has had it in his hand.”

“Then it may be nothing, my lord.”

“It may. I would rather establish that than be assured of it by men who are also guessing.”

“Shall I keep him?”

“Keep him. Halve what we send him.”

The officer looked back. “The northern houses have agreed.”

“They have signed. That is why the payments cannot be late.”

He called the carrier in. Their voices changed to the flatter rhythm of weights, hours, and animals. Riarhin waited until the groom took the grey into the stable before she eased herself backwards along the trough.

Her sleeve caught on a splinter. She stopped, freed the cloth one thread at a time, and crawled out carrying the smell with her.

Dareth heard her account without interrupting.

At the part about the winter crossing, she looked away towards the water. Then she asked how many saddle horses Riarhin had seen, how fresh they were, and whether the officer’s red cloth had been tied or pinned.

“Tied.”

“Personal service. Not command of a household.”

“Does it matter?”

“He can order any of them. None must stay with him.”

At the sheepfold, Rekedim listened to the whole report twice. The second time he asked Riarhin for Kargath’s exact words.

She gave them.

He went back over the crossings and the wells twice and left the rest of it alone.

“You’ve skipped a part,” Riarhin said.

“The glass thing.”

“Yes.”

“It isn’t on the road. It isn’t in the wagons. Nobody is being paid to move it.” He turned the drawing towards the light. “Tell me when that changes.”

She let it go. The words would return when she had room for them. For now she watched Rekedim move the wagons on the drawing.

“He was at the ferry,” Elyas said when she described the scar. “The man I asked you about.”

“Yes.”

“Then he saw us before the scouts did.”

“He saw you,” Rekedim said. “It doesn’t mean he knew where you would go.”

Elyas sat down. Riarhin could see him fitting the memory into another account of his own journey. She knew the work. It had no convenient end.

“What do we change?” Oskel asked.

“The relief horses,” Rekedim said. “We count them into the escort. We leave the archers back until the whole train is in view. If he has joined it with another company, we let it pass.”

Oskel looked at Dareth. She moved a stone on their drawing, bringing the place for withdrawal clear of the reserve horses.

No one argued against letting the train pass. They argued over how to make sure everyone knew in time.

Chapter Thirty-Five: The Cutting

There were eight wagons.

Riarhin counted them through the gaps between the advance riders. The first carried barrels beneath a patched cover. Two had reinforced boxes lashed to their beds. The remaining five looked like ordinary freight, which was a useful way to carry anything worth killing for.

More men rode with them than she had hoped. Fewer than the number at which they had agreed to leave.

She raised two fingers beneath the broken wall. Dareth saw, checked the road herself, and held her position.

The advance riders came through at a walk.

One dismounted beside the weighing house. Riarhin could see the sweat-dark collar of his coat through a split in the stone. He listened. Behind her, Oskel’s people lay close enough that she could hear one man’s breath catch in his nose.

The rider went to the edge and looked down the cutting.

It was empty. He had a good view of the road, the far enclosure, and the patch of bare earth where Riarhin had first meant to put the bowmen.

He returned to his horse.

The others passed him. They were allowed to go. Sava’s riders would watch where they went and close the far track after them if they could. If they could not, the advance party would come back into the fight. Riarhin had no control over that now.

The first wagon stopped to fit its drag.

The driver handed the reins to the man beside him and climbed down stiffly. He put one hand on his lower back before bending to the wheel. Riarhin had watched men do that at a hundred yards and inns. It was strange to be waiting for this one to finish before people began to die.

The rear wagon appeared below the rise.

Rekedim touched her boot.

She raised her hand and lowered it.

Oskel’s people stood across the entrance behind the last wagon. They carried a length of rope between two light hurdles, enough to make the rear riders check their horses. At the same time Dareth’s archers rose along the bank.

“Leave the reins,” Rekedim called in Ykrul. “Hands where they can be seen. You can walk out.”

A guard beside the second wagon drew his bow.

Riarhin shot him before he could lift it clear of the wagon cover. Dareth’s people loosed at the riders who turned towards them. The sound was smaller than Riarhin expected: strings, hooves on stone, one man shouting a word that meant nothing by itself.

The lead team surged forward.

The driver fell away from the wheel. His passenger pulled on both reins, making the animals rear against the drag. The wagon slewed across the road. A barrel struck the inside of the cover and burst its lashings.

Elyas came out from beside the enclosure with three of Oskel’s men. He took the nearer horse’s bridle and was pulled almost off his feet. Riarhin wanted to shout at him to let go. He had his sword sheathed. The animal would break his arm before it understood his courage.

The man with the crushed ear caught the far rein, turned the second horse’s head, and gave Elyas an order she could not hear. Elyas moved with him. The team’s pull shifted. For an instant both men vanished behind the animals; then the wagon stopped.

Someone beneath Riarhin’s wall screamed.

She moved to see.

A wagon guard had climbed through the old drain. He came up inside Dareth’s position with his shield ahead of him. The first woman he reached fell backwards. Another drew her knife, too close to use the bow.

Riarhin left hers against the stone and drew the straight sword from her hip.

The guard saw her coming. He turned his shield to meet the blade, leaving the woman behind him room to crawl clear. Riarhin struck the shield rim, stepped in while it was high, and thrust beneath his arm.

He drove forward against her anyway. His weight carried them both into the broken wall. Stone scraped her cheek. She kept her feet and pulled the sword free as he folded onto one knee.

The wounded archer had her hands over her throat.

Riarhin knelt, saw what the hands were trying to hold, and pressed them down harder. The woman looked at her with furious concentration. She was trying to stay.

“Here,” Riarhin called.

Someone took her place. She could not see who. She picked up the bow and moved towards the next gap in the wall.

The rear escort had not broken.

They had drawn the last wagon across the road and sheltered behind it, with two men beneath the bed shooting uphill. Oskel’s first line could not reach them without passing through the space between the rear wheel and the bank.

He moved the line back.

Riarhin saw Elyas look towards him, then towards Rekedim, who was trying to reach the second wagon. From the centre it must have looked like retreat. She hoped the young man would remember the agreement.

He did. He held where he was, keeping the drivers away from the weapons on the ground.

Oskel moved his men again. Dareth shifted three archers to the other side of the broken wall. The wagon guards pulled farther beneath the bed to avoid their arrows, leaving the wheel unprotected.

A spear went through the spokes and pinned a shield against the axle. Oskel’s people came down the bank in two short rushes.

Riarhin could no longer see the guards. She could hear them.

Beyond the far enclosure, horses were coming back.

Sava had caught some of the advance party. Not all. Two riders reached the cutting together, one with a spear levelled at Elyas’s back.

Riarhin drew. The rider passed behind the lead wagon just as she released. The arrow struck wood.

Elyas turned at the noise. He got his shield up late. The spear struck its rim and threw him against the wagon. One of the Ykrul beside him caught the shaft with both hands, hung on, and was dragged several paces before the rider let it go.

The second rider turned away from the packed road and took the steep track.

Sava’s people appeared above him. He stopped, looked once behind, then dropped his weapon.

Riarhin put another arrow on the string. Her hand was slippery. Blood ran from her cheek along her jaw and dripped onto the bow grip.

Down by the second wagon, Rekedim was pulling a leather case away from a brazier.

The man holding it would not let go.

Riarhin had no shot. A horse stood between them and Elyas was moving into the same narrow space. Rekedim struck the man’s wrist with the short iron he carried for wagon work. The case fell. The man reached into his coat.

Elyas hit him with the edge of his shield.

The man went down hard. Rekedim kicked the brazier over and dragged the case away from the coals. Then he knelt in the road, using his bare hand to put out a glowing corner.

Riarhin turned back towards the rear wagon.

The noise there had stopped.

The last man to surrender did it because his brother asked him.

Oskel stood beyond the wheel with blood on his sleeve. The man beneath it had a short spear and a wound across his forehead. He kept stabbing at anyone who came close.

Oskel called a name.

The man stopped. Riarhin could not hear what they said next. Their voices were low; neither offered his weapon. At last the spear slid out onto the road.

Oskel stepped aside to let his brother crawl free.

They did not touch. The younger man sat with his back against the wheel while Oskel went to count his people.

Rekedim opened the case on a wagon bed after they had moved the coals away.

Inside were three books, bundles of warrants, and a packet wrapped in oiled silk. The first book had been burned along one edge. Several leaves came away together when he tried to open it.

Alric arrived carrying the cleanest cloth he had been able to find. He spread it over the wagon bed and took the damaged leaves one at a time.

“Don’t pull,” he said.

Rekedim let go.

Riarhin stood beside them, watching the captives and the bank and the road beyond the last wagon. A horse lay in the ditch trying to rise. Someone went to it with a knife. She looked back at the books.

“Enough?” she asked.

Rekedim opened the unburned volume. Names, marks, amounts, due dates. He found the current payments and laid Keld’s account alongside them.

“Enough to start.”

“And the coin?”

Oskel had opened one of the boxes. Silver lay in tight rows beneath a layer of salt bags. He called Rekedim over. Several of his riders followed without being asked.

“We pay the grain sellers first,” Rekedim told them. “A rider can wait for silver; his children can’t wait for bread.”

One of the men began to object. Oskel pointed towards the five freight wagons. Whatever he said after that was lost beneath a shout for water.

Riarhin went to help carry it.

Chapter Thirty-Six: The Names Beside the Money

Five of their riders were dead by evening.

Sava made the count beside the water, touching each knot on a cord she had taken from her saddle. She left one space open for the archer whose throat Riarhin had tried to close. The woman was still breathing. Lyra sat beside her, pressing a folded pad beneath her jaw.

There were others waiting. A broken leg, two deep cuts, an arrow through the soft part of an arm. A man with no visible injury shook so badly that he could not hold a cup.

Riarhin brought more water and sat where Lyra could see her.

“Can I hold that?”

“No.”

She waited.

“Her head. Don’t move the cloth.”

Riarhin knelt behind the woman and supported her skull between her hands. The woman’s eyes turned towards her, then away. Blood had dried beneath her ear.

Lyra adjusted the pad, cleared the mouth, waited for a breath before moving anything. Then she set two fingers beside the cut and spoke the short phrase Riarhin had heard at the shelter. She kept to it this time, word for word. A torn edge drew closed beneath her touch. The bleeding slowed. It did not stop.

“That’s all,” Lyra said.

Riarhin did not move.

“Alric will show you how to keep her straight.”

“You should sit.”

“There are more.”

She stood too quickly and caught the water barrel. Riarhin rose halfway, still holding the woman’s head. Lyra waited for her balance to return and went to the man with the arrow in his arm.

Alric brought rolled blankets. He showed Riarhin how to replace her hands with them. His own hands were steady.

“She taught me,” he said.

Riarhin stayed until the archer’s breathing had settled. Then she went to find out what they had taken.

The surviving escort sat in separate groups.

Sava had insisted on moving them after the first count. Riarhin had thought she was dividing kin to prevent a rush at the guards. Instead she put Oskel’s brother with two men who had no kin among the attackers and moved the oldest captive into a group of young riders whose names he knew.

“They were easier to watch in one place,” Elyas said.

Sava looked at the guards he had posted. Then she moved one of them three paces and pointed to the path between the groups.

“They can ask to leave without asking the man who would forbid it.”

“Will he let them?”

“He can’t reach them.”

She went back to her count.

Riarhin watched a young captive lift his head. He looked first at the older man, then at the open strip Sava had left between himself and Elyas. After a while he called out a name from the pay roll.

The exchange could still be reached before the promised payments fell due, but only if they left at once.

They could not take the wounded. They could not leave the silver with six guards and fifty miles of rumour gathering around it. Oskel wanted to move the whole company east at dawn. Sava wanted shelter before moving anyone who could not sit a horse.

Rekedim listened, then asked how many of the wagon drivers could write.

Two.

He brought them to the second wagon, where Alric had weighted the damaged pages with clean stones. Each driver identified the marks on his load. Neither knew why a clerk had tried to burn the books. Both knew where they had been promised payment.

“Then you carry the news,” Rekedim said.

“With what escort?”

“Your own people, if they agree. Two of ours with each. A copy of what we have, an offer of arrears, and the names of the witnesses. The wagons will follow.”

“They will say we were bought.”

“You were. You are owed your freight.”

The older driver laughed once, without pleasure.

Elyas drew up the offer. Rekedim corrected the terms. Oskel and Dareth put their marks beside his seal, each for their own riders. Sava would not mark it until provision for the wounded had been copied onto both sheets.

The couriers left in different directions with sums small enough to lose and large enough to prove that the money existed.

Only after they were gone did Riarhin let herself sit down.

They found Orvain’s name after midnight.

Not in the pay book. In a list of advances charged to Merrow’s undertaking, written in another hand and countersigned by the man who had tried to burn the case.

Elyas read the entry three times. There was money for passage, treatment of the shoulder, clothing, and maintenance at the lower exchange. A final sum remained due upon his appearance before representatives of the houses.

“As a witness?” Alric asked.

“To what?” Elyas said.

No one answered immediately.

Riarhin took the sheet. Beneath the amounts stood a phrase in Acasi describing the completion of a service. It was the kind of phrase that could be made to mean something innocent if the rest of the page disappeared.

She read it aloud in Vhasian.

Elyas looked towards the prisoners.

The clerk sat with his hands wrapped in cloth. One wrist had begun to swell where Rekedim struck it. He had told them his name was Pell. He had told them very little else.

Riarhin crouched beside him with the sheet.

“Who was meant to hear him?”

Pell looked away.

She laid Toothpick across her knee. The small blade caught the lantern light. His gaze came back to it.

“Riarhin,” Elyas said.

“I haven’t touched him.”

“Put it away.”

She looked up. The young man’s face was pale beneath the dust. He had seen the same sheet. He knew whose blood had made the first payment necessary.

“Ask him with me here,” he said.

She put the knife away.

Elyas sat on the ground, making Pell look down to answer him.

“Orvain died at Pont-de-Tithe. His warrant is with our papers. What I don’t know is who intended to hear him at the exchange, or whether those people will let us ask without a fight.”

Pell swallowed.

“If I give you the yard—”

“I can’t promise you freedom.”

“Then what can you promise?”

“That I will write what you told us, and what it prevented, beside what you tried to burn.”

It took a long time. Riarhin sat through it without reaching for the knife again.

At last Pell named a house beside the lower exchange and described its courtyard. He said Kargath had meant to gather the houses’ agents there after the payment. Orvain was to have told them Braedyn had understood the cause and refused it. His death had cancelled the appearance, not the undertaking. Pell had been ordered to leave the final payment open until a decision was made about its use.

“Would they have believed him?” Elyas asked.

Pell flexed his injured fingers.

“They had paid to hear him.”

Elyas looked at the unsigned amount. Riarhin remembered Orvain beside the wheel housing, trying to breathe. This was something they might have asked him. There would be others.

By morning the archer was dead.

Lyra had gone to sleep sitting against the barrel. Alric had taken her place, and it was he who came to tell them. Sava closed the open space in her cord and began another for the living who could not ride.

Riarhin found Lyra awake, staring at the rolled blankets.

“She was breathing when I left her.”

“I know.”

“I checked.”

“I know.”

Lyra looked at her then, with such exhaustion that Riarhin almost wished for the anger.

“You don’t have to keep saying it.”

“No.”

She sat down beside her. They stayed there until someone came for the water barrel.

Chapter Thirty-Seven: The Lower Exchange

The exchange keeper would not admit the money until someone accepted responsibility for it.

They had brought the first two wagons slowly, with the wounded lying on sacks in the freight wagon behind. Every rut hurt someone. Sava rode beside the rear wheel, counting the intervals between stops. By the time they reached the gate, Riarhin wanted to knock the keeper down and put the people under a roof.

He stood in the entrance with a key on a cord around his neck. Behind him were storehouses, a stable, and the courtyard Pell had described. No banner flew above any of them.

“Whose goods?” he asked.

“Disputed,” Rekedim said.

“Whose risk?”

“Ours.”

“Who is ours?”

Rekedim named the three commanders. Elyas added his own name and authority. The keeper asked whether the stores he held for other people could be seized to make up any shortfall. They said no. He made them put it in writing.

Riarhin stayed in the saddle until the gate opened because she did not trust herself to stand beside him.

As soon as Sava began moving the wounded, he went to fetch clean bedding from his own house.

The first pay claim came from a woman with two empty grain sacks.

She had sold flour to six riders three weeks before, accepting their commander’s mark because Kargath’s collectors said it would be honoured at the exchange. The mark was in the undamaged book. The amount was correct. She wanted flour in return; money would cost her another journey.

Rekedim sent her to the grain store with a witnessed tally.

After that there was a line.

Alric sat beside the books. Elyas counted coin. Oskel stood close enough to hear every disputed claim and far enough away not to make the claimants speak to him instead. Dareth put riders on both roads. Sava arranged the injured in the keeper’s long room and sent everyone who was able to stand to fetch water before they could discover a reason to be elsewhere.

Riarhin went to the house Pell had named.

There was no assembly waiting to be interrupted. The courtyard held three empty wagons and a boy scraping old receiving marks from their sides. A servant was washing bowls beneath the window. When Riarhin asked for the keeper of the house, the woman called him without taking her hands out of the water.

He was a small man in a carefully brushed coat. He recognised Elyas’s seal and told her there had been no meeting of the kind she described.

“Not yet,” she said.

“Not at all.”

“Then the rooms weren’t reserved?”

“For a commercial gathering. The purpose is not mine to inquire into.”

“Who paid?”

He folded his hands.

She went back for Pell and two witnesses.

The keeper recognised Pell. He regretted it immediately; Riarhin saw the greeting begin and stop.

Pell named the room, the deposit, the number of places required at the table. The keeper admitted a booking and denied knowledge of the proposed speaker. They brought out the house book. Someone had scraped the cancelled entry thin enough to show light through the paper.

Orvain’s name remained in Pell’s account.

“That man is dead,” the keeper said.

“Yes,” Elyas replied.

“Then he cannot tell you why he was coming.”

Riarhin stood beside the door. Across the long Acasian road it had become easy to think of Orvain’s death as a finished piece of work. Now a stranger could put his absence between them and a question.

Elyas asked for the entry before the scraping. The keeper said no copy had been made.

Lyra moved closer to the table.

“Who returned the deposit?”

“The house.”

“To whom?”

He did not answer promptly enough.

“There must be a receipt,” she said. “Unless you’re saying you paid money to someone you didn’t identify.”

They found the receipt in another book. It named one of Merrow’s agents and carried a mark Pell recognised. Alric copied it. Elyas made the keeper witness the copy, including the date of the cancellation.

The man complained that they were treating him as an accomplice. Lyra told him they were recording a transaction he had acknowledged. Her voice was calm, exact, and without any interest in whether he understood the distinction.

Outside, Riarhin said, “That was well done.”

Lyra put the copied receipt into its wrapper.

“He thought we’d accept the missing witness as the end of it.”

“We nearly did.”

“You killed him. I didn’t.”

She stopped with one fold unfinished.

“I know he might have lied,” she said. “You don’t need to tell me that either.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

Lyra finished the fold and went ahead to the exchange.

The people in the pay line had begun to argue by the time they returned.

A rider claimed wages for a dead brother. The mark beside the name was genuine. So was the previous payment, which had been made to a man who said he had carried the money home. The brother said it had never arrived.

Rekedim was away inspecting the salt. Elyas asked whether the carrier could be found. No one knew. The rider had a child waiting with his horse and had come too far to leave without food.

Lyra found the entry in the book.

“It’s been paid.”

“Not to us.”

“Then your claim is against the man who took it.”

“He rode away with the others.”

“Paying it again won’t make him bring it back.”

Riarhin looked at the child. He had been holding the horse’s loose rein so long that his fingers were stiff around it.

“Give them enough grain to reach home,” she said.

Lyra looked up.

“From which account?”

“Mine.”

“Then say that. Don’t enter it as wages.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

Alric began a separate line. The rider watched them all, uncertain whether he had been refused or helped. Riarhin took him to the grain store herself.

When she came back, Lyra had checked three more claims and found a mistake in one that would have cost a woman nearly half her due. She was not refusing to be useful. That would have been easier to object to.

Elyas found Riarhin beside the well that evening.

“Would Orvain have told us?” he asked.

“Some of it. If he thought it would serve him.”

“You didn’t believe he’d come back with us.”

“No.”

“He might have. If we’d waited.”

She watched the bucket turn slowly at the end of its rope.

“He might have.”

Elyas sat on the curb. Neither of them needed the water now, but they stayed beside it.

“I don’t want to do that with Pell,” he said.

“I know.”

“Even if he tries to make it difficult.”

Riarhin looked at him.

“Then we’ll make arrangements for difficult.”

The next morning she checked the clerk’s travelling guard herself: enough people to manage a prisoner without a lone frightened man having to choose between killing him and letting him go. It would cost more. Elyas approved the expense.

Chapter Thirty-Eight: The Open Side

Kargath sent for the wagons on the second morning.

The messenger arrived with six riders and an order bearing a seal the exchange keeper recognised. He was prepared to accept delivery late, the messenger said, if the delay were explained and the responsible parties made available.

Rekedim asked which responsible parties he meant.

The man named Elyas. Then Rekedim. He called Riarhin the woman who travelled with them.

She stood beside the gate and let him.

“The goods are being disbursed against witnessed claims,” Rekedim said. “Your commander may send his account.”

The messenger stared at him. Then he looked beyond the gate.

Oskel’s brother was carrying a grain sack towards a tethered horse. Another former captive held it open while he secured the mouth. Neither stopped working.

“They were under his command.”

“They were owed wages.”

“They have not been released.”

Rekedim called the two men over and asked them in front of the messenger whether they intended to return to Kargath’s service.

The first said no. The second asked whether the remaining arrears would be honoured if he did.

The messenger said he could not bargain about individual cases.

The man went back to the grain sack.

Some did return.

Riarhin counted seven leaving before the end of the day. They took their wages and the weapons that belonged to them, after Elyas objected and Oskel made him read the terms they had offered.

“We cannot stop them joining again?”

“We can stop paying them to leave a second time,” Rekedim said.

“They’ll come back with him.”

“Perhaps.”

That evening two more arrived from Kargath’s camp. They had heard seven different accounts of what happened at the cutting. One expected the exchange to be burning. The other expected to find the prisoners hanged outside it.

Sava sent them to fetch water.

By the fifth day, no one could keep an exact count of whose men they had been before they arrived. Alric kept the pay book exact instead.

The silver did not meet every claim.

Some of the sealed bags contained clipped coin. Two of the boxes held notes secured against deliveries the issuing houses were already denying. Salt made up much of the value, and salt could not be divided among hungry people until someone agreed to exchange it for food.

Rekedim slept in the room with the accounts. Riarhin found him there before dawn, sitting upright on a stool with a folded paper in his hand.

“You’ll fall off.”

“I was reading.”

“Your eyes were closed.”

“I know what it says.”

She put a bowl beside him. Sava had sent food with instructions to watch him eat it.

He looked at the bowl, then at the page.

“There’s a house east of here that will take salt against grain. But they want the escort to remain through harvest.”

“How many?”

“Enough that the store can open without the nearest riders taking what comes out.”

“Ask Oskel.”

“He has already offered. He wants the agreement to include the grazing his mother’s people lost.”

“Does the grain house own it?”

“No.”

Riarhin sat on the edge of the bed. He ate while she read the proposed terms. They were not difficult words. It was the number of people missing from the room that made the paper heavy.

“You can’t settle it here,” she said.

“No.”

“Will you go?”

He set the empty bowl down.

“I think I’ll have to.”

Serekh’s hearing reached them in two copies, carried by different people.

Amru had kept him alive. He had also removed his authority to command scouts and placed his herd-share in trust for the dependants of the men killed under his ring. A claim for Gyllan remained open, to be heard with Lyra present or represented. The account named the apprentices’ disobedience without erasing their commander’s responsibility. It recorded Alric’s testimony that Serekh had released him and dropped the knife.

Lyra read both copies.

“They have a use for everything,” she said.

Riarhin waited beside the table.

“His animals. His knowledge. Even the men who killed Gyllan have people who will be paid because they died.”

“Their children didn’t kill him.”

“I know.”

The reply came quickly enough that Riarhin stopped preparing the rest of her answer.

Lyra put the copies together, aligning the edges.

“I know that. I keep knowing all of it. It doesn’t make this right.”

“No.”

“Then stop telling me the parts that work.”

Riarhin drew out the opposite stool. Lyra did not invite her to sit, but did not leave when she did.

After a while she asked Riarhin to witness a letter refusing payment in Gyllan’s name until she had spoken at the hearing. Riarhin signed where Alric showed her.

The clerk Pell’s papers were kept apart from the pay books.

They included valuations, letters of recommendation, lists of goods held as security, and a catalogue of books acquired from a house whose owner had died before paying his contribution. Alric went through them in the evenings when the claims stopped. Riarhin helped because he had begun making mistakes from exhaustion and would not leave a column unfinished.

Near the bottom of a page she found a name she knew.

Kalrantha.

The catalogue described a copy of an account of the Himadri temple, with two inserted leaves in another hand. There was no amulet in the inventory. There was a drawing of green glass in a brass setting, repeated on the valuation sheet, and a dispute about whether the inserted leaves were old enough to justify the price.

She read the description twice.

“What is it?” Alric asked.

“A book I haven’t read.”

He looked at her, perhaps expecting more. She turned to the next page.

The volume had not been delivered. A dealer had offered it as part of a larger security and withdrawn it after receiving a better inquiry from an Oshiiran correspondent. Pell had copied the correspondent’s name and the forwarding house on the southern coast, intending to pursue the valuation later.

Riarhin knew one northern account of the amulet. The same paragraph had appeared in three books with different titles. She could have recited all three. None named the author of the account they repeated.

This description did.

She had heard the same paragraph given back to a man in a fuller’s yard, who had said he could recite it in three languages and had paid for it in four. He had wanted the thing underneath it. He had not got it, and he had been a man who got what he set out to get: a lease, a lineage, an army’s worth of unpaid riders.

It proved nothing. A dealer’s valuation was not knowledge. But the shortest true account she could give of Kalrantha was that a careful man had spent years and money on it, come away with a rumour, and minded.

She took a clean sheet.

“Is it part of Merrow’s business?” Alric asked.

“Only as something he might have accepted for money.”

“Then I can leave it?”

“Yes.”

She made the copy herself.

Kargath’s next messenger came on foot.

His horse had been kept at a well against a debt. He was angry enough to tell them this before remembering whom he represented. He carried no demand for the wagons. He asked for a meeting beyond the exchange, without Elyas’s seal or the keeper’s protection.

Rekedim asked what would be discussed.

“The passage north.”

“Whose?”

“All who remain with him.”

Oskel took the message and read it twice. Then he laid a cord across the table, bent it around the inkpot, and held its ends apart.

“He still has men beyond us,” Elyas said.

Oskel moved one end of the cord farther away.

“That is where he wants them to be.”

Riarhin looked at the space enclosed by the curve. She could see why Kargath had sent a man whose horse had been seized. The message had arrived wearing the evidence of his need.

“He wants us to leave a way out,” she said.

“For his people,” Rekedim answered.

“And him?”

“That’s the meeting.”

Chapter Thirty-Nine: Six Moves

They met at a dry well half a day’s ride from the exchange.

Riarhin went with Rekedim, Dareth, Oskel and Lyra. Sava had found someone to sit with the wounded until Lyra returned. The girl had asked to hear the terms herself, and no one had found a reason she should not. Elyas stayed with the accounts and Pell. She had expected an argument about that. He had looked at the room full of claimants and the clerk waiting to have his statement witnessed, and said only that he wanted the terms brought back before anyone pledged his name.

Kargath had eleven people with him.

Riarhin counted them as they came down the northern slope. Then she counted the horses. Two spare animals, both laden. No cart. Whatever he meant to take away from this was small enough to travel fast.

He dismounted beside the well and gave his reins to the young officer with the red cloth. The cane came down on dry earth with a flat sound.

Rekedim waited until everyone had settled before greeting him.

Kargath replied in Ykrul, using the name Many-Scars. He glanced at Riarhin and returned his attention to Rekedim.

“You have learned to pay people with their own money.”

“Most wages are like that.”

“You have also learned what happens when it ends.”

“Yes.”

Kargath looked at Oskel.

“Your mother’s house?”

“Still owes you what it owes.”

“And you stand there.”

Oskel put his hand on the well curb. “I can stand here and remember.”

The young officer shifted behind Kargath. One of the older riders touched his elbow, stopping the movement before it became a step.

Riarhin watched their positions. The older rider stood where she could shield the officer and still reach her own horse. The officer had placed himself where Kargath could not withdraw without passing him. It could have been devotion. It was also an obstruction. Kargath looked back once, and the young man moved.

The terms took longer than the ride.

No pursuit of households withdrawing from Kargath’s service. Passage to the northern wells with enough provisions to reach them. No seizure of private herds against debts incurred in his name. Return of the records needed to distinguish private property from stores bought for the undertaking.

Rekedim agreed to nothing on behalf of the gathering. He named the people whose signatures he could obtain and those whose consent would still be required. When Kargath tried to include an unpaid garrison east of the river, Oskel refused to promise their road would remain open. No one present held it.

Riarhin sat on a stone and listened to men who had been prepared to kill one another define what each was allowed to leave behind.

At last Kargath asked, “And my passage?”

“You come to the exchange,” Rekedim said. “You answer the claims made there. The attack on Braedyn goes east with its witnesses.”

“You cannot promise what the eastern court will do.”

“No.”

“Yet you ask me to accept it.”

“Yes.”

Kargath rested both hands on the cane. The younger officer looked towards the northern slope. Riarhin followed his gaze. There was room to ride. Dareth’s people were far enough away that some of them could escape if they started together.

Kargath could see it too.

“What if I go?”

“We follow,” Dareth said.

“How many?”

“As many as are needed.”

The old rider beside the officer looked down at her own hands. Kargath turned the cane once against the earth.

He had asked the question for all of them. Riarhin could see the answer being understood separately, at different speeds.

He spoke to her in Vhasian.

“You carried my father’s knife at the ferry.”

Riarhin stood up.

“I still do.”

“May I see it?”

“No.”

A faint movement touched his mouth. It might have been amusement. He took a wrapped bundle from the younger officer and laid it on the well curb.

Three knives lay inside. They were not identical, and the differences were not wear. The weight sat in a different part of each blade. Each grip had been shaped around a different way of holding it, and one had been left deliberately short for a man who would always come in closer than he needed to. What they had in common was the turn of the guard, a narrow seam along the grip, and the fact that no one of them had been given better work than the other two.

“My brothers,” he said.

She knew the blades. One had been broken near the point and reground. Another had lost the plate at the end of its grip. They had passed through other hands since she last saw them. She did not ask whose.

Kargath took a fourth knife from inside his coat.

The family was in it: the same turn of guard, the same narrow seam. Everything else disagreed with the three on the cloth. Riarhin had handled enough of a good smith’s work to read a man out of it, and there was no man in this one. The weight sat where weight ought to sit. The grip fitted a hand in general and nobody’s in particular. It had been given exactly the work the other three had been given and none of their quarrel, and its length was not a length she could have predicted from theirs.

Its sheath had been repaired at the mouth with pale thread.

“The last,” he said.

Riarhin looked at his narrow hand around it.

“You were kept away.”

“Yes.”

She thought of all the bodies she remembered and all the names she had never learned. A concealed son did not require a miracle. Only one child absent from the room, and a knife made for a hand nobody had measured.

“What do you want?”

“An end to your pursuit.”

“Come with us.”

“That gives it to someone else.”

He looked towards Rekedim, then at the people standing behind himself.

“One person remains to be accounted for. I will answer her.”

Rekedim said, “That does not settle the claims.”

“No. The stores settle what they can. The records settle what can be proved. My people take the road under the terms you have offered. I remain.”

Dareth looked at Riarhin. There was no surprise in her face. That made Riarhin angry, briefly, though she could not have said at whom.

Kargath drew the knife an inch from its sheath.

“If I kill her, my passage is open. If she kills me, you have no one left to pursue for my orders.”

“You don’t get to pardon anyone else,” Riarhin said.

“I haven’t asked to.”

“You have men who carried them out.”

“Then name what they chose.”

The words reached a place she had been trying not to touch since the shelter. Serekh had given an order and failed to make it hold. Kargath had given orders that worked. Neither arrangement brought Gyllan back.

She turned to Rekedim.

“Can his people leave if he stays?”

“On the terms we’ve recorded.”

“And if I lose?”

He took longer over that.

“I can undertake not to pursue him. I can’t bind the houses or the duke.”

Kargath inclined his head. “That will do.”

The young officer began to speak. Kargath stopped him with an open hand.

Riarhin unfastened her cloak.

Oskel marked the ground with the end of his spear.

Not a ring. Two short lines, offset enough that neither fighter would have to stand in the other’s shadow. Dareth moved a horse that blocked the space beyond the nearer line. The older rider from Kargath’s party checked the ground for loose stone and removed one piece the size of an egg.

No one appeared to regard these preparations as ceremonial. Riarhin was glad of that.

Her hand went to Remorse.

Kargath was taller. The sword would keep him where she could see his hands and give her room to finish it if he came forward. She had begun to draw it when he laid the cane across the bundle and took the last knife from its sheath.

She stopped.

He watched her. There was no request in his face.

She pushed Remorse home and drew Toothpick.

For a moment he looked at nothing but the blade. Then he gave a small, sad nod.

Dareth asked whether both accepted the terms.

They did.

“Six moves,” Riarhin said.

Kargath raised his eyes to hers. At the board, a player offered the reforge could withdraw before the first of them. It was a courtesy she had heard him grant men who had lost less than he had.

The knife remained in his hand.

“You gave my father none.”

The thin line on her cheek had been his father’s work. There had been other cuts, and time between them. Tulgoth had used this knife carefully. She remembered the way he wiped it before beginning again, and the moment much later when she had understood that the sound from his bed was sleep.

He had deserved none. She could have said that. She could have told Kargath he was already a better man.

She said nothing.

Kargath came forward.

Her first move was a cut at his knife hand.

He drew it back and turned the blade, denying her the wrist. She had expected him to reach after the retreating edge. He kept his elbow in and gave her nothing.

On the second she pressed his blade outward and went for the opening beneath it. His free hand caught her sleeve. He was stronger than the cane had suggested. She turned with the pull before he could bring her onto his point.

The third was hers to break the hold. She cut the cloth he gripped and took her arm out of it, leaving him with a strip of sleeve. He let it fall at once.

There was room to leave him there. He had his feet beneath him again. She heard Lyra draw breath beyond the marked ground.

On the fourth Riarhin went inside his guard.

He met her instead of yielding. His knife opened her side above the belt; she felt the hot line before she felt the pain. She caught his wrist against her ribs, too late to prevent the cut, soon enough to keep the point from turning in. A little deeper would have ended it. Her body could mend nothing if she was dead.

The fifth move took his balance. She hooked her foot behind his heel and drove her shoulder against him. He pulled free of the hook, but his weight had gone back and his knife hand was still trapped.

On the sixth she put Toothpick beneath his breastbone.

His mouth opened. His left hand closed on her shoulder, neither striking nor holding her away.

She supported him long enough to free the knife. The Last Blade fell from his hand. He sank against her arm and then onto the ground.

She knelt with him until the hand came loose from her shoulder.

The young officer came forward.

Riarhin stepped back and let him kneel. He put a hand beneath Kargath’s head, lifted it slightly, then lowered it because there was nowhere better to put it.

Dareth took Toothpick from Riarhin when she offered the hilt. Rekedim looked at the cut above her belt.

“Shallow,” she said.

He checked anyway.

Behind them the older rider was loosening the grey’s saddle. Her face had not changed. When the animal turned to bite, she struck its nose with the flat of her hand and began crying so quietly that Riarhin heard it only because no one else was speaking.

They buried him above the dry well.

Oskel fetched tools from the exchange. The waiting took most of the afternoon. Riarhin sat in the shade with a cloth pressed to her side and watched the people who had followed Kargath prepare to leave him.

The young officer brought the three brothers’ knives to her.

“He said these were to go with him.”

“They will.”

“And that one?”

The Last Blade lay beside her, still in the sheath with its pale repair.

She rested her hand on it.

“This stays.”

He looked at her for a long time, then went back to the horses.

She placed the three knives beside the body herself. No one asked her to speak. When the earth was in, the older rider laid the cane across the grave, reconsidered, and took it up again. There was someone among them who could use it.

Riarhin understood that well enough to have to turn away.

Lyra came to stand beside her when the others went for the horses.

“You killed him in six moves.”

Riarhin looked at the disturbed earth.

Why so many?

She pressed the cloth more firmly to her side. When Lyra reached to inspect it, she let her.

Chapter Forty: What Remained

Lyra dressed the cut after they returned to the exchange.

Riarhin sat on the edge of a bench in the exchange keeper’s room. The girl cleaned the wound, checked its depth, and put the cloths aside. She used no magic. The needle hurt in a simple, manageable way.

“You could have refused,” Lyra said at last.

“Yes.”

Lyra drew the thread through. Riarhin watched her face, the concentration at the corners of her mouth.

“He chose who would kill him.”

“He chose to fight.”

“Gyllan didn’t choose you to decide what his death would pay for.”

Riarhin looked down at the girl’s hands.

“I didn’t make that bargain.”

“You took Serekh’s road. You used his information. You got what you needed. And now this one is dead because those terms suited you.”

She wanted to say Kargath’s terms had saved the people waiting behind him. She could hear the answer before she spoke: stop telling me the parts that work.

“I don’t know how to make it right,” she said.

Lyra tied off the thread.

“I know.”

She said it without cruelty. Riarhin found she had preferred being accused.

Then Lyra put the needle away and sat for a moment with her hands in her lap, looking at what Riarhin had set down on the end of the bench when she came in.

Riarhin watched her look at it. She watched her arrive somewhere. She watched her decide not to come back with it.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

It was the first thing Lyra had ever declined to tell her. She did it the way she did everything, carefully and without apology, and then she folded the used cloths and carried them out.

Riarhin sat with her shirt open and tried to establish what she had just been let off.

The news did not end the work at the exchange.

Kargath’s riders arrived singly or in small parties, sometimes with a seal, sometimes with nothing but a name they expected to be honoured. Men who had denied his authority brought claims for things they had sold him. One house’s agent insisted its payment had been intended for road repairs and demanded the unspent balance back.

Elyas put the agent’s signed undertaking beside the list of purchased spearheads and asked him to identify the road.

Riarhin watched the man stop smiling. It was a small pleasure. She allowed herself to have it.

Among the letters that came up from the river factors was one addressed to Gyllan and forwarded twice.

Elyas opened it because there was nobody else to open it. It was from the master of a yard beyond the last maintained crossing, returning one of the old knight’s letters of introduction with regrets. He had taken the man on in Verrus and kept him four weeks. Then his own contract had failed, and the man had gone on, with a wife and a child, and the master did not know where, and thought the gentleman who had written would wish to know that the fault had not been the man’s.

Elyas asked Alric whether the name was anywhere in their papers.

It was not. Gyllan had written those letters at the bridge, from the men’s own account of themselves, and the names had gone with him.

He put the sheet in with the evidence, where it did not belong, because there was nowhere else to put it. Riarhin watched him do it. He did not make it into anything. He did not say that it was what the bridge had cost, or that he would do it again, or that he had learned something by it. He tied the bundle and went back to the claims, and the man was simply gone, and Elyas carried that out of the room with him and did not put it down.

Pell gave a second statement, with witnesses of his own choosing. They attached the account of Orvain’s death to the papers recovered at the Pont-de-Tithe mill. Elyas required both to travel with the evidence against Merrow. Alric prepared copies for the river factors and a separate packet that Elyas would carry himself.

No one could make Merrow answer from this distance. They could make it harder for him to pretend the papers had never existed.

Rekedim announced his decision while they were repacking the books.

“I won’t be going east.”

Elyas stopped tying a bundle.

“For how long?”

“I don’t know.”

Riarhin put the volume she held into the chest. She had expected the decision since the morning of the grain agreement. Hearing it still made the room feel altered.

“The duke sent you with me,” Elyas said.

“Yes.”

“He’ll want you back.”

“I’ll write to him.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

Rekedim sat down on the closed chest.

“I know.”

Outside, someone called for a weight to be checked. He waited until the voice had moved away before continuing.

“Oskel can keep the eastern store open. Dareth can take the couriers through. Sava has the people who can’t leave yet. They need accounts the river factors will accept, and the factors need someone who knows when a mark is a promise and when it’s a warning. If I go, we leave them with a little money and a great many ways to misunderstand one another.”

“You can’t stay because everyone needs something.”

“No.”

“Then why?”

Rekedim looked down at his hands.

“Because I want to see what they make of it.”

Riarhin had not heard him say that before. Wanting, without immediately setting out the use.

Elyas went back to the bundle. He pulled the cord too hard and broke it. For a moment he stood holding the two ends.

Then he fetched another length.

“Write the letter,” he said. “I’ll carry it.”

Iserin came to the exchange three days later.

She inspected the stores, the wounded, and the accounts in that order. Her niece met her at the gate. They touched foreheads briefly. Afterwards Iserin noticed the empty place where one of Dareth’s riders should have been standing and asked a question Riarhin could not hear.

Dareth answered with a name.

Iserin stood very still. Then she took her niece’s hand and turned it palm upwards, looking at the scar where a bowstring had cut it. She held it for longer than the injury required.

Riarhin went inside before either woman looked towards her.

That evening Iserin sat with Rekedim, Oskel and Sava over a rough plan of the watercourses. Velkan had sent new accounts. Amru had sent a witness for the further hearing of Serekh’s case. There would be another gathering after the herds moved.

No one offered Rekedim Kargath’s place.

They asked whether he could undertake the exchange between the grass camps and the river factors. He asked what authority went with it. The answer was smaller than he wanted and more clearly bounded than Elyas would have been given at an eastern court.

They argued until the lamps had to be refilled.

When Riarhin passed the door later, Rekedim was still there. He had moved his stool into a gap between Sava and Velkan’s messenger. The others had shifted their papers to make room for his.

She asked Pell about the catalogue before Elyas’s escort was ready.

The clerk thought she wanted another valuation.

“The book wasn’t here. I’ve told your scholar.”

“I know. Who described it to you?”

He gave her the dealer’s name. It matched the one on the sheet. She asked how the inserted leaves had been described, what language they were in, whether the correspondent had seen them or only been told they existed.

At the third question he stopped trying to look helpful.

“I copied what I was sent. You’d need to ask the dealer.”

“Is he still at that house?”

“Unless he’s found a creditor he likes better.”

She put the copy away.

Kalrantha. A northern book repeated by northern readers, a description of two leaves no one here had seen, and a correspondent on the far side of the Amaranthine willing to dispute the account. It was not much to set against a road she knew, a room in Tarlington, and an old man who might still be waiting.

She checked the forwarding address again.

Elyas found her in the yard mending the torn seam above her belt.

“I thought you might come as far as the river.”

“I’ll take you to the eastern crossing.”

“And then?”

“South.”

He sat on the well curb.

“Is it something we found?”

“A name in Pell’s catalogue. Kalrantha. There’s an account I want to read, and someone I want to ask about it.”

“Dangerous?”

“Possibly worthless.”

“That isn’t the same answer.”

“No.”

He waited to see whether she would give him the other half of it.

“Kargath looked for it for years,” she said. “He had money and correspondents and he never came away with anything he could use. He didn’t care for that.”

“So you think he was right to want it.”

“I think he was careful about what he wanted.”

“That isn’t the same answer either.”

“No.”

He watched her thread the needle. She had perfect sight and perfectly serviceable hands. The thread still caught on the frayed end because she had refused to cut it shorter.

“Will you send word?”

“If I have somewhere to send it from.”

“Tarlington will still be there.”

She pushed the needle through the cloth.

“What do you want me to tell him?” Elyas asked.

The question was small enough to be answered. She could have said she was occupied, delayed, following something that required haste. She had used all three explanations before.

“Tell him I chose the southern road.”

Elyas waited.

“That’s all?”

She finished the stitch.

“No. Tell him I asked how he was.”

“He’ll know I offered to bring you.”

“Yes.”

Elyas stayed until she had tied off the thread. He did not offer to improve the message.

Chapter Forty-One: The Roads Apart

The escort for Viterra assembled without banners. Elyas had written ahead to ask where Veralyn’s court could now be reached; until an answer found them, they would take the eastern road.

Two of Dareth’s riders would go as far as the crossing. Beyond it, Elyas had hired guards from a carrier whose account they had honoured. Pell travelled in a covered wagon. The witnesses willing to go east rode separately, under the same escort but at liberty. Elyas checked the clerk’s bonds before the packs were loaded and made the guards repeat the arrangements for watering and rest.

Alric had bought a wooden case for the damaged pages. He lined it with cloth and objected whenever anyone put something on top of it. Lyra had Gyllan’s staff secured along her saddle. She had decided to go with Elyas, then return to Amru’s hearing once the testimony was safely delivered.

Riarhin helped her check the strap.

“It will rub there,” she said.

“I’ve wrapped it.”

She had. Riarhin took her hand away.

Rekedim came out carrying two small packets.

One was his letter for Braedyn. The other he gave to Riarhin.

Inside were needles, a little waxed thread, and the narrow strips of leather she used to pack the heels of badly fitting boots.

“I have these.”

“You had them. You gave the last to the woman with the torn saddle.”

She closed the packet.

“You’ll need someone to tell you when to stop reading.”

“Sava has begun.”

“Good.”

Neither moved. Behind him, people were waiting by the storehouse with a disputed tally. One held it towards another at an angle that kept both halves visible. Even their impatience had found a shape that allowed the argument to continue without Rekedim.

He looked back once, then returned his attention to her.

She took his forearm. He covered her hand with his other one and held it there briefly.

“Write,” she said.

“You first.”

He went to Elyas. Riarhin occupied herself with her horse’s near strap while they spoke. When she looked up again, Rekedim had his hand on the young man’s shoulder. Elyas was listening with his head lowered, no longer arguing.

Then the hand fell away. The riders began to move.

Rekedim stayed beside the gate as they passed. Before Riarhin reached the turn, someone brought him the disputed tally. He took it, looked at the two people holding its halves, and made them stand where each could see the other’s mark.

The eastern crossing of the camp river was a day and a half away, still on the steppe. Beyond it the road bent towards Casta Ferrum; they would not reach the fortress together.

Riarhin scouted the road as she always had. She found a loose shoe before it cost them a horse, a spring that was too fouled to use, and a place where the wagons could camp without putting the guards below the skyline. Ordinary things. No one thanked her for all of them. No one needed to.

At supper Alric asked whether the language in Pell’s catalogue was really old Thepyric. She said parts of it were. He wanted to know which parts. She showed him by the fire, using the back of a spoiled copy sheet.

“Where did you read it?”

“Silkstrand. A client of my mother’s kept the good books with their spines towards his guests.”

He did the arithmetic, arrived at a figure, checked it against the reign he had been made to memorise, and had the sense to write it in the margin instead of saying it aloud.

“The dealer may have made it look old,” he said.

“He may.”

“You’ll go all that way to find out?”

“I’ll go to the dealer first.”

He looked relieved that the road could be divided into something so modest.

Lyra sat beyond him, oiling the head of Gyllan’s staff. She did not join the conversation. Later she passed Riarhin the oil when Riarhin reached for it, and asked for it back when she was finished.

In the morning Riarhin took Pell water.

He was awake beneath the wagon cover, trying to ease the rope away from the wrist Rekedim had injured. She called a guard before touching the knot and held the clerk’s arm while the man replaced the binding with one that left the swollen place clear.

Pell drank. Then he asked how far they had to go.

“A long way.”

“Will they hear me?”

“Elyas will see to it.”

She took the cup and climbed down.

Lyra stood by the next wagon. She had seen the changed binding. For a moment Riarhin wanted to explain that this was what she was trying to do differently, as though the girl owed her recognition of the effort.

Lyra turned to tighten her pack.

Riarhin carried the empty cup back to the water wagon.

They parted above the crossing, where the southern track left the road before it descended to the river.

The water was low enough to show the dark shoulders of stones she had seen covered on the journey out. Beyond it lay Casta Ferrum, the return through Acasia and the long way back to the Belworth. From there Elyas would take Lyra and their evidence to Veralyn’s court.

He dismounted to say goodbye.

He embraced her. Awkwardly at first, careful of the cut. She put one hand between his shoulders and held him close enough that he could stop being careful for a moment.

When he stepped back, she straightened the buckle twisted at his collar.

Lyra waited until he had gone.

For a little while they stood beside the horse without speaking.

“Will you have enough money for the hearing?” Riarhin asked.

“Yes.”

“If you need—”

“Elyas has arranged it.”

Riarhin nodded.

Lyra touched the strap holding Gyllan’s staff, checking something already secure.

“Goodbye, Riarhin.”

“Goodbye, Lyra.”

The girl mounted. Riarhin held the bridle while she settled, then let go when she was ready.

She watched the wagons reach the water.

Elyas went down beside the first, on foot, keeping the wheel out of a rut while the driver watched his horses. Alric stood on the bank with the document case under his arm until someone made room for it on a dry load. Lyra crossed last with the rear guard.

No one looked back from the far side. They were watching the road ahead and the work still needed to get onto it.

Riarhin turned south.

The new knife sat badly in her left boot. She stopped before she had gone a mile, took it out, and repacked the sheath with one of Rekedim’s leather strips. Toothpick needed no adjustment. Remorse lay wrapped beside Silence in the bundle secured behind her saddle.

When she stood, her side pulled. She waited until the pain settled instead of pretending it had not happened.

She had belonged to households before. She could give the number, and the order, and what each had been like on an ordinary evening, which was why she preferred not to begin. They ended, or she left them, and afterwards the word for what they had been changed to fit what was left of them. It was not a grievance. It was a thing she knew about herself, the way she knew the distance to Tarlington.

There was bread in the pack, enough water to reach the next settlement, and a folded sheet with a name, a forwarding house, and an account she had never read.

The forwarding house stood on the southern coast. A long way beyond it the same road ran down into Silkstrand, where the shutters were thin and the courtyards smelled of jasmine laid over drains. She was not going to Silkstrand. She noted the fact the way she noted a ford she did not intend to use.

At the next fork the post had fallen. Both roads bore fresh wheel marks. She could have spent an hour deciding which traffic had come from the coast, and perhaps she would have been right.

A woman was cutting a damaged strap from a bundle beside a waiting cart. Riarhin dismounted and held the bundle steady while she worked.

When the woman had secured it, Riarhin took out the folded sheet.

“Which road will take me here?”
