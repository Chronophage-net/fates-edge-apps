Part I — The captain is available

Chapter 1 — Nine gallows and an unpaid bill

Korvash had got both hands beneath the table before James realized it was fastened to the floor.

“Leave that,” he said.

The table rose an inch under the swell of Korvash’s shoulders. Iron shrieked in the boards. At the far end, a bowl of stewed onions slid majestically into the lap of the man who owed them money.

For a moment everyone at the Black Goat watched the onions.

Then the man stood up.

James put a hand against his chest and sat him down again. He did it gently. There was still time for an agreeable settlement, and he had not finished his lunch.

“My friend means to move us somewhere quieter,” he said. “You were having trouble hearing.”

The man looked down at his robe. A dark patch was spreading from his thighs toward an embroidered border of running horses. Fat shone on one of the horses’ heads.

“That is dye-work.”

“It’s beautiful work.”

“It was beautiful work.”

“Korvash. Put it down.”

Korvash lowered the table. The bowl stopped against James’s elbow. The table stood a little more crookedly than before. James rescued a piece of flatbread from the onion juice and looked toward the back door.

Szik had gone through it five minutes earlier. He should have returned.

Heat rolled out of the Black Goat’s kitchen, heavy with scorched fat and garlic. Beyond it, red strips of meat swung above the snapping mouths of dogs, and a woman beat a rug as though it had wronged her. Dust burst from every stroke and glittered in the bar of sunlight across the tavern floor. James saw his own bootprint among the others, its heel worn down on the outside. A captain ought to leave a better impression. He drew the foot beneath the bench.

Their employer began patting his robe with a cloth.

“The agreed fee,” James said, “was twelve silver pieces.”

“For escort to the Violet Gate.”

“We brought you through the Violet Gate.”

“After sunset.”

“Yes. You’d have arrived sooner if we hadn’t stopped for the men trying to kill you.”

“They were not trying to kill me.”

One of the merchant’s companions coughed into his fist. He was a broad young man with a careful little beard and an ivory-handled knife he had twice touched and twice thought better of drawing. The other companion was old enough to have kept his hands visible throughout. James liked him better.

Their employer spread his damp cloth on the table.

“They had a grievance,” he said.

“They had a crossbow.”

“The two are not incompatible.”

“No. And Korvash put one of them through the door of your wagon, which prevented the grievance from traveling any farther. I could have charged for that separately.”

“You broke the wagon.”

“The gentleman who went through it did most of the breaking.”

Korvash loomed over the table, bare forearms corded and dark with sweat. His sleeveless leather coat smelled of horses; the shirt beneath it had begun the morning clean. Silver bound the two plaits hanging from his temples, and the rest of his black hair was caught behind his head. Out of his collar climbed a pale scar that crossed his throat and vanished beneath an ear. Men generally noticed the scar before they noticed how carefully he had braided his hair.

When they had first met, James had asked about the scar. Korvash had told him, at considerable length. Since then James had heard three other accounts, all delivered to people whose admiration Korvash desired. The weapon grew larger in each.

There was nothing uncertain about the hands. The merchant looked at them, then at the kitchen door, perhaps calculating whether he could reach it.

“It was a family dispute,” he said.

“Whose family?”

“That need not concern you.”

“It concerned me when I had to get under the wagon.”

The young companion’s hand moved toward the ivory knife again.

James slid his bread onto a dry portion of the table and turned his head.

“Don’t.”

The young man stopped.

It was well done. James had always possessed a good voice for that sort of thing. He had been told so by men who had no reason to flatter him, and by several who had.

Behind the merchant, the old companion quietly moved his stool farther from the table.

A woman carrying three cups came through the haze from the kitchen. She wore a yellow headcloth and a belt fitted with a ring of keys. Her sleeves were rolled above her elbows. A pale scar ran along the inside of her right arm, straight until it reached the wrist, where it divided.

“Which of you is paying for the floor?” she asked.

“No one has damaged the floor,” James said.

She looked down.

A square of board had risen with one of the table’s legs. It was held to the leg by an iron staple and had taken part of another board with it. Through the gap James could see black earth and a piece of eggshell.

He looked at Korvash.

“It was nailed,” Korvash said.

“We have established that.”

“It should have been pegged.”

James took two of the cups from her and set them on the table. She flexed her fingers, then slid one to the old companion and the other to the young man. She kept the third. Neither had ordered a drink. Both took their cups.

“Vezhri,” James said, remembering the name called from the kitchen. “We were about to resolve a matter of payment. Once that is done—”

“My uncle says you resolve it outside.”

“We’re nearly finished.”

“He says that too.”

Szik appeared behind her with a strip of roast meat in one hand.

He was smaller than Korvash by so much that people often looked past him. Even seated, James was taller. Szik’s coat was a faded green, good cloth turned at the cuffs to hide the wear. His hair lay in thin plaits against his head, except for one loose lock he had refused to bind since a woman had told him it suited him. She had told him this three towns ago. He still arranged it before entering a room.

He met James’s eye and tilted his hand.

Empty.

James kept his expression pleasant.

The merchant saw Szik and reached beneath his robe, then breathed out. He had found his purse where he expected it. Szik ate another piece of meat.

“Twelve silver,” James said. “And we need never trouble you again.”

The merchant drew out the purse.

At the next table a man put down a carved bone he had been pretending to examine. One of the women beside him turned on her stool. The prospect of money changing hands had gathered a more attentive company than the threat of bloodshed.

Their employer untied the purse and poured its contents onto the table.

Three small coins rolled into the onion juice. A fourth landed on its edge and fell. There were two buttons, a little tooth, and a round brass token with a hole through it.

The merchant stared.

Szik swallowed.

“I wouldn’t put that one in your mouth,” he said, pointing to the tooth.

The merchant sprang up, scattering cups. James rose into his path. At last the ivory knife flashed; Korvash caught the wrist behind it and slammed it palm-up on the table. Tendons stood out like bowstrings. The young man made a thin, unwilling noise, and the knife dropped from his opening fingers.

The merchant shouted toward the back door.

A boy crossed the courtyard carrying an empty basket. He saw the merchant, dropped the basket and ran.

Szik took one step after him, then stopped to avoid Vezhri’s cup.

“Your nephew?” he asked.

The merchant’s shout supplied the answer in a number of words James did not know.

The old companion rose.

“I told you not to beat him,” he said.

“I did not beat him.”

“You struck him with the belt.”

“Once.”

“The purse was still here when you struck him the first time.”

The merchant attempted to go round James. James caught the edge of his robe.

“Our money.”

The cloth tore with a sound so fine and expensive that everyone heard it.

Vezhri shut her eyes.

Korvash still held the young man’s wrist. “Shall I let this one go?”

“Yes.”

Korvash let him go. The young man took his arm against his chest and looked at the knife on the floor as though it belonged to somebody else.

The merchant was breathing through his nose. In James’s hand, six embroidered horses dangled from a strip of cloth.

“How much,” the merchant said, very quietly, “do you imagine that cost?”

James looked at the strip.

“Less now.”

The old companion laughed, which made agreement impossible.

They went out into the court because Vezhri’s uncle came in carrying a butcher’s hook and appeared prepared to use it as an instrument of persuasion.

The sun blazed on the roofs. Above the tavern wall the city climbed in dusty terraces toward the Violet Gate, whose vast stones held a purple James had never seen in good wine, though several sellers had promised him otherwise. Awnings billowed beneath it, crimson and faded blue; between them streamed horses, laden men, bare arms and bright brass. Bells clanged in the horse lines. The wind brought dung, pepper and roasting lamb, and his stomach announced that negotiations had spoiled his lunch.

Their employer left by another entrance. His old companion came back long enough to put the four coins in James’s palm.

“It’s more than I’ll get,” the man said.

James thanked him. The young man did not return for his knife until Korvash had moved away from it.

Vezhri examined the coins.

“Two for the room,” she said.

“One for the room.”

“And the meal.”

“Three meals,” Korvash said.

She looked at him.

“I’m agreeing with you.”

“The boards and the table will take more.”

“I’ll mend them,” Korvash said.

“My uncle has seen you mend.”

“He’s seen me lift.”

“That’s what frightens him.”

James put the coins into her hand. He retained the brass token, which had become his without anyone particularly intending it. There was a horse’s hoof stamped on one side. He showed it to Szik.

“Worth anything?”

“Wrong stable.”

“For what?”

“For it to be worth anything.”

Szik leaned against the wall and closed his eyes, enjoying the sun. At his throat a narrow chain glinted above the open neck of his shirt. James had once asked who had given it to him. Szik had said, “A careless man,” and changed the subject.

“You could have caught the boy,” James said.

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“He’s fast.”

“You’re faster.”

Szik opened one eye. “Thank you.”

“Was that what you were waiting for?”

“No. But I’ll remember you said it.”

Korvash sat on an overturned trough. Beneath him, the wood made a short noise. He stood up again.

Vezhri laughed. She had a low laugh, almost without breath, which altered her face more than James expected. She tucked the coins into a pouch at her belt.

“Stay away from the furniture until I’ve spoken to my uncle.”

“Tell him the joint was rotten,” Korvash said.

“I think he heard.”

She went inside. James watched the yellow cloth disappear through the doorway, then straightened his own coat. One of the six surviving nooses on his clasp had caught a thread. He freed it carefully.

“You’ve lost another,” Szik said.

“No.”

“There were seven.”

“There were six when you met me.”

“I counted seven.”

“You were drunk.”

“So were you.”

The court door opened before James could establish the superior reliability of his own drinking. An old man stooped beneath the lintel and straightened to a height that made the yard seem smaller. A silver-gray braid lay against his riding coat; dust whitened its skirts. He had a hawk’s narrow face and a good knife at his hip. Beside one eye the flesh had healed in a hollow, as though a thumb had pressed into wet clay and stayed there. His gaze passed over the men without haste.

He looked first at James’s clasp.

“Which company?”

“The Ninefold Gallows.”

The old man waited.

“The Company of the Ninefold Gallows,” James said.

“I heard you.”

“Captain James Dravik.”

“Still a captain?”

James smiled. “What do you need done?”

The old man’s gaze passed to Korvash, then Szik. It lingered on the knife in Szik’s hand. James had not noticed Szik draw it; he was cleaning a piece of meat from beneath a nail.

“A person brought out of a house.”

“Willingly?” Szik asked.

The old man’s mouth tightened.

“They have my daughter.”

Behind him Vezhri was standing in the doorway, no longer laughing. She held James’s abandoned piece of bread and the strip of embroidered robe. She put both on the ledge beside the door.

The old man drew a purse from inside his coat.

This one was heavy enough to pull the cord straight.

Chapter 2 — The chieftain’s daughter

Orun of the Gray Ash would not put the purse on the table.

He kept it in his lap while Vezhri led them behind the kitchen, into a small room hot with trapped sunlight. Beyond the open shutters horses stamped and blew; a striped awning strained above the window, dragging bands of light across Orun’s knuckles. The purse cord had cut a red furrow into one of them. He did not ease his grip.

James had requested wine. Orun had requested water. Korvash asked whether the kitchen had any more meat.

“We’ve eaten,” James told him.

“You ate. I was holding the table.”

Vezhri brought curd cheese, flatbread and a dish of sliced onion. She put the cheese near Korvash. Her uncle called something from the kitchen, and she answered without turning her head.

“What did he say?” James asked.

“He says this table isn’t fastened.”

Korvash tested it with one finger. “Sensible.”

Orun watched them until she left.

“I was told there were fighting men here.”

“There are,” James said. “Several. Most are worse company.”

“Have you been in the high house?”

“The Governor’s Palace?”

“That is what people call it.”

James had seen its roofs from the market, a clutter of pale domes and high screens above the city’s upper wall. At dusk, lamps burned through the screens. Someone had told him the people inside bathed in warmed milk. Someone else had told him the milk was blood. Both accounts had come from a man selling watered wine.

“No,” he said. “Have you?”

“To the outer door.”

“What happened there?”

“They told me she was sleeping.”

“And the next time?”

“Praying.”

“The time after?”

Orun raised his eyes.

James nodded. “You went only twice.”

“The second time I took my men.”

There it was. James settled back. His chair had a sound joint at the left and a loose one at the right. He placed his weight accordingly.

“How many?”

“Four.”

“And the men at the door?”

“More.”

Korvash tore a piece of bread. “How many more?”

“Enough that I am here.”

“Did they hurt your men?” James asked.

“No.”

“Did your men hurt theirs?”

“One of mine broke a finger.”

“Whose?”

“His own. On the door.”

Szik took an onion slice and folded it into his bread. Orun looked at him, perhaps expecting laughter. Szik ate.

“She first went at the new moon,” he said. “With two girls from the caravan ring. There was music in the lower court. People could come and go. They gave food. They showed their changed ones.”

He said the last words with distaste, but his eyes moved toward the door.

“Changed how?” James asked.

“You have not seen?”

“I’ve seen people wearing scales. I didn’t ask which ones grew there.”

“The woman at the upper door had them here.” Orun touched the hollow of his own throat. “Small ones. Green when she turned. She spoke as if nothing had happened to her.”

“Perhaps nothing had happened that troubled her,” Szik said.

Orun set the purse on the table at last. The coins struck one another heavily.

“My daughter has been there for twenty-three days.”

“Does she have a name?”

“Yara.”

“How old?”

Orun’s fingers tightened on the purse.

“Old enough to know better.”

Szik waited.

“Twenty-four.”

James reached for his cup. He had been imagining a younger woman, though he could not have said why.

“What did she say before she went?”

“That she meant to go.”

“Did she say for how long?”

“She said I would not listen.”

The awning bellied in the wind, and sunlight ran over the purse. Outside, a tethered horse hammered its hoof against the rail. The blows came through the wall into James’s chair. Orun sat as rigidly as a man enduring the sound of his own door being broken down.

Orun untied the cord. “I am not paying you to tell me I should have listened.”

“You haven’t paid us at all yet,” Szik said.

James put a hand flat on the table before the old man could gather the money again.

“Let him ask. We need to know what we’ll find when we get inside.”

“You will find my daughter.”

“Yes. Will she come when we tell her you sent us?”

Orun did not answer.

Korvash pushed the cheese toward him. The old man looked offended, then took a piece. He held it for some time before eating it.

“She wrote twice,” he said. “The first said she was well. The second said she was better than well. After that, nothing.”

“Do you have them?”

“Not here.”

“What did the second ask for?”

Orun’s gaze sharpened. “Why should it ask?”

“Letters usually do.”

“She wanted her blue coat. The good comb. A pair of boots.”

“Did you send them?”

“No.”

Szik sat back so quietly that James barely heard the chair move.

James took a drink. The wine was better than the tavern’s first offering, which had required confidence to swallow. He turned the cup in his hand.

“Someone must have brought the letters.”

“A boy. Not the same boy.”

“And you sent an answer?”

“I told them to tell her to come home.”

The kitchen fell briefly silent. In the absence of chopping, James heard the old man’s breathing. It was steady but shallow.

“What made you go back with armed men?” he asked.

Orun took his hand from the purse.

“A girl was brought out.”

“Yara?”

“No. A girl I knew by sight. She had sold dyed cord in the ring before she went to them. Her mother was with the litter.”

“What was wrong with her?”

“I couldn’t see. They had covered her.”

“Then—”

“Her foot came out when they turned the litter. The cloth was stuck to it. Wet through. They had wrapped it tight, and still it dripped.”

James put down his cup.

“The men carrying her tried to pull the cloth back,” Orun said. “Her mother caught one by the wrist. I heard her say, ‘Leave it. Leave it.’ Then she began making a sound.”

He swallowed.

“The girl was alive. I could see that.”

Nobody asked how.

The old man picked up the knife beside his plate and used its blunt back to push the remaining cheese away from the purse. It took him two attempts to line the knife neatly beside the dish.

“I know what they will tell you,” he said. “That she chose. That she is old enough. That I have no right to interfere. I have heard it. If she is safe, let her tell me so where nobody is standing behind her.”

James looked at Szik. Szik was examining a dark speck in the bread.

“We can try to speak to her,” James said.

“I want her brought out.”

“If she’s hurt, we bring her out.”

“And if she says she is not?”

“Then I’ll ask her a second time.”

That satisfied neither Orun nor Szik, but it kept the purse on the table.

Silver spilled from the purse with a sound sweeter than anything the kitchen had offered. It smelled of old leather and Orun’s sweat. He counted it in fives, shoving each little rank toward James with the edge of his hand: broad pieces, thin pieces, worn faces of rulers who would have hated to find themselves sharing a table. One still possessed a nose, though someone had tried to split it. Korvash turned that one over and inspected the other side.

“Forty now,” Orun said. “Forty when she comes out.”

“One hundred,” James said.

“Eighty.”

“One hundred and our expenses.”

Orun looked at the bread, the wine and the plate before Korvash.

“No expenses.”

“The palace may be expensive to enter.”

“You’ll have forty.”

“The palace may be very expensive to leave.”

The old man gathered one of the little groups back toward himself.

James stopped smiling.

“Eighty,” he said. “Half now. But you tell us where you’ll be, and your men keep clear until we ask for them. We cannot get her out through a fight you start at the wrong door.”

Orun’s hand remained over the five coins.

“Where would you ask?”

“We’ll find that out.”

The old man pushed them back.

They settled the place and the messenger. Orun was staying with a household beyond the eastern horse lines. Szik knew the lane, or said he did. Korvash asked whether the horses there were the dun ones with cropped manes. Orun said they were. For a little while the two of them spoke about a stallion’s damaged hock, the angle of a shoe, a rider who used too short a rein. Orun’s voice changed. He knew Korvash was Vilikari by his speech and asked which side of the western hills his people rode. Korvash told him, without mentioning ancestors or victories.

James let them talk. He wrapped the money in a clean fold of cloth before putting it inside his coat. The weight lay against his ribs. His back eased without his meaning it to.

When Orun stood, he had to put a hand on the table.

“Has she a mark we’ll know?” James asked. “Apart from being your daughter.”

“A burn on the left hand. From a kettle when she was little.”

“Her hair?”

“Dark.”

“Her height?”

The old man held a hand at his shoulder.

“Her mother’s mouth,” he added.

James nodded as if this helped.

Orun saw that it did not. He drew a breath.

“She laughs without opening it much. Unless she’s forgotten to be careful.”

They walked him through the kitchen. Vezhri’s uncle was cutting a joint with the same hook he had carried into the tavern. He looked at the old man and then at James. James touched the pocket containing the money.

The butcher’s expression softened considerably.

At the outer door Orun stopped.

“Dravik.”

James turned.

“I have heard of black banners.”

“So have most people.”

“I have not heard much good.”

“Then you’ve heard from the people who hired us.”

The old man considered him for a moment. James had been looked at that way before. He kept still and allowed the man to find what he wanted.

“Bring her to speak to me,” Orun said.

He stepped out into the glare.

Vezhri had cleared the onions from their original table. A folded cloth covered the lifted boards. The table leaned very slightly toward it, and the young companion’s knife had vanished.

James put two silver pieces into her hand.

“For the rest of the damage.”

She closed her fingers. “My uncle wanted three.”

“Your uncle has two he didn’t have.”

“He has a hole he didn’t have.”

Korvash was already on his knees examining the staple. He looked up.

“I can do it.”

Vezhri regarded him for a moment.

“I’ll find the tools.”

“Good tools,” he said. “Not whatever did this.”

Szik sat on the bench and stretched his legs out. “How old was the girl in your head?”

James took the opposite place.

“I hadn’t given her an age.”

“No. You’d given her a window.”

“A window?”

“To wait at.”

James drew the purse from his coat and began dividing the money.

“Would you prefer she’d been dragged there?”

“I’d prefer to know who’s going to bite me.”

“It’s a serpent house,” Korvash said from beneath the table. “Any of them.”

Vezhri returned with a wooden mallet, an iron bar and a small box of pegs. She put them down beside him.

“Are you going to the high house?”

James covered the money with his hand. “Why?”

“You’ve been saying it in a room with a curtain.”

Szik smiled at the ceiling.

Vezhri touched the scar at her wrist. “I know someone who takes people there.”

“An attendant?”

“Sometimes.”

“What is he the rest of the time?”

“Asleep, if he has sense.”

Korvash drove the bar beneath the damaged board. James lifted his cup before the movement could spill it.

“This someone,” James said. “Would he speak to us?”

“He’ll speak if you bring something worth hearing.”

“Money?”

“Ask him.”

She named a place by the Oasis Well and a time before the queues grew long. Szik repeated the directions. She corrected one turn, using a dye shop instead of the stall he had named.

“That stall burned.”

“When?”

“Three days ago.”

“I liked that stall.”

“So did the man who owned it.”

Korvash levered the board free without breaking it. Vezhri watched him with more interest. He kept his head down and made the next movement unnecessarily graceful.

James put the men’s shares on the bench beside him. Enough for the room, food, a proper repair to his boot, and a little more. A reasonable beginning. His hand went to the company clasp and straightened it.

Vezhri saw.

“Ninefold Gallows,” she said.

“Once.”

“Which one are you?”

He looked up.

“The gallows.” Her finger moved in a little circle, indicating the badge. “Were there nine captains?”

“There were considerably more.”

“Then somebody had to wait.”

Szik laughed. James laughed too, after a moment. She was looking at him with an expression he found difficult to dislike.

“I’ll bring you something better,” she said, taking his empty cup.

He expected wine.

What came was a narrow-necked bottle bound in green cord, and three shallow cups with tiny strokes scored around their pale rims. Vezhri drew the stopper. Anise rose from the neck, sharp enough to clear the stink of onions from his nose, and beneath it came a warm, sweet fragrance: crushed petals, heated skin, a room shuttered against the night. James sat a little straighter.

“What is it?”

“A welcome,” Vezhri said. “From the house.”

“Your uncle’s house?”

She was pouring. “For your journey.”

Korvash wiped his hands on his coat and accepted a cup. Szik smelled his, looking toward the bottle. Vezhri had kept a finger over its mouth.

James raised his drink.

“To a woman coming out of a disagreeable house.”

“To getting paid,” Szik said.

Korvash drank before either of them.

James followed. Sweetness flooded his mouth; then the bitter part uncoiled beneath it, stinging the soft flesh inside his lips. His teeth felt polished to glass. He ran his tongue over them while heat descended behind his breastbone and spread through his ribs. The old knot beneath his shoulder loosened. He drew a breath so deep that his coat pulled across his chest.

He had not known how long he had been carrying that ache.

Vezhri took the cup before he could ask for more.

“That is enough.”

“For what?”

“You have work to do.”

Her fingers brushed his as she drew the cup away. They were cool. He caught her hand lightly, meaning to thank her, and for an instant she did not move.

Then Korvash struck the peg beneath the table. The mallet’s blow rang through the room.

Vezhri took her hand back. James looked at the mark the cup had left on his thumb, a little wet curve bright in the afternoon light. He rubbed it against his coat and went to help Korvash hold the table level.

Chapter 3 — A gift for the Shedder

By the time they reached the Oasis Well, Korvash regretted his second meal.

He said nothing about it. He loosened his belt at the bottom of the hill, stopped to admire a horse halfway up, and arrived at the stone rim with a dignity that required both hands braced against his thighs.

James waited beneath a cloth awning. The city’s dust had worked into the crease at his neck. He could taste the drink again whenever he swallowed. Anise, then a faint bitterness behind the teeth.

Szik returned from the water queue carrying a cup.

“One,” he said when Korvash reached for it.

“I can see that.”

“Mine.”

Korvash drank half and gave it back.

Shops crowded three sides of the well court. On the fourth, horses and pack beasts churned the ground to dust beneath the care of people considerably thirstier than their animals. Water lay black and cool under the well’s low roof. The sight of it made James swallow. Little channels carried it through the stone, each stopped with a fitted wooden plug; a man missing two fingers passed among them, pulling one plug, driving another home, taking tokens from the hands thrust over the rim. Water flashed into clay jars. Not a drop escaped him.

He worked without haste. A woman whose turn had been delayed spoke to him with great feeling. He listened while watching a vessel fill. When it was full he stopped the channel and took her token.

“Tomorrow I’ll be dead,” she said.

“Then your son can bring it.”

She caught her full jar against her hip. “He’ll drop it.”

“Then don’t die.”

She went away still complaining, but she had put her hand to her son’s shoulder and was keeping him clear of the hooves.

James searched the awnings. Vezhri had described a man with a split yellow sash and a silver ring through his ear. There were three yellow sashes in view. Two had been split by wear, and the third was on a woman sharpening a knife against the well’s stone edge.

Szik touched his elbow.

The man they wanted was lying on a carpet behind a seller of leather buckets. The sash had been spread over his face to keep flies away. One hand rested on his belly. On the little finger of the other was a silver ring large enough to pass through an ear, though James hoped the distinction did not matter.

“Tazek?”

The hand on the belly rose. Two fingers pointed toward a cushion.

James sat. Szik remained standing. Korvash chose a low wall, tested it first and lowered himself onto it with relief.

Tazek drew the sash off his face. He was neither young nor old, with a shaved chin and a strip of beard running from beneath the lower lip almost to the throat. The ring was indeed in his ear. The one on his finger was larger.

“Vezhri sent us,” James said.

“So I had hoped.”

Tazek reached for a fig from a bowl beside the carpet and examined its split skin.

“What do you want?”

“To visit the high house.”

“Go uphill.”

“To enter it.”

“Carry a tray.”

James smiled without moving his eyes. Tazek ate the fig.

“Not carrying a tray,” James said.

“Then you need an invitation.”

“That was why we hoped to speak to you.”

“You have.”

Korvash shifted on the wall. It was a small movement, but Tazek’s gaze went to him.

James rested his forearms on his knees.

“I’m told you bring people there.”

“People who have something to bring.”

“What sort of thing?”

The contact wiped a sticky finger on the inside of his sash.

“A gift for the Shedder.”

“Would she accept silver?”

“She accepts many things.”

“Then we are fortunate.”

“She has silver.”

“So do we.”

Tazek’s eyes moved to the clasp on James’s coat. He leaned forward a little, enough for James to see the narrow band of pale skin beneath the ring in his ear.

“What have you worn for a long time?”

“My boots.”

“Something you would miss.”

“The left one particularly.”

Szik turned away. James could see his shoulders moving.

Tazek waited for him to finish.

“A piece of yourself,” he said. “A thing that knows you. Hair. A name you have not used here. Something you have never told another person.”

Korvash looked at his axe. It lay across his knees, the head wrapped in oiled hide.

“Not that,” Tazek said.

“I wasn’t offering.”

Tazek smiled among his cushions, sticky with fig juice and entirely at ease. Sweat crept beneath James’s collar. Heat pressed beneath the awning, and the fellow appeared willing to lie there until James had peeled away his name, his hair and every private pleasure he had ever enjoyed. He wondered how many gifts one could extract from Tazek by rolling him into the well.

“What name would get us admitted?” Szik asked.

“Your own.”

“I’ve already got that.”

“Then you have something to give.”

“No.”

The word came quickly. James glanced at him. Szik had put his hands in his coat pockets and was looking at the well.

Tazek reached for another fig.

Korvash unfastened one of the silver bands on his plait.

“How much hair?”

James turned. “We can find another way.”

“We’ve found this one.”

His fingers separated a small lock from the plait. He smoothed it between thumb and finger, measured its length, then took Szik’s offered knife. The cut was clean. He gave the knife back hilt first and held the hair out to Tazek.

“If she needs more, she can grow it.”

Tazek rose far enough to accept the lock. He took a little square of pale cloth from inside his coat and wrapped the hair without touching it to the carpet.

“What is your name?”

“Korvash.”

“Your father’s?”

“You have my hair.”

After a moment Tazek inclined his head. He made a mark on the cloth with a reed dipped in a stoppered bottle. James watched the liquid cling to the tip. It was brown in the shade, green where the sun struck it.

“Come to the lower court at the next feast,” Tazek said. “Ask for the man who has the peacock screen.”

“When is the feast?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Will this admit three?” James asked.

“One gift. One guest.”

Korvash reached for the wrapped hair.

Tazek drew it back.

“Three,” he said. “For Vezhri.”

James rose. Sweat had glued the back of his shirt to the coat. He wanted water, shade, and to be far enough from Tazek that he could dislike him without having to maintain his face.

Szik was no longer beside the cushion.

He stood at the edge of the awning, one hand out, watching something beyond the well.

A young man lurched through the animal queue in a white coat torn open at the throat. One sleeve was gone. Gold flashed along his bare arm, beautiful until he came close enough for James to see the wet flesh between the patches. His right foot dragged a furrow in the dust. A girl with empty jars stopped before him; he swayed, trying to choose a side, while the animals crowded his back.

Behind him, three men in dark red coats came through the crowd.

The young man saw Tazek.

“No,” the contact said softly.

The young man stumbled toward them.

Szik caught him before he struck the low wall. The exposed arm felt wrong beneath Szik’s hand; James saw the thief’s fingers shift their hold. The young man was trying to speak. His lips had dried white at the corners.

“Tazek,” he said. “You said. You said when—”

The first red-coated man came beneath the awning.

“He’s ill.”

“Then bring him water,” James said.

The man ignored him. He was handsome, clean-shaven, with a narrow gold clasp at his collar. His coat concealed armor badly. A pattern of small scales showed along the backs of his hands.

“Beltek,” he said to the young man. “You will hurt yourself.”

Beltek’s grip closed on Szik’s sleeve.

James knew that grip. Men caught at a sleeve like that when the cart was leaving and they were too weak to stand.

“He seems to want to stay,” James said.

The man looked at him for the first time.

“This is a house matter.”

“Then why have you brought it to the well?”

“Stand aside.”

James did not.

The second man reached for Szik. Szik moved behind Beltek with an ease that made James blink. The hand closed on empty cloth. Korvash stood, taking the axe by its haft.

“Careful,” James said.

He meant Korvash. The red-coated man took it for himself and stopped.

For a moment they looked at each other.

Stillness spread from James’s eyes. The crowd blurred around the red coat, leaving the man’s face terribly distinct: sweat at the temples, a shaved hair clinging to the jaw, the tiny red wound left by his razor. James felt he could reach into that face without lifting a hand. Somewhere a horse snorted. He heard it from a great distance.

“Let him drink,” he said.

The man’s eyes widened. His right hand opened.

Behind him the third attendant shoved through the queue. His hip struck a filled jar. It fell against the stone and broke.

The woman who owned it cried out. A mule lunged from the sound, dragging its handler against another animal. Water spread across the paving, carrying fragments of red clay between people’s feet.

The moment ended. The first man drew his sword.

Szik pulled Beltek under the edge of the awning and disappeared behind the bucket stall.

James snatched a leather bucket from its peg and swung it full into the swordsman’s face. The rim cracked against teeth. It was a splendid noise for so little damage, but the man reeled a pace and James’s own steel came free with a hungry scrape.

“Go,” he told Korvash.

Korvash had already pushed the low wall’s loose upper stone into the path of the other two. One stepped over it. The other fell, and the woman whose jar had broken hit him with the wooden yoke she had used to carry it.

Tazek was gathering his figs.

The man’s point drove him back beneath the sagging awning. Buckets knocked against his right shoulder; shrieking people clogged his left. No room to swing. James caught the thrust low on his own blade, felt the iron jar his wrist, and kicked the fellow squarely in the shin.

The swordsman hissed.

The hiss went on and on. The man’s mouth widened, black behind the bloody lips, and something in its depths shifted forward.

James abandoned any ambition to find out what it was and went after Szik.

Chapter 4 — Across the horse ring

They plunged into a lane that shut out the sun. Korvash thrust his axe upright between the walls; the wrapped head rasped plaster from both sides. Ahead of him Beltek clung to Szik’s shoulder, dragging the ruined foot. Every time it struck the ground a wet gasp came out of him. Szik kept telling him to lift it. Beltek kept trying, and the red coats hammered after them.

James looked back.

The handsome attendant had cleared the bucket stall and was coming after them without apparent difficulty. A wet patch darkened the front of his coat where the bucket had struck. One side of his mouth was bloody. He had wiped it across his cheek, making his expression look worse than it was.

James turned a stack of empty wicker baskets into the lane.

The man jumped them.

“Quicker,” James said.

“You carry him,” Szik answered.

A gate opened ahead. They burst into a yard hung with dyed felt. Long red lengths dripped from poles above a shallow stone trough. A woman looked up from stamping cloth with a shaped block. James pointed behind him.

“Armed men.”

“I can see three.”

“More coming.”

“Out.”

She pointed with the block. They went through the indicated door, bringing down a curtain of wet yellow felt. It wrapped around Korvash’s head and shoulders. He emerged from it shining and furious.

“Don’t cut it,” James said.

“I wasn’t going to cut it.”

The woman struck the first pursuer with the block. Her shout followed them into the next street.

They burst into the horse ring’s white glare. Dust filled James’s mouth. Animals jammed nose to tail beneath the awnings, sweating and stamping, with ropes strung between them at ankle height, chest height, and one height he discovered with his forehead. The blow snapped his head back. He ducked too late and staggered after Szik, who had dragged Beltek between two dun mares.

A horse trader caught Szik by the collar.

Szik turned out of the grip. His coat remained in the man’s hand for an instant, empty at the shoulder. Then he had it on again and was past.

James would have liked time to examine what he had seen.

He had to jump a rope instead. Behind him the handsome attendant called to the crowd to stop a sick man. People shifted, uncertain whether to help. A boy took a mare by the bridle and led her into the pursuer’s path, perhaps by accident. The attendant slapped the animal’s flank with his free hand.

The mare kicked.

It did not catch him. It caught the rail behind which two younger horses had been standing. The rail broke at its pegged joint, and both animals pushed through the gap.

The broken rail flew into the crowd. Men bellowed, leather snapped, and the two young horses came plunging through dust thick enough to blind them. One wheeled toward the trough. The other bore straight down on Beltek.

Korvash stepped in front of it.

Korvash turned with the rush and caught the trailing rein low. Leather burned through his fist. He ran beside the animal, hauling its head just far enough to bend its charge into a circle. His boots ploughed the dust; the horse flung foam across his shoulder and tried to wrench free. He spoke to it in Vilikari, the deep voice astonishingly gentle beneath the thunder of hooves.

For three strides the animal carried him. On the fourth it yielded.

“Take this,” Korvash told a stable worker.

The worker stared.

“Take the rein.”

The worker took it.

Szik had reached the far side of the ring. Beltek was bent over, retching. James seized his other arm.

“We can’t stop.”

“He can,” Szik said. “He’s doing it.”

The attendant came between the posts with his sword held down, making a path with the threat of it. The other red coats were behind him. One had a swollen eye from the water woman’s yoke.

James pulled Beltek toward a covered passage. Carved tiles along its lintel showed steaming bowls and people in attitudes of ease. He hoped it was a bathhouse. It would be a poor time to discover another temple.

Steam struck them like a wet hide. James blinked through tears, half blind after the glare outside. A man rose behind a low counter, saw the naked swords burning dimly through the vapor, and sat down again.

“Through?” James asked.

The man pointed with both hands.

Szik took the left passage. Korvash appeared behind James, ducking beneath the lintel. He smelled strongly of wet dye.

“Do I look yellow?” he asked.

“Later.”

The passage opened into a washing room where three men sat on a marble bench with bowls between their feet. Steam clouded the upper windows. Beltek slipped at once. James caught him under the arm and nearly went down with him.

“Shoes,” one of the seated men said.

“We’re leaving,” James told him.

“Take off your shoes.”

Szik had already reached the opposite door. He tried it, pulled again, then put a shoulder against it.

“Locked.”

“Unfasten it.”

“It’s fastened on the other side.”

The first attendant entered behind them. One of the bathers picked up his bowl and moved aside with considerable annoyance.

“Beltek,” the man said. “You are making this worse.”

Beltek’s fingers dug into James’s coat. The gold on his arm had softened in the steam. Its edges lifted from raw red flesh, and a thick, sweet smell rose between them, like a blister opened after days beneath a dirty bandage. James fought the urge to wipe his hand.

James handed him to Szik.

“Get it open.”

Then he put himself between the young man and the sword.

Steel met steel with a shrill cry. The attendant yielded at the touch, lured James’s point wide, and cut back at the wrist. Good, damn him. James jerked away. The edge whispered through his cuff and kissed skin; he struck the bench with his heel and braced there before the wet tiles could take his feet from him.

“Who is your master?” he asked.

The attendant’s mouth twitched. “You have been drinking in his house.”

James had not been in the palace. The thought flashed through him and vanished as a second red coat filled the door. Korvash drove the wrapped axe head into the newcomer’s chest. The man folded around it, flew backward and struck a basin hard enough to wrench it from its stand.

“Careful with the—” the oldest bather began.

Water spread over the tiles.

James slid one foot back, keeping the other planted. The swordsman advanced without looking down. His feet placed themselves neatly, too neatly, upon the wet stone. James gave ground until he could feel the steam against the back of his neck.

A copper pipe ran along the wall behind the bench. Water dripped from a joint into a bowl placed beneath it. The bowl was almost full.

James kicked it.

The swordsman moved his leading foot away from the splash. James stepped into the space it had occupied, caught the man’s sword arm above the elbow and struck him with the pommel beneath the cheekbone.

The impact shot pain into James’s wrist. The man’s head turned with it; his mouth opened against James’s sleeve, and white teeth unfolded from the gums. James felt their points catch the cloth. He shoved with all his strength.

The attendant struck the copper pipe. A joint tore loose, and steam shrieked between them. Through it James saw the man’s hands fly to his face. Then the pool’s rim caught James behind the knees and he went backward into scalding water, roaring like a man being boiled alive.

Above him the roof made a cracking noise.

Korvash had driven the second man against a timber upright. It shifted where damp had softened its foot. The beam it held began to descend, slowly at first, pulling a strip of roof plaster with it.

Korvash looked up.

He let go of the man, set his axe against the wall and put both palms beneath the beam.

The timber dropped into Korvash’s palms with a thud James felt through the water. His shoulders bowed. Plaster burst against his hair, and veins swelled along his neck, dark as cords beneath the scar. He planted his feet wider, searching for purchase on the flooded tiles.

The third attendant stopped in the doorway.

“Out,” Korvash said.

The bathers went. One retrieved his clothes first. James hauled himself from the pool with his sword still in his hand, the coat pulling at him like another man.

Korvash’s knees bent. His boots slid a little on the wet floor.

Then he straightened.

The beam rose.

Korvash thrust upward. Timber groaned against stone; the cords of his neck thickened, and his red-streaked teeth showed in a grin. James knew the face of a man fighting not to be crushed. This was something else. The great brute was enjoying it.

“Door,” he said.

Szik kicked the lower panel. It broke outward. The bolt remained in the upper half.

“Go under.”

Beltek crawled through. Szik followed. James went to Korvash.

“Can you lower it?”

“Get the axe.”

“Korvash—”

“The axe.”

James picked it up. Its weight dragged at his wrist after the sword.

Korvash looked toward the failed upright. James seized its top and hauled it back beneath the beam. It had split at the bottom. He wedged its sound side against the broken basin stand.

“Let it down slowly.”

Korvash lowered his hands.

The upright took the load, groaned and bent. They did not wait to learn whether it would hold. Korvash thrust James toward the broken door and followed him through on hands and knees.

In the laundry court beyond, Szik was arguing with a woman over a sheet. Beltek had collapsed against a tub. James put the axe into Korvash’s hands and stood bent over, water pouring from his clothes.

Behind them, something large struck the bathhouse floor.

The woman stopped arguing. Szik took the sheet.

They found shelter in the back of the Silver Stall, which was neither silver nor a single stall. It occupied two courts and a narrow building lined with tack. The stable worker who had taken Korvash’s rescued horse recognized him and brought his mistress.

She was a square woman with gray at the temples and an old limp which grew more noticeable when she hurried. She inspected the wet men and the injured initiate, then pointed to a space behind the harness racks.

“No flame in here.”

“We haven’t got one,” Szik said.

“People find them.”

She brought a stool for Beltek and a shallow bowl of water. Korvash drank from his cupped hands while James wrung out his coat. The dye had run from Korvash’s shoulders to his waistband. When he unfastened the leather coat, his shirt beneath was yellow across the chest.

Szik looked at him and began to laugh.

“Enough,” Korvash said.

Szik tried. It made the laughter worse.

James checked the cut in his cuff. The blade had taken cloth and the smallest shaving of skin. He pressed it once and forgot it. His attention kept returning to Korvash’s hands. They looked ordinary, broad and scarred, a little red across the palms.

“How heavy was it?” he asked.

“The beam?”

“Yes.”

“Heavy.”

“You lifted it.”

“I noticed.”

“Could you always do that?”

Korvash wiped his face with the sheet. “You’ve never asked.”

Beltek began to cry.

His face scarcely moved. Tears cut pale tracks through the dust while he stared at his arm. One of the golden patches had curled away from the flesh, exquisite as a lifted piece of foil. Beneath it the skin wept. The boy who had been promised beauty sat rigidly on the stool, afraid to touch himself.

James crouched before him.

“Who can we fetch?”

Beltek tried to answer. His voice failed twice before he said, “My aunt.”

“Where?”

“Behind the dyers. Blue lintel. Three doors.”

Szik stopped laughing.

“Beltek?” he said. “Neresh’s sister’s boy?”

Beltek looked at him.

Szik made a face James had never seen before, an expression of recognition mixed with a wish that recognition had come later.

“You know his people?”

“Yes.”

“Go.”

Szik went.

The stable mistress returned with a covered dish and a clean rag. She looked at Beltek’s arm, then at James.

“Don’t pull any of it off.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Men have been going to all morning.”

She knelt beside the stool. Beltek held out the arm before she asked. James moved aside and took the bowl of dirty water to the court.

The horse Korvash had stopped stood eating beneath a little roof, its tether slack. A boy was running his hands down its legs, careful around the joints. At the other end of the court, two men were repairing the broken rail that had let it loose. Their argument concerned whose peg had split. Neither was looking at the horse.

James stood in the sun until some warmth reached the wet places against his skin.

Korvash came beside him.

“There were teeth,” James said.

“I saw.”

“The man tried to bite me.”

“You put your arm in his mouth.”

“I was hitting him.”

“He objected.”

James looked at him. Korvash’s mouth was steady, but his eyes had begun to crease.

James laughed. It hurt a little, somewhere near the ribs. He laughed again, and Korvash joined him, softly at first, then with his whole chest. The men at the broken rail stopped arguing to stare.

Neresh arrived carrying a knife she had not bothered to conceal.

Two younger men followed her. One held a covered basket. The other carried nothing and watched every corner of the court. Szik came last, speaking to the stable mistress as though he had always intended to return with this company.

Neresh was lean, with white appearing along the edges of her braids. A small silver ornament lay against one cheek. She went past James without a glance and knelt before Beltek.

He began speaking rapidly. She put a hand behind his head and pressed her forehead against his. For a while neither moved.

Then she drew back, took the clean rag from the stable mistress and looked at the damaged arm.

“What did you drink?”

Beltek told her.

“How often?”

He told her that too, more quietly.

She closed her eyes. When she opened them, she was looking at the man with the basket.

“Fetch the woman from the second court. Say I will bring what she asked for. All of it.”

He put the basket down and left.

Beltek clung to her sleeve. “Don’t send me back.”

She turned her head slowly toward him.

“Look at me.”

He looked.

“Who is going to take you out of my house?”

He shook his head.

“Then stop asking.”

She kissed him above the eyebrow and stood.

Now she looked at James.

“Which of you?”

“He got to Szik first,” James said. “Korvash did most of the lifting.”

“Which of you spoke when they came for him?”

James hesitated.

“I did.”

Neresh took a braided cord from her wrist. It was black and red, the colors darkened by years of wear. She put it into his hand and closed his fingers over it.

“If somebody must die for this, tell me who.”

James looked at the cord.

Behind Neresh, Szik was shaking his head very slightly.

“Your nephew needs you,” James said.

“He has me. You have that.”

The stable mistress supplied a small handcart. They padded it with a folded horse blanket, lifted Beltek onto it and tied a cloth above him to keep the sun from his face. He caught James’s coat as they were leaving.

“Were you looking for someone?” he said.

James crouched beside the cart.

“A woman called Yara. Orun’s daughter.”

Beltek nodded. “She’s there.”

“Is she hurt?”

“No. She goes inside.”

“Inside where?”

The young man swallowed. “Where the bowls are.”

Neresh took his hand from James’s coat. “Later.”

The cart rolled away between the stalls, one wheel making a sound like a small bird. James watched until it turned out of sight.

The stable mistress touched Korvash’s arm.

She had a little brass piece in her hand, stamped with a hoof. James almost laughed, then saw that its shape was different from the useless token still in his pocket.

“For the horse,” she said. “A remount, when you need one. Bring this.”

Korvash accepted it with grave pleasure. He put it in his belt pouch, then looked at the woman more closely.

“The near foreleg was scraped. Nothing deep.”

“I’ve seen.”

“He’ll rub it against the trough.”

“I’ve moved him.”

He nodded, satisfied.

As they left, James opened his hand and looked again at Neresh’s cord. There was a little knot at one end, made with such force that he could not loosen it with his thumbnail.

Szik saw him trying.

“Don’t.”

“What happens if I do?”

“You’ll have to tie it again.”

James looked up.

“And she’ll know you’ve been fiddling with it.” Szik took the cord, laid it flat and returned it. “Put it somewhere safe.”

James put it beside Orun’s money. Then he took his coat off, draped it over one shoulder, and followed the others toward the upper streets with the warm wind drying his shirt against his back.

Chapter 5 — Under an open sky

The temple had no roof, which pleased James until he discovered how much of the afternoon remained above it.

White paving threw the sun into his eyes. Four slender pillars rose into the naked blue, bearing nothing; long strips of azure cloth stirred between them in a wind that never touched his face. He narrowed his eyes at that. Around the court ran a covered walk whose shadow looked deep enough to drink. Two people slept beneath it. A third was mending a sandal with the sole clamped between her knees.

At the center of the court an old woman sat beside a shallow brass dish.

Szik went straight to the shade. Korvash followed him. James remained in the sun because the woman was looking at him and he had not decided how to begin.

“You’ve lost the rest,” she said.

He touched his clasp.

“The sleeves.”

His coat was still over his shoulder. He put it on, feeling foolish, and felt more foolish when the damp lining touched his back.

“We were told to come here.”

“By whom?”

“The keeper at the Silver Stall.”

“What have you swallowed?”

James looked toward Szik.

The thief had crouched beside a water jar and was examining its ladle.

“A drink,” James said.

“That narrows it.”

“It was served at the Black Goat. Sweet. Anise, I think.”

The old woman held out a hand. James gave her the useless brass token from his pocket before realizing what she wanted.

She examined it.

“They stopped taking these last spring.”

“I know.”

“Your hand.”

He held it out.

She turned it palm upwards, inspected the fingers, then raised her eyes to his face. Her own eyes were a pale hazel, sharp beneath the loose skin of the lids.

“Look beside me.”

He looked beside her.

“Now at me.”

He did.

Her fingers tightened once on his wrist.

“Sit down.”

There were cushions, but they lay several paces away beneath the walk. James waited for her to indicate one. She pointed at the stone beside her.

He sat on his folded coat.

Water and oil lay in the brass dish around three small objects. A knucklebone, perhaps. A strip of black metal bent almost into a ring. Something that might have been a seed, or a tooth worn smooth in a stranger’s mouth. Beside the dish stood a bowl of gray powder. James had expected flames, a carved idol, at least a decent mask. The old woman’s bare hands seemed unpleasantly sufficient.

“What is your name?” James asked.

“Why?”

“I prefer to know the person who is holding my wrist.”

She released him.

“Better?”

Szik laughed from the covered walk. Korvash had found a place to lean his axe and was unfastening his belt.

The old woman pointed to them.

“Those too?”

“We drank together.”

“Bring them.”

“They can hear you.”

“Then they can come.”

They came. Szik brought the ladle, received a look and took it back. Korvash lowered himself to the paving and stretched one leg with a sigh.

The woman inspected each of them. With Szik she asked him to follow the passage of her fingertip. With Korvash she pressed the inside of his wrist and watched the skin return to color. She asked about the cups, the marks along the rims, the cord around the bottle. James told her everything he remembered. Szik supplied two details he had missed. Korvash remembered that the drink had been poor in quantity.

“Was it venom?” James asked.

“Prepared.”

“For us?”

“For people willing to drink it.”

“We weren’t told.”

“You were told something.”

“A welcome.”

She looked at the three of them and did not ask what had made them trust it. James had begun supplying answers in his head and liked none of them.

“Can you take it out?”

“Not by putting something else in.”

“Can you do anything?”

“Show you where it reaches. A little.”

“What will it cost?”

She named a sum. James had expected either less money or a more alarming kind of payment. The plainness of the amount encouraged him.

He took out the purse.

She pushed it away.

“After. If you still want to stay.”

“Is it dangerous?”

“Yes.”

Korvash rubbed one of his palms with his thumb.

“How dangerous?”

“You will see something you may want.”

James looked into the dish. The water was barely deep enough to cover the bent metal.

“That happens most days.”

“Then you should know whether to stay.”

He stayed.

The woman moved them into the shadow of one pillar. It was narrow enough that James had to turn sideways to keep his face out of the sun. She set the dish before him and placed the dark metal across its rim.

“Your own cup would be better,” she said.

“We left them.”

“Good.”

She mixed a little of the powder into the oil with one finger. A smell rose from it, dry and bitter, like leaves scorched before they could burn. James watched her hands. The nails were short, the little finger of one hand bent permanently toward the palm.

“Nothing you see has agreed to happen,” she said. “Remember that.”

He nodded.

“Say it.”

“Nothing I see has agreed to happen.”

“Look down.”

He looked.

The oil spread in a thin ring. At its center the water reflected the empty sky. For a while that was all. His neck began to ache. Beyond the wall a seller called out the virtues of melons, repeating a phrase whose ending rose into a song. James followed it through three repetitions before the voice stopped.

He could still hear the seller drawing breath. It deepened into a great animal breathing beside his ear. Leather creaked; a hoof struck wet earth.

Cold seized his bare hands.

James stood in rain-blackened grass, with a line of tents descending the slope before him. Smoke rose thick and straight between them. He knew its taste: damp wood, wet wool, the greasy flare when a cook threw fat upon the fire. Beyond the tents stood a ridge he could have drawn blind. His body knew the cold of it before his mind supplied the name.

He had not seen it in six years.

His coat was dry. The clasp at his breast was whole. Nine little nooses shone around the sword, each polished to a brightness he had never had the patience to maintain.

“Captain.”

The man holding his horse’s bridle was young, with a flush along his cheekbones from the cold. James remembered him with a bandage round his jaw. He had been unable to swallow broth without tipping his head back.

Now he smiled.

“Waiting for you.”

James took the reins.

The horse lowered its head as he put his foot in the stirrup, standing so perfectly that he mounted without pulling against its mouth. A good horse. Better than any he had owned.

Along the slope, men began coming out of the tents.

He knew some by name. Others he knew by shoulders, a limp, the way a hand rested on a sword belt. There were more than he remembered. More tents too, continuing past the place where the stream should have been. Black cloth stirred above them. At first he thought the breeze had risen. Then he saw that the hanging banners were moving against it.

The men formed before him, rank upon rank, black banners writhing above their heads. James rose in the saddle to see them all. Strength filled his back. The wound beneath his shoulder had never been made; his knee was sound, his fingers supple, and the horse beneath him trembled with power waiting for his heel.

“Captain,” the men said.

The sound traveled along the line.

A man near the front went to one knee.

James did not tell him to rise.

Another knee struck the black grass, then another. The ranks sank before him in a long, whispering wave. He could see to the foot of the slope now, over a thousand bowed heads, and the breath he drew seemed too large for his chest.

Near the place where the stream had been, a gray-haired officer remained standing.

Othmar.

James felt the name in his mouth before he spoke it. The officer’s coat hung badly at one shoulder. He held a folded paper in his right hand.

He was waiting for an order.

James tried to turn the horse.

“Yes.”

The cobbler laughed without raising his head.

Two men carried a long mirror between them, its face turned toward the wall, and still managed to stop three times to admire their progress in the polished border. A rider dismounted outside a neighboring stall and began an argument before both feet had reached the ground.

“What?” James said.

“I didn’t say anything.”

“People do.”

Szik bent over his plate.

Chapter 8 — The weather promised

The sky-speaker arrived while Korvash was refusing a sleeve.

“No,” he said.

The tailor pulled the cloth farther up his arm.

“No.”

“It will sit better when—”

Korvash bent his elbow. The seam gave a tiny crack. The tailor stopped pulling.

Dark silk flowed from James’s shoulders. Its lining slid over his shirt with an intimacy that made him reconsider several principles; the fronts fell open around his sword belt, allowing dignity and a quick draw. Leaves climbed the embroidered borders. Follow them too closely and they became little snakes, mouths open, each biting the next one’s tail. He had asked for a plain robe. This was the plain one.

Szik wore gray and looked so respectable that James distrusted him.

The old man entering the room wore a coat with patched elbows and no ornaments at all. A girl followed carrying a flat covered basket. Malvek came out to meet them, still disputing the angle of his shoulder fastening with Hadrun.

“Now?” the merchant asked.

“Unless you want the weather later.”

The sky-speaker’s voice was mild. Malvek made room at the table.

James watched as the girl uncovered the basket. It held a shallow dish, a little bag, and a bone no longer than his thumb. The old man took the bone between two fingers and held it toward the courtyard light. He asked for coals.

Malvek called for them. The tailor tried to turn Korvash’s wrist palm upwards. Korvash lifted him several inches from the floor before noticing that the man had failed to let go.

“Put him down,” James said.

“I’m trying.”

The tailor landed, retrieved his dignity in silence and went to find another robe.

A servant brought live coals. The sky-speaker knelt, laid the little bone among them and scattered a pinch from his bag. Smoke writhed around his fingers. The words he murmured had a dry, rasping sound that set James’s own throat itching. The girl watched the smoke with her lips parted; James found himself watching it too.

One end of the bone blackened. A crack split it with a report like a knuckle breaking.

The sky-speaker took it out with tongs before it could burn through. He turned it once, twice, then closed his eyes.

Sweat ran from beneath the old man’s cap. The tendons of his free hand stood up against the table’s edge, and for a breath James thought he could hear wind rushing through the room. The curtains hung still. Nobody reached to loosen that grip.

At last he said, “After sunset. Before the middle watch. Hard from the east.”

“How hard?” Malvek asked.

“Take down what you can afford to lose.”

“And what I can’t?”

“Take that down first.”

The girl wrapped the dish. The old man remained kneeling a little longer, his eyes still shut.

“Rain?”

“Wind.”

“Will it last?”

“It showed me no more.”

Malvek nodded and counted money into the girl’s hand. She passed part to the old man without looking at the coins.

When they had gone, Hadrun sent servants to the outer awnings. Through the window James saw the first rope released and a great sheet of striped cloth sink toward the paving. Two boys ran to catch its edge before it reached a puddle.

Szik came beside him.

“That one reaches the palace wall,” he said.

A long canopy stretched above an alley between Malvek’s property and the terraced houses farther uphill. Its supports rose almost to the height of the lower palace screens. Beyond it, narrow roofs overlapped like badly laid tiles.

“You’re planning to leave before we arrive?” James asked.

“I like to know where the ground is.”

“It’s below.”

“Some places more than others.”

Szik leaned out far enough to make a servant waiting beneath the window protest. He withdrew, smiling apologetically at the woman, and looked toward the tower beyond the roofs.

“Stone rail,” he said. “There. If the cloth is gone.”

James marked it. He could imagine crossing from the upper balcony to the canopy’s iron frame, from there to the flat roof with the stone rail. He could also imagine falling into the alley. He did not give that possibility much time.

The tailor returned with a garment of dark green, broad through the shoulders and open beneath the arms. Korvash accepted it. Against the red shirt it was magnificent. Even the tailor seemed pleased.

“Turn,” he said.

Korvash turned.

“Again.”

Korvash turned back.

The tailor stood away. “There.”

James raised his cup in approval. It contained water. He had drawn it himself.

Before leaving, Hadrun inspected their weapons.

He did not take them away. He looked at James’s sword, tested the scabbard’s fastening, and made him show the little knife at his belt. Szik produced two knives. Hadrun waited. Szik produced another, much smaller one.

“That one would not hurt you,” Szik said.

“Then you won’t need it.”

“I use it for thread.”

Hadrun gave it back.

Korvash unwrapped the axe head. The guard captain examined it without touching the edge.

“Inside the outer rooms only. They may ask you to leave it at the inner door.”

“They may.”

“If you refuse, refuse somewhere Malvek can’t hear you.”

A servant brought narrow braided ties in the household colors. Hadrun fastened one beneath James’s shoulder clasp, another to Szik’s belt, and a third around Korvash’s upper arm. They carried no markings that James recognized from the cups. He inspected his own anyway.

Hadrun noticed.

“It’s thread.”

“Most things begin as something.”

“That is a disappointing sentence from a man who used to give clear orders.”

He adjusted the knot and pulled it tight.

The party assembled in the courtyard. Malvek wore both boots, a robe the color of ripe plums and a little cap embroidered in silver. Beside him stood a woman whom he introduced as his cousin. She had a fan made from pale feathers and eyes which moved over Korvash’s shoulders with professional thoroughness.

“The screen?” James asked her before he could stop himself.

She turned toward him.

“Peacocks,” he added.

“Of course.”

“Does it travel with you?”

“Only when I expect disappointment.”

Malvek took her arm and began moving toward the gate. James decided not to pursue the subject.

They climbed through the evening throng, silk brushing against rough cloaks, jeweled hands lifted clear of sweating pack animals. The patrol had gone, leaving arguments burning in its wake. Above the roofs the sky was an untroubled blue. No wind stirred the awnings. Yet shutters were banging closed along the lane, and men passed heavy bars through the staples behind them.

Korvash walked beside James, trying to keep the robe clear of the axe. At each step its hem brushed one boot, and he lifted it with an expression of increasing hostility.

“You look splendid,” James told him.

“I know.”

“Then stop fighting it.”

“It started.”

Ahead, Malvek’s cousin laughed at something Hadrun said. James had not known Hadrun could make a woman laugh. He wondered what else had improved in the man’s absence.

The palace reared above broad steps worn hollow by generations of feet. Steady flames burned in its wall niches, leaving gulfs of darkness between them. Bronze clothed the ancient doors, black above the reach of men and bright where hands had passed over it. Serpents writhed around the borders. Some wore human faces, smiling through coils that gripped their own throats. Near the lower corner countless fingers had rubbed one almost smooth.

At the entrance, a steward received Malvek by name. Behind him stood the handsome attendant from the well.

James felt Szik stop half a step to his left.

The attendant’s cheek carried a purple swelling. A little linen covered the place beneath his jaw where James’s pommel had struck. He looked at the three of them, taking his time.

Malvek was discussing the order in which his cousin should be announced.

James held the attendant’s gaze. He was prepared to fight on the steps, to run down them, perhaps to put Malvek between himself and the first sword. He was not prepared for the man to smile.

“Welcome,” the attendant said.

His eyes went to Korvash’s shortened plait.

“We were hoping you would come.”

The steward lifted the invitation. A second man brought a shallow bowl of water to wash the dust from their fingers. James watched Szik dip one hand, then the other. Ordinary water ran over the rim into a larger vessel below.

Their weapons were noted, their household ties examined. The handsome attendant stepped aside without asking for any of them.

James followed Malvek into the cool of the first hall.

Behind him, the doors remained open. People were still arriving, laughing, greeting one another as they climbed. He looked back once at the clear strip of sky above the stairs, then the steward spoke his name and he had to turn toward the light inside.

Chapter 9 — Beautiful things with teeth

The woman beside the fountain had silver down her spine.

She bent to set a dish upon the water, and her gown opened down the long line of her back. From nape almost to waist the scales overlapped like hammered silver, their edges blue in the lamplight. When she straightened, light ran up them in a single shiver. James forgot the doorway, Malvek, and every sensible reason he had come to the house.

The dish moved away across the fountain. A flame burned in its center.

“Captain.”

Malvek was looking at him.

James turned. “Yes?”

“The other way.”

The merchant indicated the hall beyond the fountain, where his cousin had already found somebody more interesting than the people she had arrived with.

James followed, reluctantly.

The hall swallowed them in gold light and music. Far overhead, painted leaves vanished into the darkness between great beams. Bronze cages held the lamps; through their pierced sides light slid across bare throats, jeweled wrists and the shining faces of dishes. Musicians occupied a raised carpet at the far end. Deep strings throbbed beneath two shrill pipes, and a woman struck a drum against her thigh, watching the dancers as though she meant to have their breath out of them.

Roasting meat. Warm oil. A sharp, sweet spice that caught behind James’s nose and made him hungry. He had expected incense to veil the house’s secrets. Instead the fountain court breathed green coolness into a room where the food smelled magnificent. Ruin, if this was ruin, had hired excellent cooks.

A steward brought them to a curved couch beneath a painted wall. Malvek’s place was marked with a cushion finer than the others. His cousin took it before he could sit down.

He considered asking her to move, then took the next place.

Korvash lowered himself cautiously onto the couch’s end. It held him. He relaxed.

Szik remained standing.

“You can sit,” James said.

“I like the view.”

He was looking at the doors.

Hadrun spoke to the steward, received a direction and went toward the passage leading to the service court. The permanent guards were to eat separately. Before leaving he bent close to James.

“Stay with Malvek until he stops noticing you. Then do what you like.”

“How long?”

“Until the first man mentions prices.”

It took less time than James expected. A merchant with a beard divided by two gold rings approached the couch carrying a cup and a grievance. Malvek’s cousin rose, taking her good cushion with her, and the conversation closed around the newly arrived man.

An attendant placed a dish before James.

There were little folds of dough filled with something fragrant, glazed roots, meat cut thin and rolled around pale curd. James selected the safest-looking fold and bit through it. Hot juice reached his tongue. He had to close his eyes for a moment.

“What?” Korvash asked.

“Try one.”

Korvash did. His expression changed.

The attendant smiled. “There will be more.”

“Good,” Korvash said with feeling.

James looked for Szik. The thief had crossed to a low table where cups were being filled. He inspected one, spoke to the man pouring, then accepted water from another vessel. He caught James watching and lifted the cup in a small salute.

A laugh rose beside the musicians. A man shrugged out of his robe and spread his arms. Glossy darkness ran from wrists to shoulders; as he turned his palms, the flesh gathered in smooth, deep folds that flowed over the muscle beneath. A woman laid both hands on one arm. He answered her question at length, basking in the touch, while another guest leaned forward for a closer look.

Nearer the fountain, an older man sat with a boy and a girl old enough to be his grandchildren. He was eating with his left hand. The right lay open on the table, fingers spread. He kept looking at it, then closing it slowly, delighted each time it obeyed.

James thought of Beltek’s arm and looked away.

The woman with the silver spine was standing behind his couch.

“You are trying to decide,” she said.

“About what?”

“Whether you ought to be enjoying yourself.”

“I seldom have trouble with that.”

“Then the food is worse than I thought.”

She smiled. Her mouth was wide, with one tooth slightly turned beside the front pair. The imperfection gave James something to look at besides the silver. Her hair had been piled loosely above her neck, pinned with long dark needles. A few strands had escaped and lay against her temples.

“May I?” she asked, indicating the place beside him.

He moved.

She sat, taking a dish from the attendant without looking away from James.

“Zareli.”

“James.”

“The man at the door called you Captain.”

“He had been told to.”

“Should I?”

“Only if you want something difficult done.”

She chose a piece of fruit from the dish. “Can you make that man stop discussing wool?”

Malvek had begun to gesture with both hands.

“No.”

“An honest captain.”

“An experienced one.”

She laughed and offered him the dish. He took a slice of pear dressed in something sharp and sweet.

Close beside him, Zareli smelled of warmed linen and crushed leaves. Her shoulders were bare and brown; the silver ended before it reached them. A fine scar crossed the soft skin near her elbow. James found his eyes returning to it, a place where the beautiful strangeness of her met something he knew how to touch.

“Did it hurt?” James asked.

She looked at him, then down at the arm.

“This? A kitchen knife.”

“I meant—”

“I know.”

He smiled despite himself.

“Yes,” she said. “For three days. On the second I threw a bowl at the person helping me. On the fourth I sent for him and apologized. He brought a wooden one.”

“Would you do it again?”

She thought before answering. James liked that more than he expected.

“I might ask better questions.”

“Before?”

“Ideally.”

She took his wrist and turned it gently. Her fingers were cool. On the inner edge of her own wrist, almost concealed by a bracelet, three silver scales overlapped one another.

“You’ve begun,” she said.

“So I’m told.”

“Did you want to?”

“I didn’t know I was doing it.”

Her hand stopped moving.

“Who gave it?”

“A woman I thought was offering a drink.”

Zareli released him and looked toward the hall’s inner door. James followed the glance, expecting an attendant to be summoned. She did not summon anyone.

“That should not have happened,” she said.

“No.”

“Have you spoken to the house?”

“I have only just arrived.”

“Then do. They will be listening tonight.”

She lifted a cup from a tray, saw him look and turned it so he could inspect the rim.

“Wine.”

He hesitated, then took it.

It tasted of wine. Excellent wine. He drank a little more.

The dances began before the meat was cleared.

Malvek’s cousin had found her peacock screen after all. It stood near a side door, its silk birds embroidered so densely that the feathers appeared to rise from the surface. A young man beside it held her fan. When she moved into the open space before the musicians he followed with the cushion, visibly uncertain what to do with it.

Korvash watched her dance.

She watched him watching.

James leaned closer. “You’ll have to stand up eventually.”

“I’m considering.”

“What?”

“The floor.”

“It held the fountain.”

Korvash rose.

He moved better than James expected. A big man often allowed his size to do the work, but Korvash placed his feet carefully and waited for the rhythm before committing his weight. Malvek’s cousin took his hand. They circled one another, her skirt opening around her ankles, his splendid robe finally finding something useful to do.

The drummer grinned.

The drum quickened and Korvash answered it. He spun the woman beneath his arm, caught her returning hand and drew her close enough to whisper in his ear. Whatever she said went through him like strong wine. He threw back his head, laughing, and swept her into a turn that sent red skirts and green silk flying. Palms cracked together around the room.

James found himself clapping too.

Zareli stood.

“No,” he said.

“I haven’t asked.”

“You were going to.”

“Now I am.”

He let her draw him up.

The dance was unfamiliar. For the first few measures he watched her feet, aware of every eye in the hall that probably had no interest in him. Zareli moved close, put his hand where it belonged at her side and told him to stop looking down.

“I’ll tread on you.”

“You might. Then I’ll tell you.”

Her body guided the turn before he could think about it. He caught the rhythm on the next pass. His repaired heel took his weight without rocking. There was no ache beneath the shoulder. He found himself moving easily, his coat swinging clear, her skirt brushing his boots.

She turned, and silver slid beneath his palm. Smooth one way, delicately ridged the other, alive with the movement of her back. He felt her breath through it. For one beat he held still with her; then her weight shifted into his hand and he followed, hungry for the next turn.

She looked up at him, pleased.

He had not realized how much he wanted to be looked at that way.

When the musicians paused, they remained standing together for a moment. He could hear her breathing. From the couch came the clatter of Korvash retrieving his cup. Malvek’s cousin had possession of the cushion again and was fanning herself with great satisfaction.

Zareli took James’s hand and led him toward the fountain court.

At its entrance, an attendant knelt beside a low stand holding a shallow green bowl. A narrow wet line shone along the marked lip. The attendant folded a cloth over one finger, drew it across the line and waited. The lamps nearest the bowl dimmed slightly. She lifted the bowl away and replaced it with another before wiping her hands.

James almost asked what she was doing. Zareli’s fingers tightened lightly around his, and he followed her through the doorway instead.

Outside, the air was cooler. Little flames floated on the fountain. Zareli knelt to free a dish that had drifted against the rim, and James recognized the movement from their arrival. In the hall she had seemed to belong only to the dance.

“You do that?” he asked.

“The lights?”

“Yes.”

“Someone has to fish them out.”

“Do you work here?”

“Sometimes. I teach the girls who want to dance. Boys too, if they stop pretending they already know how.”

“Was I pretending?”

“Badly.”

He laughed. She smiled up at him, and for a while there was no reason to ask anything else.

Thaliss entered without announcement.

The change reached the court as a thinning of sound. James heard the pipes finish their phrase, then the drum alone, then a last soft beat beneath the movement of people returning to their places.

Zareli released his hand.

“Come.”

Under the central lamp stood a tall man in white. His robe fell from broad shoulders to narrow hips, opening over dark plates that overlapped upon his breast. With each breath they lifted and settled, almost like a second creature sleeping against him. Black hair lay sleek against his head, broken by one pale streak at the temple. The light made his stillness more conspicuous than the dancers’ movement had been.

There was no crown. James had expected a crown.

The man greeted Malvek’s cousin by name, asked after the older guest’s recovered hand, and waited while a servant helped a woman into a chair. Only when she was settled did he take his place upon the shallow dais.

“Eat,” he said to the people who had put down their dishes. “I have no wish to compete with the cooks while they can hear me.”

Several guests laughed. James sat beside Zareli.

Thaliss let the room quiet itself.

“This morning a man came to me who had spent thirty years asking the same thing of his right hand.”

The older guest lifted the hand from the table. The children beside him smiled.

“He was told patience would improve him. He was told suffering would improve him. He was told he would learn to use the other.”

Thaliss looked at the man, allowing him to nod.

“He wished to hold a cup. We began there.”

An attendant brought the man a cup. He took it with the recovered fingers and raised it, carefully at first, then higher as the room applauded. One of the children embraced him so suddenly that he spilled a little wine.

Thaliss waited for the laughter to subside.

James found himself leaning forward.

The priest spoke of other changes. A woman whose body had made her feel hunted. A worker unable to breathe the dust of his own trade. A man who had been known by a failure until nobody remembered what he had attempted before it. At that James looked away, but Thaliss was no longer looking toward him.

“Those who love you may ask you to remain,” he said. “They have learned where to put their hands. They fear discovering that the place has moved.”

Zareli’s shoulder touched James’s.

“Some will come with you. Some will not. You cannot do their changing for them.”

It was well said. James distrusted it and wanted to hear more.

Thaliss spread his hands. The dark plates against his chest lifted slightly, then settled.

“Tonight there are people among us who have begun and do not yet know what they seek. Sit with them. Speak with them. Do not decide for them because you have already endured what frightens them.”

His gaze moved across the hall. It rested on James for a moment, no longer than upon anyone else.

Then attendants brought fresh dishes, the musicians resumed, and the room began to breathe at its ordinary pace.

James turned to Zareli.

“Does he always speak so well?”

“When he has slept.”

“And when he hasn’t?”

She lifted her cup. “Then the cooks hear more of him.”

Across the room Korvash stood with three men who had been admiring the axe. One of them was pointing toward a great stone weight set near the wall. Korvash looked at it, then at Malvek’s cousin.

Szik had seen it too. He caught James’s eye and made a small, despairing gesture.

James began to rise.

A shadow fell across his shoulder.

Thaliss stood beside the couch, smiling as if they had already been introduced.

“Captain Dravik,” he said. “You have questions.”

Chapter 10 — The private invitation

James had brought questions sharp enough to draw blood. With Thaliss standing over him in the lamplight, he could scarcely find their edges.

Thaliss remained standing until James indicated the empty place. He sat without summoning an attendant, his white robe folding cleanly over his knees. Zareli inclined her head to him.

“Was the dance to your liking?” he asked her.

“The music was.”

James looked at her. She was smiling into her cup.

Thaliss noticed and laughed softly.

“You have been welcomed badly,” he said to James.

James put down his wine.

“Who told you?”

“People come to this house by more than the main door. They tell us what happened on the way.”

“Beltek came out.”

The priest’s expression changed very little.

“Yes.”

“Was he being allowed to?”

“He needed tending.”

“He needed people to stop drawing swords at him.”

For a moment James heard the harshness in his own voice and wished he had begun more carefully. Then he remembered the curled edge of the golden skin and let the harshness remain.

Thaliss looked toward the fountain court.

“The men responsible have been spoken to.”

“One is at your door.”

“He is responsible for it.”

“Does he bite everyone who disagrees with him?”

Zareli’s cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

Thaliss turned back. “Did he bite you?”

“He tried.”

“I will ask him.”

James could think of nothing useful to do with the answer.

“What was in the drink?” he asked.

“A preparation used to begin a shedding.”

“I did not ask to shed.”

“No.”

“Can you stop it?”

“I can help you through it.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Thaliss laid his palms open upon his knees. Fine marks crossed the skin beneath the fingers in orderly rows. James knew rope scars, sword scars, burns from a carelessly lifted pot. These looked deliberately made, and the priest offered them to the light without the least concern.

“The first change has already occurred,” he said. “Your body will not forget it because I tell it to.”

James looked toward the stone weight. Korvash had lifted it from its stand and was holding it against his chest while the men beside him counted. Malvek’s cousin stood with her fan closed, watching closely.

“What have you felt?” Thaliss asked.

James thought of the quiet inside his shoulder, the horse in the vision, the man at the well who had stopped because James looked at him.

“Less pain.”

“Do you want it returned?”

“No.”

The answer had come too quickly.

Thaliss did not press it. James disliked him a little less for that, and then more for making him dislike him less.

“People have been brought here who did not understand what they were accepting,” James said.

“People accept many things without understanding them.”

“Do you tell them?”

“When they come to me, yes.”

“Then tell me.”

The priest looked at him for a long moment.

“You may lose the shape of some appetites. You may gain others. You may discover that a person who loved you cannot bear the change. You may discover the opposite.”

“That could mean anything.”

“It means I cannot promise the same result to every body.”

James leaned forward. “And the pain?”

“Some are ill. Some very ill. We keep people here because they may need help.”

“And if they want to go?”

Thaliss’s gaze moved to Zareli. “She goes.”

“I have rooms near the eastern gate,” she said. “My sister keeps them when I’m here.”

James believed her. It made the rest more difficult.

“Yara,” he said.

Thaliss’s eyes returned to him.

“Orun’s daughter.”

“Yes.”

“Is she well?”

“Ask her.”

“I intend to.”

“I will have someone take you.”

It was so easy that James felt the suspicion come before the relief. Thaliss rose.

“There is a quieter gathering beyond this room. Some who have begun prefer to remain apart from those who are only visiting. Yara is there.”

He indicated an attendant near the inner door.

“When you are ready.”

James stood too, instinctively, and found himself face to face with the priest. Thaliss smelled of clean linen and a faint bitter resin.

“You need not take anything further tonight,” the priest said. “But if the first change has left you unsettled, there is a draught prepared for those continuing.”

“What does it do?”

“Eases the passage. Deepens it.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

“No.”

Thaliss touched his own breast, lightly, above the dark plates.

“They may happen together.”

He moved away before James could reply.

Korvash had put down the stone weight and was quarrelling amiably about whether his elbows had touched his sides.

“They did,” said a man with a pattern of scales behind one ear.

“They were near them.”

“They touched.”

“Then hold it yourself.”

“I did.”

“For less time.”

The man grinned and reached for a cup an attendant was carrying. It was shallow, pale, marked around the lip.

James arrived as Korvash took another from the tray.

“Wait.”

Korvash looked at him over the rim.

“You know what that is.”

“Yes.”

“The woman said—”

“She is not here.”

“That does not improve the drink.”

Korvash put the cup down without emptying it. For a moment James thought he had succeeded.

Then the big man said, “My knee has hurt since I was nineteen.”

James stopped.

“Every cold morning. Every wet road. I don’t tell you each time it happens.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No. Today it doesn’t.”

He took the cup again.

James caught his wrist, then released it. Around them, the others had stopped pretending not to listen. Malvek’s cousin watched with her fan against her lips.

“Ask what else it does,” James said.

“I have.”

“Whom?”

Korvash nodded toward the scaled man. “He’s been here longer than we have.”

The man lifted his own cup. “Two years.”

“Would you change it back?” Korvash asked him.

“No.”

Korvash drank.

James could have struck the cup from his hand. He stood close enough. He thought of Orun outside the door, sending other men to put a hand upon his daughter, and found he had let the moment pass.

The attendant offered him the tray.

“No,” he said.

She inclined her head and went on.

Szik stood beneath the painted wall talking to a man whose sleeves had been tied back. Between them lay three cups. James reached them in time to hear Szik say, “Half.”

“It does not work by halves.”

“Then give me less and charge me the same.”

“It isn’t for sale.”

“Better still.”

“Szik.”

The thief turned.

“We’re going to see her,” James said. “Now.”

Szik glanced toward the inner door. “They let you?”

“They offered.”

“Then they want you.”

“Apparently.”

The thief took one of the cups, inspected its contents, then poured a little back into another.

“Don’t,” James said.

Szik looked at him without smiling. “I remember what she said.”

“Then why?”

“Because yesterday I got through a hand that should have caught me.”

“You’ve done that before.”

“Not that hand.”

He drank what remained and put the cup down.

Rage flooded James’s throat. He had bargained them into this glittering snake pit, and now they stood before him swallowing its venom as though he were a child spoiling their feast. He wanted to seize both collars and drag them down the palace steps. Out among horses, dust, ordinary knives. Into the first foul tavern where men were allowed to remain what they were.

Szik watched his face.

“Come on,” the thief said. “We’ll find the girl.”

At the inner door, Zareli touched James’s arm.

“Are you leaving?”

“Just this room.”

She stood close enough that the silver at her wrist lay beneath his thumb. Her hair had loosened during the dance, and one dark needle had begun to slip. He put it back carefully.

“Thank you.”

“There. You can dance with someone else without losing pieces.”

“I might wait.”

He smiled at her. It was an ordinary, lovely moment, and he wanted it to last long enough that he could step into the next room without feeling he was abandoning it.

“Stay,” he said.

Her face changed.

Her mouth stilled. The breath he had felt so warmly against him stopped halfway out. Her eyes held his, wide and bright, while her fingers went slack beneath his thumb.

An attendant brushed past with a dish. Its edge caught Zareli’s sleeve and tugged. She did not turn. For a sickening instant James felt how easy it would be to keep her there.

James’s fingers tightened on her wrist.

“Zareli?”

She blinked. He looked down at his fingers closed on her wrist.

“I meant—”

She took her hand away.

“I heard you.”

“I’m sorry.”

She was breathing faster now. One hand went to the place he had touched.

“Don’t look at me like that.”

“Like what?”

She shook her head, angry or frightened; he could not tell which, and she had already turned away. He took one step after her, then stopped.

An attendant stood beside the inner door with a tray.

“Continuing?” she asked.

James looked at the shallow cup. His mouth had gone dry. Somewhere behind him Korvash laughed, sounding wholly himself.

“Does it settle this?” James asked.

“What have you felt?”

He could not tell her. The words would make the thing too ugly beside the warmth that still lingered from Zareli’s fingers.

“Uneasy,” he said.

“It may.”

He took the cup.

Yara waited beyond the door. His men had gone ahead. The draught might steady him, might let him speak to a woman without freezing the breath in her throat. Each reason came readily, bright and serviceable as a newly sharpened blade. He raised the cup before another could be needed.

It tasted less sweet than the first. Or perhaps he had learned to taste what lay beneath it.

Bitterness burned over his tongue. Warmth filled his hand, then his arm, pouring into the places where age and old blows had lodged. The bruised ache in his ribs loosened and slid away. He breathed deep, and hated how good it felt.

The attendant took the empty vessel and stepped aside.

James crossed the green metal strip set into the threshold. A tremor climbed through his boot and into the marrow of his leg. The stone itself lay still. Within the passage the lamps flared brighter, revealing one rank of carved scales after another along the walls.

Ahead of him, Szik stopped and looked back.

Korvash laid a hand against the wall, smiling at something James could not hear.

From the feast behind them came the first notes of another dance.

Chapter 11 — Yara is not grateful

James recognized the burn before he recognized anything of Orun in her.

She sat on the dry fountain’s rim, cradling a cup. The old burn curved pale across her left hand; around it, bronze plates were rising through the skin, small and close-fitted as armor made by a patient smith. They vanished beneath her sleeve. Her dark hair fell loose from two braids joined behind her head, and she watched the man beside her with an amusement James had seen nowhere in her father’s face.

A man beside her was trying to make a small stone turn on its edge. He set it spinning, watched it fall, then tried again. Yara waited until he looked away and changed the stone for a flatter one. The next attempt worked.

He laughed. So did she, opening her mouth before she remembered to close it.

James stopped a few paces away.

The music dwindled behind him. Water whispered inside the walls, following channels he could not see. In warm alcoves bodies reclined beneath gauze, a bare heel here, a shining shoulder there, languid as creatures basking upon stones. Two attendants rolled back a carpet. The paving beneath it gleamed green, though the lamps were amber.

Yara looked up.

Her eyes were almost the color of her scales. The pupils narrowed as she turned toward the lamp.

“Yara?”

She put down her cup.

“Who wants to know?”

“James Dravik.”

“That doesn’t help.”

“Your father sent me.”

The man beside her stopped the spinning stone with one finger.

Yara looked past James to Korvash and Szik.

“How much?”

The gallant speech he had prepared died behind his teeth. She was looking at him as though he had arrived with a halter.

“He’s worried.”

“How much?”

“Enough that he came looking for help.”

She stood. She was taller than the measure Orun had given him, though perhaps the old man had been thinking of the last time she had stood quietly at his shoulder. Her dress was blue, open at the throat, with a cord wound twice around the waist. She wore soft shoes that fitted poorly at the toes.

“He told you they took me.”

“He told us you were here.”

“What did he say happened to my letters?”

“He said they stopped.”

“They did. What happened to his answers?”

James did not reply.

Yara smiled without pleasure. “There.”

Korvash looked at the dry fountain, then the little stone. Szik put his hands behind his back.

James lowered his voice.

“We brought a man out yesterday. Beltek.”

Her expression changed at once.

“Where?”

“With his aunt.”

“Is he walking?”

“With help. A woman is tending him.”

Yara released a breath. The man with the stone had risen too. She touched his arm without looking at him.

“Neresh?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

James waited. He had expected gratitude for that part at least. She was looking toward the inner passage instead, her mouth drawn tight.

“Beltek was being brought back here,” he said. “With swords.”

“I know how they fetch people.”

“Then you know why your father—”

“No.”

The word was sharp enough to turn a head in the nearest alcove. She saw it, stepped closer to James and spoke more quietly.

“My father was telling people I’d been taken before Beltek tried to leave. He doesn’t get to become right because somebody else was cruel.”

James felt himself begin to answer and stopped. She was twenty-four. She did not need him to explain her father to her in a room he had entered five minutes earlier.

“Come out and tell him,” he said instead.

“I told him before I came in.”

“Tell him again.”

“Will you take me back afterward?”

It was an easy promise to make. He had made harder ones with less hesitation. Something in her face kept him from giving it quickly.

“If that’s what you want.”

She examined him. Then her gaze moved to his eyes and remained there.

“Oh,” she said.

“What?”

She looked at Szik, then Korvash.

“Oh, you fools.”

Korvash frowned. “We’ve done nothing to you.”

“How many cups?”

“Two,” Szik said before James could answer.

“Here?”

“One here.”

Yara laughed once, a short incredulous sound.

“He sent you to fetch me and you’ve begun yourselves.”

“The first wasn’t offered honestly,” James said.

“And the second?”

The man beside her picked up his stone and put it into a pouch. James disliked the small movement.

“We need somewhere quieter,” he said.

“Do we?”

“Yes.”

“Because you dislike being heard?”

“Because people are listening.”

She looked round. At the far wall an attendant had stopped rolling the carpet. Nearer the passage, a man in red stood with his hands folded at his waist.

Yara’s amusement left her.

“Come this way.”

She led them into a little room packed with warm folded cloth. Bruised herbs thickened the air. Beyond the bench and pierced window, evening had swallowed the city; its first lamps trembled like sparks caught below a grate. James felt the warmth settling into his shoulders again and resented the house for making even this room comfortable.

The man who had been playing with the stone followed to the door.

“Irdan,” she said, “wait for me.”

He nodded. His gaze passed over James with a suspicion James found entirely reasonable and still resented.

Yara closed the door most of the way.

“I’ll speak to him,” she said.

“Your father?”

“If he comes without men. Here, in the outer court. Tomorrow.”

“He’s been twice.”

“He brought four men the second time.”

“One broke a finger.”

“That would be Shavret. He keeps using his hands where his head would do less damage.”

Korvash smiled.

James drew a breath. This was progress. He should have accepted it, arranged the hour and taken the others down into the city while the doors were still open.

Instead he said, “You can come tonight. They cannot keep you.”

Yara looked at him with sudden fatigue.

“I know where the door is.”

A knock sounded behind her.

Before she could answer, a young woman slipped through the opening. She carried a basket of cloth and held her right arm close to her body. A dark patch marked the sleeve beneath the elbow.

“Your tending,” she said. “It’s been moved.”

Yara turned.

“When?”

“Now.”

“Thaliss said after—”

“I know.”

The woman’s eyes went to James and away.

Yara took her by the uninjured wrist. “Neshet. What happened?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s blood.”

“It stopped.”

James saw the young woman look toward the door. She had used the same tone as men who wanted a surgeon to pass them over in a crowded tent.

“Irdan too?” Yara asked.

Neshet nodded.

For a moment the two women stood without speaking. Then Yara took the basket and set it on the bench.

“I’ll come.”

Neshet left without the basket. Yara went after her.

James reached for her arm.

She turned before he could tighten his grip. Her hand closed around his wrist. The new plates on her fingers were cool and hard against his skin.

“Don’t.”

He opened his hand.

She let go.

“We can wait outside,” he said. “All of us. Talk there.”

“You can wait here.”

“That girl is frightened.”

“I saw.”

“Then—”

“I know her.”

Yara looked at him long enough that he heard what she had said. He stepped away from the door.

She passed him, collected Irdan with a touch at his shoulder, and went down the passage. The man in red followed them at a distance.

James stood watching until the turn hid them.

Behind him Szik was examining the window screen.

“What?” James asked.

“The pins are inside the stone.”

“We’re not leaving through a window.”

“Good.”

Korvash came to the doorway. “The outer door’s shut.”

James went past him into the room with the dry fountain. The attendants had finished taking up the carpet. Beneath it, thin green lines met in a pattern at the fountain’s foot. They continued across the floor toward the door through which the company had entered.

The handsome attendant stood against the closed door, red coat bright above the green lines.

“Some privacy,” he said.

James looked at his bandaged jaw.

“For whom?”

The man smiled. White points showed below his lip. He let James look at them.

Chapter 12 — The next skin

Yara had stolen better shoes twice and returned both pairs.

The first belonged to a woman whose toes were shorter than hers. The second fitted well but squeaked. Neshet had heard her halfway across the warm room and laughed until she cried, because Yara had been attempting to look solemn for the morning tending.

Now Neshet walked ahead without looking back. Her silent shoes skimmed the stone. Yara heard Irdan breathing beside her and, behind them, the slow scrape of the red coat’s sole. The passage held heat like a kiln cooling after the fire had been drawn. It should have pleased her altered skin. Instead she kept counting the steps to the next door.

She wanted her boots. She wanted the blue coat that knew where to fold at the elbows. Wanting them made her so angry with her father that for several steps she forgot to be afraid.

At the first turn Irdan touched the wall.

His right hand opened fully against the stone. He did it whenever they passed this place, testing the reach he had regained. Six months earlier a falling bale had crushed the fingers together. He had shown Yara how he used to hold them inside his sleeve to stop people offering advice.

He smiled at her now.

“You were fierce with him.”

“He deserved it.”

“The large one didn’t seem to.”

“The large one drank two cups and then took offense at being called a fool.”

Irdan laughed softly. He had a little gap between his front teeth. When she first met him, she had wanted to put the tip of her tongue there just to discover how his mouth would feel against hers. She had done it four nights later, in the stair to the linen room, with a pile of folded cloth between them. He had dropped half of it. Neshet had refused to help them gather it up.

Yara looked at her friend’s darkened sleeve.

“What cut you?”

“A fastening.”

“Let me see.”

“After.”

Red glass hooded the lamps in the tending room. Their warmth touched Yara’s face, and pleasure spread over her so swiftly that she had to resist leaning into it. Outside she shivered in extra cloth. Here she wanted the gown open at her throat, light on the new bronze, heat reaching every part of her. Her body had learned this room too well.

A woman near the brazier was asking for the shutter to be opened. One of the men beside her complained of cold. Neshet crossed to the shutter and opened it a little, then brought the man a folded cover. The woman tilted her face toward the narrow flow of air, eyes shut.

Yara knew them both. The woman had worked above cooking fires for fifteen years and could no longer bear a warm room. The man had once slept bare-armed in winter and now shook beneath two blankets. They quarrelled over the brazier each morning and shared fruit each afternoon.

Neshet began laying out cloths one-handed.

Yara caught the edge of her sleeve.

“Now.”

Her friend stood still. After a moment she let Yara lift the stained cloth.

The cut ran along the tender inside of the forearm. Dried blood clotted its edges, and beneath them something hard pushed outward, ridging the skin as a root lifts earth. Yara could see where a hurried cloth had merely smeared the blood back into the wound.

“That needs looking at.”

“It has been.”

“By whom?”

Neshet pulled the sleeve down.

Yara lowered her voice. “Did they offer another cup?”

Her friend nodded.

“You don’t have to.”

“They say it will settle.”

“They said you could wait.”

Neshet looked at her then, with an expression Yara had seen before and failed to keep in mind. Beltek had worn it on the morning he asked how long the gates stayed open. She had told him there were people at the doors all night. She remembered being pleased with the answer, as though she had helped.

A bell sounded beyond the room.

The man at the brazier put down his fruit. The woman by the shutter opened her eyes. Irdan straightened.

All at once.

Yara’s hands wanted to flatten against her thighs. The impulse came from deep inside the wrists, so intimate that she almost obeyed before knowing it was an order. She crooked her fingers and held them there until the pull ebbed.

Neshet gathered the tending cloths. “Come.”

The bowl chamber rose through veils of suspended silk. Old stone showed between the new screens, the bones of a room once built for public receptions. Far overhead painted riders hunted through gaps in the cloth. Their quarry had gilded eyes. Those little points shone down from darkness the lamps never reached, watching the living enter beneath them.

Yara had liked the room when she first entered it. She still liked the high spaces and the way a voice could sound close from the far side. She disliked remembering how she had stood exactly where Thaliss placed her and lifted her chin for him to see the first bronze on her neck.

Three bowls stood at different heights around the central dais. The highest rested beneath a hanging lamp upon a shelf reached by narrow steps. The middle bowl was set in a metal cradle fastened to a freestanding screen. The lowest stood on a pedestal near the floor. From each, a thin line of marked metal led toward the next. The lines disappeared beneath rugs in some places and showed naked against the stone in others.

Neshet went to the lowest bowl.

Neshet wound cloth around her left forefinger and wiped across the bowl’s lip. The shining thread of venom parted. Yara’s shoulders loosened as though a weight had been lifted from the back of her neck.

Her friend changed the cloth, bent close and drew a glass rod along the lip. Liquid joined liquid. The weight settled back, light enough to mistake for her own weariness.

“Why?” Yara asked.

Neshet did not look up. “It has to be clean.”

“No. Why does it feel like that?”

The attendant’s injured hand tightened against her body.

Thaliss entered from the raised passage.

He had removed the white outer robe. Beneath it he wore a close dark garment that left his arms bare. The plates at his chest were not an ornament. Yara could see their edges lift from his skin as he breathed.

She had known they were part of him. Still, her stomach tightened when a plate slid beneath its neighbor at his throat and the soft flesh under it briefly showed. He moved with the ease of a man unaware that anyone might find him monstrous.

“Yara,” he said. “You have guests.”

“My father’s.”

“They have traveled far to reach you.”

“From the Black Goat.”

Irdan smiled. Thaliss did too, but later.

“We should attend to you before you return to them.”

“What are we doing?”

“What we discussed.”

“We discussed it for tomorrow.”

“The house is ready tonight.”

Neshet stepped away from the bowl. Thaliss took her place, resting two fingers against the metal strip beside it.

Irdan lifted his right hand.

The woman from the warm room, entering behind him, lifted hers. Three people standing by the far screen did the same. Yara felt her fingers rise and pressed them hard into the cord at her waist.

Thaliss watched her.

“It is easier if you let it pass.”

“What is?”

“The first resistance.”

Irdan’s hand remained raised. His face had become tranquil in a way Yara had never seen while he was awake.

“Irdan,” she said.

He looked at her.

“What did Neshet call you in the linen stair?”

Neshet made a tiny sound. Yara ignored it.

Irdan’s mouth opened.

“We need not carry the shame of what is past,” he said.

His voice; she knew every warmth and break in it. But the words came in Thaliss’s measured cadence, slipping out of the mouth she had kissed while Irdan’s wet eyes looked straight through her.

She stepped back so hard that her heel struck the pedestal.

Thaliss took his fingers from the strip. Irdan lowered his hand and looked round with a little frown, as if someone had moved a chair while he was preparing to sit.

“What?” he asked.

Yara could not answer him.

“Practice,” Thaliss said. “Until each body learns the passage. Some move together sooner than others.”

“Then let him choose when.”

“He did. As you did.”

“I chose this.” She held out her left hand, the bronze plates catching the light. “I wanted this.”

“And you may keep it.”

The relief was so immediate she hated herself for it.

Thaliss approached. Yara stood still until he raised a hand toward her face, then moved away. He let his hand fall.

“There is no need to be afraid of every door because you have not opened it.”

She thought of James reaching for her arm. She had disliked him for assuming she needed to be taken somewhere safe. She disliked him more now that a part of her wished she had gone with him before the outer door shut.

“I want to speak to my guests.”

“After the tending.”

“Now.”

Thaliss’s face altered. She had interrupted something he had expected her to admire.

“Take a little time,” he said. “Neshet will sit with you.”

He turned toward Irdan.

The young man stepped forward before being asked.

Neshet brought Yara to the narrow room beside the chamber and closed the door. A square opening above the lintel admitted the sound of the bell when it rang again.

Yara put both hands against the wall.

“Did you know?”

Neshet stood beside the door, the basket pressed against her hip.

“How long?” Yara asked.

“I didn’t know what it was.”

“Don’t.”

Her friend flinched.

Yara turned to the shelf of cloth, the chipped cup, the pin Neshet had stuck into the frame to hold a loose fastening. Her own thumbnail had made that scrape in the wood. A hundred mornings her hands had moved among these things without fear. She laid one palm against the shelf and pressed until its edge hurt, demanding something solid that belonged to her.

“Beltek tried to tell me,” she said.

Neshet put down the basket.

“Yes.”

“I told him to wait.”

“I told him the same.”

They stood without looking at one another.

Beyond the door someone moved a heavy vessel across stone. The sound traveled through Yara’s palms.

“The men who came for me,” she said. “They’re still outside.”

“In the fountain room.”

“Will the door open?”

Neshet’s hand went to the cord beneath her collar. A small flat token hung there. She drew it out, looked at it, then let it fall against her breast.

“I have to stay.”

“Why?”

“Irdan. The others. They don’t know where the linen passage comes out.”

Yara looked at the basket. A loose loop of cord lay over its handle.

“Your sleeve,” she said.

Neshet looked down. The blood had begun to show again.

Yara took a clean cloth and stepped close. Her friend allowed her to lift the injured arm. As she wound the cloth, Yara caught the token’s cord with one finger and drew it through the open neck of the dress. The knot lay loose at the back. Neshet had always tied it badly because she could not make her right hand do the work.

Yara undid it.

Neshet looked into her face.

For a moment neither moved.

“I can say you took it,” Neshet whispered.

“I did.”

She tucked the token beneath her own cord and finished the bandage. Then she lifted the basket.

“Sit down before somebody sees you fall.”

Neshet sat.

At the door Yara turned back. She wanted to promise she would fetch everyone, that James and his enormous companion would know what to do, that the bronze on her own hands would not betray her before she reached the outer room. Neshet was watching, waiting for something Yara could not give her.

“I’ll come back,” Yara said.

Then she went through the door with the basket against her hip, walking at the pace of someone who had been sent for more cloth.

Chapter 13 — The strength of an ox

The guard’s teeth were white, fine and much too long. James had spent an unreasonable amount of time considering what they would do to a throat.

“Open the door.”

“When the tending is finished.”

“We’re not being tended.”

The guard’s bruised cheek tightened. He had taken the bandage off his jaw. There was a little square of plaster below his ear, and dried blood in the corner of his otherwise handsome mouth.

“You have been offered the use of the room.”

Korvash stood beside the dry fountain, with one hand on its carved rim. He was pressing his thumb into the stone. James could hear the small, gritty noise it made.

“We’ve used it,” Szik said. “Lovely room. Done now.”

Beyond the guard, a bolt slid into place.

James drew.

The guard sprang inside the draw. Both hands locked on James’s wrist and drove it up; the sword struck stone with a scream of sparks. A knee buried itself in James’s thigh. He punched the plaster beneath the man’s ear, felt it crumble against his knuckles, and took a hot hiss full in the face.

Something hot moved behind James’s eyes.

The man hesitated.

James shoved him away, but the hesitation was over before he could follow it. The guard caught a hanging strip of carpet and flung it across James’s blade. Weight dragged the point down. A second man came through the side passage with a short club and struck James above the elbow.

He lost the sword.

It went flat across the paving and stopped at Korvash’s foot.

Korvash looked down at it. Then he looked at the two guards.

The club man took one step backwards.

Korvash lifted the fountain.

Old mortar cracked like bone. A black seam opened between the fountain bowl and its pedestal, shedding powder beneath Korvash’s fingers. The bowl was wide enough to bathe in. He tore it from its foot and heaved it against his chest, water from the hidden drain pouring down his red sleeves. His breath came in a great, delighted gust.

“Move,” he said.

James moved.

Stone thundered against the outer door. Once: the boards bowed. Twice: a hinge burst out in a cloud of plaster. Carved lintel crashed upon the guard’s shoulder and knocked him clear, cursing. Korvash drove forward with the broken doorway; the bowl tipped from his arms, struck the threshold and split into three vast pieces.

Korvash stood breathing above the wreckage. He looked pleased with himself.

“Come on,” James said, retrieving his sword.

Through the gap he could see the room in which they had eaten. Servants were carrying away the last dishes. A woman still held a cluster of grapes over her plate. At the noise she had turned with her mouth open, one grape between her teeth.

Then two iron bars dropped across the passage beyond the door.

Someone on the other side began shouting for the guests to keep back.

Szik put his face between the broken boards.

“Not that way.”

The handsome guard was getting up. His right arm hung badly, but his left hand had found a knife.

James put his point beneath the man’s chin.

“Tell them to lift those.”

The guard looked past him.

A bell sounded somewhere in the house.

James’s sword arm straightened.

He thought at first that another blow had numbed his elbow. Then his fingers began opening of their own accord. James clenched them until the hilt crushed the flesh of his palm. Beside him Korvash drew one immense breath and could not let it go. Szik’s head turned slowly toward the inner passage, his eyes rolling back toward James while the rest of him obeyed something elsewhere.

The bell ceased.

James staggered. The guard’s knife cut through the space his ribs had occupied.

Korvash hit the man with a piece of door.

He went down beneath it. Korvash stood on the plank while James backed away, sword up, trying to make his breath regular. The sound of the bell had gone on inside his teeth.

“We need the other way,” Szik said. He was rubbing his neck.

“There isn’t another way.”

“There’s always—”

“Don’t.”

The small door behind the fountain opened. Yara stood in it holding a basket.

She took in the broken stone, the guard under the plank, and James’s sword.

“Oh, well done.”

“Can you get us out?”

“Through the linen stair. If you can keep quiet for the length of a passage.”

Korvash looked regretfully at the man beneath his foot.

“He’ll get up.”

“Then hurry.”

They followed her. James went last, and saw the handsome guard turn onto his knees as the little door closed. He had lost the knife. His expression promised that he would remember the rest.

The passage was narrow and hot. Yara walked quickly with the basket braced against one hip. At the first turn she pressed a flat token into a hollow beside the doorpost. Metal clicked within the wood.

“Where’s the fellow?” James asked.

“What fellow?”

“Your friend.”

“In the tending room.”

“Leave him. You can’t—”

She stopped so abruptly that Szik walked into her basket.

“Left at the next turn. Then up. If you meet a woman with a bandaged arm, send her to me.”

Yara opened the next door and disappeared through it.

James reached after her and caught only the edge of the basket. It tipped. Cloth spilled across his boots.

From the room beyond came a sound he had not heard before: many people trying to catch their breath together.

He pushed the door wider.

Screens divided a long chamber beneath a painted hunting scene. A green line ran along the floor and climbed a low pedestal. On it stood a shallow bowl with a wet, shining rim. Another bowl hung higher, above the screens; a third rested in a metal cradle fastened to a partition in the middle of the room.

People filled the spaces between the bowls, standing or kneeling with the dreadful patience of animals tied for slaughter. A man in yellow rocked on his heels with both hands buried in his armpits. Irdan sat bolt upright upon a bench, palm raised to the room. Tears shone on his motionless face. Around him the breath of strangers rose and caught together.

At the far end Thaliss stood barefoot upon a dark slab, his toes spread against its markings. The plates upon his breast lifted. Soft flesh stretched between them, shining and wet, and James’s own chest tightened with the man’s next breath.

“Put down the sword,” Thaliss said.

Yara was already at the bench.

“Irdan. Look at me.”

The young man looked at her. His hand stayed raised.

Thaliss came off the slab. As his foot left it, a woman beside the low bowl slumped against its pedestal. Her bandage had soaked red from wrist to elbow.

“Neshet,” Yara said.

James heard running behind him.

He turned and met the first guard at the door. Steel rang against his blade. This man had a narrow sword and knew how to use it in a passage. He gave James nothing to strike but the point. The man behind him carried a hooked staff.

James retreated into the bowl chamber.

Szik slid past him and threw the spilled cloth into the swordsman’s face.

That bought them two breaths. Korvash used the first to swing his axe clear and the second to change his mind. Too many people stood behind the guards. He caught the staff instead, hauled its owner through the doorway, and drove him into the partition.

The middle bowl rang in its cradle.

“Keep away from that,” Thaliss said.

The guard clung to the staff. He was a big man, and when Korvash jerked him forwards he got his feet against the partition and pulled back. The screen bent. Metal staples began to draw out of the wall.

Korvash’s mouth opened in a delighted snarl.

James had seen him like this over a wager involving a loaded wagon. It had cost them a wheel.

“Korvash. Let go.”

The swordsman cleared the cloth from his face. James caught the next thrust on the strong part of his blade and drove him sideways. His heel struck something warm and yielding. A rope lay looped across the floor beside a little open store cupboard.

An attendant knelt there, trying to secure a fallen woman’s wrists. He laid two fingers on the rope.

“Hold.”

It tightened.

The woman cried out. The attendant touched it again, hurriedly.

“Loosen.”

Szik was watching.

The staples shrieked free of the stone.

Korvash hurled the guard through the partition. Carved wood burst beneath the man’s back. The metal cradle slewed sideways, bringing its bowl down against the bench with a clang that swallowed the shouting. Irdan toppled; Yara flung both hands beneath his head before it could strike the floor.

James put his left hand out to stop the broken screen falling onto them. His sword hand followed without his intending it, grabbing for the bright edge of the metal cradle.

The edge went into his palm.

For an instant the metal was merely cold and hard. Then his weight drove it through the flesh beneath his little finger. The edge slid toward his thumb, and blood sprang into his closing palm. James let go with a howl.

The bowl struck the floor mouth downward.

White fire leaped in every lamp.

James could see the bones of his fingers through the blood. He shut his eyes. Somebody screamed. The partition’s upper beam came down across Korvash’s left shoulder, and the big man made a short, astonished noise.

When James could see again, the chamber had changed.

People were moving separately. The man in yellow had torn his tunic open and was dragging air into his chest. Irdan was on hands and knees, being sick. Neshet crawled toward Yara with her injured arm held against her body. The swordsman had dropped his weapon to cover his face.

Thaliss alone was standing still.

Blood ran over Thaliss’s upper lip. He lowered himself without haste and planted both palms on the marked line. His shoulders knotted. The white glare sank into a deep, furnace red, and the plates of his breast dragged against one another with a sound James heard even through the cries.

“Bring me the lower vessel,” he said.

No one moved to obey him.

Korvash was down on one knee beneath part of the partition. He shoved the wood off with his right arm. His left shoulder sat lower than it should, and his face had gone gray around the mouth.

James tried to lift his sword. Pain ran to the elbow. He changed hands, almost dropped it, and got the blade between himself and the attendant who was approaching with a chair.

“Don’t,” he said.

The attendant stopped.

Across the room Szik had gathered the warm rope from the floor. He was crouched at the open cupboard, cutting a cord that secured a narrow black object to its shelf. The attendant with the chair saw him.

“That’s for the—”

Szik shut the cupboard door on the chair and snatched the object free. A black blade, curved like a tooth, showed for a moment before he pushed it into its leather cover. He tucked it beneath his belt.

“Upstairs,” he said. “Now.”

Yara got Neshet under the good arm and brought her toward the little door. Irdan tried to stand, fell against the bench, and stayed there breathing through his open mouth.

James caught Yara’s wrist as she passed.

He had her. The door was clear. Szik was in the passage and Korvash had got upright. James needed only to pull.

Neshet’s knees folded.

Yara turned to take her weight. James’s grip dragged both women sideways, and Neshet’s forehead struck the doorpost.

“Let me have my hand.”

Her voice was quite level.

He let go.

Yara eased Neshet down beyond the threshold, out of the room’s heat. She pressed a fold of her skirt against her friend’s temple, then looked back at Irdan.

James wanted to tell her the young man could crawl. He could see the distance. Ten paces, perhaps less. He could also see Thaliss lifting his head, and the guards beginning to move again.

“The stair,” Yara said. “At the end. I’ve left it open.”

She went back for Irdan.

James took two steps after her. Someone caught the back of his robe.

Korvash.

“Captain.”

The big man’s face was wet. His left arm hung uselessly. Behind Yara, the man with the chair had put it down and was helping Irdan to stand.

James backed through the door.

Neshet was still sitting against the passage wall. As he passed she raised her good hand, not to him, but to Yara coming toward her with Irdan between herself and the attendant.

James shut the door before he could see whether they reached it.

Chapter 14 — A very useful rope

The linen stair ended at a shutter secured by two wooden catches. Szik lifted the first. The second had warped into its socket.

Below them someone tried a door.

“Quickly,” James said.

Szik put his knife beneath the catch.

“Slower, then. Would you like slower?”

The wood split. Wind drove the shutter inwards and hit him in the face.

He sat down hard on the top step.

Through the opening James saw a strip of night sky, a snapping length of cloth, and the stone rail Szik had described before the feast. It looked further away from here.

Szik wiped his nose. His fingers came away bloody.

“Found the wind.”

Wind clawed along the narrow balcony. James edged out with his sword in his left fist and the wounded right crushed beneath his arm. Blood had soaked through his shirt, warm against his belly, cold wherever the gusts found it. The robe-end he had wrapped around the hand was already sodden. Each movement opened the cut afresh.

Below, the feast court was emptying through two wide doors. Servants were guiding the guests away from the inner rooms. James recognized Malvek’s silver cap and the cousin’s feather fan. Hadrun was with them, one hand on the merchant’s back, moving him with firm, unceremonious shoves.

James called his name.

The wind took it.

Hadrun looked up anyway.

For a moment James thought he would stop. Then Malvek caught his sleeve, and Hadrun put his head down and went on.

Across the alley two workmen were taking down the long canopy. They had freed its lower edge and were hauling the cloth onto the opposite roof. The iron frame remained, but half its struts had been unhooked. One swung against the wall beneath James’s feet.

“That was our way,” he said.

“It still is.”

The stolen rope lay across Szik’s shoulders, brown and glossy in the light from below. Something shifted under its close braid when his grip tightened. Warm resin scented the cold air around it. Both blunt ends wore little dark caps; neither moved, though James had an unpleasant desire to know which was the head.

Korvash watched it move.

“Put that down.”

“Then we stay here.”

“I don’t like it.”

“You don’t have to marry it.”

Szik laid it along the balcony, doubling it back as he measured. Ten paces of rope, a little more than twice the gap to the other roof. He made a loop in one end, touched the crossing strands with two fingers, and said, “Hold.”

The loop closed on his hand.

He swore.

“Loosen.”

It slackened. He pulled his fingers free and sucked them, glaring at the coil as though it had deliberately misunderstood.

James heard the lower stair door open.

“Can you use it or not?”

“Yes.”

Szik made the loop again, this time with his fingers outside it. He threw it toward the opposite rail.

It fell short and struck the wall.

One of the workmen looked up. Szik pulled the rope back and threw again. The loop caught a stone finial, slipped, and hung beneath its carved head.

“Leave that!” the workman shouted.

“Gladly. Pull it tight first.”

“That’s new stone!”

The shutter banged behind them. Korvash caught it with his good arm before it hit James in the back.

Szik gave the rope a long, hard pull. The loop held beneath the finial. He touched the line near his own end.

“Hold.”

It stiffened through his hands, taking up the slack. He fastened the loose end around the base of the balcony rail with an ordinary knot.

“Touch it when you tell it,” he said. “And don’t tell it anything clever.”

He swung over the rail.

James looked down. There was an awning frame, a drop beyond it, and people in the alley moving lamps out of the wind. Szik lowered himself until his feet found the nearest iron strut. It dipped beneath him. He leaned forward on the rope, took three quick steps along the strut, and jumped.

The far workman caught his sleeve.

Together they fell behind the stone rail.

Szik’s head appeared again. He was grinning.

“Fine!”

The workman hit him in the back of it.

The first pursuer reached the top of the stair. James thrust through the shutter gap and felt his point catch cloth. The man retreated. James pulled the door closed with his elbow.

“Go,” he told Korvash.

The Vilikari looked at the iron frame.

“It won’t take me.”

“No. Use the rope.”

“With this?” He lifted his left hand an inch, and stopped.

On the opposite roof Szik was pulling the line free of the finial. James nearly shouted at him before he saw what he meant to do. Szik passed it around the thick corner pier instead and tied it low. The workmen helped him haul. One had understood the problem; he put his shoulder against the line to test the anchor.

Szik threw the free length back.

Korvash caught it with his right hand. He dropped his axe into its belt loop, passed the rope behind his back, and tried to make a loop beneath both arms.

James had to do it for him. Every touch with his right hand left blood on the rope. He kept the loop low, under Korvash’s chest, and made a hitch in it. He could feel something pulse beneath the braid.

“Hold,” he said.

The hitch closed.

Korvash stared at him.

“Don’t say that again.”

“Then don’t fall.”

The shutter burst outwards.

James stepped into its path and drove his shoulder against it. His sword scraped the wall. On the other side a man shouted for a hook.

Korvash got one leg over the rail, then the other. His right hand held the line. He lowered himself, searching with his foot for the iron frame.

The frame tore out of the wall.

Korvash vanished below the rail. Rope hissed over stone, jerking the loop up under his ruined shoulder. His scream rose out of the alley, huge and raw, stripping all the laughter from the strength they had admired.

Across the alley the two workmen and Szik were dragged to their knees. The pier held. A stone facing broke off and fell beside Korvash, striking the torn canopy below.

The loop had caught him high under the left arm. His legs kicked against the wall. He could not get his good hand between the rope and his throat.

“Loosen it!” James shouted.

“I have to touch the loop!”

Szik was already climbing onto the rail.

James put his back against the shutter, wedged his sword through its iron handle, and leaned out as far as he could. Korvash’s right hand was a pace below his own.

“Give me your wrist.”

Korvash tried. His fingers struck the wall.

“Look at me.”

The big man looked up.

James felt the heat stir behind his eyes. He turned his face away so quickly that he struck his cheek against the rail.

“Your right hand. Up.”

This time Korvash reached him.

James locked his left hand around the wrist. Korvash’s weight hauled him hard against the rail; behind James’s back the shutter battered his wedged sword until the steel began to bend. He could not lift the man. He braced his knees against the rail and held him facing the wall, teeth clenched, buying Szik a few more breaths.

Szik came along the taut upper line on his belly, legs hooked over it. The workmen were shouting at him to stop bouncing. He reached the loop, caught Korvash’s belt with his free hand, and planted his feet against the wall.

“Now,” he said.

James held tighter.

Szik touched the hitch.

“Loosen.”

The loop opened. Korvash dropped the width of a hand and roared again. James’s shoulder hit the rail. He felt his boots begin to slide.

Szik dragged the loop down over Korvash’s ribs, beneath the injured arm, to the broad leather belt.

“Hold.”

It closed there.

For a moment nobody moved.

Korvash hung with his face against the wall. Then his right arm bent. He found a projection in the stone with one foot and took his weight off James’s hand.

“Let go,” he said.

James let go.

Szik climbed backwards. The workmen hauled, and Korvash pushed from the wall. He went across the alley sideways, scraping a long white line through the plaster with one boot. They got him over the far rail by his belt and the back of his green robe.

James heard him being sick.

The hook came through the shutter beside his head.

He ducked, pulled his sword out of the handle, and stepped aside as the door opened. A guard stumbled onto the balcony carrying a short bill. Behind him, at the top of the stair, was the handsome man with the bruised jaw.

His left hand held another knife.

James cut at the bill’s shaft. With his wrong hand he struck too shallow. The guard shoved him into the rail.

“Alive,” the handsome man said. “Mind his eyes.”

James flung the bloody end of his robe into the guard’s face and stepped inside the bill’s reach. He struck with the pommel. The man reeled. James shoved him toward the stair, where he collided with his companion.

“Line!” James shouted.

Szik threw it.

James caught the rope against his chest. He had no time for a loop. He wrapped his left arm around it, put his sword into its scabbard with a clumsy scrape, and climbed onto the rail.

His robe caught on the bill’s hook.

For an instant he was held between the guard and the opposite roof, the embroidered cloth drawn tight across his shoulders. He could hear stitches breaking. The guard hauled him backwards.

James released the robe’s clasp.

The six little nooses came away in his hand.

He jumped.

Silk tore from his shoulders and streamed away in the wind. His boots struck the opposite wall; the impact drove the breath clean out of him. Rope seared his ribs. A hand caught his hair, another hooked his belt, and he was dragged over the rail onto hot stone, choking, with his feet still dangling above the alley.

Szik rolled him clear.

On the opposite balcony the guard stood holding a length of embroidered cloth. The wind wrapped it around his bill and pulled him sideways.

The handsome man watched from the shutter.

James spat blood off his lip.

“You can keep it,” he called.

Then he was too busy breathing to say anything else.

They left by the workmen’s ladder. Szik touched the recovered rope and said, “Coil,” and it wound itself into a compact roll against his wrist. Both workmen stepped away from it.

“The pier,” one said.

James looked at the broken stone facing.

He gave the man three silver pieces. The man looked at them, looked at Korvash, and decided not to ask for more until Korvash was somewhere else.

Below, a woman with a long pole was keeping people away from the dangling canopy. Two boys held a cart across the alley to stop anyone walking beneath the loose iron. Nobody had been struck. A fruit seller was inspecting a fallen stone embedded in his empty tray.

Korvash sat down on the cart shaft. The woman stopped him putting his bad arm through the gap between the boards.

“Don’t sit there if you’re going to faint.”

“I’m not.”

He fainted.

By the time they got him upright again, six people had told James what had happened at the palace. The accounts included a fire, a chained woman, a bull loose in the feast, and the death of the serpent priest. A servant running from the outer court recognized James and pointed him out.

“That’s one of them.”

People made space. It was unexpectedly pleasant.

Szik took Korvash’s axe while James helped him walk. The big man could move his fingers, though not his shoulder. They put his good arm across James’s back. James’s own hand had stopped bleeding freely and begun to throb with each heartbeat.

“Did you kill him?” the fruit seller asked.

“We brought half his house down,” Szik said.

It was a generous estimate. James did not correct it.

At the alley’s mouth he looked back. Three tall windows burned red above the lower roofs, undimmed by the wind tearing through the city. Somewhere inside that glow stood Yara, beside the people she had refused to leave. The blood drying on his hand began to itch.

The wind struck his bare neck. He closed his hand around the six-noose clasp until its points hurt, then put it in his pocket and helped Korvash toward the lights of the Black Goat.

Part III — A city full of admirers

Chapter 15 — The heroes’ table

By the time they reached the Black Goat, Korvash had recovered sufficiently to object to the way he was being carried.

“I am walking.”

“Then do some of it,” James said.

A boy ran ahead of them into the tavern. There was a scraping of benches, a brief dispute about which table, and then four men came out to take Korvash off James’s shoulder. The largest got under his good arm. Another tried the injured side and received a look which discouraged further assistance.

Inside, someone cheered.

Korvash straightened. James felt him become almost light.

The table they had broken stood near the hearth, braced with new timber. Its top had been wiped clean. A woman moved her supper to make room, taking her stool with her but leaving the jug.

“For the shoulder,” she said.

Korvash drank from it.

He set it down, considered what had just happened in his mouth, and drank again.

James dropped onto the bench. Now that the floor was solid beneath him, his knees began to shake. He could feel the alley opening under his boots, the rope burning his ribs, fingers wrenching his hair to drag him over the stone. He hid his hands beneath the table until the trembling eased. The room smelled gloriously of meat and spilled beer.

Szik laid Korvash’s axe against the wall, beyond the easy reach of the people gathering round them. He kept the coil beneath his gray robe.

“Did you really throw a fountain?” someone asked.

Korvash looked at James.

“Part of one,” James said.

“All the useful part,” Korvash said.

A man laughed so hard that he had to put his cup down. The story began there, with the fountain. Before Korvash had reached the broken door, a dish of meat arrived. Before James had explained why the bars had made it impossible to leave by that route, someone bought wine. Szik supplied the measurements of the drop, adding a little whenever he was interrupted.

“It was one story,” James said.

“Above the other stories.”

“I could see the ground.”

“You had a better place.”

Szik demonstrated Korvash’s journey across the alley with a piece of bread suspended from a spoon. Korvash took the bread off it and ate it. The room approved.

Laughter burst out of James hard enough to stab his ribs. He bent over it, helpless. Korvash roared beside him, tears streaming, his ruined arm cradled across his belly. Szik’s bread-and-spoon performance gave way in James’s head to the workman cuffing him, then to the guard’s face as the fountain came through the door. He tried to drown the laughter in wine and poured half the cup down his shirt.

The woman who had surrendered the table leaned past him to cut Korvash’s meat into smaller pieces.

“Let him do it,” Korvash said, pointing at James.

She looked at James’s bandage.

“With what?”

That started them again.

A broad-backed man carrying a leather satchel stood at the edge of the crowd until Vezhri’s uncle cleared a place for him.

“Ruvet,” the uncle said. “If you want that arm to remain attached, stop laughing and let him see it.”

Ruvet’s beard was tucked inside his collar. He set the satchel down and took Korvash’s wrist. His hands were dry and surprisingly small.

“Can you feel that?”

“Yes.”

“And that?”

“Less.”

He looked into Korvash’s face, then pressed gently beneath the shoulder. Korvash stopped laughing.

Ruvet asked for a back room.

“I’m eating.”

“You can take it.”

They went into the little room behind the hearth with the dish, the jug, and two men strong enough to do what Ruvet asked. James heard a low conversation, a chair being moved, and then a noise which made everyone in the tavern look toward the door.

Szik lowered his cup. Behind the door, Ruvet said something they could not hear. Korvash answered, and James let out a breath.

When Korvash returned, his left arm was bound against his chest and his red shirt had been cut down one side. He looked at the empty place where his meat had been.

Szik put the dish back.

Ruvet inspected James’s hand next. Wine had made James optimistic about this. The optimism lasted until the strip of robe came away.

“Those fingers. Move them.”

James moved them. The little finger was the worst, but it moved.

Ruvet sluiced the cut. Pain flashed from James’s palm to his elbow. The man spread the torn edges, drew out a blood-black thread of embroidery and held it up, demanding to know who had packed such filth into a wound.

“Only the finest,” James said.

The man did not smile. His needle entered the deeper part. James fixed his eyes upon a crack in the wall while each pull of thread traveled through his flesh, a small exquisite torment that no amount of cheering from the room could improve. When it was done, Ruvet bound palm and wrist, leaving the fingertips bare.

“No sword in that hand. Come back if it grows hot, or if the fingers swell. And don’t put your friend’s weight on it.”

“I didn’t.”

“Then find another way to injure it next time.”

Korvash paid him before James could object to the sum. Ruvet took the money, finished the last of their wine, and went out with a parcel of supper from the kitchen.

The room welcomed them back to the business of being brave.

A pair of drovers disputed which roof they had crossed. Someone fetched a piece of charcoal to draw it on the table. The landlord took the charcoal away and provided a broken tile. A woman with a scar across her lip invited Korvash to settle another matter involving a gate and her brother. He accepted before she had finished. Szik told her to return when Korvash could lift his cup without help.

James received an offer to guard a spice caravan, an offer to recover a stolen wife, and an offer to sell him the horse upon which the stolen wife had departed. The second and third came from the same man. James declined both, feeling unusually well qualified.

Hadrun arrived while the drovers were demonstrating the canopy with a towel.

He stood in the doorway long enough to be noticed, then crossed the room and put a bottle on the table.

“From Malvek.”

James looked up at him.

“His regards?”

“His household tie.”

James looked down at the bare shoulder of his shirt.

“It went with the robe.”

“So I told him.” Hadrun pulled up a stool. “He says if you do anything else tonight, please do it in your own clothes.”

Szik uncorked the bottle. Hadrun put a finger over his cup before it was filled.

“Working.”

“You could have stayed to watch us die,” James said.

“I had a merchant.”

“We had one too.”

“Mine paid.”

For a moment the noise around them seemed to diminish. Hadrun looked at James’s bandage, and James looked at the neat beard covering the missing tooth. Then Hadrun moved his finger off the cup.

“One.”

Szik poured.

Hadrun heard the account of the roof with his head cocked slightly sideways. When Szik reached the workmen, Hadrun interrupted to ask whether they had been paid. James told him they had.

“Good. Malvek knows their employer.”

“Does Malvek know everyone?”

“Everyone to whom you might owe money.”

The landlord brought another lamp. Outside, the wind had dropped enough for the shutters to be opened. The smell of hot dust came in, and the sound of someone arguing over a camel that would not get to its feet.

James was reaching for a piece of flatbread when Orun came through the crowd.

The older man had not put on his coat properly. One side hung from his shoulder, and there was a horsehair rope still wound around his hand. He looked from James to Korvash, then at the empty space beside Szik.

“Where is she?”

James put the bread down.

“Alive.”

“Where?”

“In the house. We saw her.”

Orun pulled the unused stool away from Hadrun and sat without appearing to notice him.

“Did you speak to her?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

His own grand voice seemed still to hang above the table, proclaiming the leap, the sword, the wind. Under Orun’s gaze he could scarcely push air past his tongue.

“She’s changed.”

Orun’s hand tightened on the rope.

“Her face?”

“Some scales. On her hand. She knows who she is.”

“Then why isn’t she here?”

“The house has a working. We saw it take hold of people. Their hands moved when he moved.”

Orun leaned toward him.

“Yara?”

“She was in the room.”

James watched the older man take the account and fit it to the thing he had feared. His shoulders dropped.

“I told her,” he said.

James rubbed his uninjured thumb along the rim of his cup.

“We damaged part of it. There’ll be another way in.”

“Tonight?”

“Look at us.”

Orun looked. He put the rope on the table and pressed both hands against his knees.

“She must have been frightened.”

James remembered her level voice asking for her hand. He drank.

Hadrun had not moved. He had heard the differences between this account and the first, when James had been careless enough to say the girl had gone back. Now he looked at the bottle Malvek had sent, and turned it so its better side faced the room.

Orun stayed for a while. He asked whether Yara was eating, whether she slept in a room with a door, whether her burn still showed. James answered what he could. Before leaving, Orun asked them to send to the horse lines if anything changed. He left his rope on the table. Korvash called him back for it.

The crowd had thinned around them. Somewhere behind James a man was still explaining the fountain.

Vezhri came from the kitchen carrying a covered dish.

She looked at the bandages before she looked at James. Her yellow headcloth had come loose at one ear. He remembered the little bottle and the three shallow cups.

“You knew.”

She set the dish down.

“I heard you went into the tending rooms.”

“Did you think we wouldn’t?”

“I thought you’d ask him. I told you to ask.”

“After you put it in us.”

She glanced toward her uncle. He was arguing with someone at the other end of the room.

“There are things I can tell you now.”

“Now.”

“Yes.”

“After that.” James raised his bandaged hand. “You can be quiet.”

He looked directly into her face.

Vezhri’s mouth remained open around the word she had meant to say. Her fingers stiffened upon the cloth. Only the pulse beneath her jaw moved, fluttering so fast that James could see it from his chair.

A warm stillness gathered behind his eyes. The room seemed to fall into its proper places. At last she was listening.

He turned away.

When he looked back she had gone. The cloth had slipped off the dish. Beneath it lay strips of soft meat, cut small enough to eat with one hand, with a spoon for the sauce.

Nobody touched them.

Later, Szik began telling Korvash about the height again. Korvash disputed it. James closed his eyes and listened, his bad hand resting on his knee, while the landlord counted cups and the wind shook loose grit from the shutters.

Chapter 16 — The empty mounting

Irdan could not remember the first kiss.

He remembered the linen stair, and the basket Yara had stood on to reach a shelf. He remembered telling her that the basket would not hold her. He even remembered the face she had made at him. But when she asked what happened next, he looked ashamed and said he had probably spoiled it.

“You didn’t.”

“Then why won’t you tell me?”

Yara sat beside him on the passage floor with the remembered warmth of that kiss still cruelly clear in her mouth. Opposite them Neshet leaned beneath a blood-spotted cloth, eyes half shut. Broken timber grated over the chamber floor beyond the open door. Every scrape made Irdan’s fingers jump.

“Because you might remember it yourself.”

Irdan picked at a loose thread in his tunic. His right hand trembled. He put it beneath his thigh.

“I remember wanting to,” he said.

Yara rested her head against the wall.

“That will do for tonight.”

Neshet shut her eyes. She had been awake whenever Yara looked at her, even when she pretended otherwise. The bandage around her forearm was too tight now. Yara loosened it, and Neshet made a small sound through her teeth.

An attendant brought a basin. He was the man who had put down the chair and helped Irdan. His name was Pellash. Yara had known him for three weeks and had spent most of that time thinking of him as the man who always found a reason not to carry the heavy basket. Now he had carried two people out of the chamber, and his clean robe was streaked with their blood.

“Here,” he said.

“Water?”

“What else would you wash with?”

She looked at him.

He lowered the basin carefully.

“Yes. Water. From the kitchen.”

Yara wet a corner of Neshet’s sleeve and cleaned the blood from her face. The bruise at the temple had begun to rise. Neshet opened one eye.

“Am I handsome?”

“Very.”

“Then fetch the mirror before it goes.”

Irdan smiled. Yara bent closer to the basin.

Inside the chamber someone called for Neshet.

“She can’t,” Yara said.

Pellash stood.

“I’ll tell them.”

He went in. Yara heard his voice, then another, then the scrape of something heavy. She looked around the doorpost.

Thaliss knelt amid the wreckage of the middle station. His white robe lay folded beyond the marked line, untouched by the blood soaking the cloth beneath his nose. Two attendants had carried the lower bowl to his knees. A third held a lamp so close that its heat reddened his cheek, but dared not draw it back.

The middle cradle had broken into hooked pieces. One lay by the bench, with a thread of James’s robe caught on it. Thaliss examined the piece nearest his knee, turned it over, and set it aside.

“Not with a new bracket,” he said. “It would cool before the joining took.”

The lamp bearer shifted his feet.

“We could heat—”

“The stone underneath.”

He pointed to a hairline crack beneath the torn mounting. Green light kindled deep inside it and ebbed. Yara had taken it for a reflection. Now she saw it burning through the stone itself.

Thaliss slapped a hand over the break. His fingers spread farther than fingers ought to spread. The breath stopped in his throat.

Pellash took one step toward him.

“Stay there.”

The words were quiet. Pellash stopped.

Yara watched Thaliss turn his palm until the heel covered the break. Blood from his nose fell onto his wrist. He did not wipe it away.

“Bring the narrow stand. From the receiving room.”

“You wanted that tomorrow.”

“I want it now.”

The attendant hurried out through the far door. As he passed Yara he looked down at Irdan, then away.

The young man beside her began to shiver.

Yara went into the chamber.

“Release them.”

Thaliss did not raise his head.

“Get them clear of the line.”

“We have.”

“Further. Into the servants’ court.”

“Then let them go home.”

He looked up at her. Sweat ran between the plates on his chest.

“Can he walk there?”

She hated that she had to think before answering.

Thaliss turned back to the crack. His palm had begun to darken along the line. A smell like scorched hair reached her, although there was no hair on his hand.

“The injury in him is not finished changing,” he said. “If you take him beyond the house tonight, he will feel all of it.”

“Because you made him raise his hand.”

“Because something was broken while it was open.”

“You made him raise it.”

Thaliss shut his eyes. For a moment she thought he would ignore her. Then he said, “Yes.”

The stand arrived. It was a low bronze tripod, too delicate for the weight it would carry. Thaliss told them to put a stone beneath one foot. He directed every movement while keeping his hand over the crack. When the bowl was finally within reach, he drew a narrow strip of green metal from the broken cradle and pressed one end beneath its base.

The other end he laid against his forearm.

Pellash looked away.

Yara did not.

The green strip entered his arm. Skin rose along its edges, parted and closed again, swallowing the metal a finger’s breadth at a time. Thaliss bared his teeth. His other hand clawed his thigh until blood darkened the cloth. Under the flesh the strip bent into a curve no smith had made, climbing from wrist toward elbow.

He lifted his palm from the floor. Green fire remained in the crack. High upon the wall the upper bowl gave one clear, merciless note.

Irdan stopped shivering.

Thaliss remained on his knees, with his newly marked arm resting beside the receiving bowl. He was breathing as a man breathed after running too far in heat. An attendant offered him a cup. He pushed it away without looking.

“Clear the screens. Leave the upper station. We’ll need the whole room.”

“For tomorrow?”

“For the public tending. Send the invitations at first light.”

The lamp bearer hesitated.

“How many?”

“As many as want to come.”

Yara glanced at the space beyond the fallen partition. Without the screens it was a great hall. She could see the upper gallery from here, the shallow tiers she had always assumed were steps for servants. People could stand in them and look directly down at the slab.

“I can tend the lip,” she said.

Thaliss studied her. She held out her empty hands.

“Neshet showed me. She needs to rest.”

“No.”

“Pellash has everyone else to—”

“I have said no, Yara.”

He laid his fingers on the bowl’s rim. Their tips were bright with prepared venom.

She looked at the old place by the floor, now empty. The stained cloth lay beside it where Neshet had dropped it. Nobody was watching that part of the room anymore.

Yara picked it up with the broken wood and took both into the passage.

When the dragging and hammering finally ceased, Irdan slept in a narrow room off the servants’ court. Neshet lay on a pallet beside him. Air slipped through the grille carrying ash and the smell of onions cooling in their fat. Yara sat with her face turned toward it, breathing the kitchen’s ordinary smells as greedily as she had breathed the heat of the tending room.

Yara had washed the basin twice. She had helped Pellash change Irdan’s tunic, had untied his shoes, and had discovered that the right one contained a small polished stone. He had meant to replace the flat piece she had cheated him with at the fountain. She put it on the windowsill where he would see it when he woke.

Pellash brought another blanket. He looked into the empty basin.

“More?”

“No.”

He sat on the end of the pallet. For a while they listened to Neshet’s breathing. It had steadied, though now and then her fingers moved across the cover as though searching for a dropped needle.

“Will you help me move them?” Yara asked.

“To where?”

“Here. To the court. The others as well.”

“They’ll move them for the gathering anyway.”

“I want them near the passage. Before he starts.”

Pellash rubbed a white patch of dried plaster from his thumb.

“You’re going out.”

“Yes.”

“With your father?”

“No.”

He glanced at the token beneath her waist cord. She had tucked it out of sight badly. Everyone in the house knew its shape.

“Neshet will have to answer for that.”

“She can say I took it.”

Pellash considered this without approval. He stood, went to the door, and looked down the passage.

“They won’t put the fevered ones in the tiers,” he said when he returned. “They’ll want them where they can be brought through quickly. If I set the bedding here, no one will want to carry it twice.”

“Can you?”

“A few. Not everyone. And I’m not going to fight the guards.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

“Your three men did.”

“They weren’t my men.”

He nodded toward Irdan.

“He thought they were.”

Yara looked at the little stone on the sill.

Pellash drew the blanket over Neshet’s feet.

“Knock on that grille when you come back. Three quick ones and another. I can hear it from the passage.”

She repeated the pattern softly on the bedframe. He shook his head and made the last knock slower. She tried again.

“That.”

Before dawn she went to the kitchen for bread. The baker gave her the burnt heel without asking why she was awake. She ate it beside the ovens, licking oil from her fingers, and listened to two servants disputing whether the foreign men had brought down a wall or only a screen. One claimed the captain had stolen Thaliss’s sword. The other said he had escaped naked.

Yara laughed before she could prevent it.

Both women looked at her.

“He had a shirt,” she said.

She left with a basket of stained linen on her hip. The tending cloth lay beneath the rest. At the work gate she used the token, kept her face toward the sleepy porter, and asked him whether the outer washer had opened yet.

“Sun’s barely up.”

“They’ve sent me anyway.”

He lifted the bar. Outside, the stones were cool through her thin soles. She set the basket down beyond the corner, took the cloth from it, and left the linen stacked neatly against the wall.

Then she began walking toward the horse lines, changed her mind before the next turning, and asked a bread seller how to find the Black Goat.

Chapter 17 — The coldest man in the room

Korvash woke hungry.

James discovered this because Korvash was standing above his bed with a plate, trying to decide whether to wake him or eat what was on it.

“Yours,” he said, when James opened his eyes.

“Then why are you holding it?”

“Testing your appetite.”

James sat up and discovered that the night had left knives inside him. One lay beneath the ribs, another in his right hand, swelling against Ruvet’s neat bandage. A deep breath found both. His mouth tasted of the Black Goat’s better wine, which he now suspected was merely its ordinary wine in a better jug.

Korvash, apart from the arm tied to his chest, looked magnificent. He had washed the yellow from his neck, combed both plaits, and put on the sleeveless leather coat. His skin shone. There was an energy in the way he held the plate which made James want to throw something at him.

“Does it hurt?”

“Yes.”

“You look cheerful.”

“I’m alive.”

“Be alive more quietly.”

On the other bed Szik pulled the cover over his head.

Korvash set the plate down and went out. James ate a little of the bread. He could hear people below, already loud, and the landlord telling someone that Korvash’s grandfather had pulled a horse out of a river by its tail. Korvash’s grandfather had done no such thing, to James’s knowledge. The uncle had pulled a goat out of a latrine. The difference would probably survive the morning.

He dressed slowly. The rescued company clasp looked absurd on his old coat, but he put it there anyway.

Szik’s hand appeared from beneath the cover.

“Close the shutter.”

“It’s closed.”

“The other one.”

“There isn’t another one.”

“Then find something to put over that one.”

James hung a towel across the narrow gap. Szik sighed and emerged. His eyes were watering.

“Bad wine,” James said.

“You drank more.”

“I have practice.”

Szik blinked at the shaded wall. He did not make the answer James expected.

Downstairs, a man had brought a door for Korvash to lift.

It lay flat across two benches, painted blue, with a heavy iron lock still attached. Korvash stood over it considering the best place to grip. The woman with the scarred lip watched with folded arms.

“You said it was a gate,” Korvash told her.

“It keeps him out. That makes it a gate.”

The landlord appeared from behind the hearth.

“No.”

Korvash looked up.

“Not you again.” The landlord pointed at the repaired table. “I’ve paid the carpenter once.”

“We paid you.”

“You paid for supper.”

“I helped mend it.”

“You held it while a man did work.”

Korvash began to explain that holding was sometimes the larger part of work. He hooked his good hand beneath the door and heaved. The weight dragged across his chest into the bound shoulder. His grin vanished. James heard a breath stop between his teeth, and for an instant the great man’s face was the color of old tallow.

James caught the near edge before it slid off the bench. It cost him a sharp pain through the bad palm, though he had used the other hand.

The landlord waited for Korvash to sit down.

Korvash withdrew his arm and sat down.

The woman patted his uninjured shoulder.

“My brother will still be a fool next week.”

She called for two men to help her carry the door out. Korvash watched it go with the grief of a man watching his horse sold.

At breakfast, Vezhri served the other tables.

James saw her uncle ask her something. She shook her head. The uncle looked toward James, then brought their food himself. It was good food, with very little conversation attached.

Szik kept his back to the window. He had tied a strip of thin cloth around his forehead so its edge shaded his eyes. James thought it made him resemble a poorly disguised thief, but he kept this observation to himself because they needed bread and the bread basket was near Szik.

Neresh’s daughter came while they were eating. Her mother wanted to see them. Beltek had slept. His foot was less swollen. Could the captain come before the day grew hot?

James was glad of something straightforward to do.

Sun poured down the dyers’ walls and struck the running gutters white. Beneath the glare their purple and red waters looked black as clotted blood. Szik hugged the shrinking shade. At a crossing he stopped with his sleeve pressed to streaming eyes, and a small hand darted toward his belt.

James caught the wrist.

The boy attached to it looked offended.

“Not that one,” James said.

The boy twisted free and disappeared beneath a cart. Szik had not moved.

“Did you know him?”

“No.”

“Then why—”

Szik followed James’s hand to his purse. He felt for the tie and said something too low for James to catch.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

He walked on. At the next bright crossing he used Korvash’s shadow.

Beltek was in Neresh’s inner courtyard, on a low bed beneath a vine. His gold patches had dulled at the edges. His aunt had arranged a padded support beneath the injured foot, and a girl was peeling fruit beside him with elaborate concentration.

He smiled when he saw them.

“Did you get her?”

James sat at the foot of the bed.

“Not yet.”

The smile went away.

“She’s alive. We spoke to her.”

“Was she in the room?”

“Yes.”

Beltek turned his head toward the wall. His breathing had quickened. The girl stopped peeling.

James had come prepared to tell him about the fountain, the roof, all the things which ought to make a young man laugh. He found he could not begin any of them.

“You’re safe here,” he said.

Beltek nodded too quickly.

“No one’s going to take you back.”

The young man’s hand plucked at the cover. James reached across and put his own uninjured hand over it.

“Look at me.”

Beltek looked.

“Stop worrying. You’re safe.”

The plucking stopped.

Everything else stopped with it. Beltek’s eyes remained wide and wet; the breath still fluttered too fast in his chest, but he could no longer move to ease it. The fingers beneath James’s hand were rigid as sticks. James had silenced the signs of fear and left the fear trapped inside him.

The girl stood up. The fruit rolled off her lap.

“Beltek?”

James let go and looked away.

Behind him Korvash shifted his weight. Nobody spoke until Beltek drew a ragged breath and covered his face with his arm.

Neresh came out through the hanging cloth.

She took one look at the bed and told James to come with her.

Neresh’s room was cool, its bare walls holding the voices close. An iron knife lay beside a bowl of nuts. She crushed one beneath its handle and flung shell and kernel back together. James watched the knife settle beside her hand.

“He was better before you came.”

“I was trying to—”

“I know.”

She put the knife down.

“I have a way of ending this man’s work. You needn’t go through his door again.”

James looked toward the courtyard.

“Who?”

“Someone who will listen at the Iron Well. Come after the morning callers have gone. Bring the girl, if you can find her. I would rather hear what happened from someone who still has both hands free.”

He did not ask whether she meant Yara or her own niece, and Neresh did not clarify it.

When they returned to the tavern, Vezhri was alone near the hearth, cutting yesterday’s bread into a pot. Her uncle was outside arguing about the door.

James stopped beyond the table.

“I won’t look at you.”

Her knife stopped.

He turned a stool around and sat facing the empty fireplace. After a moment Szik sat beside him. Korvash remained standing, then remembered the morning’s lesson and leaned carefully against the wall.

Vezhri resumed cutting.

“The first cup,” James said. “Tell us.”

For several strokes she said nothing.

Then she said, “Venom. Prepared in the house. It doesn’t work like a bite.”

“We know it doesn’t.”

“You didn’t know what you were asking.”

“I didn’t ask.”

The knife stopped again.

“No,” she said. “You didn’t.”

He waited. From outside came the landlord’s voice insisting that a door could not be left against his wall merely because it was heavy.

“Thaliss gave me the bottle,” she said. “Not for you by name. For people who wanted an introduction. Men came here boasting they would be welcomed, then ran at the first thing they felt. If they had the beginning before they went, they would know it wasn’t something to fear.”

Korvash made a noise in his throat.

Vezhri put the knife down.

“I took it. I still take it. This arm used to lock so hard in winter I couldn’t carry a tray. Now I can.”

James looked at the ashes rather than turn toward the scar.

“Did he tell you he could do that? What happened last night?”

“No.”

“Did you ever see it?”

She did not answer at once.

“At the tending, we stood together. I thought I was good at it.”

Szik shifted on his stool.

“How much did you give us?”

“The shallow cups. One each.”

“Enough?”

“For a beginning. He says the next must be chosen.”

Szik pushed his breakfast cup away.

“We’ve shared jugs since.”

“Not that bottle. It has to be prepared. You won’t give it to someone by drinking their wine.”

He left the cup where it was.

James felt Korvash look at him.

“Was it something to fear?” Vezhri asked.

“Yes,” Korvash said.

He said it before James could decide how to answer.

For a while they listened to bread falling into the pot.

“I came last night to tell you where the kitchen door was,” Vezhri said. “In case you went back.”

James kept his face toward the hearth.

“Does he know where we are?”

“Everyone knows where you are.”

The front door opened. A stripe of sunlight crossed the floor. Szik flinched and put a hand over his eyes.

Yara stood in it.

Her blue gown was dirty at the hem. One thin shoe had split at the toe. She looked at Korvash’s binding, James’s bandage, the fine remains of breakfast, and the boy outside who had followed her there carrying a stick like a sword.

“Which one of you killed Thaliss?”

The boy looked eagerly at James.

“No one,” James said.

“That will relieve him.”

She shut the door on the boy and came to the table.

“Where’s my father?”

“Horse lines.”

“You’ve spoken to him.”

“Last night.”

“What did you tell him?”

James heard Vezhri carry the pot away. Szik had become interested in a crack in the plaster.

“That you were alive.”

“And why I stayed?”

He looked at her bare hands. The bronze lay beautifully along the knuckles. He could remember his own hand closing over them.

“We’ll tell him together.”

“You’ll tell him.”

Yara pulled out a stool. Korvash pushed the bread toward her without asking. She took a piece, ate it so quickly that she had to stop to swallow, and took another.

Then she unwrapped a small stained cloth and laid it on the table, well away from the food.

“He’s moved the bowl,” she said.

Chapter 18 — Names at the Iron Well

The Iron Well crouched within its yard like a black mouth in a wasted face.

Mud brick enclosed it. The gate hung from leather loops, and a dead fig tree clawed the sky beside the wall, its branches polished by wind and the hands of waiting people. Ropes had worn the well’s broad iron collar smooth. There was no rope now, nor any bucket, nor the splash of someone drawing water. James disliked the silence before he had crossed the threshold.

Neresh was waiting beneath the tree with a man in a pale cap.

Hadrun stood beside them.

James stopped in the gateway.

“Malvek thirsty?”

“Malvek is angry. I’ve taken the morning away from him.”

“What for?”

Hadrun looked at the company clasp on James’s coat.

“Collection.”

Yara went past both men and joined Neresh. She had borrowed a pair of sandals from the landlord’s wife. They were too large, and the borrowed straps had rubbed one heel raw. She had refused an offer of money for better ones until after she had spoken to her father, a decision James thought unnecessarily exact until he remembered whose money he had offered.

Neresh introduced the man in the pale cap as her mother’s brother. She did not give his name. He had a soft, round face and a narrow mustache that followed the upper lip almost to its corners. His hands rested on the head of a walking stick. When he smiled at Yara, she smiled back despite herself.

“Your friend is at home with us,” he said. “You may see him afterward.”

“Thank you.”

“No thanks yet. We have a proposition.”

He moved toward the well. There was a low bench beside it and a piece of shade large enough for two people. Korvash accepted the shade. Szik remained near the gate, where he could see the yard without facing the bright sky above it.

James looked down the shaft.

Far below lay a circle of water that swallowed the daylight. He leaned over the collar. The darkness did not change, yet he had the sudden, bodily certainty that something beneath it had turned its face upward.

He stepped back, cold along both arms.

Neresh’s uncle opened a cloth on the bench. Inside lay a pair of shears, a short length of black-and-red braid, and three small stones.

“I understood our introduction settled the favor,” James said.

“It did,” Neresh said. “This is another offer.”

“Paid how?”

“With what you bring to it,” her uncle said. “You have an injury. We have one. We join them, and someone goes to settle the man.”

“Thaliss.”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

The man smiled as though James had asked for a cook’s seasoning.

“Someone who can reach him.”

James saw the bars crashing down, his fingers opening against his will, the sword skidding across stone. Let someone else go through that door. He could sit here in the shade with a cup, his bandage clean, while the thing below the well reached into Thaliss’s splendid house. Relief warmed him so swiftly that he began searching for a respectable name to give it.

“What happens to the people inside?” Yara asked.

“The house will be quieter without him.”

“Irdan’s hand started shaking when the bowl broke. When he joined it again, it stopped. What happens if you kill him?”

Neresh’s uncle looked at her more closely.

“What would you prefer?”

“I want to know what you would do.”

“That depends upon the way we find.”

“Would you use the attendants?”

“If one came to us.”

“Would you send one back?”

He did not pretend not to understand.

“If that were the way.”

Yara folded the stained cloth more tightly between her hands.

“Neshet can barely stand. Pellash is moving sick people. They aren’t going to kill him for you.”

“They might wish to.”

“Then ask them. Don’t arrange it here.”

Neresh rubbed the edge of her sleeve between finger and thumb. She had not looked at James since they entered.

“Beltek wished to leave,” she said. “He sent us word twice. The house answered both times that he was not ready.”

Yara’s chin lifted.

“I know.”

“Now you know.”

Yara looked away first.

The uncle waited until the silence had settled, then picked up the shears.

“Captain. We can waste the cool part of the day making each other sorry. Or you can tell me whether you want him dead.”

James did. He wanted it with an uncomplicated appetite which had survived both breakfast and the shame of Beltek’s courtyard. He looked at his stitched hand.

“Yes.”

“Good. Then give me a name.”

“Thaliss.”

“A name you have carried longer.”

A sound rose from the shaft: one thick drop striking water, or something wet drawing a breath. Szik moved closer to the gate. The shears lay bright and open upon the cloth.

“What sort of name?” James asked.

“Someone who would know why you brought it. Someone whose absence has not let you stop answering them.”

James looked at Neresh. She gave him no help.

“Must they be dead?”

“No.”

“Does anything happen to them?”

The uncle laid the shears down.

“Not by our hand.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer I can give you.”

Hadrun stepped into the edge of the shade.

“Othmar.”

James turned so quickly that pain went through his ribs.

Hadrun’s face remained almost friendly. That was the worst of it.

“You have been carrying him,” he said.

“No.”

“You haven’t put him down in front of anyone I know.”

Neresh’s uncle held the braid above the cloth, waiting.

James could have said the name. It had already been spoken, after all. He could have looked into the shaft, agreed, and let the morning continue toward something that might spare them another palace door.

Othmar’s handwriting came back to him with hideous clarity: the long uprights, the joined last letters, ink drying on damp paper beneath a guttering lamp. He remembered scraping out the second attempt. The third had been good enough to ruin a man.

“No,” he said again.

The uncle lowered the braid.

“Then another.”

“We’re not doing it.”

Neresh stood away from the tree.

“James.”

He had not heard her use his name before.

“I want him dead,” he said. “I won’t give you somebody else to reach him.”

“The name is not a victim.”

“You said you couldn’t tell me what happens.”

The uncle wrapped the shears in the cloth. He did it without haste or resentment.

“I cannot.”

Korvash got to his feet. The movement was awkward with the bound arm, but when he stood beside James the yard seemed smaller.

Hadrun looked from one to the other.

“You still owe me,” he said.

“What did you come to ask for?”

“Your name beside mine when Malvek hires the west-road escort. He’s heard about you all morning. It would improve the rate.”

James laughed once, without pleasure.

“That’s it?”

“For this morning.”

“Then ask for that. Leave Othmar out of your bargaining.”

Hadrun’s hand moved toward his beard, then stopped.

“Easy for you to say now.”

“Yes.”

James walked to the gate. He could feel everyone watching his back. At the threshold he turned to Neresh.

“Can Yara still see Beltek?”

“Of course she can.”

He nodded and went out.

They found a strip of shade behind a shut grain shop. Yara had gone with Neresh, but Korvash and Szik followed James without speaking. He would have preferred an accusation.

James sank onto an empty sack in the grain shop’s shade. Chaff scratched through his trousers; old mouse droppings soured the air. Across the lane an infant howled against the indignity of a bath. He envied it the freedom to make that much noise.

Korvash leaned against the wall.

“Othmar,” he said.

James looked down at his hand.

“We held a mill outside Vaust. Before either of you joined. The ground below it was wet. Two companies went out along the causeway and one came back.”

Szik sat on his heels, carefully out of the sun.

“The wounded were in the mill yard. We’d brought them up in carts. Then the word came to hold the upper road, and nobody sent carts back.”

He could see the ruts filling behind the wheels. One man had lain beneath a cart with his hands over his belly, shouting at anyone who tried to move him into the rain. James remembered the white soles of his feet. He could not remember his name.

“So you did,” Korvash said.

“Yes.”

“That’s the tale you told us.”

James could remember telling it. He had been standing on a bench, with one boot on the table, in a room where somebody liked the sound of his voice enough to buy more wine.

“We left the road. Got most of them out. Lost the road while we were doing it.”

“How many?” Szik asked.

“Thirty-one brought back. Four died before the next town.”

“And the road?”

“I don’t know.”

Szik waited.

“I never got a number. Men behind us, wagons. Some made it through the fields.”

Korvash slid down the wall until he was sitting. He moved his bound arm a little and set his jaw against the pain.

“When they asked why I’d moved, I said Othmar sent an order. He was on the ridge. They couldn’t fetch him immediately. So I wrote one.”

James held out his bandaged right hand, absurdly, as though there were still ink on it.

“His name. His hand. I said a runner brought it.”

Szik looked at the hand and then at James.

“Did they hang him?”

“No.”

“Did they hang the runner?”

“There wasn’t a runner.”

“Did they find one?”

James shut his eyes.

“No. Othmar came back with men who’d been beside him the whole afternoon. They couldn’t make the order stand. But his captaincy went with mine. For the quarrel, they said. For the road. I let the argument stay unsettled.”

“Where is he?” Korvash asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You could ask Hadrun.”

“Yes.”

The infant across the lane fell silent. Water splashed into the gutter.

Szik drew a line through the dust with the side of his shoe.

“When you tell me to go left,” he said, “I’d like to know whose left it is.”

James looked at him.

“Mine.”

“Good.”

He got up. For a moment he had to press his sleeve over his eyes before he could face the lane.

Korvash stayed seated, broad back against the cracked wall. James waited for the disgust, the accusation, anything that would let him answer. The Vilikari put out his good hand.

“Help me up.”

James did. The weight pulled at his shoulder, and Korvash smelled of hair grease, wine and the sharp salve Ruvet had put beneath the binding.

They went to wait for Yara by Neresh’s gate. James did not tell the story again when she came out. She did not ask for it. Beltek had given her a small paper packet of dates, and she passed them round while they walked.

At the next corner she stopped beside a wall covered in old notices. A man was pasting a new sheet over a torn proclamation. It bore the house’s coiled mark, drawn large enough to be seen from across the lane.

Beneath it, in a careful local hand, were the words: water, shade, and the tending of those who suffer. The paste still glistened. Beyond the wall the city baked beneath a sun that made all three promises sound like mercy.

The man smoothed the sheet with the flat of his palm.

“Tomorrow at last light,” he told them. “All welcome.”

“Not the day after?”

“Changed this morning. There’ll be men at the wells to tell people.”

She looked at James.

Yara walked on. After a moment James followed, with a date stone in his cheek and the taste of its sweetness still in his mouth.

Chapter 19 — Half a share

At the Oasis Well, the keeper was refusing to be famous.

A woman had recognized Korvash and called him the man who broke the serpent’s house. Korvash had inclined his head with the careful modesty James knew meant he was delighted. The keeper went on tipping water into a marked jar.

“A whole share,” Korvash said, laying his pebble on the coping.

“Half.”

“Yesterday it was a whole.”

“Yesterday there was more water.”

The keeper’s left hand, with its three remaining fingers, covered the token until the jar had been moved away. He had a knife at his belt and the expression of a man who had been answering the same question since dawn.

Behind them the queue crowded into a reed screen’s narrow shadow. Pitch softened on patched buckets. Water skins hung limp from darkened shoulders. A boy hugged a clay pot against his belly with the care he might have given a sleeping child, turning with it whenever another body pressed too near.

“Has something happened?” Yara asked.

“Wind filled the north trough with grit. The carriers from the upper cistern are late. That’s what has happened. If the trough clears and the carriers come, I’ll give the rest.”

“Could you write that down?” a man in the queue asked.

The keeper looked at him.

“On what?”

A few people laughed. The man did not. He was holding two empty skins, and the straps had rubbed dark patches into his shoulders.

Korvash lifted the half-filled jar with his right hand. James saw the effort of keeping it away from the injured side.

“Put it down,” he said.

“I’ve got it.”

The argument began behind them over a place in the queue. Someone had left to fetch a smaller vessel. Someone else had moved forward. By the time James turned, two women were holding their jars away from a man who had seized another man’s collar.

Korvash set the jar down.

“Don’t,” James said.

The Vilikari was already between them.

He caught a wrist and shoved the second man with his bound forearm. Pain struck before either man could. Korvash went white. The fist gripping the collar jerked harder, wrenching the injured shoulder round.

Yara stamped on the fellow’s foot. He yelped and lunged for her; she was gone, and Szik had whisked the jar out from under them. James drove his left arm between the men and charged one backward into the screen. Reeds cracked, dust burst into his eyes, and the whole contrivance descended on three heads.

The keeper came round the well carrying a dipper.

“Break that,” he said, “and you can all stand in the sun.”

The man beneath James stopped struggling. The other looked at the dipper as though deciding whether it counted as a weapon. Korvash sat on the coping, very carefully.

Together, without further heroism, they lifted the screen back onto its posts.

The boy with the clay pot had not spilt anything. He looked immensely proud.

“Why don’t you go to the house?” one of the women asked the quarrelling men. “They’re giving water tomorrow. Perhaps they’ll give you some sense with it.”

“My sister’s going,” said the man with the skins. “Her leg’s been bad since harvest.”

“Mine went last year.”

“And?”

“She walks.”

Yara turned toward the woman.

“Does she go back for tending?”

“Sometimes.”

“Ask her to wait.”

The woman drew her jar closer.

“For what?”

Yara opened her mouth, then closed it. James watched her look at the queue, the children, the small amount of water in Korvash’s vessel.

“There was trouble in the house last night,” she said. “People were hurt.”

“So I heard.” The woman glanced at Korvash. “By a fountain.”

Yara’s scales darkened along her fingers. She let the woman pass.

James paid a boy to carry their water back to the tavern. Korvash objected until the boy offered to race him for the fee, then accepted the arrangement with a dignity somewhat spoiled by Szik’s laughter.

When the queue moved away from the coping, James asked the keeper about the house’s water.

“They have their own covered cistern,” the keeper said. “Roof water, brought water. Deep enough if they’ve kept it clean.”

“Do you send any?”

“Two carts this afternoon. Paid for before this wind. I won’t take it out of these people’s shares.”

“Which entrance?”

The keeper studied him.

“Why?”

“We left something there.”

Yara made a disgusted sound.

“People,” she said. “We left people. Some can’t walk.”

The keeper’s gaze moved to her hand. He nodded toward the southern lane.

“Back court. The carts turn by the bakehouse. Wide enough for a litter, if that’s what you mean. Don’t put a sword through my carters.”

James said he would not.

The keeper held out Korvash’s pebble.

“Come back after the upper carriers. You’re still owed half.”

Orun sat among the horse lines with a bridle stretched across his knees, worrying sound leather with a needle. Horses cropped their feed around him. He had set a second bowl beside his own and left it empty.

He looked up when Yara called him. For a moment he stayed seated with the leather stretched between his hands. Then he stood so quickly that the bridle fell into the dust.

She let him embrace her. James saw her close her eyes against his shoulder.

Orun held her away to look at her face. His fingers touched the bronze at her wrist and stopped.

“Does it hurt?”

“No.”

“The rest of you?”

“Father.”

“Come inside.”

“No.”

He looked toward the little shelter behind the tethered horses, where one of his men was already getting to his feet.

“We can leave now. The mares are sound. I sent for your things.”

“When?”

“This morning.”

She laughed once. He flinched.

“I asked you weeks ago.”

“I know. I’ve sent now.”

He picked the bridle up and began winding it around his hand. His eyes moved to James.

“She’s out,” he said. “You’ll be paid.”

Yara shook her head.

“I’m going back.”

“No.”

“There are people—”

“Then he can get them. That is what I paid him for.”

“You paid him to get me.”

Orun turned fully toward James.

“You said there was a working. You said she couldn’t leave.”

“I said she was in the room.”

The older man stared at him.

James felt Szik go still beside him.

“She could have come with us,” James said. “She went back for a friend. I let you think it was the working.”

Orun’s grip tightened until the bridle creaked.

“Why?”

James had several answers. None would improve this particular morning.

“I didn’t want to tell you she’d refused us.”

Yara looked down at her borrowed sandals.

Orun took a step toward James. Korvash shifted, but James held up his good hand without looking at him.

“You ate with me,” Orun said.

“Yes.”

“You let me ask you whether she was afraid.”

James nodded.

Orun’s arm moved. For a moment James thought he would strike him with the rolled bridle. Instead he flung it into the shelter. One of the horses jerked back against its tether.

Yara was beside the horse before its tether stopped vibrating. She spoke low, and the lifted head came down toward her. Bronze rested against the velvet muzzle. The horse breathed warm into her palm while Orun stood watching, the anger still in his face and nowhere to put it.

“Irdan is hurt,” she said, still facing the horse. “Neshet too. They helped me when I came. I’m not leaving them there.”

“I can take them.”

“They aren’t here yet.”

“Then let the captain finish his work.”

“He doesn’t know the house.”

Orun looked at James again. This time there was no appeal in it.

“The other forty stays with me.”

James nodded.

“Until they are out. Until I know what I’m being told is what happened.”

Yara’s head came up.

“They?”

“The people you won’t leave.”

Yara did not argue. She stroked the horse’s upper lip with one thumb until the animal began nudging for food.

When they left, Orun remained standing beside the shelter. He did not call her back. One of his men came out carrying the bridle and began brushing dust from the leather.

At the turn Szik asked Korvash for the remount chit.

“Why?”

“To see what they’ll give for it. We may need something that can carry you.”

“I can walk.”

“Then I’ll ask for a horse that admires you.”

Korvash worked the pouch loose with his good hand and tipped the brass chit into Szik’s palm. Szik held it up to the shade, checked the stamp, and put it away.

Chapter 20 — What the cloth carries

The woman at the Temple of the Open Sky made them wait while she ate an egg.

Yara approved of this until she realized how slowly the woman intended to eat it. She broke the shell in small pieces, peeled each away, and set the fragments in the bowl beside her. Above them, the empty sky was hard and blue. Szik had found the only strip of shade and occupied all of it.

James shifted on the low bench.

“We’ve brought something.”

“So you said.”

The woman salted the egg.

Dust had gathered at the rim of the court’s stone basin. Beyond it the pillars cut clean lines into the pitiless blue. Yara had hoped to feel the house fall away from her here, to breathe beneath open sky and be free of it. Yet bronze still lay over her knuckles. When she flexed them she saw Irdan’s hand rising toward a voice he could not refuse.

When the woman had finished, she washed her fingertips and held out her palm.

Yara gave her the wrapped cloth.

The woman opened it without touching the stain.

“Who prepared this?”

“Neshet tended the bowl. I took it from beside her station.”

“Has anyone washed it?”

“No.”

“Good. Tell me what you saw.”

Yara told her. She described the three bowls, the wet lip, the moment Neshet broke the line, the strip of green metal and the way it entered Thaliss’s arm. The woman stopped her twice to ask where people were standing. Once she made Yara get up and place stones in the dust to show the room.

James began to explain the broken screen.

“Let her finish,” the woman said.

He sat back.

When Yara reached the moving hand, she found she could not describe it without raising her own. Her fingers opened. The bronze caught the sun.

The woman looked at them without admiration or disgust.

“Does it answer here?”

“Sometimes I feel something.”

“What?”

“As though I’ve forgotten what I was going to do. But I haven’t.”

The woman nodded, though Yara could not tell whether she understood.

“Anything else from the chamber?”

Szik brought out the black fang.

James stared at it.

“When were you going to show us that?”

“When you stopped bleeding on everything.”

He laid it on the bench in its leather cover. Yara recognized the shape from the store cupboard, although she had never seen it handled. It had always been wrapped in dark cloth, set apart from the rods used for tending.

The woman asked Szik to draw it.

Szik drew it. Sunlight died along the fang’s curved black back. The edge showed pale by contrast, too fine for Yara’s eye to settle on, while little hooked marks crowded the base where the fingers must grip without a guard. She had seen such marks beneath Thaliss’s palms. She wanted the dark wrapping back over them.

Szik tested the edge against the copper binding of its own sheath. It pared up a fine bright curl. On the thicker seam it stopped; he began to put his weight behind it.

“Break it later,” the woman said.

He stopped, leaving the seam scored.

“Stone?” James asked.

“Something that has learned the shape,” the woman said. “Put it there.”

She laid the stained cloth on a flat tile. Szik set the fang beside it.

Nothing happened.

James leaned forward.

The woman moved the cloth closer with a dry stick.

The stain gathered itself. Brown-green liquid drew out of the cloth’s threads and crawled toward the fang’s point, leaving clean linen behind.

Pressure seized the root of Yara’s tongue. She swallowed against it, tasting the old sweetness of the draught. The liquid crept nearer. Her body seemed to be waiting for it to arrive.

The woman moved the tile away.

The line spread back into the cloth.

Szik had stopped rubbing his eyes.

“Do it again,” James said.

The woman looked at Yara.

“Would you?”

Yara nodded before she was entirely sure she wanted to.

This time the cloth approached slowly. Dull green fire filled the hooks on the fang. Yara watched her own right hand turn palm upward. No pain, no struggle—only the hideous ease with which flesh could obey without asking her.

She seized the wrist with her left hand.

“Again?” the woman asked.

James said, “If we can see which—”

“No,” Yara said.

The woman drew the tile away at once.

Yara sprang up and nearly fell. Her knees would not hold steady. Fury rushed into the weakness: at James leaning forward to see more, at the woman’s patient questions, at herself for offering her body as though foreknowledge would make it hers when the command came.

Korvash moved the bench behind her knees. She sat down without thanking him. He set the water cup within reach and went back to the wall.

James looked at the tile.

“I only meant—”

“I heard.”

He closed his mouth.

Stone covered the fang with a small, final clack. The woman sat beside her. Bronze gleamed on Yara’s shaking hands while they waited; no one reached to hide it or scrape it away.

After a while Yara took the water.

“The cloth still knows the preparation,” the woman said. “The fang gives it somewhere to reach. Distance weakens it here. Inside his joined stations, you’ve seen what happens.”

“If we cut the metal?” James asked.

“You broke some. Did that end it?”

“No.”

“Then don’t promise people it will.”

“James held Beltek still,” Korvash said. “In his aunt’s yard. No bowls.”

James looked at him sharply.

“How?” the woman asked.

“With his eyes.”

She turned to James, who kept his face toward the tile.

“Just Beltek?”

“Yes.”

“Then I wouldn’t trust breaking these to take that from you.”

James shifted on the bench. Yara looked at his hands, which had never changed their shape.

She put the cup down.

“Neshet breaks the lip with the cloth. The lamps change. She restores it with the rod.”

“And what did he do when the middle bowl fell?”

“Joined the break through himself.”

The woman looked at the stones marking the chamber.

“Then he is carrying more than before. You might interrupt the common passage at the receiving lip. Long enough for people to move as they choose. I cannot tell you how long. Or what he will use instead.”

James leaned forward again, but this time he waited.

“What about the bodies?” Yara asked.

“The changes?”

“Irdan’s hand. Neshet’s arm.”

The woman folded her own hands together.

“If he is holding pain away, pain may return. If he is holding a wound together, prepare to bind it. I can’t examine them from here.”

Yara saw Irdan’s hand beneath his thigh. She had been angry with the trembling because it frightened him. Now she tried to imagine telling him she intended to do something that might bring it back.

“Can you come?”

“I can wait outside. I will need shade, water and someone who can carry.”

“That we can do,” Korvash said.

The woman looked at his bound arm.

“Someone else.”

He frowned, then nodded once.

They sat awhile arranging only what the woman would need: two low beds, clean cloth, a place out of the rush from the doors. Yara could see those things. She could find them.

When they were ready to go, the woman wrapped the stained cloth in an unstained one. She put the fang back into its cover and handed it to James, asking him to keep them apart until they were needed.

Yara took her parcel.

“Will he recognize it?” she asked, nodding at the fang.

“He may recognize what carries it.”

James shifted the covered blade away from his bandaged hand. Yara noticed that he had begun holding his fingers open, as though to be sure he still could.

At the gate she looked back. The woman had returned to her meal bowl. She was picking the small pieces of eggshell out of the dust one by one.

Yara went to Neresh’s house and asked to borrow a litter.

Chapter 21 — A horse for one man

James found Szik sitting a bay mare as though he had never wanted anything else.

The bay stood in the awning’s shade, one hind hoof resting, sunlight burning copper along her flank. Her mane was cropped short. A white mark lay beneath one ear; when Szik shifted in the saddle, that ear turned toward him while the other kept watch on the yard. A sound animal, beautifully indifferent to every disaster James had brought with him.

The mistress of the yard stood at the mare’s head.

“One man, one saddle, three days’ feed,” she was saying. “After that you feed her yourself. The chit buys the hire. It doesn’t buy the mare.”

“Of course.”

“And if she comes back lame—”

“I’ll come back worse.”

“That won’t help her.”

James stopped by the saddle racks. The mistress noticed him first. Szik followed her glance and smiled with only half his mouth.

“Good horse.”

“So I see.”

“She could carry him.”

“Could he get on her?”

“We could put him on.”

James rested his good hand on the rack. A place beyond the awning had been spread with fresh sand. Yesterday’s loose horse might have gone through here; he could not remember. There had been too many things trying to kill them to take in all the good footing.

“Where does this road go?” he asked.

Szik looked along the lane.

“Out.”

“After that.”

“Away.”

The mistress let go of the bridle.

“I’ll leave you to name the other directions.”

She went to settle a dispute over a saddle girth. Szik watched her go, then eased one foot free of the stirrup.

“She’s sound,” he said. “That matters.”

“Yes.”

Szik slipped past the hanging stirrup into a gap between mare and rack that James would have needed to turn sideways to enter. He came out smiling, then caught himself.

“I like that part,” he said.

James looked at the narrow gap.

“I can see why.”

“And the chit’s his. I wasn’t going to sell her.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You’ve been looking.”

James looked instead at the mare’s clean legs, the old scar above one knee healed soundly. Beyond the city lay wind and open ground. He could almost feel a saddle under him, the horse’s back carrying him away from shuttered rooms and marked cups, until he slept beside some distant wall and no bell rang inside his teeth.

Szik rubbed the saddle’s worn front edge.

“How long does the eye thing last?”

“I don’t know.”

“Perhaps further from the house.”

“Perhaps.”

“Not much use being quick if I can’t see where I’m going.”

James had no useful answer. Szik swung down. In the movement he stayed close to the mare’s shadow, and James saw him measure the bright strip between awning and gate.

The brass chit lay on the saddle rack. James could have taken it. Korvash would have let him, once he explained. He left it where it was.

“Ask whether she can wait until tomorrow.”

“For him?”

“For whoever needs her.”

Szik picked up the chit and turned it between his fingers.

“I was only trying the saddle.”

James nodded.

At the gate he looked back. Szik was speaking to the mistress, with the bay’s bridle in one hand. The other hand had gone into his pocket.

The invitation was waiting at the Black Goat beneath a plate, which had prevented Korvash from dropping grease on it.

The house welcomed Captain James Dravik and his companions to the public tending. It regretted the disorder of the previous evening. Courage should not be divided against courage through misunderstanding. Thaliss would receive them openly, before the assembled people, and answer what they wished to ask.

James read it twice.

“Fine hand,” Korvash said.

“Yes.”

“Could be a trap.”

“Could be.”

“Still. Fine hand.”

Yara took the sheet.

“He wants you where he can see you. And where people see him welcoming you.”

“They already think I killed him.”

“Then he’ll have a surprise for them.”

She folded the invitation along its old crease. Neshet’s token showed briefly beneath her cord when she leaned over the table. James wondered whether she had eaten since the dates, then saw the empty bowl by her elbow and was spared asking.

Hadrun arrived before sunset. He had taken off his fine belt and wore the plain one James remembered, with a buckle that had been repaired badly and continued to hold. He read the invitation standing up.

“This could do you good.”

“Last night did me enough.”

“You walk in, he welcomes you, you speak before witnesses. Whatever the truth of it, you stop being the man who brought a fountain down on a feast.”

Korvash frowned.

“That was me.”

Hadrun ignored him.

“Malvek will want the west-road escort settled soon. He’s seen that you can get out of a house. Show him you can enter one without breaking it.”

James took back the sheet.

“I need you outside.”

“To watch?”

“To keep the south lane open. By the bakehouse. People may come out hurt.”

“How many?”

“I don’t know.”

“Who brings them?”

“The people already there. Yara. Us, if we can.”

Hadrun pulled out a stool and sat. He looked at Yara, at Korvash’s arm, at the invitation. Whatever he saw made him lose the persuasive warmth with which he had entered.

“How long?”

“Until—”

James stopped.

Hadrun waited.

“Until the last of them comes out would be a stupid thing to ask,” James said.

“Yes.”

“From the opening of the gathering. Until we clear the court, or the lane won’t hold.”

“Won’t hold according to whom?”

“You.”

Hadrun leaned back.

“And if you’re still inside?”

James looked at the window. Beyond it, a woman was leading a sleepy child by the hand while carrying the child’s shoes in the other. The small bare feet placed themselves carefully on the cooler side of the paving.

“You call. If I can come, I come.”

“If you can’t?”

“You leave.”

Hadrun picked a splinter from the table’s repaired edge.

“I want that said in front of people.”

“All right.”

“People who will remember the part about leaving.”

“All right.”

“And my men get paid. Beforehand.”

James caught the purse cord and stopped. The bag had grown miserably light: clothes, supper, stitches, a piece of broken stone, each had eaten its share. He could face the palace doors again more easily than he could pour out that small heap before Hadrun and find his courage three silver short.

Yara stood.

“Neresh has carriers coming. I’ll ask her what she’ll pay to have them come back.”

Hadrun watched her go.

“She negotiates better than you.”

“She’s had a difficult day.”

“So have I.”

James put the purse on the table.

They counted what remained.

Chapter 22 — The bargain under the lamps

The landlord would witness nothing until the supper rush had passed.

Neresh accepted this. The well keeper had closing work of his own. Hadrun’s men were not due until the second lamp was lit. James found himself waiting for three people whose evening would have continued perfectly well without him, and was obliged to admire how little any of them cared about his impatience.

Ruvet came to inspect the wounds while they waited.

He unbound James’s hand, washed it and made him move each finger. There was swelling, but less than James had feared. Ruvet was pleased in the grudging way of a man who disliked giving his patients reasons to ignore him.

Korvash’s shoulder pleased him less.

“You lifted something.”

“A door.”

“Why?”

Korvash glanced toward the entrance, where the scar-lipped woman had briefly appeared, seen Ruvet and gone away again.

“It was there.”

Ruvet tightened the binding. Korvash stared into the distance until it was done.

“No lifting. No hanging. No demonstrations.”

Korvash rubbed his knee with his good hand.

“This one still doesn’t hurt.”

Ruvet glanced down. “Enjoy walking on it, then.”

Korvash nodded, more readily than James expected.

“What about—”

“No.”

James paid him with money he had set apart from the count. It hurt more than the washing. He remembered giving three silver to a man for a piece of stone and wished he had bargained.

In the kitchen yard, Yara and Vezhri eased a litter around the back-door turn, squinting through the smoke from supper. Pale new wood braced its old dark rails. Neresh had lent it; Pellash’s patients would fit, if the carriers could get them through without striking the wall. A second frame was coming from the horse lines. James watched Yara brace the rail while Vezhri cleared the corner. Even empty, it barely made the turn.

Vezhri showed Yara how the kitchen passage joined the servants’ court. She drew it with a stick in the dirt, crossed out one line and drew another.

“They walled that door last winter. I always forget.”

Yara crouched beside her.

“Who’ll be there?”

“Cooks. Washers. Whoever’s brought water. The porter at the work gate is usually—”

She stopped.

“Usually?”

“Tomorrow he may be inside.”

Yara looked at her.

Vezhri rubbed the scar on her forearm. It was a small movement, made while she considered where to put the stick next.

“Don’t take another cup,” Yara said.

Vezhri’s face closed.

“You’re wearing what it gave you.”

“Yes.”

For a while neither spoke. Then Vezhri drew the turn beside the oven, marking it wider.

“You’ll get the litter through if you lift the back first.”

Yara looked at the drawing until she could have walked it with her eyes shut.

They made the bargain at a small table beneath two lamps, away from the people still trying to buy Korvash drink.

The landlord sat with a cup of watered wine. Neresh brought the money she had promised, enough for Hadrun’s men but not enough for James to feel wealthy again. The well keeper had washed and changed his shirt. Without the token bag he looked younger.

Six swords stood along the wall within hearing of the lamps. Their belts were patched, their boots whitened with road dust. One man kept flexing a stiff finger below his sword hilt. Another had brought his younger brother to remember where he was going. Hadrun let the boy stay. James looked from face to face before speaking.

James said what the lane was for. Hadrun said where his men would stand. Yara corrected the position of the bakehouse door. They settled it without drawing the whole palace again.

“If the way becomes untenable,” Hadrun said, “I decide.”

James agreed.

“If I can still call, I call twice from the gate. If you can hear me and come, you come. If you cannot, my men leave with whoever is in the lane.”

James agreed again.

“No order from inside cancels that.”

The landlord looked up from his cup.

“No order from inside,” he said.

Neresh counted the money into Hadrun’s hand. Each man saw it before he saw his own portion. There would be a little more afterward, paid by Orun if his riders and the carriers came through together. Orun had agreed to that much through Yara. He had not sent James a message.

The keeper took a small charred piece of wood from beside the lamp and drew a short black stroke across the inside of Hadrun’s wrist. Hadrun pressed that wrist against James’s left forearm, leaving a matching mark.

“So we know whose promise we heard,” the keeper said.

The keeper wiped his fingers on a cloth. The mark on James’s arm smeared when he moved his sleeve.

Hadrun looked at it.

“Try to keep it until tomorrow.”

“I’ll have a man carry it.”

One of Hadrun’s swords laughed. The landlord stood, satisfied, and went back to his cups.

Afterward Hadrun stayed a moment beside James.

“Othmar was in the south last winter,” he said. “Training a merchant’s sons. That’s all I know.”

James nodded.

“Don’t send him a fine account of yourself.”

“I haven’t written it yet.”

“For once.”

Hadrun went to his men. James watched him shoulder through the crowd and thought of the long joins at the end of a signature.

They ate upstairs.

Salve and Korvash’s sweet hair grease filled the little room. Under the shutter cloth Szik had added, lamplight warmed the faces and mercifully spared his eyes. He could almost pass for the thief who had entered the city, and took advantage of it by pretending the afternoon had been entirely ordinary.

He had brought the mare’s saddlecloth from the Silver Stall. It lay folded beneath the bench. The mare herself was being held until the gathering, with the chit still to be surrendered when taken. Two other mounts might be available, the mistress had said, provided no paying caravan called for them first.

“I asked for three magnificent animals,” Szik said. “She offered two possible animals and a magnificent bill.”

Korvash took a bite of bread.

“Can they carry a litter between them?”

“She said they could carry a fool each. I told her we had more.”

James laughed into his cup.

They fell upon the food. Below them a voice rose in praise of a woman who kept marrying horse traders and finished with the best stable in the district. Korvash swallowed, wiped his mouth and supplied another verse at a volume the room could barely contain. Szik listened with grave attention, then corrected the price of the second horse.

“You’ve missed the point,” Korvash said.

“At that price she should have kept the husband.”

James put his cup down before he dropped it.

Yara arrived during the dispute and sat on the edge of the pallet. She had found a pair of boots. They were old, sound, and laced with mismatched cord. James did not ask whose they had been. She stretched her legs and admired them with an extravagance he recognized from his own first good sword.

“My father is bringing four riders to the south lane,” she said. “He wants someone there who knows the horses.”

“Good.”

“He will want to give orders.”

“So will Hadrun.”

“I told him.”

“Good.”

She took the piece of cheese Korvash offered her. They listened to the song downstairs reach its last, improbable purchase.

James fetched the black fang from where he had wrapped it separately in his spare shirt. He put it on the floor between himself and Yara.

“The house knew us when we went in. The line knew me. You saw how it answered through that thing this afternoon.”

Yara set the cheese down.

“It answered through me.”

“Yes.”

“And it may again.”

“Yes.”

He had meant to make an argument for giving it to her: she knew the stations, she could approach without a sword, she had seen the tending done. He let her look at it instead.

She picked up the covered blade with two fingers. Her bronze scales dulled where they touched the leather.

“The cloth goes on the other side,” she said. “Until I use it. No one puts them together to see whether it works again.”

“No.”

“If I say stop, you stop.”

James nodded.

She tucked the fang inside her belt, away from the wrapped cloth, and finished the cheese.

They slept badly and rose late. In the afternoon they dressed for the invitation. Korvash could not get his cut red sleeve over the binding, so Yara helped him remove the sleeve entirely. Szik chose the gray robe because it had deep pockets and had so far failed to catch on anything. James brushed his old coat until its thin places shone.

He fastened the six-noose clasp himself, using his left hand.

From the street came wheels, hooves and the uneven cries of people giving directions over one another. The gathering was already drawing them south.

Yara braced one boot on the sill and hauled its borrowed lace tight. When she straightened, bronze caught the afternoon light above her blue gown. She left her throat bare. At her belt the fang lay hidden on one side, the cloth wrapped separately on the other.

James took up the invitation.

“Ready?”

Korvash reached for his axe with his good hand. Szik checked the shutter before opening the door, judging the light.

Yara went down first.

Part IV — The serpent takes the room

Chapter 23 — Fire in the grass

The smoke reached the Silver Stall before the people did.

At first James took it for cooking. Then the sweetness thickened into the dry, choking smell of a whole hillside burning. A black curl of grass drifted out of the brown air and lodged in Korvash’s plait.

Szik plucked it out.

“Your ancestors are smoking.”

Korvash took the fragment from him and crumbled it between his fingers. Above the roofs, a brown seam had appeared against the sky.

The mistress of the yard was already moving horses. She had tied her skirts up and put a boy at the gate with instructions to keep it clear. The boy was succeeding until a cart entered without its driver, hauled by a mule that had decided the Silver Stall was the end of all roads.

“Where’s our bay?” Szik asked.

“Under the long awning. Don’t stand there.”

James stepped clear as a woman forced her way in with two children and a chicken clamped beneath each arm. Behind her limped a man carrying a bundle. One trouser leg was wet to the knee. His boot had been cut away, exposing an ankle red and glossy as fresh meat; each step dragged a hiss through his teeth.

“East grass,” he said when the mistress asked. “Beyond the outer pens. We thought the ditch would stop it.”

The younger child was carrying the man’s other boot.

The lane filled with ash-streaked people carrying whatever their hands had found: bedding, bridles, a little chest wound three times in rope. An old woman’s pot banged against her knee. They had stopped running. Between stone walls, with the roar of the grass behind them, they stood panting and waited to be told they had come far enough.

The mistress sent the family to the water trough. The man refused to sit until his bundle was off his back. Inside it something moved and began crying.

James caught the bundle as the man’s leg gave.

The baby within was hot, furious and unhurt. James gave it to the woman with the chickens, who thrust one bird at Korvash. He received it automatically with his good hand. The bird looked round the yard from this new height and settled.

A carter wanted the two horses the mistress had provisionally held for them. He had come with money and two empty shafts. James watched the coins enter her palm.

“We asked for those.”

“I said if they were free.”

“They were.”

“They aren’t.”

She went to help harness them.

The burned man shook until his teeth clicked. His wife struggled with the baby, the children and the remaining chicken while the gate boy pointed toward shelters opening on the western caravan ground. A long walk. The man planted his good foot and tried to rise. Pain emptied his face; he sank back against the trough, panting.

Szik disappeared under the awning.

James thought, with a sharpness he disliked, that he had chosen an excellent moment.

Then the bay mare came out, saddled. Szik led her to the trough and gave the mistress the brass chit.

“For him,” he said.

She examined the stamp, looked at the man, and nodded.

“The west ground. Bring her back here when he’s down.”

The burned man protested that he had no money for a horse.

“Neither have I,” Szik said. “Put your good foot there.”

It took three of them to get him into the saddle. James held the stirrup with his left hand; Korvash had to return the chicken before he could help. The mare stood beautifully, suffering their mistakes with a backward glance which reminded James of Ruvet.

Szik gave the bridle to the older child.

“Walk. She knows more about it than you do, but don’t let her buy anything.”

The child nodded solemnly.

James lifted the abandoned boot and tied it to the saddle. It seemed an absurd thing to save, but the man thanked him for it more earnestly than he had thanked anyone for the mare.

When the family had gone, Szik watched the bridle disappear into the crowd.

“The saddle fitted,” he said.

James dusted his coat. He had soot on the sleeve and a small bloody mark near the cuff. The invitation was still dry.

Orun’s riders reached them at the next turning, prevented from going any further by the people coming toward them. Orun himself was at the front. He had put on a leather coat and carried a spear upright to keep its point clear of the crowd.

“The southern road’s filling,” he told James. “They’re bringing the herds through.”

Yara went to his stirrup.

“Pellash needs the bakehouse lane.”

“I know what he needs. I can’t push cattle through people.”

“Then keep them from turning into it.”

Orun looked past her at the narrowing lane. For a moment he seemed about to object to being told how to place his riders. Then he pointed two of them toward the next junction.

“Turn the animals west. Let the walkers through. No riding at them.”

One man nodded and went. The other asked where west would put them.

“Somewhere with more room than here. Go and find it.”

He sent the remaining riders to Hadrun. His own horse stood restless under him, disturbed by smoke and strange beasts. Yara took the bridle near the bit. The horse quieted.

“You could stay with me,” Orun said.

She shook her head.

He looked down at the boots she wore.

“I brought yours.”

“Keep them dry.”

His mouth moved. She released the bridle before he found the next words.

The palace’s public approach was clear enough to enter. Men stood at the corners offering directions to shade and water, and the promise had brought people whom no sermon would have fetched from home. James recognized the woman from the well with the sister whose leg had mended. Both women carried cups.

Green silk stirred above the palace door in the smoke-darkened light. Behind it a bronze lamp burned cleanly, and cool air breathed out over the steps. Low strings sounded within. A drum beat softly; above it a voice held one long, beautiful note. James found himself searching for the singer before the smell of soot on his own sleeve recalled him to his purpose.

Yara stopped beside a shuttered grille in the side wall. She bent to retie a bootlace and knocked three times quickly, then once more.

Nothing answered.

James kept his gaze on the invitation while she waited.

From behind the grille came the scrape of wood, then two quiet taps. Yara rose.

“Beds are there,” she said.

At the public door an attendant read the invitation and looked up into James’s face. There was no surprise in her expression.

“The captain. We were told to expect you.”

She moved aside. Inside, someone had scattered pale petals over the stone. They stuck to the soot on James’s boots as he crossed the threshold.

Chapter 24 — The new place of the bowl

The woman in front of Yara had flowers growing along her arms.

Scales, when Yara looked again: translucent petals layered over the flesh, their lifted edges filled with blue light. The woman had rolled her sleeves high to display them. Her small son kept touching the blue, then examining his finger to see whether he had taken any away. His mother watched him with such naked pride that Yara smiled before she remembered to be afraid.

The boy caught her smile and hid behind his mother’s skirt.

She had forgotten how much she liked being here.

Music rose from beneath the western gallery, stirred by the draught from the doors. Water splashed freely into waiting cups. Lamps swung above the crowd and burned in the wall recesses, setting altered bodies aglow: bronze, green, the blue of a beetle’s wing. A silver-throated woman sang to someone hidden among them. The low notes were rough as a caress over scarred skin; the high ones seemed to open the roof.

Yara let it draw her three steps further into the room before she remembered to look for the exits.

The public doors were behind her, to the west. Along the south wall, low openings led to the kitchen passages and servants’ court. The high bowl stood above the eastern end, on its stone shelf beneath the old crossbeam. She had seen that beam only as a dark shape during tending. Now men had cleaned it and hung three lamps from its iron eyes. The narrow stair beside the shelf was open to view.

The broken screens were gone. Low tiers had been made ready for spectators, with cushions hired in different colors. There were scratches where heavy things had been dragged across the floor. New cloth covered most of them.

She found James by the western tier. He was looking at the beam too. When he noticed her, he touched his coat near the clasp and nodded.

Yara moved down the southern side.

The old low station was empty. She had expected that. Its round mark remained on the paving, lighter than the surrounding stone, with a black line where the wet preparation had dripped for years. She stepped around it and went toward the place where Thaliss had joined the broken circuit.

A golden screen blocked the way.

It had been brought from another part of the house, a huge freestanding thing with painted birds among its bars. Its feet stood astride the old passage to the center. Beyond it the narrow bronze tripod was empty too.

Yara stopped.

Thaliss occupied the raised seat at the eastern end, the receiving bowl cradled beside his left ribs on what seemed a bracket fixed to the chair. Green metal climbed from its base into his bare forearm. New lines gleamed across his ribs, pressing beneath the skin. Yara felt the careful plan she had carried through the city come apart in her hands.

She could not walk up with a cloth as if answering an ordinary tending summons. Between her and the bowl stood half the room, a flight of shallow steps, and the attention of everyone who faced the seat.

The fang warmed at her belt.

She pressed her elbow against its cover. That made the warmth worse. She moved her arm away.

“Yara.”

His voice carried without effort. Nearby conversations fell away, one after another.

Thaliss lifted his unmarked hand toward her.

“I was hoping you would return.”

She looked toward the south passage. Pellash stood just inside it, holding a roll of bedding. His eyes moved to the raised seat and then back to her. She gave him the smallest shake of her head.

He turned away, taking the bedding with him.

“I wanted to see my friends,” she said.

“So you shall.”

People made room for her. A man moved his cup so she would not knock it with her sleeve. The woman with the blue arms smiled encouragement. It was difficult to imagine refusing a welcome which so many people meant kindly.

At the foot of the steps, Yara paused and touched her left ribs, where Thaliss now carried the bowl. Then she looked directly at James.

His face changed. He had seen.

She climbed the steps.

Close to him she saw what the lamps concealed from the floor. Flesh stretched thin and fever-bright between the breast plates. His marked arm lay rigid along the bracket, fingers curved as if closing them would break something inside the wrist. At the base of his throat a pulse hammered far too quickly. The sweet resin scent he wore could not quite cover the sourness of sweat.

He offered her a low stool on his right.

She sat. From here she could see the room as he saw it: every face turned toward him, each person keeping space around the body they had brought or the body they hoped to receive. A thin man in a worker’s tunic held a polished wooden foot in his lap. An old woman had braided her hair with green ribbon. Two young men were admiring each other’s altered eyes while pretending not to.

“Beautiful, aren’t they?” Thaliss said quietly.

“Yes.”

“You needn’t sound ashamed of it.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

His gaze rested on her throat, on the bronze she had left uncovered.

The fang’s warmth spread along her hip. Thaliss’s eyes moved down, not quite reaching it.

“You’ve brought something home.”

Yara kept both hands on her knees.

“Do you want it?”

“In time.”

A pair of attendants approached with shallow cups. One was Vezhri.

Yara had not expected to see her so soon. The tavern worker wore the same yellow headcloth, freshly tied, and a plain dress with sleeves loose enough not to catch the scar on her arm. She looked at Yara with something like relief.

“You found them.”

“Yes.”

Vezhri’s gaze flicked toward the western tier. James had moved from the place where he had been standing. Korvash remained visible above the crowd, but Szik was gone too.

Thaliss accepted a cup and set it untouched beside his free hand. The other attendant offered one to Yara. She shook her head.

He waited, uncertain whether to insist.

“No,” Thaliss said. “She has already chosen.”

Yara felt the fang move against her hip. Only the warmth, she told herself. Leather softening. Her hand wanted to check, and she held it still.

The singing ceased. The room settled into the small sounds of people preparing to listen.

Thaliss rose.

The bowl rose with him.

Yara’s breath caught. Beneath the dark waist cloth lay a broad metal belt, carrying the bracket. The chair had merely borne its weight while he rested. Green metal entered his arm above the belt and wound over his ribs. Wherever Thaliss went, the receiving station would go with him.

“Some of you have come for relief,” he said. “Some for curiosity. A few because you have nowhere else to sit out of the smoke.”

A little laughter moved through the room.

“You are welcome for any of those reasons.”

He stepped down once, then again. The people nearest him leaned back to give him room. Yara stood, following because she had no other way to remain near the bowl.

He spoke of a woman whose hands could no longer close, of a man who had spent twenty years ashamed to be seen without a coat. He did not promise that everything would be painless. He promised that pain need not be the final shape of a life.

Yara thought of Irdan and hated that she still wanted to listen.

Along the southern wall Pellash appeared again. He had Neshet under the good arm. Another attendant supported Irdan. They were taking them toward the servants’ court, slowly, as though simply seeking air.

Yara lowered her eyes before Thaliss could follow her gaze.

The bell sounded. Thaliss drew wet fingers along the receiving lip, and green fire pulsed across his waist.

Yara’s right hand rose. She watched it leave her side as though she were watching someone else reach for a cup.

She caught it with the left and pressed both against her stomach. Around the room other hands rose: a cup paused halfway to a mouth, two fingers spread from a sleeve, Vezhri’s palm turned toward the lamp.

Thaliss smiled at the people watching.

“Let the body find what it remembers.”

She tried to step sideways toward the bowl. Her foot remained planted. Every scale on her body prickled cold, a thousand little points beneath which her own flesh waited obediently for another will.

Then the bell stopped.

She took the step, too quickly, and stumbled. Thaliss caught her beneath the elbow with his free hand. His fingers were gentle.

“Slowly,” he said. “There is time.”

Behind him, James was moving people toward the door.

Chapter 25 — A captain’s voice

The woman would not leave without her cup.

James had got her to the edge of the western tier, had pointed out the open door and the cooler passage beyond, and had persuaded her that the tending might be better watched from there. Then she remembered the cup beneath her cushion and went back for it.

By the time she returned, four other people had followed her.

“What are you doing?” a man asked James.

“Making room.”

“There’s room.”

“More room.”

The man looked round the half-filled tier. James could see him deciding whether the captain was drunk.

Korvash stood beside the public doors. Every time he moved to clear them, someone greeted him. A young man asked whether he had really carried the stone bowl over his head. Korvash said no, only to his chest, and demonstrated with his good hand. This produced further questions.

James wanted to shout.

The bell struck. The young man’s hand rose to his chest; a cup shattered behind him. Vezhri turned her open palm toward the lamp. James’s wounded arm jerked against its binding, and a streak of pain ran through the stitches. He seized the rail and held until the pull slackened.

“Out,” he said.

The man in front of him frowned.

James looked into his eyes.

“Go out now.”

The man wheeled and drove straight into the crowd. A woman stumbled beneath his shoulder, dragged another by the sleeve, and struck the doorpost hard enough for James to hear bone against wood. The man kept pushing, his feet obeying the order while people cried out around him. Korvash thrust between the bodies, trying to give them room.

James broke his gaze and plunged after him.

“Stop. Wait.”

The man did not stop until his shoulder struck the wall. He looked back, terrified, with the woman’s sleeve still caught between his body and the stone.

James got his good hand beneath her elbow and lifted. Korvash took her weight from the other side. They freed the sleeve. The woman slapped the man hard across the mouth.

He did not defend himself.

“Leave him,” James said. “It was me.”

She stared at him, then at the man. James had no time to find words for it. A child was crying beneath the rail, caught between two pairs of legs.

He crouched and put out his uninjured hand, keeping his eyes on the floor.

“Come under here.”

The child shook her head.

“Your mother can reach you from this side.”

A hand appeared over his shoulder. The child took it and crawled through. James stayed where he was until she was clear.

When he stood, he pointed at the young man who had wanted the story about the fountain.

“Can you stand there? Beside the rail. Tell them there’s a child under it if they start pushing.”

The young man looked at Korvash, who nodded.

“Yes.”

“You two. Would you take her outside? She’s hurt her wrist.”

One woman refused. The other accepted, and the first followed anyway to retrieve the cup she had left with her friend.

James pulled a cushion from the doorway and threw it into the tier. Without it, people could see the raised threshold. He stood on the inner side and guided them over it one at a time.

The music had faltered. At the far end Thaliss was speaking again, his calm voice carrying above the disturbance. He was asking people to sit, to let the frightened find their ease. Some obeyed. Others wanted to know why the captain was sending people out.

James wished he had brought a man whose sole task was to answer questions badly on his behalf.

Szik appeared beside him carrying a small red bag.

“What’s that?”

“An opportunity.”

“Put it back.”

“It’s votive silver. Cups, rings. They left it in an open recess.”

“Put it back.”

Szik looked toward the recess. Two attendants were already crossing in front of it. He tucked the bag beneath his robe.

“Later.”

James swore at him, quietly and with precision, then pointed toward Yara.

She had touched her ribs before Thaliss rose. The bowl moved with the priest, held against his side like a thing grown there. Yara stayed close, smiling at something he said. Her hands were clasped tightly in front of her.

“The bowl’s on him,” James said.

“I can see.”

“Can you get above her?”

Szik looked up. The western gallery joined a narrow walk along the north wall, ending beneath the beam over the upper station. The lamps would make it difficult for him. James saw him calculate each patch of brightness.

“Perhaps.”

“Try.”

The man who had been compelled to leave stood outside the doors, wiping blood from his lip. James wanted to say something to him. Before he could, a bronze shutter descended halfway over the entrance.

Korvash caught it with his good hand.

Bronze drove Korvash down at the knees. Two men caught the edge beside him, teeth bared beneath the weight. James followed the moving rope up the side wall to its wheel. Someone beyond the door was paying it out. He dragged his sword free with his left hand and struck.

The rope parted in a spray of fibers. The shutter fell another finger’s width, then checked against its counterweight. Men swore beneath it. James made them ease out one at a time, each waiting for another to prove the bronze would hold.

From the southern passage came a cry.

Pellash had got Neshet to the opening, but people seeking a different exit had crowded round them. The litter waited beyond the turn. They could not get it through.

For the first time, James saw a clear path to Thaliss.

Thaliss came down the last step with Yara beside him. No guard between them. Ten strides of clear stone, less if James ran. He saw the thrust before he made it: under the chin, clear of the breast plates, the bent blade driven deep enough to silence that beautiful voice. Even left-handed he could reach him.

Thaliss turned toward the commotion at the south door.

James took one step.

Neshet screamed. The crowd had crushed her wounded arm against the frame.

James turned and ran for the passage.

“Szik. Go to her.”

Szik was already on the gallery stair. He looked down, saw where James was running, and climbed faster.

At the south door James put his shoulder into the crowd from the side, where there was still room to move. He did not shout at everyone. He found the large man wedged nearest the frame and spoke directly into his ear.

“Back two steps. Turn your hip. She’s trapped.”

The man moved. The space was enough for Pellash to get Neshet’s good arm free. James put himself in the gap and took the pressure until they were through.

Beyond them Irdan stood with both palms flat against the wall. An attendant was trying to pull him away, but his hands would not move.

The second bell began.

James felt the pull in his own fingers. He pressed his sword hilt against his thigh and waited, staring at a crack in the stone until he could make himself breathe.

Behind him Thaliss said, “Stay where you are.”

People who had never taken a cup went on moving. Others stopped so abruptly that those behind collided with them.

The bell held its note.

James hooked an elbow around Irdan’s waist and dragged. The young man’s palms clung for an instant, then tore free with a wet squeak. He sagged between James and Pellash, leaving red smears on the stone.

“Take him,” James said.

“I am taking him.”

“Then keep taking him.”

Pellash gave him a furious look which James was glad to see. Together they got Irdan through the doorway. Above them something heavy struck the golden screen, and the room filled with the sound of singing metal.

Chapter 26 — The screen of gold

The handsome guard had climbed onto the screen’s lower crosspiece.

From there he could reach the gallery stair and the south passage without having to fight through the people between them. He carried a narrow sword in his left hand. His injured right arm was bound close to his body, not so differently from Korvash’s. James noticed this with no satisfaction at all.

“Get away from the door,” the guard said.

James put his sword up.

“Gladly. Move yours.”

The guard’s blade hissed down. James caught it crookedly; the jar drove pain through even the hand he had not used. He lurched into the screen’s shadow and felt the second cut flay a strip from his coat.

The man was better with his left than James was. Perhaps he had always used it. James wished he had taken more notice of his hands the first time they met, instead of the teeth.

The guard advanced along the crosspiece. The screen rocked on its feet. Behind its bars James could see Yara standing beside Thaliss, her face turned away from the fighting. She was trying to reach the bowl with her right hand. Each time she moved, her hand faltered.

The guard cut low.

James stepped onto the opposite foot of the screen to avoid losing a knee. The sudden weight tilted it toward him. Gold-painted birds swung overhead. One came loose and struck the floor between two spectators, who fled before it had stopped spinning.

“People will be killed,” the guard said.

“Then come down.”

“You broke the tending. You have no idea what you opened.”

James thrust through the bars. The guard turned his blade and caught the point against a painted branch.

“Then let them leave.”

“They cannot finish outside.”

“Some don’t want to finish.”

The guard’s expression changed.

He kicked the screen’s upper rail.

Gold lurched across James’s sight. His foot slipped and his wounded hand snatched a bar. The stitches pulled. He cried out, feeling blood spring hot beneath the bandage, and tore his hand away. His sword point dropped as he fought for his footing.

The guard saw the opening.

James threw himself sideways as the thrust came. The blade went beneath his arm and caught in the cloth of his coat. He drove his shoulder into the guard’s legs through the bars. Both men went down on opposite sides of the screen, which struck the stone with a crash and lay tilted against a bench.

For a moment James could see only gold leaves an inch from his face.

Then the guard’s sword came through them.

He rolled away, leaving another piece of coat on a branch.

At the south door Korvash was shouting for a table.

James could not turn his head far enough to see why. He crawled clear of the screen and rose, sword out. The guard was doing the same beyond it.

A serving table slid across the floor behind James, pushed by three men. Korvash caught its edge with his good arm and swung it toward a narrow side door opening onto the passage they needed.

Something struck the other side of that door.

The door bellied outward. A gray hand thrust through, fingers webbed in glistening membrane. Nails scraped long white furrows in the table. From beyond came a wet rattle, broken into sounds almost like words. James felt the hair rise beneath his torn collar.

Korvash braced his right shoulder against the table and spread his feet.

“Here,” he said. “On this side. Push.”

Two men obeyed. The third backed away.

A pale face pressed into the gap. Its mouth spread too wide; the eyes strained to focus on the men before it. A green collar cut deep into a neck that had outgrown it. The mouth labored over another broken sound, and the hinges shrieked under the weight of the body trying to follow.

An attendant ran up carrying a tray.

“He needs the next draught,” she said.

Korvash looked at the little cup. His face was wet with sweat. The bound shoulder trembled beneath the cloth.

“Which of us?”

“You. To hold it.”

He stared for a heartbeat longer, then turned his face against the table edge.

“No. Get another man.”

The attendant did not move.

“Or push.”

She put the tray down and pushed.

The door shuddered. Korvash bent both knees and shifted his weight lower, keeping the bad side clear. One of the men wedged a broken screen-foot beneath the table leg. The table held.

Above them Szik had reached the north walk.

James saw him only because a lamp swung toward him and he flattened himself against the wall, sleeve over his eyes. The red bag bulged beneath his robe. He waited for the lamp to swing away, then moved in its wake. The coil lay across his shoulder, glossy in the light.

The guard came round the end of the fallen screen.

James retreated toward the western tier, drawing him away from Korvash and the passage. The guard followed him over cushions and smashed cups. A woman’s abandoned shoe turned beneath James’s heel. His left forearm burned; each parry dragged a little farther from the line it should have held, and the guard was beginning to see it.

At the edge of the tier he found the difference in height he needed.

He stepped down, let the guard commit to the cut, then drove upwards at the wrist. His point touched flesh. The guard jerked back, and James had a moment in which he could have followed with the killing thrust.

His wounded right hand reached for the rail to steady him.

It failed to hold.

His grip failed. The guard drove the sword basket across his face and hurled him among the cushions. White light burst behind his eyes. He tasted iron, felt a tooth grind against his lip, and kicked at the approaching shin before he could see it clearly.

The kick landed. James rolled beneath the bench and clawed his way out on the far side with blood running over his tongue. So much for the elegant killing thrust. He still had a sword and something left in his legs.

Above, the lamp chains began to vibrate.

Not from the fighting. All of them together.

The guard looked up.

James did too.

Thaliss stood at the center of the cleared floor, his marked arm stretched toward the high bowl. A line of green light ran over his ribs. The receiving vessel trembled at his waist. People near him began moving their arms in the same slow, lifting gesture, though some were crying while they did it.

Szik had got beneath the old beam. He was staring down at something beyond the north walk’s broken balustrade.

Then he took the red bag from under his robe and hung it over his shoulder, freeing both hands for the rope.

James could not see whom he had found.

The guard attacked again before he could look longer.

Chapter 27 — Hold means hold

A woman hung below the northern walk by one arm and the ties of her apron.

Her oil can knocked against her hip, pouring a bright thread into the gulf beneath her. The gallery crush had burst the balustrade outward. One ragged bar held her apron; one arm held the rest of her. Her free hand scraped the wall, finding only plaster that crumbled under her nails.

Szik knelt above her.

James saw him lower a loop, saw her miss it, and saw him lower it again. The rope moved too readily in the air, feeling for nothing, only settling where its weight took it. The woman caught the loop and got it under one arm.

“Both!” Szik shouted.

She shook her head. Her other arm was holding her body against the wall.

Szik fastened his end around the foot of the great crossbeam. Then he climbed over the broken rail.

The guard’s next thrust drove James backwards. James caught it on the screen bar he had picked up with his bad hand, using the length rather than the strength of his grip. The impact sent pain through his palm. He dropped the bar and backed away again.

“You’ll bleed out before he finishes,” the guard said.

James had no breath for an answer. Blood was running between his fingers. He kept moving, toward the southern wall now, where the fallen screen would force his opponent into a narrower approach.

Above them Szik had reached the woman. He got the rope beneath her second arm, touched the loop and spoke. It tightened. She released the wall, and her whole weight came onto it.

The apron ripped. She dropped a hand’s breadth, and the sliding loop snapped tight beneath her jaw.

Szik seized the back of her dress. The red bag swung between them, struck the wall and spilled a single bright ring through its loose mouth. James saw it fall all the way to the floor.

Her mouth worked without sound. The heels drumming against the wall grew slower.

Szik tried to get a hand to the loop while keeping hold of her dress. The bag’s strap had caught on the broken rail above him. He was pulled sideways, unable to descend the last few inches.

The sound beam lay above Szik, the stair a few steps beyond it. The strap held him close enough to climb back. He looked once at the woman’s open mouth, drew his little knife and slashed through the bag’s strap.

Silver rang upon the old drain grille. A cup spun in the lamplight and dropped through the lifted edge; the red bag caught, tore, and spilled the rest after it in a glittering rush. Then it too went into the dark.

Szik did not look at it.

He hooked one foot beneath the woman’s knees, taking enough of her weight to reach the loop.

“Loosen.”

Her head fell forward. Szik caught her hard against his chest, cursing into her hair as the first choking breath came through. He worked the rope beneath her arms, then passed another turn low around her hips to make a rough seat. His fingers touched each turn before he ordered it to hold.

A man in the gallery had finally reached the beam. He shouted something James could not hear. Szik shouted back, very clearly, that if the man pulled before he was told, Szik would come up and throw him off.

The man waited.

At the south passage a metal grille began to descend.

James heard Pellash shout. He turned too far; the guard’s point scored his shoulder through the coat. Heat ran down his upper arm. He drove the guard back with two ugly, desperate cuts and ran for the passage.

Iron teeth descended across the passage. Beyond them Pellash bent over Neshet and the litter while Irdan struggled on the floor, his useless hand folding beneath him. Others crowded their backs. James rammed his sword between the grille and its track. The falling weight took it with a scream of metal, bowing the blade.

He wedged the hilt against a projecting stone and let go before it could take his fingers. The grille stopped a little above waist height.

“Under,” he said. “Go under.”

Pellash stared at the bent sword, then dragged the litter toward it. The frame would not fit with Neshet lying high on the bedding.

“Off it first,” James said.

“I know.”

Hands took hold of him from behind.

Vezhri’s fists locked in his coat. Her face had gone white, the eyes open and emptied of everything he knew in them. She hauled him toward the center of the room with a strength that dragged his boots across the stone.

James caught her wrists.

“Let go.”

She did not.

He almost looked directly at her. The habit rose so quickly that he felt himself begin before he stopped. He turned his face aside and peeled one finger free, then another. She sobbed without changing her expression.

Across the hall marked attendants seized the people they had welcomed. One dragged an old woman from her son’s grip. The woman with flower-blue arms barred the western exit beneath the half-fallen shutter, her beautiful hands braced where no one could get past. Her boy struck her skirt with his little fists and screamed for her to move. She did not look down.

The bell had fallen quiet. Thaliss had not raised his voice. His marked hand was lifted, palm toward the high station; the lines across his ribs shone steadily, and the people answered.

Korvash could not leave the side door. The thing behind it had got a shoulder through the opening and was pressing its distorted face against the table edge. The men beside Korvash looked ready to run.

“Stay low,” he told them. “It can’t get its feet under it. Stay low.”

The attendant who had offered the cup was crying as she pushed. She went on pushing.

James pried the second fist loose and folded Vezhri’s arms across her chest. She fought him without pause, a sob torn out of her at each breath. He held her from behind with her feet on the floor, taking the blows of her head and heels while Pellash slid Neshet beneath the grille.

Another woman came to help with Irdan. Her sleeve was green, her hands unmarked. James remembered her at the feast with the recovered grandfather, laughing over something ordinary. Now she put both hands beneath Irdan’s shoulders and dragged without waiting to be asked.

“Captain!”

Szik had got the lamp tender down onto the northern tier. She lay coughing beside him. The coil was still fixed above; he was climbing back to free it, blinded whenever the swinging lamps crossed his face.

James could not spare a hand to answer.

Vezhri’s head struck his chin. He tasted blood again. Her body was shaking so hard that he could feel it through his coat.

Across the room Yara looked toward them.

James saw her take in Vezhri, the blocked doors, Irdan beneath the grille. Her face changed. She put one hand beneath her gown, where the stained cloth lay separate from the fang.

Thaliss turned toward her.

Chapter 28 — The lip of the cup

“Your father has horses waiting,” Thaliss said.

Yara stopped with her fingers on the cloth.

“I know.”

“Does he know what you intend to do?”

“Yes.”

“Does he approve?”

She looked up at him. The breast plates had spread wide, exposing wet new flesh that shone like the inside of a wound. He stood painfully straight. Beneath the skin of his flank something moved against the rigid belt.

“Does that matter to you?”

“It matters to you.”

The answer was close enough to truth to hurt.

Beyond him Vezhri was twisting in James’s arms. The tavern worker’s face was wet. Yara could hear the dragging of the litter through the passage, and Neshet trying not to cry out as they moved her.

“Let them go,” she said.

“The frightened will run into the smoke. The unfinished will take their wounds with them. You’ve seen what it costs when the passage breaks.”

“You can stop moving their hands.”

“And let them tear themselves apart resisting?”

“She is resisting now.”

Thaliss glanced toward Vezhri. For a moment his expression softened. He lowered two fingers. Vezhri sagged against James, who nearly fell with her.

Then a man beside the west door stiffened and pushed the shutter down again. Someone outside shouted.

“You could help me,” Thaliss said.

The receiving bowl’s sweetness filled her nose, fruit and minerals and the sharp savor of a bloody knife. Green venom shone around its lip. Each scale upon her body stirred toward it, tiny movements she felt and could not forbid.

“I am trying.”

“Then stay.”

He lowered his free hand toward her, palm open.

“You needn’t go back to a life that has made no place for what you are. Keep this. Keep what you chose. There is work here you understand. People who know you. Let me finish what I began.”

“I mean to keep it,” she said. “I liked being here.”

He smiled. She had wanted that smile badly, once.

“Tell Pellash he can take them outside. I’ll stay while they go.”

“Until they are ready, I cannot.”

Vezhri cried out behind him. Yara heard James asking her to stop pulling.

She looked down at her hands. She had liked them before, most days. She liked them now with an excitement that had survived everything she had seen. The little bronze plates caught at the seams of her borrowed sleeves. The joints moved further than they used to. Once, as a child, she had been told to keep her hands still whenever visitors came because she made people nervous with them. She could remember the hot shame of that more clearly than the visitors’ faces.

Thaliss was watching her.

“You can help them be unafraid.”

“I frightened him sitting down.”

Szik laughed so hard that he had to set his cup aside.

The fourth place was laid before Yara arrived.

James looked at it, then at Szik.

“Expecting someone?”

“It was there.”

“It has food.”

“Food turns up. We’re in a tavern.”

Korvash moved the fourth plate a little further from his own elbow, making room as carefully as he had cut meat badly with one hand the night before. His shoulder would take time. Ruvet had given him a list of things he must not do, and Szik had added climbing to it in a different hand. Korvash had noticed the handwriting and argued the addition before admitting he had no intention of climbing anything.

James’s own sword lay upstairs. The bend had been eased by a smith who made no promises about the temper. He had charged for the work and suggested that James could purchase a better sword when he began getting into better fights. James had not liked him, but the blade now sat properly in its scabbard.

Yara had left the black fang at the temple, wrapped separately from every stained thing they had brought out. The woman had found a stone box for it and told James that the box was not for sale. He had only asked.

The six-noose clasp gleamed on a plain dark coat bought secondhand, its collar smelling faintly of another man’s rose oil. It fitted reasonably well across the shoulders. Three people had told him so. He still preferred the lost embroidered robe.

Yara came in wearing a soft green dress open at the throat.

Bronze caught the lamplight above the neckline, bright against the fading bruises. She had braided her hair back and used the old comb to hold it. Her own boots were clean beneath the hem. She raised a hand to the landlord, and a ripple of light traveled over the scales.

She sat in the fourth place and took a piece of bread.

“How are they?” James asked.

“Neshet swore at the woman changing the dressing. Irdan played three games and lost two. Pellash has found rooms above a washer’s yard, which will be unpleasant in summer and cheap all year.”

“Sounds well.”

“It smells terrible. I’ve promised to help clean it.”

Korvash offered his good arm. She looked at it and shook her head.

“You can pay for buckets.”

He considered this and agreed.

Vezhri brought a fresh dish from the kitchen. She set it where everyone could reach, then put a little bowl of hot pepper beside Yara.

“Your father said you liked it.”

Yara looked up in surprise.

“He came here?”

“This morning. To pay the water carters.”

“What water?” James asked.

“The last load at the beds. They emptied it before anyone asked whose share it was.”

Vezhri left them to consider this. James decided not to pursue the finances of water. He was enjoying having money again.

The food was better than the palace’s in one respect: nobody required them to cross a marked threshold after eating it. Otherwise the palace had won narrowly on the little dough packets. James felt entitled to miss those. He speared another piece of dark meat and dragged it through the sauce. Pepper, charred onion, something sour enough to make his tongue ache pleasantly. He soaked bread in what remained and decided the landlord could keep that secret.

The singer began while they were arguing about whether the coil ought to be washed.

He had a narrow three-stringed instrument, a hat with a silver button and the confidence of a man who had never had to fight anyone described in his songs. He stood near the hearth until enough people had noticed him, then struck a bright opening chord.

He sang of the Ninefold Gallows marching out of the west.

“Not all of us,” James murmured.

He sang of their captain, whose sword was as straight as his honor.

Szik put his forehead on the table.

“Leave it,” James said.

The captain entered the serpent’s halls with a thousand men. Korvash was taller than the palace gates. Szik was the lost prince of a kingdom whose name the singer pronounced differently when it returned at the end of the verse. Yara appeared in chains of gold, suffering beautifully and apparently unable to speak.

She ate another piece of bread.

James raised a finger.

The singer continued. Korvash uprooted a mountain, which struck James as wasteful when there had been a perfectly serviceable fountain at hand. The prince crossed a river of fire on a thread of moonlight. This description pleased Szik so much that he lifted his head again.

Then the captain carried the maiden down the stair.

“No,” James said.

The singer stopped on a chord which had not been intended to last so long.

“No?”

“Also, you’ve put the stress on the wrong part of ‘Gallows’.”

Szik made a small wounded sound. Yara covered her mouth.

“The maiden,” James continued, “went up the stair. She cut the working and tipped the bowl. You can leave me the sword if you like, but she did that.”

The singer looked at Yara.

“Are you the maiden?”

“I’m eating.”

He considered the bronze at her throat, then the three men, then the number of people listening.

“A new verse costs more.”

“You haven’t been paid for the old one,” James said.

“Then I can offer favorable terms.”

Korvash began to laugh. He had a deep laugh when he was not trying to protect his shoulder, and even now it filled their corner. Szik joined him. Yara tried to explain to the singer that he could use the same tune, only change who was doing things, which he received as an insult to a skilled trade.

James reached for the money beneath the table.

The bag would not move.

He tugged once, then remembered.

“Szik.”

Szik looked at him through tears.

“The rope.”

The little man bent down, put two fingers on the coil and attempted the command. What came out was half a word and another helpless burst of laughter.

The rope held.

The landlord arrived with a second dish and looked at James’s hand beneath the table.

“Trouble?”

“No.”

“You’re making the face.”

“What face?”

“The one from the first day.”

Yara took a breath, managed not to laugh for almost its full length, and offered to help. Then Korvash repeated the line about the straight sword, and she had to sit back down.

The singer waited with one hand extended. The landlord put down the dish and extended his own.

Beneath the table the living coil held their purse with perfect fidelity.
