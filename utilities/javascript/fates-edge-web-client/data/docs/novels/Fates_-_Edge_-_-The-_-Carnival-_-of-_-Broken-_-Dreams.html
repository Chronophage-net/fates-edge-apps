The Countess’s Irregulars — Book Three

Part I — Everybody wins something

Chapter 1 — The smaller dragon

The dragon bit a warehouse and lost three teeth.

One struck Rosceline’s shoe. It was made of painted wood, cheaply, with an iron tack still showing through the root. She picked it up while the creature’s rear half continued around the corner and its front half remained embedded in the plaster.

Inside it, a man swore.

The trumpeter took a larger breath and began again.

“Stop playing,” the driver shouted.

The trumpeter could not hear him. He had chosen his profession well.

Daska laughed and went to help. She had bought a paper of hot nuts from a woman whose brazier stood beneath the warehouse awning. She gave the nuts to Pello without asking whether he wanted them and put her shoulder beneath the dragon’s jaw. The canvas throat opened above her, exposing ribs of willow, two frightened boys and a man clinging to a pot of lamp oil.

“Put that down,” she told him.

“The flame’s the best part.”

“We haven’t seen all the parts yet.”

Rosceline stepped back under the awning. Rain ran down the creature’s gilded scales and dripped from a row of little brass bells. Beyond its trapped body, the rest of the parade had folded into a swearing knot. A woman on stilts was waiting with one hand against a balcony. Her painted smile had begun to run. Two children beneath her were trying to make her blink.

“You might lift the tail,” Rosceline suggested.

Briska had already crawled under it. Her boots protruded from a fringe of crimson cloth. “You might stop telling people to lift things until you know what’s fastened to them.”

“I have found that the fastenings usually make themselves known.”

“Yes. Through injury.”

Pello put the nuts on a barrel, moved a handcart out from behind the rear wheel and took hold of the long iron brake. He looked toward Daska. She nodded.

The driver shook his whip at the warehouse door as if the building might be persuaded to move. A man in a leather apron appeared and demanded to know who had authorized the dragon.

“I have a permit.”

Ilaro, who had been sheltering beside Rosceline, held out his hand. The driver gave him the folded paper with the eagerness of a man finding someone willing to believe in it.

Ilaro read beneath the edge of the awning. Rain tapped the paper. He moved it farther under cover and started the last line again.

“This is for a smaller dragon.”

“We improved it.”

“The street hasn’t improved with it.”

There was a sharp crack beneath the tail. Briska came out backward, carrying a short steel pin whose head was thick with green paint.

“Your release was painted shut.”

“That paint costs money.”

She looked at the driver until he glanced away.

Daska lifted the jaw. Pello released the brake, and the dragon shifted against her shoulder. Its throat scraped the wall with a noise that made Rosceline think of expensive furniture. The man with the oil put his pot down at last and pushed from inside. The front wheels turned. Something tore, and the creature came free with half a painted saint attached to its nose.

The warehouse owner made a wounded sound.

Rosceline offered him the tooth. “For the repair.”

He did not take it. She put it on the barrel beside the nuts.

The parade moved. The trumpeter, encouraged by the apparent success of his playing, grew louder. Daska wiped plaster from her sleeve and recovered her food. She offered some to Briska, who took one with two fingers and bit it before discovering how hot it was.

“It’s been over a fire,” Daska said.

Briska stared at her, chewing carefully.

They followed the dragon through the widening street toward the inland fairground. Silkstrand’s wet paving reflected ropes of yellow lamps. Water from the dyers’ quarter slid beneath a narrow bridge, violet near one bank and the color of old wine near the other. Above it the parade’s paper fish turned in the rain. Their little metal tongues rang when the wind caught them.

Rosceline had not meant to spend the evening with all four. She had asked Daska whether she was going, and Daska had asked Pello, and by the time Rosceline arrived at Briska’s workshop Ilaro was already waiting in the chair with the least light. Nobody had proposed a plan. She had liked that until Briska began questioning the dragon’s steering.

Eight weeks had passed since the mountain. Briska’s sleeve no longer hid the whole scar on her forearm. Pello still came down from a high step with care. Daska’s left hand had closed around the dragon’s jaw without difficulty. Rosceline watched it now as the monk shook nuts into her mouth.

“You’ll be sick before we reach the food.”

“Then I’ll have room.”

The fairground had been a field that morning. Now canvas peaks rose above the warehouses and the calliope played from somewhere behind them, a high, reedy sweetness that came through the brass like a child singing in a locked room.

A woman stood on the entry platform in a coat of blue velvet. Its skirts were stiff with little mirrors. She greeted a cooper by name, asked after his wife, and told the next person he had chosen an excellent night to be unhappy.

“Maestra Pann,” said the board above her. “Three Nights Only.”

Pann’s eyes found Rosceline in the crowd. The smile she gave her was neither larger nor smaller than the one she had given the cooper. Rosceline disliked how much she noticed.

They passed beneath the lamps.

Warm sugar, wet rope and horse piss crowded the air. A girl on a narrow wire walked above the midway with a bowl balanced on her head. A man below invited bets on whether she would spill it. The girl looked down at him and said that if he shook the post again she would pour it into his trousers.

Daska stopped to watch.

Rosceline stopped beside a little booth where a man was trying to return a bird.

The bird was in his mouth.

Every time he opened it, a pure, beautiful whistle rose above the midway. He looked increasingly miserable. The woman behind the counter had sugared fingertips and a white turban so large it resembled something being offered at a wedding. She held out her hand.

“Your original, darling. The little one. With the cut edge.”

The man fumbled in his coat. He produced a cream ticket, creased and damp. When he put it down, his fingers stayed on it.

“I was told it would bring pleasure.”

“Hasn’t it?”

“To everybody outside my house.”

She smiled. He did not.

“I would like my ordinary whistle.”

“Then make the return.”

She pointed to a fold near the ticket’s lower edge. He pressed the corner across his written name. The paper resisted a moment, springing up against his thumb. He pushed harder.

“I give it back,” he said.

The whistle stopped halfway through a note.

He stood with his mouth open. The woman took the folded ticket, placed it beneath a small brass weight and invited him to try.

His ordinary whistle was a damp hiss followed by a squeak.

“Can he return that one too?” Daska asked.

The man laughed before the woman could answer. He looked surprised by the sound, tried it again, and went off through the crowd with his hands empty.

Ilaro watched the ticket beneath the weight.

“He could have done it himself?”

“It’s his wish.” The woman licked sugar from her thumb. “Until his settlement bell. Afterward we would have had a longer conversation.”

Pello had taken a step away from the counter. Rosceline could see what he had seen: no prices on the sweets, only little blank cards beside them.

She left the sweets alone.

Farther along, a knife-thrower split an apple above his partner’s head. The blade struck the board so hard that the apple’s wet halves slapped her cheeks. She spat a seed at him and received more applause than he did.

Beyond that was a blue placard with a finch painted at the top.

Rosceline stopped.

“Nerisse Dalca,” she read. “Her farewell.”

“Again?” Briska asked.

“She has been threatening it for years. I should like to see whether she means it.”

The first two evenings were at the Blue Finch. The third, in larger lettering, was advertised beneath the carnival’s own name. Rosceline smoothed a wet curl from the edge of the placard. Nerisse’s drawn face had lost twenty years and acquired a chin she had never possessed.

“Is she good?” Daska asked.

“She used to be.”

At the Blue Finch, twenty minutes later, Rosceline discovered how little she liked having said that.

The room was full. Wet coats steamed from hooks along the wall. A woman carried a tray above her head through a gap that kept closing. Near the stage, a man with one empty sleeve had placed his drink on the chair beside him and was refusing to surrender it. Lusette, Nerisse’s niece, took the glass, moved it to a shelf and sold the chair before he could find the beginning of his objection.

Nerisse stood beneath three lamps. Her dress was dark red, with a little repair near the waist that the light almost concealed. She had put no powder over the lines at her mouth.

The accompanist began. She waited a beat longer than Rosceline expected, then sang into the space he had left.

Rosceline sat down without arranging her coat.

The song concerned a woman waiting at a harbor for a man everybody else knew was dead. It should have been intolerable. Nerisse made the woman impatient with the gulls, then with the neighbors, then so briefly hopeful that Rosceline felt something move behind her ribs before she could decide whether to allow it.

Beside her, Daska’s nuts cooled unopened on her knee.

The accompanist turned a page. He leaned toward Nerisse and opened his mouth to call the change.

Nothing came.

His lips moved again. Nerisse glanced at him, found the next phrase herself and went on singing.

The man put a hand to his throat. Rosceline watched it stay there until he needed both hands for the instrument.

When the song ended, the room applauded. She applauded with it.

Chapter 2 — All the chairs

Celise had put the letter in the middle of the table.

Ilaro noticed that before he noticed the five chairs. Usually a letter in the Countess’s house lay beside its intended reader, leaving everyone else to wait for the account. This one had been opened flat, with a plain stone at each corner. A second sheet carried the terms of the proposed work.

He chose the chair with its back to the window.

Rosceline sat opposite him. She had arrived wearing a gray coat that seemed new until he saw the old cuffs. Pello stayed standing while Celise moved a vase away from the space beside his chair. Daska took a piece of bread from the plate. Briska looked at the stones holding down the letter and turned one over to examine the cut underside.

“They’re garden weights,” Celise said.

“The face is badly dressed.”

“The letter hasn’t escaped.”

Briska put it back.

The Countess entered through the garden door. Her veil moved in the draft, briefly revealing the line of her mouth. She asked Daska whether the bread was fresh, accepted the answer without testing it, and sat at the end of the table.

“You heard Nerisse last night.”

“Half the city heard Nerisse,” Rosceline said. “If she had filled the room like that last winter, we would have been spared the farewells.”

“She knows.”

Ilaro began reading.

The first lines were formal. Nerisse apologized for troubling someone whose kindness she had already used too often. The handwriting changed halfway down, becoming smaller and more forceful.

Basile tried to speak and could not. It happened inside the house. Outside he said he was well. When he came back it happened again. I think something has been taken too soon. They will not let me see what I signed.

Below that, in a different ink:

Please have it brought back before the last bell. I will make the return myself.

The letter had been delivered during the night.

“She bought the audience,” Rosceline said.

Ilaro kept reading to the bottom. There was a line on the reverse, visible through the paper, and he wanted to see whether it altered anything before the room decided what had happened.

Celise lifted the weights for him.

I was not buying a new voice. I do not want you to think that.

He read the sentence twice. Then he turned the sheet back.

“What did she promise?” he asked.

“Her account says her singing voice after the farewell,” the Countess replied. “Three full performances in exchange. The carnival keeps the original ticket. She has a copy.”

“And you’ve seen neither?”

“Correct.”

“Then we begin there,” Rosceline said. “Pello can look at the office, I can speak to the—”

“Wait.”

She looked at him. He could feel her preparing a pleasant answer that would leave the discussion exactly where she wanted it.

“I haven’t finished with the work you’re asking us to accept,” he told the Countess.

“Please do.”

He placed one finger on Nerisse’s last sentence. “Suppose we bring the ticket and she has changed her mind.”

“Then you bring me that answer.”

“Are we paid for bringing the answer?”

“Your expenses and the advance. The remainder is for the recovered ticket and the return completed before settlement.”

Rosceline moved her cup. It made a small sound against the saucer.

“I am not hiring you to carry her out of the city,” the Countess said.

Daska swallowed her bread. “Good.”

“Nor to close the carnival. I have an interest in the Finch’s premises, and Nerisse has received people for me when receiving them here would have been inconvenient. I would prefer that she survive retirement with the ability to complain about it.”

“She’ll find a way,” Rosceline said.

The Countess’s mouth moved beneath the veil. “Yes. But I would rather hear her.”

Ilaro looked at the second sheet. Twelve hundred silver marks, divided among the five. Two hundred in advance. Agreed expenses separately. Celise had left room for particulars beneath the sum.

“The return,” he said. “We saw one made last night. Is there more to it than the original and the person who asked?”

“The person must withdraw deliberately before the stated settlement. A copy will not serve. Burning the original gives up the means of returning the wish. It does not give back what was promised.”

“Who taught you that?” Briska asked.

“Someone who tried burning one.”

There was a brief silence. From the garden came a gardener’s irritated request that somebody stop moving his bucket.

“Does the carnival answer to you?” Daska asked.

“No.”

“They have your kind of manners.”

“A regrettably portable accomplishment.”

Ilaro had been about to ask about the bell. The gardener spoke again, louder, and the question went out of his reach. He looked down at the word settlement until it came back.

“There were two bells on the grounds. Which is the last one?”

“We should ask before we rely on it,” Pello said.

Ilaro nodded and wrote that down.

Rosceline watched his pencil. “We can presumably speak to her without taking the whole table.”

“Once we know what everybody has agreed to.”

“I was agreeing.”

“You were assigning.”

She leaned back. Daska looked into her cup. Briska examined the weight again, this time without turning it over.

Ilaro disliked having said it sharply. He disliked more that the sharpness seemed necessary. The room had been pleasant before the commission began, and he wanted that back without pretending the paper between them was unimportant.

“I want the same question answered before five people act on five versions,” he said.

Rosceline considered him. Then she reached for the sheet and read it herself.

Celise brought the advance in five small purses. Nobody touched one until Nerisse’s letter had gone around the table. Briska asked for money to hire equipment rather than build anything before they knew the ground. Pello asked whether the Countess had an ordinary carrier who could be paid openly. Celise gave him a name and said that the woman would refuse anything she considered foolish, regardless of whose name he used.

“That sounds expensive,” Daska said.

“She usually saves me money.”

When they stood, Rosceline took a folded paper from inside her coat. She placed it beside the Countess’s hand, not quite under it. Ilaro saw two lines crossed through and two left untouched. He looked away before she had to ask.

“This would be the third,” Rosceline said.

“If you accept it.”

“I have.”

The Countess wrote a short note beside the next line and returned the paper. Rosceline folded it into the same narrow shape and put it back inside her coat.

Outside, the morning smelled of water warming against stone. The carnival’s posters had appeared on walls that had been bare the night before. On one, Nerisse’s painted mouth was open above a promise that there would be no empty seats.

Ilaro stopped in the strip of shade beneath an arch. Daska waited without asking why. He put the two bells into separate lines in his notebook, leaving space beside each.

Then he closed it and went with them to hear what Nerisse wanted now.

Chapter 3 — One more evening

Nerisse was singing to six empty chairs and a man repairing the seventh.

In daylight the Blue Finch looked smaller. The dark corners had become plain corners with damp at the bottom. A crack ran through the painted bird above the stage, giving it a second beak. Someone had chalked prices on the undersides of the chairs. Rosceline saw one when the repairer turned his chair over and shook a loose tenon out of it.

Nerisse stopped halfway through the phrase.

“That was meant to wait until I finished.”

“So was paying me,” he said.

She looked at him, then laughed reluctantly. “There is money now. Ask Lusette.”

He set the chair down with less noise.

Nerisse came to the edge of the stage. Her hair was pinned carelessly and she wore a man’s old coat over a dress Rosceline remembered from a better season. She recognized Rosceline at once, then looked past her toward the others.

“You brought a committee.”

“They have developed the habit of coming with me.”

“Are they musical?”

“Some of their objections have a recognizable refrain.”

Daska crossed to the stage and held out a hand. “I liked the harbor song.”

Nerisse took it. “You were here.”

“All the way at the back. I could still hear every word.”

The compliment reached Nerisse before she had prepared herself against it. Her fingers tightened briefly around Daska’s. She asked which verse, and the monk described the one in which the neighbors started bringing the woman soup. Nerisse smiled.

“They had already buried him. She hadn’t.”

“I know. I wanted her to throw the soup at them.”

“Some nights she does.”

Rosceline waited for them to finish. She had come to discuss a stolen voice, and already Daska looked as though she might purchase another evening of it.

Basile sat behind the instrument with a cloth over his knees. Up close he was broad and untidily shaven, with two nails missing from one hand and a shirt collar that had been turned rather than replaced. He rubbed the strings with the cloth, working slowly around a little dark patch on the wood.

“Your letter reached the Countess,” Rosceline said.

Nerisse’s smile altered. “I was frightened.”

“A sensible response to what you described.”

“Pann has explained. These things can overlap. An advance against the payment. It should have come from me. She will have the clerk put it right.”

Basile stopped polishing.

“Has he?” Daska asked him.

“Basile is much better this morning,” Nerisse said.

The monk waited, looking at the accompanist.

Nerisse glanced between them. “Tell her.”

He put the cloth down.

“Outside, I can tell her anything she wants.”

His voice was rough but audible. He stood, came around the instrument and went toward the front door. Near the middle of the room it thinned. Rosceline heard the end of his next sentence as a dry whisper, the sort a sick man makes when he is trying not to alarm the person beside his bed.

He opened the door. Street sound came in: an argument over a cart wheel, a hawker selling onions.

“Here,” Basile said, perfectly clearly. “I could call a song from here. The instrument would be difficult.”

Lusette appeared from the kitchen carrying a basin. “I suggested putting you in the doorway. He said the passing carts would be in the wrong key.”

“Most of them are.”

Lusette smiled at him. Then she saw the number of people in the room and set the basin down.

She was a square-faced woman in her late twenties, with sleeves rolled to the elbow and a burn on the inside of one wrist. Unlike the figure on the posters, she had Nerisse’s chin.

“If you’re here to look at the bar, I’ve paid for it.”

“They aren’t,” Nerisse said.

“The man yesterday said he wasn’t either.”

Pello put a hand beneath the portable bar’s lower rail and tested its weight without moving it.

“Wheels?”

“Two at the back. Lift the front.”

He looked at the worn floor underneath. “The axle wants moving forward.”

“I know.”

He took his hand away. She looked at him with slightly less hostility.

Nerisse went to a cupboard behind the stage. Rosceline watched her choose the key from a ring, find that it was the wrong one and start again. Whatever Pann had said had not entirely settled her.

“I have the copy. It says what I told you.”

She brought out a long envelope. Inside was ordinary cream paper bearing her name, the three dates and the titles of the advertised performances. Rosceline laid it on the nearest table. Ilaro bent over it without touching it until Nerisse nodded.

Three houses filled to the last place, to hear Nerisse Dalca sing farewell.

Below, in smaller letters:

In consideration, the voice of the house, to be received at the closing bell after The South Window, on the last night of the present engagement.

There were three little crescent cuts along the bottom of the copy, drawn in ink. Beside them was a note identifying the original by its actual cuts and Nerisse’s thumb pressed into blue wax.

“My voice,” Nerisse said. “That is what we discussed.”

“Then why write house?” Ilaro asked.

“Because that is how they write.”

Rosceline knew the answer. She had obtained signatures from people who wanted the large sentence badly enough not to argue with the small one. Nerisse was looking at her as though she might be the person in the room capable of understanding that the signature had not been an accident.

“You were going to stop singing anyway,” Rosceline said.

“Yes.”

“Then you thought you were paying with something you had finished using.”

Nerisse sat. “I have sung to four people, Rosceline. Four. Two of them had come to ask whether the room was available for a funeral. I sang the whole evening because the other two had paid.”

She touched the copy with a finger, very lightly.

“Last night they stood outside. Lusette could have sold the same chair three times.”

“I nearly did,” her niece said.

Nobody laughed. Lusette picked up the basin again and carried it back toward the kitchen with her shoulders set.

Rosceline sat opposite Nerisse. “Did they tell you how to make the return?”

“With the original. I fold the corner over my name and give back what I asked for. Before the bell. Vellum keeps it in the office until the last performance. He showed me its place.”

“And when you asked for it?”

“He said they were preparing the account. Pann said there was no need to spoil an evening over a clerical mistake.”

“There may be every need.”

Nerisse looked toward the instrument. Basile had returned to it. He sat without trying to speak.

“One more evening here. Then the great tent. They have lamps that make the whole room disappear except the person you mean to see. There is an orchestra, Rosceline. Not a man trying to make six strings do the work of twenty.”

“Basile can hear you.”

“Basile knows.”

From the stage he raised one hand, without turning it into either agreement or refusal.

Rosceline wanted to shake her. She could also imagine the lamps. That made the desire to shake her worse.

“If we find the original, will you make the return?”

Nerisse drew the copy closer. “Find out whether Pann has put it right first.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It is my answer.”

Daska sat on the edge of the stage. The boards gave a little beneath her weight.

“We can look,” she said. “We can come back and tell you what we find. But if somebody else is paying for your evening, you’ll have to hear that too.”

Nerisse stared at her. “You think I would ask for that?”

“I don’t know you.”

The reply was quiet enough to leave room for an answer. Nerisse did not give one.

They left the copy at the Finch after Ilaro had recorded its wording and the identifying cuts. Nerisse put it back in the envelope and kept it on her lap. Rosceline watched her hold the paper while Basile began the next song without waiting to be asked.

Outside, Daska walked beside Rosceline through the smell of frying onions.

“You were going to bring her with us.”

“I was going to make certain she did nothing foolish while we worked.”

“You’ll need a large room for that.”

Rosceline looked at her. Daska was not smiling.

“If she asks to leave, I’ll take her out,” the monk said. “If she asks to stay, you don’t get to make that my errand.”

“And if her wish is taking Basile’s voice?”

“Then we find out. You heard me tell her.”

Rosceline looked ahead to where Pello had stopped beside a carrier’s cart. He was examining the wheels while Briska spoke to the woman on the driving board. Ilaro waited in the shade of the cart’s tilt.

“You have become very particular.”

“About some things.”

Daska moved ahead to help the carrier shift a crate. Rosceline followed at her own pace, with Nerisse’s full room still uncomfortably bright in her memory.

Chapter 4 — A proper apology

The stair should have broken.

Briska saw the defective pin before the performer put his foot on it. She caught the side rail, lifted the tread and slid the pin through its proper hole. The performer, a broad man in a false stomach, climbed two steps, braced for a fall that did not happen and went over backward with a look of deep personal betrayal.

He landed on a padded mat. The false stomach stayed on the stair.

From the empty benches, Daska began laughing.

The carpenter beside Briska closed his eyes.

“That,” he said, “was the part I had finished.”

“The pin was only half seated.”

“Yes.”

“The tread would have dropped.”

“Yes.”

“Under a person.”

He waited.

Briska looked at the performer, the mat and the little leather tether that had brought the false stomach safely down against the step.

“You could mark it.”

“I did.” He pointed to a yellow cross on the side rail. “Then you put your hand over the mark.”

The performer sat up. “Can we keep that? I like the stomach being left behind.”

“We can keep it if you like paying for another spine,” the carpenter said.

His name was Cazet. He wore a leather apron with six pockets and a narrow leather thong where the seventh had torn away. The nails in his mouth were clean. Briska approved of that more than she wanted to.

She had offered her labor after noticing a footlight shutter hanging loose beside the main stage. Cazet had asked her to show him what was wrong, listened to half the answer and handed her a screwdriver. They had been getting on adequately until the stair.

He crouched beside it and showed her the intended failure. The pin released two linked treads; the side rail stopped the collapse at the same height each time; the leather caught the belly; the man fell backward onto the mat if he did what he had rehearsed.

“And if he doesn’t?”

“Then I learn about it for the rest of the journey.”

The performer came over and offered Briska his hand. “Salo.”

She took it. His palm was callused in the same places as Daska’s, though larger. There was an old break in one finger.

“You’re careful,” he said. “That’s new.”

Cazet threw a shaving at him.

They rebuilt the release with Briska holding the frame while Cazet placed the pin deliberately badly. She added a small stop to keep it from slipping farther than he intended. He tested it three times, then nodded once and left it there.

Salo climbed. The stair folded. His stomach went up as he went down. Daska laughed harder this time, because he looked at the escaping belly before he hit the mat.

Briska found herself smiling. She hid it by checking the stop.

The main tent was still empty. Benches faced a broad platform with a false parlor painted across its back. A real door stood in the painted wall. Beyond it ran a staff passage to the loading side. A pair of narrow scenery panels could be drawn across the passage, closing it from view. Cazet showed her how one panel traveled badly because its lower guide had filled with grit.

“People kick it,” he said.

“That packs the grit in.”

“People who kick things don’t stop to ask.”

She removed the guide, cleaned it and made him bring the panel through its whole travel. It opened onto a gap wide enough for a person carrying a box. A handcart would need the second panel moved as well.

Outside that gap, Pello was speaking to a porter in a rust-colored coat. Ilaro stood beside the bell frame near the tent’s far corner. He had asked Briska to look at the ropes when she had time.

The small brass bell hung above a painted wheel. It called the carters and loading gangs. The iron one beside it had no decoration. Its mouth hung at chest height above the platform; the long rope let a worker ring it from the ground. Its clapper was wrapped in cloth while the grounds were being prepared.

“That one settles the evening,” Cazet told her. “Nobody touches it until the maestra gives the cue.”

“After the last song?”

“After whatever she says is last. Nerisse’s farewell tomorrow. Usually they ask for two more. Usually the crowd gets them.”

Ilaro wrote in his notebook. “And the little bell can ring first without settling anything.”

“It can ring all day. We still won’t load any faster.”

At the front of the tent, a little commotion had gathered around Rosceline.

She stood on a square of polished boards facing someone in a white half-mask. The mask covered the upper face, leaving a narrow mouth and a faint scar beside it. Their coat was divided into pale and dark lozenges. A rapier rested along one arm as lightly as a riding crop.

“She wants permission to interrupt tomorrow’s farewell,” Cazet said.

“Why is she standing in a box?”

“Because she asked while somebody else was speaking.”

Briska put down her screwdriver.

The Harlequin tilted their head as she approached. “Your associate has offered an apology.”

“I have offered three,” Rosceline said.

“Three explanations. An apology would have been shorter.”

Daska came to stand beside Briska with her arms folded. Pello watched from the loading gap. Ilaro closed his notebook but stayed where the tent’s open side gave him air.

Rosceline drew a breath.

“I regret that my enthusiasm for your performance prevented me from hearing the end of it.”

“There was no performance.”

“How modest of you.”

The rapier moved.

Briska heard its point cut a thread before she saw the loose edge of Rosceline’s cuff lift. Rosceline kept her hand still. Daska stopped looking amused.

“An apology,” the Harlequin said.

Rosceline looked at the blade. Her own narrow sword hung beneath her coat, familiar enough to Briska now that she knew where its hilt would be. She watched Rosceline decide not to reach for it.

“I spoke over you because I expected what I had to say would interest you more.”

“Better.”

“I was mistaken.”

The Harlequin lowered the point. “Were you?”

“For the purposes of obtaining permission to do it again, entirely.”

The mouth beneath the mask curved.

They stepped off the square and invited Rosceline to follow. She did, slowly enough that Briska understood she had disliked the last exchange much more than she intended to show.

The permission was printed on a narrow strip, with space for Pann’s signature. One declared interruption, before the final farewell song. It would last no longer than a small glass kept in the Harlequin’s booth. The subject must be announced beforehand. The right to speak did not include the right to compel an answer, strike someone or remove property.

“What does it cost?” Rosceline asked.

“Three silver marks.”

“For the apology as well?”

“That was free.”

She paid with the expression of a woman who would have preferred the reverse.

Pann arrived as the Harlequin finished the card. Her mirrored coat was gone; she wore a plain blue waistcoat, boots muddy to the ankle and a little bell tied at her wrist. She read the proposed interruption and looked at Rosceline.

“A disappointed purchase.”

“I have considerable experience.”

“So have most of our customers. You will have to distinguish yourself.”

Pann signed. She handed the card back and asked Briska whether Cazet had made her repair something he intended to break.

“The reverse,” Briska said.

“Then you’re learning quickly.”

The ringmaster turned toward the grounds. Cazet called after her about a new delivery of rope. It was waiting beyond the last row of lamps, near the carrier’s gate. Pann stopped beneath the final lamp and sent an usher to bring a sample.

Briska watched the man go. It was not far. Pann could have reached the load before he did.

Then Cazet asked for his screwdriver, and Briska looked down to discover she had put it into her own bag.

She gave it back. He counted his other tools in silence.

Part II — Behind the painted faces

Chapter 5 — The voice of the house

The washroom had one window, a cold stove and a chair that did not match anything else in the Blue Finch. Ilaro took the chair. Lusette set a basin on the stove and told the others to keep out until she called them.

“They keep answering for each other.”

“I noticed.”

“My aunt thinks a pause is something she has to fill.”

Ilaro smiled. Lusette did not, and he let the smile go without trying to explain it.

She had given him a cup of water. He drank half. The room was cool enough to ease the pain behind his right eye, and the narrow window looked onto a wall rather than a bright street. For the first time since the parade, he could hear a single conversation without another one pressing against it.

Basile came in carrying the cloth from his instrument.

“I don’t sing much,” he said before sitting. “If this is about a rival. There isn’t one.”

“It’s about when your voice failed.”

“Last night.”

“Before you started playing, or during?”

Basile rubbed the cloth between his hands. “During the harbor song. I needed to tell her we’d gone too high for the next verse. She used to take it there. She can’t now.”

“She made the change without you.”

“She should. She’s sung it with me for fourteen years.”

Ilaro had thought the missing call might have been an alteration in the arrangement, a cue Nerisse no longer needed. Watching a man open his mouth from across a crowded room was not the same as knowing what he intended to say.

“You tried again?”

“Three times. Then I played the change so hard she had to hear it.”

“Did it hurt?”

“No. That was the queer part. There was nothing to blame. My throat moved. I could feel the air.”

He lifted a hand to the base of his neck, then put it down without touching himself.

“It worked when I went out for beer. Failed when I came back. This morning I spoke in the kitchen, but by the time I sat at the instrument it had gone thin again.”

“Where do you sleep?”

“Upstairs. Back room. Why?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Basile looked at him with tired relief. “That’s a better answer than I’ve had from anybody today.”

They called Tovin next. He was the man repairing the chairs. Sawdust clung to the turned cuffs of his trousers, and he had brought a broken rail with him because he did not want anyone to mistake it for firewood.

“Mine’s mostly when I shout,” he said. “I couldn’t get her attention from the top landing. Thought she was ignoring me.”

“Nerisse?”

“Madal. She had the stair full of costumes. I had a chair under each arm. I could see the top of her head. Nothing was coming out.”

“Were you at the performance?”

“I was fixing chairs behind the kitchen.”

“Did you sing along?”

Tovin looked at Lusette.

“Nobody asks him to,” she said.

He made a face at her, gently, and turned back to Ilaro. “No.”

Ilaro put a line through the word singers. He could still see it beneath the pencil. He wanted it there until he understood what had taken its place.

Madal came without being called. She was carrying a red sleeve turned inside out over one arm, the beads cupped in her palm so they would not snag on the doorway.

“If everybody’s to sit in here, somebody can fetch my needle case.”

Lusette went for it.

Madal settled on the cold stove and began sewing as soon as Lusette returned. She had lost her voice that morning, Lusette said, while arguing with Nerisse about the red dress. She was clearing her throat now, annoyed by the interruptions to her work.

“The fiddler sang all evening,” Lusette added. “This morning he was shouting at a man who’d borrowed his bow. You can hear him from here.”

“Does he live here?”

“With his wife, two streets away.” She pointed upward. “Basile has the back room. Madal’s above the front, Tovin’s by the roof hatch. Mine is next to my aunt’s.”

Ilaro turned the page. “Have you had any difficulty speaking?”

“Not yet.”

The words made Madal stop sewing. Lusette looked at the sleeve instead of at her.

Ilaro set down the pencil. “These people share a house. The ones outside it haven’t reported the same trouble. That is what we have. It does not tell us whether every person upstairs will be affected, or whether the walls matter, or whether somebody has written particular names.”

“She gave them our names,” Lusette said.

The room became very quiet.

“For the farewell places. Pann said the household should be named, to make sure nobody who belonged here was left without a seat. My aunt thought it was considerate.”

“Were you asked?”

“To spell Madal’s properly. They’d put an extra letter in.”

Madal pulled the thread through the sleeve. It made a small, tight sound.

Ilaro wrote the four names on a fresh sheet. He asked each person to check their own. Tovin had to borrow his pencil. Basile corrected nothing but read the whole sheet before handing it back.

Rosceline appeared in the doorway as Lusette took the last turn.

“We have a way into the office.”

“Then we have something to look for.”

He showed her the names. Her expression changed when she reached Lusette’s. She glanced at the young woman and then back at the page.

“Does Nerisse know?”

“She knows we’ve been talking,” Lusette said. “That’s different.”

Rosceline folded the sheet once, considered it and gave it back. “Keep that with you. I don’t want to discover later that she thinks I invented the people.”

It was an ugly remark, and he understood why she made it.

When the others had gone, Ilaro stayed in the chair for another minute. Madal remained on the stove, sewing. She did not ask him to leave or offer him a remedy for his head. The sleeve turned slowly in her hands, bright red against the gray basin.

“Will you be able to sell that?” he asked.

“The beads. Perhaps the cloth. Depends who remembers wearing it.”

“She does.”

Madal nodded. “She remembers every night it went well.”

Ilaro finished his water. He waited until she had drawn the needle through before getting up, so he would not knock her elbow on the way past.

Chapter 6 — First to leave

Mistress Trissot’s handcart was ugly, narrow and exactly what Pello had asked for.

Briska examined the axle while its owner watched from a stool. The yard smelled of hemp, old cabbage and warm horse. A wheelwright was hammering somewhere beyond the wall. Each blow made the cart’s loose side board rattle.

“That’s meant to come off,” Trissot said.

“I haven’t touched it.”

“You’re looking at it as if you intend to.”

Pello lifted the board from its sockets and laid it against the wall. He measured the cart with the span of his arms, looked at Briska and waited.

“It will pass,” she said. “If both panels open. One won’t do.”

Trissot held out her hand for the deposit. Rosceline had supplied it without asking whether a narrower person might be cheaper. Briska had been grateful and suspicious in almost equal amounts.

“No fire,” the carrier said. “No dead people. If a live person becomes dead while aboard, you tell me before you return it.”

“Furniture,” Pello said.

“Furniture has caused worse trouble.”

He gave her the coins.

They would collect the cart before the following evening’s final show. It would carry a pair of repaired scenery panels to the loading gate, then wait beside the carrier’s wagon while the carnival finished. Trissot would have a man there to take the panels onward after they were returned. Pello paid for the delay as well as the wheels. Briska watched him insist on that second payment when Trissot tried to dismiss it.

Outside the yard she asked why.

“If he’s paid to wait, he waits.”

“If things go badly, he may prefer to leave.”

Pello shrugged. “He’ll be deciding. I won’t be surprised.”

He had a rust-colored coat folded over one arm. It smelled of someone else’s tobacco. At the grounds, he put it on before they passed the goods gate, and a porter who had been looking for help gave him half a crate without asking his name.

Briska followed them between the wagons.

The records wagon stood near the entrance to the goods lane, its shafts pointing out. Other vehicles had been parked nose to tail while the tents went up; their wheels would have to be dug out of the mud and their loads rearranged before departure. This one had dry boards beneath its wheels and a clear turn toward the gate.

Pello put down his end of the crate.

“First,” he said quietly when the porter had gone.

Briska looked along the lane. He was right. Whoever held the papers would be gone while the performers were still taking down their canvas.

The wagon had a front door reached by three steps and a rear shutter above a loading shelf. The shutter’s latch could be reached from inside, but the shelf beneath it was too high for an easy descent. A fall would put someone between a wheel and the nearest tent stake.

“How heavy is your investigator?” Pello asked.

“Ask him.”

Pello looked at her.

“Lighter than the crate,” she said. “But less conveniently shaped.”

She could fit a short loop beneath the shelf as a foothold. It would not open the shutter. Somebody would have to go in by the door to release that. The nearest canvas screen would conceal a person waiting behind the wagon if the lamps stayed where they were.

Pello pointed to a narrow interval between the mirror attraction and a row of tethered ponies. “Out there. If the lane fills.”

“Through the mirrors?”

“Beside them.”

She looked at the ropes, the screen and the distance to the public midway. It was an ordinary route made difficult by people, which she preferred to the other sort.

At the main tent, Cazet was shaving a wooden runner beside the scenery panels. He had sorted his tools into two trays, one close to his knee and the other where Briska could reach it.

“You think I steal.”

“I know you borrow permanently if nobody interrupts.”

She took a plane from the farther tray. It was better sharpened than the one he was using. She considered whether to tell him, then went to work on the runner.

They tested the panels first with Pello carrying a broad box, then with Salo wearing his false belly. Salo had to turn sideways through the single opening. With both panels drawn, he could walk through and strike a pose. The second runner dragged under load.

Briska proposed a spring that would pull the panel clear once the catch was lifted. Cazet offered her one from a broken drum mechanism. She fitted it, checked the travel and released the catch.

The panel shot across, struck the end stop and returned hard enough to catch Salo’s stomach between itself and the frame.

He looked down. “This belly’s having a difficult day.”

Briska caught the panel before he tried to move.

“Don’t pull. The cloth will tear.”

“That’s what you’re worried about?”

“At present.”

She removed the spring. Cazet leaned against the stage with his lips pressed together. She gave him a look.

“Say it.”

“I was admiring the speed.”

“We’ll use a line.”

“A very advanced line?”

“Two people, one at each panel, if you prefer.”

He stopped smiling. “A line will do.”

They ran it through two smooth eyes and back to a cleat inside the staff passage. Pello pulled. Both panels moved, slowly enough to stop if someone caught a sleeve. Briska pulled them shut and tried again herself.

The work took longer than it should have because people kept using the passage. An acrobat brought a basket of shoes through it. A cook carried a pot of onions. Salo’s small daughter came looking for him with one stocking around her ankle. He sat on the stage and fastened it while she explained, with considerable indignation, that one of the ponies had eaten the ribbon.

“The red pony?” he asked.

“The other red pony.”

“I’ll speak to him.”

Briska let the girl pass before testing the line again.

Her parent would have sold Cazet the spring, the improved runner and an agreement for replacing both. She could almost hear the patient explanation: a solution nobody pays to maintain is merely the beginning of another problem. She had hated hearing it while standing behind a stall. She caught herself checking whether the wooden runner could be removed without dismantling the whole frame.

It could now.

Before leaving she smoothed a rough place on the cleat and drew one slow breath over the wood. The little flawed casting she carried for the First Breath pressed against her ribs beneath her coat. Cazet noticed the breath and said nothing. He was oiling the folding stair’s release, doing it with the same attention he had given a real door.

At dusk the five met behind the Blue Finch.

Ilaro sat on an upturned basket, the sheet of household names across his knee. Rosceline listened while Pello described the wagon and Briska described what would and would not open. Daska had a piece of bread in one hand and kept the other free to trace the route on the yard’s packed dirt.

“I take Vellum away from the door,” Rosceline said. “You two go in.”

She meant Ilaro and Pello. Ilaro lifted his eyes.

“I need to read the paper before anyone takes it. We know the wording, not what the original does.”

“Yes.”

“You won’t call him back until you have the signal.”

“I heard you.”

“I’m making certain.”

Rosceline looked at the bread in Daska’s hand. “Is everybody going to say it?”

Daska finished chewing. “I’m at the back. If you need the other way out, I’ll keep it open.”

Briska saw Rosceline’s mouth soften before she turned away. It was a small thing, and perhaps she had imagined it.

They waited until the Blue Finch’s lamps were lit. From inside came Basile’s opening chord. Briska put the ordinary cord loop, a small pry and two clean cloths into the side pocket of her bag. She left the spring on the kitchen sill.

Lusette picked it up as they went through.

“Is this useful?”

“Not yet,” Briska said.

“I know a man like that.”

She set it aside for her portable bar and went to open the doors.

Chapter 7 — No writing yet

Vellum had a beautiful hand and an ugly way of using it.

He wrote insufficiently particular across Rosceline’s proposed description, dried the words and returned the sheet without looking at her. The quill had made each letter narrow enough to suggest that more generous writing would have encouraged waste.

“A disappointed purchase,” she said, “is perfectly particular to the person disappointed.”

“The maestra must know what will be carried onto her stage.”

“A box.”

“Containing?”

“The remains of an expectation.”

He looked up.

Rosceline smiled. “A neckcloth. Some letters. A small object whose value has declined since it was given to me. I do not intend to bring the gentleman.”

“Is he alive?”

“I am choosing to believe so. Otherwise my disappointment becomes a rather different act.”

Vellum checked the point of his quill. His cuffs were so clean that she began wondering who washed them. A small key hung from a chain inside his waistcoat, and he had a habit of touching it when someone came too near the office steps.

Pello came too near carrying a tray of cups.

“Not here,” Vellum said.

Pello stopped at the exact place where stopping would oblige the clerk to move. One cup tipped. Vellum caught the edge of the tray with both hands, furious before anything had spilled. Pello steadied it, apologized and went away.

The chain lay against Vellum’s waistcoat. The key no longer did.

Rosceline waited until the porter’s coat had disappeared behind the canvas screen.

“Perhaps this is a question for your colleague.” She indicated the sweet booth a little way across the lane. “I should like the room to attend to me. Not merely remain in the same building.”

“The permit secures your turn.”

“It does not secure their interest. I have been married; I understand the distinction.”

“Have you?”

“More than once, if one counts promises.”

He did not smile. She liked him less for making the work so one-sided.

The office wagon stood behind him, its green paint blackened by the rain. At the rear, beyond his sight, Briska had fitted the little cord step. Ilaro waited beside a stacked crate with his hat pulled low against the lights. All Rosceline needed was enough time for them to go in, identify the original and come out by the shutter.

Vellum put the quill down. “The Cotton Witch handles attention. I shall explain the permitted limits. We have had difficulties with people purchasing effects their performance could not contain.”

“I promise not to exceed my audience’s capacity for delight.”

“That is not the limit I meant.”

He shut the front door and tried it with his hand. The latch held. When he reached for the missing key, Rosceline put her card in front of him.

“The signature is already there.”

He looked at Pann’s name, frowned and led her across the lane. He seemed to decide that locking the door could wait until she stopped talking. She intended to justify that decision for as long as possible.

The woman in the white turban received them with a look of pleasure that Rosceline would have trusted more if it had required an effort. Her copper pan gave off a warm smell of burnt sugar. Fine threads of it trembled between her fingers as she lifted a little white cloud onto a stick.

“A performance,” the Witch said. “How much of the room?”

“All that can see me.”

“And for how long?”

Rosceline showed her the permit.

The Witch touched its edge. “A short glass. You could have longer.”

“The permission is for one,” Vellum said.

“I was speaking of desire.”

“I was speaking of what we have sold.”

The Witch looked at him, and for a moment Rosceline saw the impatience of someone interrupted during a lecture. Then the sweet smile returned.

“What would you give for it?” she asked.

“An equal interval in which I do not have to listen to anyone else.”

“That is another purchase.”

“So it is. I see why people like you.”

Rosceline let herself look past the booth. Pello’s rust coat was still beside the crates. An usher had stopped to speak to him, with the office door directly behind his shoulder. She turned back before her interest could draw Vellum’s.

The Witch rolled the spun sugar between her palms. It did not stick.

“There are gracious prices. A little grace of your own. The ease with which you recognize an old friend’s footsteps. Your pleasure in a particular color.”

“I am attached to my colors.”

“Then something you do too much.”

Vellum made a small sound.

Rosceline turned toward him. “You have a recommendation?”

“I have other work.”

“And yet here we all are.”

She asked about a period of silence because it seemed the answer most likely to annoy him and keep the conversation going. The Witch’s eyes warmed.

“Your voice. An equal interval following the first. That would be neat.”

“I was thinking about a possibility, not accepting an offer.”

“Of course.”

Vellum reached for a fresh slip. Rosceline put two fingers on the counter before he could begin.

“No writing yet. I will hear the exact terms with the people who have to work beside me.”

His glance sharpened. “The Countess’s people.”

She kept her fingers where they were.

“She knows a great many people.”

“Nerisse said she would send some.”

“Nerisse has always been optimistic about what her friends will do for her.”

He looked toward the office wagon.

Rosceline moved slightly, enough to put the bright counter lamp between his eyes and the dark lane. “Is she satisfied with the adjustment?”

“Which adjustment?”

“The one Pann promised her.”

The clerk’s mouth closed. He checked the quill, though he had not used it since they left the steps.

Behind him, a child began crying because the sugar animal she had purchased was melting. The Witch leaned down and reshaped it with two light touches. It stood on the paper again, smaller but recognizably a horse. The child’s tears stopped. Rosceline watched the mother reach for her purse and the Witch wave it away.

“Some work is its own advertisement,” the woman said when she straightened.

Vellum turned fully toward the wagon.

Rosceline caught his attention with his own name. “Mr. Vellum. If I make the small purchase tomorrow, who ensures that what is taken is mine?”

He looked back. “Whose would you prefer?”

“I prefer to know who answers the question.”

“The terms will be witnessed.”

“By you?”

“By the Harlequin, if you insist.”

Beyond his shoulder, the usher took a cup from Pello’s tray and walked away. Pello waited until he had passed the wagon, then slipped through the office door with Ilaro behind him.

Rosceline leaned an elbow on the sweet counter.

“I do insist. I should also like to know whether silence prevents me writing something rude.”

“No,” the Witch said.

“Then you may have found something I can afford.”

Chapter 8 — The actual door

The hook was empty.

Pello held the office door while Ilaro read the little brass label beneath it. Nerisse’s name had been scratched into a removable strip. Two pinholes marked where a folded ticket had hung behind it. The adjacent hooks carried other wishes in neat packets, each with its lower corner left free.

The thing they had come for had been there. He disliked how much more definite that was than finding no sign of it at all.

“Moved,” he said.

Pello eased the door shut. He put Vellum’s key on the desk where its owner would see it if he came in. Then he opened the rear shutter from inside and signaled Briska.

Ilaro looked at the day’s working book. There were entries for supplies, staff advances, lost property and the cancellation of a wish to be taller. A note beside that last transaction directed somebody to shorten the customer’s new trousers at no charge.

He found Nerisse’s name beneath a heading for the closing performance.

Original transferred. Stage compartment. Presentation rack to be opened during final attraction. Personal attendance required.

The entry bore Vellum’s initials and the time of that afternoon’s rehearsal.

“He moved it after she told them we were coming.”

Pello looked toward the shutter. Ilaro shook his head before the young man could signal departure.

“There may be the price.”

He turned one page. The writing stopped halfway down. Below it was a space that looked darker than the paper above, though no ink lay there. When he moved the lamp, the blankness moved against the light as if something thin and living had pressed itself beneath the sheet.

He did not touch it again until Pello had come beside him.

“Hold this toward the lamp. Not directly. There.”

The paper was heavy vellum, warm at the edges. Pello held it while Ilaro moved the shade. Light came through the blank spaces. Letters formed between the ordinary lines, pale cuts through a darker surface.

Voice of the house.

There were names beneath it.

Basile’s came first, then Tovin’s. Madal’s had been corrected, one letter scraped away and written again. Lusette’s remained clear at the bottom. Beside each was a mark of the same shape, filled to different depths.

Ilaro took out the sheet the household had checked. He compared the names one by one. His hand had begun to ache from keeping the pencil too tightly between his fingers.

Pello moved the lamp a fraction.

“Don’t.”

The young man stopped.

“Sorry. The shade, not the flame. I need the light where it was.”

Pello set it back. Then he put a folded rag beneath the shade so it would stay in place without either of them holding it.

Ilaro copied the marks as well as the names. Under them, in ordinary ink, stood a shorter instruction.

Principal retained for further engagement. Draw from attached household pending settlement.

He read that twice.

Nerisse’s name was not among those being charged.

At the shutter Briska made a small, urgent sound. Across the lane Vellum said, “Put that down.”

“Gladly,” Rosceline answered, “if someone would tell me where.”

Pello glanced out. “She’s lifted the sugar pan.”

A scorched sweetness reached the wagon. Ilaro started to turn toward the shutter.

Pello pressed the vellum flat before it could slip. “Finish.”

Ilaro copied the instruction. He wanted the line below it as well; it might tell them how to undo what they had found. He could also hear someone’s boots in the lane, coming toward the steps.

He folded his copy and put it into the inside pocket of his waistcoat. The source sheet went back under its covering page. He closed the book at the angle in which he had found it. There was nothing he could do about the lamp being warmer than it had been.

Pello went through the shutter first. Briska took his elbow as he lowered himself onto the little cord step. Ilaro followed, sitting on the sill and turning with care. His coat caught on a splinter. For a moment he was half outside, unable to move without tearing it.

The front latch clicked.

He pulled. Cloth ripped behind his shoulder. Briska’s hand closed on his wrist, and the wagon wall slid past his face as his feet found the loop.

Inside, Vellum said, “Who moved the lamp?”

Pello took Ilaro’s weight for the last short drop. Briska shut the shutter. They crossed behind the screen just as someone went around the front wheel.

Daska waited beside the ponies. She looked from Ilaro to the lane and moved without asking for an account.

They nearly reached the public walkway.

One of the ushers stepped out in front of Pello and caught his sleeve.

“That’s Maret’s coat.”

Pello turned the sleeve inside out around the usher’s hand, slipped free of the coat and left the man holding it. The movement was so quick that Ilaro understood it only after the empty garment dropped.

“Here,” the usher shouted. “These people.”

Daska took Ilaro by the back of his collar and put him through a canvas flap.

Light struck from six directions.

He was in the mirror attraction. Tall frames stood on wooden tracks, dividing the tent into little rooms that were not rooms. Lamps hung above them in red glass bowls. A woman in a feathered hat appeared to stand beside him, behind him and at the far end of a corridor that could not fit inside the tent.

“Where’s the door?” she demanded.

“Not that one,” Daska said.

The woman had been reaching toward a dark rectangle. Daska caught her wrist before her knuckles struck glass.

Pello passed them along the wall, watching the floor. Briska held a lamp low. Ilaro followed the strip of real mud beneath their feet. In the nearest mirror his reflection had not turned yet. It remained facing the place where they had entered, listening to something behind the canvas.

He looked away.

A hand struck the outside of the tent. The usher’s voice came through it, ordering somebody to close the far exit.

Pello stopped beside a frame. A red rope went through an iron eye at its base and disappeared beneath the next panel. He stepped over it. Briska lifted the lamp higher for Ilaro to see.

“Don’t put weight there.”

Behind them came a wooden scrape. One of the moving mirrors slid toward the path they had just used. The woman in the hat screamed. Daska pushed her forward and turned back.

A worker had been caught on the wrong side of the moving frame. His shoulder was wedged between its back and a tent pole, his face flat against the canvas. Someone outside kept pulling the operating rope.

“Stop!” he shouted. “That’s me.”

Daska seized the frame with both hands. It moved another inch before she braced her foot against the track. Glass shivered from top to bottom.

Briska thrust the lamp at Pello and dropped beside the lower wheel. She drove her short pry under it, lifted and kicked the rope off its guide. The frame slewed sideways. Its upper corner struck the pole.

The mirror broke with a noise like a roomful of teeth being crushed.

Ilaro raised his arm. Something hot scored the back of his hand. Daska dragged the worker clear, half carrying him until his feet found the ground. The woman in the hat had begun laughing in sharp, frightened bursts. She could not seem to stop.

“Bring her,” Briska said.

Ilaro took the woman’s elbow. She came without resistance, looking over her shoulder at the pieces of herself still standing in the broken frame.

Pello found the outer canvas seam by its ties. He drew his knife, cut two cords and lifted the flap. Night air came through, smelling of manure and onions.

The actual ground was lower than the tent floor. Ilaro stumbled. The woman caught his sleeve, and for a moment they supported each other badly. Then Daska was beside them and the moment passed.

They emerged between the ponies and a cook’s cart. Rosceline waited on the other side, holding a folded cloth against her palm.

She had paid for the cloth, she told Briska while the artisan checked beneath it. The Witch had been very particular about that. Her palm was blistered red; she stopped discussing the price when Briska touched its edge.

“Which way?” she asked Pello.

He pointed toward the public midway. They went into it together, abandoning speed for the appearance of people who had purchased an unpleasant entertainment and were looking for something better.

The woman in the hat stopped at a bench. She removed the hat, stared at the broken feather and began swearing with admirable steadiness. The worker sat beside her and held his shoulder.

Daska waited until he could lift the arm. Then she told him who had kept pulling the rope. He already knew.

They left the pair there with a cup of the cook’s water.

Beyond the last lamps, Ilaro stopped walking. The pain in his head had become large enough to obscure the rest of the street. He wanted to sit down before it noticed he was still standing.

Rosceline pointed at a low wall. He sat. Pello stood between him and the carnival lights while Briska wrapped the cut on his hand.

“Did you get it?” Rosceline asked.

“No. It’s beneath the main stage. Guarded until the last show.”

Her mouth tightened. “And?”

He took the folded sheet out of his waistcoat and put it in her unburned hand.

She read without speaking. Daska leaned over her shoulder.

“He’s keeping Nerisse,” Rosceline said at last.

Ilaro closed his eyes. “Read the line before that.”

She did. When he opened his eyes again, her face had lost its practiced interest.

“We go back to the Finch,” she said.

Nobody objected. He stayed on the wall until Briska finished the knot. Then he stood, slowly, and let the others set the pace.

Chapter 9 — The last potato

Lusette stood beside the open doors with a slate in her hands.

Rosceline saw it before she saw the young woman’s face. On the slate, in thick white letters, was the price of the remaining standing places. Beneath that someone had written No credit. The second line had been underlined hard enough to break the chalk.

A man tried to argue. Lusette opened her mouth, stopped, and turned the slate toward him again.

Rosceline put a hand on his sleeve.

“Pay or move. She has a room to fill.”

He looked at her face and chose to pay.

Lusette took the coins. She was trying not to look relieved by the help. Rosceline recognized the effort and let her continue with it. She went into the house without asking for an explanation she could already see.

Nerisse was midway through the second set. There was a high color in her cheeks, and the red dress caught the lamps each time she turned. Basile had put a little board beside his instrument with the next three keys written on it. When she glanced down, he touched the one she needed.

The room listened.

Rosceline stood at the back with the stolen names inside her coat. The little burn on her right palm stung beneath its cloth. At the stage edge Nerisse lifted a hand toward her, recognizing a friend or a problem. Then her eyes went past Rosceline to Lusette’s slate.

For a moment the hand stayed raised.

Basile waited at the end of a bar. Nerisse took the next breath and continued. The held note was beautiful. Rosceline wished it had not been.

She remained standing through the rest of the set. Daska found a place beside the side wall. Ilaro went to the washroom with Briska; Pello stayed near the doors and quietly took over the disputes that required a voice.

There were three of those before the evening ended. One man insisted he had bought a seat that did not exist. Pello found the seat in the kitchen, put it at the end of a row and made two people move their knees. Another complained that he could not see around the woman in front of him. Pello looked at the woman, then at the man, and went back to the door.

Nerisse sang her last song standing quite still.

When the applause began, she closed her eyes. Rosceline had seen her acknowledge rooms far more important than this one with less pleasure. The people rose; chairs scraped; someone called for the harbor song again. Nerisse opened her eyes and shook her head, smiling, as though refusal had become a delightful luxury.

Lusette waited until the room had emptied before she put the slate down.

In the kitchen, the potatoes had gone cold in their pan. Tovin warmed them again while Madal found enough plates. Basile sat by the back door with his coat on, keeping close to the strip of air in which speech sometimes returned to him. He looked at the food before looking at Nerisse.

“There’s bread,” she said.

He nodded. Madal put the loaf beside him.

Rosceline laid Ilaro’s copy on the cleared corner of the table. She kept her burned hand away from the grease. Nerisse read the ordinary lines first, then the names underneath.

“That isn’t the ticket.”

“It is what he has written against it,” Ilaro said.

“You copied this?”

“Pello held the light.”

The young man nodded when she looked at him. He had put his bread on the edge of his plate, leaving the center for the potatoes Tovin was serving.

“Pann said it was temporary,” Nerisse said.

“Pending settlement,” Rosceline replied. “That part was accurate.”

Lusette drew the slate toward herself. She wrote, wiped away two words with the heel of her hand and wrote again.

Did you ask whose?

Nerisse looked at her niece. “She said she would speak to the clerk.”

Lusette left the question where it was.

Tovin put the potato pan down harder than he intended. The handle struck a cup and knocked it over. Pello caught the cup; Daska moved the paper before the water reached it. For a short while everybody had something to do.

Rosceline could have used the interval. She knew exactly how to turn the wet table and the household’s anger into a decision Nerisse would later describe as her own. She began with the practical part.

“You will remain here tomorrow. We will bring the original and you will make the return before anyone asks for another song.”

“No,” Nerisse said.

Rosceline had not expected the answer to come so readily.

“They are taking Lusette’s voice.”

“I can see that.”

“Then look at her.”

Nerisse did. Lusette’s face was flushed above the slate. She had been speaking for the whole house since she was old enough to reach the latch, and now she had to wait while other people took turns with her silence.

“I have promised the performance,” Nerisse said.

“You promised your voice,” Daska answered. “You can change your mind about that. You don’t get to promise hers.”

“I didn’t know.”

“You do now.”

Nerisse stood. Her chair scraped back against the stove. Madal caught its upper rail before it struck the hot iron.

“You think I wanted this? You think I looked at them and decided they had nothing better to do than pay for me?”

Nobody answered quickly enough to help her.

She sat again, more carefully. The kitchen was hot now, with the stove drawing properly and too many people breathing in it. Rosceline could smell the sweetness of the stage powder beneath Nerisse’s sweat.

“I want to stand in that tent,” Nerisse said. “Once. I have wanted to stand somewhere like it since I was a girl. I am ashamed of wanting it tonight, and I want it anyway.”

Daska looked down at her plate. She moved a potato through the grease without taking it.

Basile pulled his coat tighter. When he spoke, his voice was thin but there.

“Then stand there. But don’t make me sit here forever.”

Nerisse covered her mouth with her hand.

Rosceline looked away. She did not like watching people discover that the sentence they had prepared would not serve. She had made a profession of causing it and disliked it most when it happened without her assistance.

Pello waited until Nerisse lowered her hand.

“If I put the original in your hand, will you return it?”

“At the tent?”

“Before the bell.”

She looked at the copy of the names. “Yes.”

“Even if they’re still asking you to sing?”

It took her longer that time.

“Yes.”

Lusette wiped the slate. She wrote one word, large enough for her aunt to read from across the table.

Yourself.

Nerisse nodded.

Ilaro drew his notebook toward him. Rosceline raised a hand before he opened it.

“We are trusting her to give up something she has just told us she cannot bear giving up.”

“We’re asking her to,” Daska said. “That was the work.”

“And if she doesn’t?”

“Then she doesn’t.”

Rosceline looked at the monk’s hand on the table. The left fingers lay comfortably now, except for the smallest, which would not quite rest flat. She remembered them dragging a chain around black stone while Rell stood below the ladder offering terms.

She took her eyes from the hand.

“We need time after the song,” she said.

Ilaro opened the notebook. “We need time before it ends. Whatever they usually do afterward, we don’t rely on it.”

Nerisse described the final program. Her last advertised song was The South Window. The carnival usually allowed requests after its principal attraction; Pann had promised two encores if the crowd wanted them. The iron bell would settle the engagement when Pann gave the cue. It could not take Nerisse’s payment before the advertised farewell, but an encore was a favor, not part of what she had purchased.

“Then the last verse,” Ilaro said. “We plan to have it done before the last verse.”

Ilaro folded his copy of Vellum’s instructions and put it back inside his waistcoat. Then he asked Nerisse to show them the return fold using her ordinary copy. She brought it from the cupboard. The drawn cuts marked the corner that would cross her name on the real ticket. She folded the copy and unfolded it. Nothing happened.

Pello watched closely. Then he gave it back.

By the time the questions were finished, the potatoes had cooled again. Daska asked whether anybody wanted the last one. Tovin said yes at the same moment she put her fork into it.

She cut it in half.

“That’s not half,” he said.

“It is from here.”

Lusette laughed without sound. The movement brought a brief fear to Nerisse’s face, then something softer and more painful. She reached across the table and laid her hand near her niece’s. Lusette left it there, close but untouched.

Rosceline helped Madal carry the plates to the basin. Her palm hurt when the warm water reached the new blister. She bit back a complaint and found Madal looking at her.

“Give me those. You’ll only drop one.”

Rosceline surrendered the plates. It was easier than making a generous gesture of it.

Part III — One interruption

Chapter 10 — A turn of the glass

Cazet wanted to know who had broken the mirror.

Briska pointed to herself, then explained the wheel, the rope and the worker’s trapped shoulder. He listened without speaking. When she finished, he asked which frame.

“The tall one with the red lower rail.”

“It should have had a stop.”

“It did. The stop had split.”

He put his breakfast down and went to look. She followed him across the morning grounds, past a woman washing paint from her face into a bucket. Without the lamps, the midway looked like a collection of objects that had survived a bad move. A paper moon lay face down in the mud. The dragon’s missing teeth had been replaced with pieces of white-painted shingle.

At the mirror tent the worker Daska had freed stood arguing with an usher. He had one arm in a sling. Cazet asked him a question Briska could not hear. The man answered, and Cazet turned to the usher.

“If you can’t hear someone inside, you stop pulling.”

“The rope was—”

“You stop pulling.”

Briska waited while he inspected the frame. He removed the broken stop and put it in his apron. He would make another, he said, before anyone used the track again.

Back at the main stage he handed Briska the other tray of tools.

“You’re working, then?”

“If you’ll have me.”

“I’ll have the panels opening when I ask. What you do after that is becoming expensive.”

She accepted the tools without promising anything she could not keep.

Pello arrived carrying two repaired pieces of scenery on Trissot’s handcart. The carrier’s man stayed at the goods gate to wait for the evening load. He had a stool, a basket of food and the expression of someone who had been paid to remain unimpressed.

Pello took Briska to the far end of the staff passage. Beneath the platform, behind a low locked hatch, a rectangular compartment had been fitted between the joists. Through the gap above it she could see a row of hooks and a little presentation rack. Two ushers sat nearby with a tray of breakfast between them. One had the rust coat folded at his feet.

“They remember,” Pello said.

“Yes.”

He showed her the route Vellum would have to take when he opened the compartment: out through the low hatch, up the ordinary staff steps, along the passage behind the parlor wall, then onto the stage beside the settlement table. Cazet’s folding stair stood against that wall, waiting for Salo’s earlier routine. Its release was within reach of the scenery cleat.

“You won’t get near the rack while those two sit there.”

Pello nodded. “During the song they go to the aisle. One stays. I need him looking up.”

They returned to the Finch for the rest of the company. Nerisse had gone through her program with Basile, marking where the carnival orchestra would take the part he usually played. He had written two corrections in the margin. She had accepted one and argued about the other. He looked pleased by the argument.

Ilaro was listening while Basile played two hard chords, then stopped to pencil a mark above them.

“That’s the mason arriving,” the musician said. “In the short setting they bring him in after the first verse. She’s barely had time to dislike the window.”

“I asked for the middle verse,” Nerisse said.

“I’m putting it back.”

He played the turn into the longer passage. Nerisse sang the first line, the woman moving her table into the sun, and then the next. Ilaro had been about to interrupt. He waited until she had finished.

“Which version will they play?”

Basile tapped the penciled addition. “This one. I’ll send it over with the rest. She needs a breath before those two chords, or the mason will find her dead.”

Nerisse threw the rolled program at him. It missed. He picked it up with an expression of professional disappointment.

Ilaro spread a rough drawing on the kitchen table. He had given up trying to make the tent round and drawn the paths that mattered. The stage lay at the top of the page, the goods gap to its left, the bell frame to its right. The public entrance was at the bottom. The hatch and the staff passage had their own marks behind the stage.

“Pello takes the original when Vellum brings it up. Daska keeps Nerisse beside the goods opening. She makes the return there, and we all leave through the panels.”

“We have established that,” Rosceline said.

“I’m establishing where each person is when it stops being easy.”

She let him continue.

Daska would stay near Nerisse at the stage’s goods side. Briska would open the panels. Ilaro would watch the running order from the shaded prompt position. He laid a square of black card on the drawing: if the time shortened, one of Cazet’s runners would bring it to Daska. She would get Nerisse ready at once.

Pello would keep the recovered ticket until Nerisse was beside him. Rosceline would take the declared interruption before The South Window. She would hold the room while the compartment was opened and the ushers went to their posts.

“Holding a room is uncertain,” she said.

“It has to keep the ushers looking away long enough for Pello to reach him.”

“I can make that part less uncertain.”

Briska looked up from the drawing.

Rosceline described the Witch’s offer. An interval of attention followed by an equal interval without her own voice. She made it sound almost ordinary until she reached the second interval. Then Daska put down the cup she had been turning between her palms.

“Yours.”

“Who else’s would I offer?”

Nobody answered.

Rosceline’s face closed. Briska regretted the silence and did not know what she could honestly put in its place.

“We hear the terms,” Pello said.

“That is why I am telling you now.”

They went together to the Harlequin’s booth.

The small glass stood on a table with its sand in the lower bulb. The Harlequin measured five minutes with it; Briska had asked. Beside it lay Rosceline’s signed permit. The Cotton Witch came carrying no sweets this time, only a narrow strip of pale ribbon and a folded paper. Vellum was absent. Briska was glad of that until she wondered what he might be doing instead.

The Witch laid out the exchange. The attention would begin when the Harlequin turned the glass for Rosceline’s declared act. At the end of the first turn, her voice would be unavailable for an equal duration. She could move, write, make signs and leave. No other person would pay the price. The audience would attend, not obey. The workers could continue their work and the maestra could still recognize something she disliked.

“If she withdraws before it begins?” Ilaro asked.

“Nothing is taken.”

“After it begins?”

“Both intervals run. Attention cannot be put back into the moments in which it was given.”

Briska pointed at the glass. “If that breaks?”

The Harlequin touched the upper bulb. “The glass measures. It does not make the time.”

Rosceline read the paper herself. Then she passed it to Ilaro, who read the exact wording aloud. Pello listened from beside the booth’s open side. Daska watched the Witch’s hands.

“At the end of her turn, I turn the glass again,” the Harlequin said. “At the end of that turn, the voice returns. The closing bell does not alter this exchange.”

Rosceline signed with the name they knew her by. The Witch tied the pale ribbon around the foot of the glass. It tightened without a visible knot.

Briska felt the hair along her forearm rise.

Nothing happened to Rosceline. She thanked the Witch with an extravagance that made Daska shut her eyes briefly.

Outside, Rosceline stopped them before they separated.

“While I cannot speak, you will have to refrain from interpreting every expression as agreement.”

“We manage now,” Briska said.

Pello put a hand on the stage drawing beneath Ilaro’s arm. “If I’m late, stay where you said.”

Rosceline looked at him. “If you can’t get near the ticket, I may have to move.”

“Wait until you see me.”

“Pello—”

“If I’m late.”

The fairground was filling around them. A man carried a stack of benches so high that only his boots were visible. Rosceline stepped aside to let him pass. When he had gone, she looked back at Pello.

“I’ll stay.”

He took his hand from the drawing.

Briska returned to the stage with him. They tested the panels once more, slowly, with Ilaro standing in the gap and then Nerisse. Daska watched the singer’s long skirt clear the lower rail and asked her to wear shoes she could walk in.

“I can walk in my own shoes,” Nerisse said.

“Then show me.”

The singer walked the route. At the little step down she caught the hem with her heel. Daska waited while she freed it, then asked her to do it again.

Briska knelt to smooth the rail’s upper edge. Above her, Nerisse lifted the skirt a little higher and made the step without catching anything.

“There,” she said.

Daska nodded. “Again.”

Chapter 11 — Beyond the lamps

Pann found Rosceline watching a boy mend a paper moon.

He was older than she had first thought, perhaps twenty, with a careful little beard and glue drying in the hair on his wrists. The moon had split across its smiling face. He held the edges together while the paper dried, unable to scratch his own nose.

Rosceline scratched it for him with the clean end of a paintbrush.

“Higher,” he said.

“You are becoming demanding.”

“I’ve been holding this since lunch.”

She scratched higher. He sighed with such uncomplicated pleasure that she almost envied him.

“You could do useful work here,” Pann said behind her.

Rosceline set down the brush. “I usually prefer work with a more flattering audience.”

“He looks grateful.”

“He has glue in both hands. His standards are temporarily low.”

The ringmaster stood beneath a rope of unlit lamps. In daylight her blue waistcoat showed small, careful repairs. A button at her throat had been replaced with one from a different set. Rosceline had expected something more immaculate and was annoyed to find the imperfection attractive.

They walked beside the great tent. Through a lifted side flap Rosceline could see Daska making Nerisse repeat the little descent. The singer’s patience had worn thin; the monk’s had not yet become pleasant. Briska stood at the scenery cleat, testing the pull.

Pann watched for a moment.

“Nerisse used to rehearse like that.”

“She is rehearsing like that.”

“Because someone is making her.”

“We all require encouragement.”

“And what encourages you?”

Rosceline let the question sit between them. Pann did not hurry to improve it. They reached a small platform beside the lane, empty except for a stool and a lamp whose glass was smoked at the top.

“Stand there,” Pann said.

“I have paid for one square of boards already.”

“This one is free to look at.”

Rosceline put one foot on the platform, then the other. It was lower than it appeared. From the ground it had seemed an insignificant little place for a singer or a juggling child. From its center she could see three lanes meeting, the line at the sweet booth and the people moving between them.

Pann changed the angle of the smoked lamp. Nothing lit. It was broad afternoon. Yet the people nearest the platform began looking toward Rosceline.

A woman carrying a bucket slowed. A pair of workers stopped their argument long enough to hear what she might say. The boy with the moon turned his head without releasing his grip.

Rosceline could have spoken to any one of them and made the others want to be next.

She felt the opportunity as clearly as a hilt settling into her palm.

“They haven’t bought anything,” she said.

“Neither have you.”

“And if I step down?”

“Then they continue their afternoon.”

She stayed another moment. The silence was rich with other people’s expectation. Nobody was asking whether she had told them everything. Nobody had a notebook open.

“A dreadful little lamp,” she said, and stepped down.

The woman went on with her bucket. One worker resumed the argument at exactly the word he had abandoned. Rosceline disliked the small emptiness that followed.

Pann had seen it. Of course she had.

“You know how to use a room. Most people only want to be in the middle of it.”

“Most rooms are badly arranged.”

“You would have a say in that.”

Pann took Rosceline’s bandaged hand as though she meant to inspect it.

“The Witch tells me you had an accident.”

“The Witch has charged me for it.”

Rosceline tried to withdraw. Pann’s other hand settled behind her elbow and held it straight. Her thumb pressed slowly into the blister beneath the cloth.

The pain was immediate and intimate. Rosceline’s free hand went toward her sword. Pann glanced down at it without easing the pressure.

“Nerisse stays for the closing. So do you.”

“My agreement allows me to leave.”

“Then you will have something to complain about.”

Rosceline put her free hand over Pann’s wrist. She could draw or she could prise the thumb away; she could not do both quickly enough to conceal either. The boy mending the moon had turned his face back to his work.

Pann released her before she chose.

Rosceline kept the burned hand at her side. Moisture was spreading through its bandage. When Pann resumed walking, she followed one pace behind, where the ringmaster would have to turn to see her draw.

They walked farther along the lane. Beyond the last lamps a mule stood with one hind hoof lifted, patiently allowing a man to remove a stone from it. The road beyond the mule led back toward Silkstrand’s houses and ordinary afternoon business.

“You know why we’re here,” Rosceline said.

“I know why the clerk has demanded another lock.”

“He has been using it badly.”

“He uses everything badly when he thinks somebody is watching his hands.”

“And Nerisse?”

Pann stopped beside the final lamp post. “Nerisse wanted a farewell that felt like the beginning of something. I can give her that.”

“At other people’s expense.”

“Do you propose to teach me how to keep an audience fed, Madame Rosceline?”

There was no accusation in the question. That made it difficult to enjoy an answer.

Rosceline looked beyond the post. Pello had left his own plain coat on a carrier’s bench near the mule. A little boy was considering one of its pockets with the open concentration of a beginner. Rosceline called to him. He withdrew his hand and ran.

She went out to the bench and took up the coat.

Pann followed one pace.

Her boot reached the edge of the lightless circle beneath the final lamp. The movement stopped so abruptly that her upper body carried forward without it. She caught the post with one hand. The little bell at her wrist rang once.

Rosceline turned with the coat over her arm.

Pann’s foot had not crossed the worn line in the mud. She set it back, adjusted the cuff of her waistcoat and smiled.

“Your friend should choose his attendants more carefully.”

“He generally does.”

Rosceline returned to her side of the post. She did not look down again. Pann did not thank her.

For a while they watched the man work the stone from the mule’s hoof. When it came free, he showed it to the animal before putting it into his pocket. The mule lowered its foot and shifted its weight.

“After your interruption,” Pann said, “come to me. We could discuss an engagement of your own.”

“I have work to finish.”

“A person may have more than one employment.”

“And less than one evening in which to consider it.”

“Then consider it while you work.”

Pann went back toward the tent. The lamps along the lane stirred though the wind had dropped. Rosceline remained by the post with Pello’s coat in her hands. She was still thinking about the platform when he came to find it.

“Did you leave anything valuable in the pocket?”

He took the coat, checked one seam and shook his head.

“How discouraging for the child.”

Pello put it on. “What did she want?”

Rosceline looked at him. He had not asked where she had been or whether she intended to explain. He asked what they needed to know.

“To employ me. She knows about the office. She has watched the goods opening, and she intends to keep us there until the closing.”

“Will you?”

“I said I had work to finish.”

He waited. She could have told him about the lamp. Instead she held out her hand, showing him the damp bandage. “She was quite definite.”

Pello nodded and went to tell Briska.

At the main tent, the artisan heard the account and swore softly. She checked the panels again, then the loading lane beyond them. Trissot’s man was still there with the cart. She asked him to keep the opening clear, and he nodded without getting off his stool.

Ilaro listened while standing out of the draft. He asked whether Pann had mentioned changing the song or the settlement. Rosceline said she had not.

“She can still drop the encores,” he said. “I’ll send the card if anything shortens.”

Daska was watching Nerisse drink a little honey water. The monk looked at Rosceline’s hand, then at Pann on the far side of the stage, and said nothing.

Rosceline went to rehearse her act on an empty strip of boards. She opened the box, took out the neckcloth and began speaking to a gentleman who was not there. The first version sounded too much like a woman explaining a disappointment.

She tried it again as a woman trying to sell one.

That was better.

Chapter 12 — A full room

Ilaro’s place was behind the painted parlor, where the light reached him only after passing through a piece of canvas.

Cazet had found a stool that did not rock. Beside it stood a little shelf for the program, the cue card and a cup. The arrangement would have looked considerate if Ilaro had not watched the carpenter charge Briska for the canvas. He found that reassuring. It could be asked for again without anybody needing to feel generous.

The passage to the bell frame lay to his right. Through a narrow gap in the scenery he could see the iron bell’s rope and the foot of the person assigned to it. The brass departure bell was farther out, beyond the tent’s edge. Their ropes ran through separate guides.

He checked those twice, then left them alone.

Jorise, the runner Cazet had lent them, arrived carrying a bowl of soup. She was a narrow, dark-haired woman with a quick, practical walk. She set the bowl on the floor, where a passing sleeve could not catch it, and asked which card she was taking where.

Ilaro put the black card beside her bowl.

“If I give you this, take it to Daska at the goods-side wing. It means there’s less time than we expected.”

“Why?”

He looked at her. He had been about to say it was not her concern. Her face made him hear how that would sound.

“We’re taking Nerisse’s original ticket so she can return the wish. The clerk doesn’t intend to give it back.”

Jorise picked up the card.

“If the ushers stop me, I come back. I’m not hitting anyone for Cazet’s wages.”

“Yes.”

“You’ll pay me yourself.”

He did, from his advance. She checked the coin with a fingernail, put it away and sat beside her soup.

Out front, the benches filled. Ilaro could hear the change in the tent’s sound as bodies absorbed the little echoes and replaced them with coughs, gossip and the shuffle of coats. A woman asked whether the seat beside her was taken. The answer was long enough to suggest the man she had asked hoped it might be.

Near the stage, somebody began clapping too soon. The sound stopped in embarrassment. Salo came out to rescue it with his false stomach.

Ilaro watched through the gap. The performer climbed the folding stair, patted his belly and tried to sit on a chair that Cazet had built to move away from him. The stair dropped. The belly rose. Salo followed it with such outraged dignity that the room laughed before he reached the mat.

He had done it three times that afternoon. The knowledge did not make it less funny.

Jorise ate her soup without looking up. “He’ll try the new turn if they laugh enough.”

“Is it ready?”

“No.”

Salo considered the extra turn, felt the audience waiting and made a smaller bow instead. Behind the curtain Cazet let out a breath. Ilaro wrote nothing about it.

The ringmaster crossed the stage. Her mirrored coat caught the lamps and broke them into little wandering pieces of light. She spoke to the people near the front, then lifted her voice so those at the back felt they had merely arrived closer.

Nerisse entered on her arm.

The singer wore the red dress. Madal had taken it in one last time. A line of darker beads hid the repair at the waist. Daska stood in the wing with Nerisse’s plain walking shoes under one arm; the singer had agreed to change for the exit, and the monk was making certain the shoes did not disappear among the costumes.

Ilaro looked toward the compartment. One usher remained beside it. The other had gone to settle an argument over seats. Vellum was nowhere he could see.

Nerisse began without speaking first.

The song was lighter than the harbor ballad. A girl had promised three suitors the same dance and arrived at the feast with a fourth. The verses gave each man a reason to think he had misunderstood, and every reason made his position worse. Nerisse took pleasure in the misunderstandings. The orchestra followed her with a polish Basile’s six strings could never have supplied, but she left the musicians no time to congratulate themselves on it.

Ilaro remembered hearing the song years ago in a tavern where he had been waiting for somebody who never arrived. He had spent the evening angry at the delay and remembered the tune anyway. Now, when Nerisse changed one old phrase, he almost sang the earlier words aloud.

He stopped himself because he wanted to hear hers.

At the side opening he saw Lusette with a slate tucked under her arm. She had come for the last performance. Tovin stood beside her; Madal had taken a place a little farther forward where she could see whether the beads were holding. Basile remained at the Finch with the instrument. He had told Ilaro he had packing to do.

The song ended. Nerisse acknowledged the orchestra before the audience. It was a gracious thing, and the first violinist’s face showed how rarely he received it from a hired star.

During the next piece, Pello passed behind the parlor wall carrying a bundle of ordinary rope. He put it beside the scenery rack and continued toward the low hatch. His movements were so unhurried that Ilaro had to resist looking after him.

Briska came the other way. She tested the pull once and gave Ilaro a small nod.

The goods route could open.

Rosceline waited with the little box on her lap. She had changed into a pale coat that made the repaired cuff look intentional. Her right palm was covered by a thin glove. The left hand rested on the lid, still enough to suggest she had become more nervous than she wished to admit.

She caught Ilaro looking and raised an eyebrow.

He lifted his cup, offering it. She shook her head and returned her attention to the song.

There was time in which nothing went wrong. Ilaro drank his water. Jorise finished the soup. Through the gap he watched Nerisse stand close enough to the front row that an old woman could reach out and touch the hem of her dress. The woman did not touch it. She sat with both hands folded instead.

Pann stood at the opposite wing. Her gaze moved from the singer to the household at the side opening, then toward the staff passage. She did not need to know every part of the plan to know where its people had placed themselves.

Ilaro felt the old urge to get ahead of the evidence. He could decide now what she intended, set everybody moving and perhaps be right.

He put one finger against the edge of the card and waited.

The next song finished. The Harlequin appeared at the front of the stage with the small glass in one hand. Rosceline stood and gave her box a little shake. Something inside it made a deliberately disappointing rattle.

The Harlequin named her interruption. A few people groaned, expecting an advertisement. Rosceline stepped into the light as though she had been waiting all evening to apologize for exactly that.

Ilaro watched the glass turn.

Chapter 13 — The declared interruption

Rosceline had the room before she had decided what to do with the man in the third row.

He was enormous, comfortably seated and already looking forward to being disappointed. His wife had put a paper of sweets on his knee. Every time he moved, she steadied it without looking down.

“I have come,” Rosceline told them, “to return a gentleman.”

The room laughed in the places she expected. The man in the third row did not. He looked at the little box beneath her arm with genuine curiosity.

“He was considerably larger when I acquired him. I mention this because the seller has suggested ordinary wear.”

The laughter grew. She set the box on the table the Harlequin had provided and lifted its lid.

The room followed her hands.

She had held audiences before. There were always people at the edges to be drawn back, a whisper to be interrupted, a glance that meant somebody had begun thinking about the money in their pocket. Now she could feel those small departures begin and fail. Eyes returned to her without deciding to. A woman near the entrance kept turning her head toward the door and back again, puzzled by her own interest.

Rosceline took out the neckcloth.

“This was offered as proof of constancy. I have since learned it was sold by the yard.”

The man in the third row smiled. His wife took a sweet from the paper on his knee.

Rosceline shook out the silk. It was a cheap length she had bought that morning. Under the lamps it looked costly. She had chosen the color to make the room want it before she told them why they should not.

“I require a witness. Preferably someone who has made a promise and subsequently regretted the clarity with which it was expressed.”

Several hands rose. The large man’s wife lifted his.

He looked at her, amused, and stood. The paper slid from his knee. She caught it before the sweets fell and sent him toward the stage with a little push at the back.

Rosceline offered her hand. He took it gently, feeling the bandage beneath the glove.

“Hurt yourself?” he asked.

“An excess of interest in confectionery.”

“It happens.”

His voice was unexpectedly soft. Up close, his hands showed the thickened skin of a man who carried heavy things. She put the neckcloth into them and invited him to examine it.

“Would you say that it is sound?”

“It’s thin at the fold.”

“The gentleman assured me it had never been used.”

“You can see the fold.”

The room laughed harder than her line had deserved. He had made it his line, and she liked him a little for the theft.

At the far wing Pann raised two fingers to an usher. Rosceline saw the gesture while holding the neckcloth toward the lamps. It was the sort of small movement an audience never watched because a woman in pale silk was giving them something else to look at.

The usher spoke to the first violinist. He took the two encore sheets from his rest and laid them aside, glancing once at Pann.

Through the gap behind the parlor, Rosceline caught sight of Vellum at the low hatch. He had a leather wallet tucked beneath his arm and an usher on either side of him. Pello stood beyond their reach, carrying his coil of rope.

Vellum did not come up. Pann was watching the glass.

Rosceline kept the cloth raised.

“And if I told you I had been promised it would last for life?”

The man rubbed the worn fold between a finger and thumb. “Whose?”

She laughed with the room. She had to. There was no improving it.

The glass stood at the Harlequin’s elbow, its lower bulb filling steadily. She had promised Pello five minutes. The clerk was waiting them out.

She put the neckcloth around the volunteer’s shoulders. He lifted his chin to help, as naturally as if his wife had been doing it. His trust made her fingers clumsy for half a second.

“You look reassuring,” she said. “That was also part of the original trouble.”

“I’ve brought my own troubles.” He looked toward the third row.

His wife called, “Only the ones that would fit through the door.”

Rosceline waited for the laughter. Behind it she heard wheels in the loading lane. Briska had arranged for a cart. It ought to have been a reassuring sound.

She removed the cloth and laid it beside the box.

The next object was a little brass ring. She held it between two fingers so the nearest rows could see how modest it was.

“The value of this was said to be entirely sentimental.”

“Usually means they’ve lost the receipt,” the man said.

“You are becoming expensive assistance.”

“I haven’t asked for anything.”

“Give it time.”

He smiled. Two ushers took their places at the public aisle. Neither looked at her. She put the ring on the volunteer’s open palm and closed his fingers around it.

“Imagine that you were offered a future.”

He looked down at his closed hand. “I’ve got one.”

The answer was so plain that several people laughed late, mistaking the pause for preparation. Rosceline saw the wife in the third row stop chewing. Her face had changed a little.

“Yes,” Rosceline said. “I see that you have.”

She let him keep the moment. It cost her part of the interval. When she looked toward the hatch again, Pann was sending the nearer usher up the steps. Vellum followed him.

She put the lid on the box.

“Then perhaps you can advise me. I have a gentleman reduced to a neckcloth, a ring and three letters which grow less informative as their punctuation improves. I would like to return him before the shop closes.”

“What do you want back?”

She had written an answer for that. Something about the time spent listening to him, followed by a joke the Harlequin would have disliked. Instead she found herself looking at the man’s hands and thinking of the small scratched-out word in Sarem’s book.

The room waited.

“I was hoping you wouldn’t ask,” she said.

They laughed. She was grateful enough to dislike them briefly.

At the goods wing, Daska stood beside Nerisse. She had persuaded the singer into her walking shoes; the high pair stood beneath the painted chair. Nerisse held her skirt clear, waiting for her entrance. Beyond them the goods panels were still closed. Pello had not appeared.

“There is one difficulty with returning a gentleman,” she told the room. “The seller generally insists that he be brought back in the condition in which he was purchased.”

The large man looked into the box. “You’d need a bigger box.”

This time Rosceline’s laugh was real. She gave him the brass ring for his wife and took back the neckcloth. He went down the steps to applause, moving carefully so he would not stumble with everybody looking.

She watched him sit. His wife examined the ring, put it on a finger too small for it and held it there with her thumb.

Rosceline turned back toward the room.

“Which leaves us,” she began, “with the question of—”

The sentence continued in her mind. Her mouth made the next word.

Nothing reached the air.

At the Harlequin’s elbow, the glass turned again.

Chapter 14 — The short setting

Jorise came back with the black card as the audience laughed at Rosceline’s silence.

“He won’t let me through.”

Ilaro looked past her. The usher had closed the passage to the goods wing with his body. Rosceline had bowed, but the man with the bell was watching Pann instead of her. The encore sheets lay beneath the orchestra’s rest.

“You did what I asked,” Ilaro told Jorise.

He took the card. He would have to go himself.

The orchestra began The South Window. Nerisse stepped into the light. Behind her, in the staff passage, Vellum was trying to get past Pello with the leather wallet beneath his arm.

Pello let his coil of rope fall across the nearer usher’s feet. The man kicked it. Pello caught the upper loop and pulled, bringing his ankles together. He fell shoulder first against the wall.

The other usher had gone to the public aisle. Ilaro could see the clear space he had left and nowhere to reach it without crossing the clerk’s path.

Vellum drove an elbow into Pello’s face. The young man stumbled back. Briska moved beside the folding stair and caught Ilaro’s eye.

“Stay out of the passage.”

Pello went forward again, one hand on the wall to keep Vellum from slipping around him. The wallet’s hard edge caught his mouth. Blood struck the pale wood beneath his chin.

Vellum backed onto the stair.

Briska pulled the pin. Salo would have let the dropping treads carry him backward onto the mat. The clerk tried to climb. His shin struck the side rail and his face hit the post above it.

The sound made Ilaro’s teeth hurt.

Pello took the wallet. Three packets; he passed over the first two. The third opened to blue wax and the crescent cuts along its lower edge. Briska leaned close enough to read the name before he wrapped it in his clean cloth and put it inside his coat.

The wallet slid beneath the stair. Vellum reached after it, then gripped his shin instead.

“That belongs to the carnival.”

“So does the stair,” Briska said.

She went to the scenery pull. The usher on the floor drew a knife and sawed at the rope binding his ankles. Pello watched him while Briska opened the panels.

Cold air entered the passage. Through the gap Ilaro saw Trissot’s man get up from his stool beside the cart.

For a moment the whole way out was there.

Then Pann stepped beside the opening, still on the platform.

“Bring the wagon across.”

Cazet, farther down the passage, turned. “There are people on this side.”

“I know.”

Two carters had been waiting at the shafts of a broad scenery wagon. They backed it across the opening. Trissot’s man caught at his handcart, trying to pull it clear. A rear wheel rode over the handle and crushed the bed against its axle. The carrier let go and jumped away.

Briska reached the gap before the wagon stopped. She tried to catch the upper panel and keep it from being forced inward.

“Leave it,” Cazet shouted.

She let go as the wagon’s nearer front wheel mounted the sill. The lower guide split. The panel jumped its runner, struck Briska above the ear and slammed against the painted wall. Ilaro caught the shelf beside his stool before it fell on Jorise. When Briska straightened, there was blood beneath her hair.

The wagon’s stacked timber covered the opening from the broken sill to well above his head. Someone outside put a wedge beneath the wheel. Briska struck the load with the flat of her hand.

“Move it back.”

Pann had already turned away.

Cazet tried to lever the fallen panel clear. One end of its iron runner had folded into the floor. Briska crouched beside him, looked beneath the wreckage and stopped reaching for her tools.

She looked at Ilaro. “We can’t get out there.”

The song continued beyond the wall.

Ilaro had missed a line. He could hear the strings, the singer, the usher cutting at the rope. He tried to separate the music from the noise of Cazet levering timber.

Two hard chords sounded.

Basile’s hand came back to him, penciling the middle verse onto the page. The mason was not supposed to arrive yet. Nerisse had only just begun singing about the window.

He looked at the running order on his shelf. It named the song. Nothing about the setting, nothing about its length. The ticket had named the song too.

They had been waiting for the last verse of a performance Pann no longer intended to give them.

“Was that the ending?” he asked Jorise.

“She’s put the short one back. They’re meant to finish there.”

Nerisse faltered. The violin carried on toward the final cadence, inviting her to follow it. At the goods wing, Pann raised her wrist toward the bellman.

Ilaro pushed the black card into Jorise’s hand.

“Stay with Cazet.”

He went around the painted wall. The floor was bright and there was no useful shade between him and the bell frame. He took off his coat while walking, catching the injured hand in its sleeve. He pulled it free too hard and felt the bandage open.

At the goods wing, Pann was speaking to Salo. He shook his head, glanced toward the place where his daughter had watched rehearsals and stepped into Daska’s way. She told him to move. He caught her upper arm, still wearing the smile from his performance.

Daska turned as if beginning their rehearsed fall. When he shifted with her, she drove a foot against the painted chair and sent them both sideways. The chair broke. They struck the mat, Salo underneath, and Pello passed them with one hand pressed to his coat.

Salo hit Daska below the ribs. She lost her grip. He rose onto one knee and found Rosceline’s rapier beneath his eye.

Ilaro kept going.

The bellman below the platform took the rope in both hands. The clapper was bare now, its protective cloth discarded on the frame. It moved. Ilaro thrust his coat around it and seized the narrow part through the cloth.

The pull nearly tore it from him.

He braced a foot against the frame and held the clapper away from the rim. The bell shook above his hands without sounding.

“Off,” the man shouted.

Ilaro tried to answer. Nothing useful came, only a gasp. He held on.

The orchestra had stopped. Nerisse stood with Pann beside the music stand. Pello had reached her. He was taking the folded cloth out of his coat.

The bellman let go of the rope. Ilaro had a moment in which he could breathe, then the man came up the far steps toward him.

“You’ve no business here.”

Ilaro’s hat had fallen off. He could see it beside the man’s boot, absurdly close, and could not remember whether his notebook was in the coat now wrapped around the clapper. He had written the household names there first. He wanted to save it.

The man drove an elbow into his side.

Ilaro bent but kept his hands closed. The bell rocked, its rim grazing his knuckles. Across the platform the goods opening had become a wall of timber. There was no one coming through it.

Pann looked toward him.

“Get his hand off it.”

The bellman took out his knife and caught a fold of the coat near Ilaro’s wrist.

Chapter 15 — The missing sentence

Rosceline saw the knife at Ilaro’s wrist and shifted the point of her rapier.

Salo caught her sword hand.

He had been waiting for her to look away. He knocked the blade aside and closed his fist around the guard, crushing her burned palm against it. The pain went through her knees. She tried to curse him. Her mouth shaped the words without making a sound.

He pulled her down beside the broken chair.

Pello was at the music stand with Nerisse. He had opened the cloth on his palm and put the ticket in her hand. Across the platform Ilaro clung to the iron clapper while the bellman sawed at his coat.

Rosceline could reach none of them.

She drove a knee against Salo’s thigh. He grunted and shifted his weight, pinning her sword arm against the floor. His breath smelled of stage paint and the meat he had eaten between performances. She clawed for his face with her free hand and caught his lower lip.

He tore away. Blood ran over his teeth.

Beyond him, the goods opening was packed solid with timber. Briska stood beside the fallen panel with one hand to her bleeding ear. Nobody would be coming through it to lift Daska or carry Ilaro away.

Pann rested a hand on Nerisse’s shoulder.

“Stay,” she said. “There will be another room tomorrow.”

Nerisse was looking at the audience. They were still waiting for the last line. The violinist had lowered his bow, but several people were leaning forward as if their attention might help the singer recover her place.

“Let me amend the engagement,” Pann said. “You needn’t go back to selling chairs.”

Pello kept the empty cloth open between his hands.

Rosceline wanted him to make Nerisse fold the paper. After everything, after the wagon and the blood and the intolerable sound of Ilaro trying to hold his breath against pain, he was standing there waiting. She wanted to hurt him for it.

Nerisse looked down at the ticket.

Pann’s hand moved from her shoulder to her wrist. Her thumb held the lower corner flat.

“You’ve had a fright. Don’t make your last decision in front of all these people.”

Rosceline tried to turn her rapier within Salo’s grip. The point scraped the floor beyond his boot. Through the gap beneath his arm she could see the small glass on the Harlequin’s table. Sand was still running. Her voice would return with an exactness that now seemed obscene.

The bellman’s knife cut through the coat. Ilaro cried out.

A few people in the nearest row stood. Others told them to sit down. Someone called Nerisse’s name, warmly, giving her the word she had missed.

She took the breath for it.

Then she pulled her wrist against Pann’s hand.

The ringmaster tightened her grip. Nerisse bent her head and bit her.

Pann’s fingers opened. Nerisse wrenched the ticket free, folded its lower corner over her name and held it down with both thumbs.

“I give it back.”

Her mouth was red. She said it again, no louder, pressing the paper flat while Pann caught at her sleeve.

The blue wax split.

The bellman tore Ilaro’s coat away. The iron clapper struck the rim with a sound that filled the tent.

Rosceline felt Salo start above her. Daska’s arm came around his throat from behind. The monk hauled backward, her face white with the effort. His grip loosened enough for Rosceline to wrench her sword hand free.

She rolled away from the chair and got to her feet.

The room sounded different. A child was crying near the entrance. One man applauded alone, then stopped when his wife touched his arm. At the back, two spectators resumed an argument about whether they had been promised refreshments. Nerisse stood beside the music stand, holding the folded ticket against her breast.

Pann went to take it.

“The return preceded the bell,” the Harlequin said.

They stood at the edge of the platform with the glass behind them. Their rapier was still sheathed. They were looking at Pann’s hand on the singer’s sleeve.

Pann released the cloth.

“You could still have had the room,” she told Nerisse.

Nerisse gave Pello the ticket. He wrapped it without opening the fold and secured it inside his coat.

Rosceline crossed to the bell frame. Ilaro was on one knee, blood running from his wrist into the torn coat. The bellman put his knife between them.

She struck his forearm with the guard of her rapier, kicked his knee and drove him against the frame. The knife fell. His hand struck the boards as he tried to catch himself.

Rosceline put her heel on it.

He screamed. She kept pressing, even after his other hand had opened to show that it held nothing. Something shifted beneath her sole. Ilaro looked up at her.

She lifted her foot.

Daska had let Salo go. He lay against the mat, coughing. The monk had one arm locked across her ribs and the other held out for Briska. Neither could lift Ilaro by herself.

Pello came back for him. He put one of Ilaro’s arms over his shoulder and helped him stand while Rosceline kept the bellman against the frame. Nerisse stayed close behind, clutching the torn edge of her sleeve.

She gathered Ilaro’s hat and coat from the floor before following them.

The man in the third row had risen. He looked at the blood, then at Ilaro struggling to put weight through his feet.

“Bring him down here,” he said.

His wife gathered their coats. “We’re leaving.”

The nearer usher stepped into the aisle.

“So are we,” the woman behind him said. She had been waiting for someone to move for some time, and resentment gave her voice more authority than kindness would have done.

Rosceline went to the ordinary front steps. They were steep and narrow. None of them had rehearsed getting an injured man down them. Pello took Ilaro’s weight from above while the volunteer steadied him from below. At the last step Ilaro’s knee folded. Both men caught him. The audience pressed back around the benches, irritated and frightened by different things.

Briska followed with Daska. Nerisse lifted her skirt and came after them in the walking shoes Daska had made her put on. Behind the painted wall Cazet was guiding Jorise toward the public side. She had the black card still in her hand.

An usher caught Nerisse’s sleeve. Rosceline touched the point of her rapier to his wrist. He let go without looking at her face.

Rosceline could not answer her. She held the blade where the usher could see it and waited while Pello got Ilaro past a woman unwilling to lift her basket.

Daska moved the basket herself.

The man would have gone farther, but Pello shook his head. “We have him.”

“That cart was my morning’s work tomorrow.”

“We’ll come to the yard.”

“You’ll come tonight.”

Part IV — The unfilled seats

Chapter 16 — Loading out

The noise that woke Ilaro was an argument over the bar.

He had slept sitting down in the Finch’s washroom, with a folded towel between his head and the wall. Someone had put a blanket over his knees. He could not remember who, and the question was pleasant enough to leave unanswered.

In the passage Nerisse was saying that the wheel would not pass through the kitchen door.

“Then take the wheel off,” Lusette said.

It came out as a scrape. Ilaro heard her stop, swallow and try again.

“The wheel.”

He remained still. Nobody hurried to speak for her.

“You needn’t do it this morning,” Nerisse said.

Lusette answered too quickly and lost the first word. She put a hand against her throat. Her aunt reached toward it, then lowered her arm before touching her.

“It’s my bar.”

“I know whose it is.”

“Then I’ll decide where it goes.”

From the kitchen came the sound of Tovin setting down a tool. After a little while he asked which side of the door had the wider turn. Lusette went to show him.

Ilaro pushed the blanket off his knees. The window above the washstand had begun to brighten. He turned his chair away from it and waited for the small turning sensation in his head to stop.

The dressing at his wrist had stuck to the knife cut. He poured water over the cloth before trying to lift it. Halfway through, Madal came in for the basin and found him holding the loose end between his teeth.

“Give it here.”

He let her finish. She had a rough little voice this morning, too, but it was hers. She tested a sentence about the price of needles while winding the fresh strip. The first version failed on the last word. The second carried.

“Better,” she said, inspecting the bandage.

He was not sure which improvement she meant.

Basile had put his instrument into its case beside the back door. He came to look at Ilaro’s hand, decided he had nothing useful to say about it and asked whether Nerisse could be required to pay him before the other people who were owed money.

“What did she agree with you?”

“A wage, every week. There’s no paper.”

“Who heard her agree?”

“Madal. Lusette. Usually anyone in the kitchen.”

Ilaro wrote down the dates Basile gave him. The voice stopped once, on a number, and Basile waited with his eyes shut until it came back. He did not want a case against Nerisse. He wanted enough to pay for the room he had found near the harbor. The landlord would hold it until noon.

“I can ask her with you,” Ilaro said. “I can’t put you ahead of people I haven’t heard from.”

Basile looked at the note, disappointed but listening.

“Don’t make it a quarrel. We’ve had the quarrel.”

They found Nerisse in the little room where Madal kept the costumes. She had her jewelry box open. The good silver comb lay on the table beside two bracelets Ilaro had seen her wear during the first performance.

She heard about the harbor room and asked Basile why he had not told her sooner.

“I thought I might not be able to live in it.”

Nerisse closed the jewelry box. For a moment Ilaro thought the interview was over. Then she opened it again and took out the matching comb.

“There’s more for the pair.”

“I can wait until tomorrow for some of it,” Basile said.

“Your landlord can’t.”

She wrapped the combs in a handkerchief and asked Madal to take them to the woman on Partridge Street, who knew the silver was good and would waste less time pretending otherwise. The bracelets went with them. Nerisse kept a little glass brooch shaped like a bird. She pinned it to her plain dress without looking in the mirror.

Nerisse sat at the dressing table. The pen scratched. She described the ticket, wrote that she had made the return herself and paused over what to call the people whose voices had begun coming back.

“Write their names,” Ilaro said.

She did. Madal had already left with the jewelry, so Nerisse asked how the dresser spelled hers. Ilaro told her. She scraped away the extra letter, blew the dust clear and started the line again.

Outside the dressing room, Daska lay across three chairs while Briska sat on the floor beside her. A woman from the next street was feeling beneath the monk’s ribs. She pressed once, listened to the answer and told Daska to stop trying to prove how deeply she could breathe.

He stood near the window holding a cup of water that nobody had yet accepted. His swelling eye had closed halfway.

“He won’t enjoy his breakfast,” Daska said when the woman had finished.

Briska looked up. “Salo?”

“Yes.”

“Does that improve this?”

“No. I’d like it to.”

Tovin came through carrying a wheel and answered his question without understanding why he had asked. Ilaro marked that down. Basile spoke again from the passage. “Later.”

Chapter 17 — One remains

Rosceline found a pin in the borrowed coat while waiting for the others.

It had been put through the lining to hold a seam and left there. She removed it carefully. The woman who had lent the coat would have been offended by a suggestion that she had done it on purpose, which did not make Rosceline less certain that she had.

Celise brought a little dish for the pin.

“Was there something wrong with your own?”

“Several people had strong opinions about it.”

“I can have it cleaned.”

“You are very kind.”

“There is blood inside the left skirt. The lining is silk. Tell her I will pay for whatever she needs to use.”

“I’ll tell her.”

The others arrived before Rosceline could decide whether her meant the laundress or the Countess. Daska lowered herself into the chair nearest the door. The carrier had not wasted the chance to specify what a cart ought to cost.

Ilaro stayed standing until Celise moved a lamp away from his chair.

The Countess came in without the veil she wore before unfamiliar visitors. Rosceline had seen Serafine’s face often enough to distrust the intimacy of that. She was beautiful in a way that let people imagine they had discovered something private by looking carefully.

“Nerisse?” she asked.

“At the Finch,” Ilaro said. “Her household is speaking again. It isn’t steady yet.”

He took the confirmation from his coat and laid it before her. Pello unfastened his own coat, brought out the clean cloth and opened it beside the letter.

The ticket’s folded corner stayed down.

Serafine turned it toward the window without unfolding it. The split wax caught the light. She read Nerisse’s account, stopped at the corrected name and continued to the signature.

“Who saw the return?”

“Pello was beside her,” Rosceline said. “And the Harlequin confirmed it in Pann’s hearing. She stopped trying to take the ticket.”

“She wanted Nerisse to continue?”

Ilaro took out the second paper. He explained where he had copied it and which part he had been able to read. He did not repeat the whole pursuit. When he lost the name of the room beneath the stage, Briska supplied compartment, and he continued.

Rosceline listened with her hands in her lap. She knew where she could improve the account. The pause before the copied instruction was too long; Pann’s face when Nerisse bit her would have been worth describing. But Ilaro had the paper and the Countess was reading it. Rosceline looked at the pin in its dish until he finished.

“The carnival is leaving,” he said. “They were still taking people into the office this morning.”

“I can send the names of Pann and Vellum to the houses that receive my letters,” Serafine said. “And what you can substantiate about Nerisse’s bargain.”

“People will go anyway,” Daska said.

“Some will.”

“She sang very well,” the monk added.

Serafine looked at her for a moment. “I know.”

She put Nerisse’s confirmation and the returned ticket together. Celise brought the payment. The remaining thousand silver marks had been divided into five bags, each heavier than Rosceline wished to carry through the streets in somebody else’s coat.

Briska waited until Serafine had counted her share before offering the expenses. There was the cart, the damaged mirror, the hired canvas, the runner’s payment and the coat Pello had not returned to its owner. The first allowance covered several of them. Celise asked questions about the rest.

Celise put the carrier’s bill between the bags of silver. The agreed hire was covered. The replacement was not.

“She’ll accept another cart of the same capacity,” Celise said.

Briska studied the drawing Trissot had put on the back. “I could build it.”

“Before she loses another day’s work?”

The artisan laid the paper down.

Pello opened his bag and put twenty-five marks beside it. Ilaro tried to untie his own with the bandaged hand. Briska took the bag, opened it and gave it back without counting anything out for him.

Daska pushed her share across the table, keeping her other arm against her ribs. Briska added hers.

Rosceline added her share. She disliked that Pann’s carters would probably be paid for breaking the thing as well as moving their own wagon. There was nobody at the table who needed her to explain that to them.

Celise put the replacement money with the bill. The money for Maret’s coat would go through the porter who had introduced Pello; nobody proposed making him enter the carnival to demand it.

Rosceline took her separate agreement from the pocket where she had moved it before borrowing the coat. She unfolded it herself. The first two recoveries had been completed in different inks. Beside the third stood Serafine’s note accepting this commission.

The Countess read it, drew a line through the open space and wrote the completion beneath.

One remained.

Rosceline knew where Sarem’s book was kept. The room lay two doors beyond the garden passage. She could have asked Celise to bring it while the others settled the final expenses. They would not have refused her the time. That had become another of the unpleasant things about them.

She wanted to hear the catch in his voice before the laugh. She could remember it with exactness until she tried, and then the sound turned into her own expectation of it. Last night, waiting for her voice to return, she had been frightened that she would do that to everything he had said.

Serafine blotted the new line and offered the paper back.

Rosceline folded it along its old creases. “That is all, then.”

“For this recovery.”

“For this recovery,” she agreed.

She put the agreement inside her coat while the others were still at the table.

In the garden passage Daska waited for her to catch up. The monk held her bag of silver against her uninjured side, annoyed by its weight.

“I thought you might go to her,” she said.

Rosceline stopped beside a pot of winter herbs. A gardener had cut the dead leaves away and left the sound ones awkwardly exposed.

“Pann was very persuasive.”

“I saw.”

“I said I would stay.”

Daska looked at her. The mountain was there between them, as present to Rosceline as the smell of the cut herbs. Rell’s lowered sword; the space in which she could have taken another answer. She felt the old, immediate resentment at being made to remember it by someone who had done nothing except remember it herself.

She began, “If you intend—”

“I heard you say it. You stayed.”

Daska went down the passage. Rosceline remained by the pot until Celise came out carrying the dish with the pin.

“Do you want this?”

“Very much.”

She took it and secured the borrowed seam properly before following.

Chapter 18 — The chairs are for sale

Lusette had painted the name of her bar on the removable front board.

The letters were white on a blue ground, uneven where she had tried to make the first one more elegant than the rest. The Traveling Finch. Briska read it while lying on the floor with one shoulder beneath the axle.

“The brace is on the wrong side,” she said.

Lusette bent to look. “That’s where Tovin could reach.”

“I know why it’s there. It holds the end that isn’t trying to move.”

“Can you put it on the other side?”

“If you take the bottle rack out.”

Lusette began lifting the rack. Briska stopped herself from describing how to do that, since the younger woman had built it and was already setting it down undamaged. She waited with the screwdriver in her hand.

The floor smelled of spilled beer despite the scrubbing. Beneath the bar, where no customer had looked, someone had carved a small and anatomically ambitious horse into the underside of a shelf. Briska found it while feeling for the second screw. She chose not to improve that either.

Above her, the Finch was being sold a piece at a time.

The first buyer had taken the best lamp. A pale rectangle remained on the wall where its backplate had kept the smoke off. Two women were discussing a stack of cushions. Tovin demonstrated the repaired chair by sitting on it with his feet clear of the floor, then made the prospective buyer try it himself.

Nerisse stood beside the little stage with the glass bird at her throat. A woman had offered for the red dress. Nerisse named a sum the woman considered an insult. They had been smiling at each other ever since.

“The repair is visible,” the woman said.

“I stood before several hundred people in it last night.”

“I heard how that ended.”

“Then you know nobody was discussing the repair.”

Briska tightened the brace and slid out from beneath the bar. The movement pulled at her bruised head. She sat for a moment with her back against the cabinet while Lusette gathered the loose screws.

“Should it take more weight now?” the younger woman asked.

“It should take the same weight without spilling it at the first turn. Put the heavy bottles low. Don’t hang a bucket from the handle.”

“Where should I hang it?”

Briska looked at the bar. She had nearly answered nowhere, which would have been correct for an object that never needed washing. She pointed to the crosspiece beneath the shelf.

“There. A short hook. You don’t want it catching the wheel.”

Lusette found the chalk and marked the place. Her voice was still rough. She used it sparingly, though not apologetically.

Pello came through the door carrying a bent piece of iron wrapped in sacking. Trissot had let him take it from the ruined cart. He unfolded the cloth to show Briska an axle end curved nearly into a hook.

“For your bag.”

She turned it over. The bearing surface at one end was still sound. The rest would need cutting away before she could use it.

“Not with the clean tools,” she said. “Underneath.”

He did. Then he helped her stand without pulling on the arm she had braced against the floor.

Across the room, Daska leaned against a pillar. She had tried moving chairs until Madal took one out of her hands and gave her a bundle of curtain rings to sort. She was doing that with considerable concentration. Ilaro sat near the door, where he could hear the room without having it on both sides of his head. He had finished the account for the Countess. The notebook was shut beneath his hat.

Rosceline stood with Nerisse and the dress buyer. Briska could hear only pieces of her contribution: the cost of beadwork, the distinction between a repair and an alteration, and a woman’s foolishness in judging red cloth by the light from a northern window.

The buyer asked her to step aside so that she could see it properly.

Rosceline stepped aside.

Briska turned back to Lusette before either woman noticed her smile.

“Try it loaded.”

They put the empty bottles in first, then filled two jugs and set them on the lower shelf. Lusette pushed the bar toward the door. Pello stood beside the wheel at the turn but did not touch it. The whole thing passed through with a finger’s breadth to spare.

Outside, Lusette stopped and looked along the street.

It was an ordinary afternoon. A cart of onions had halted opposite the Finch, forcing people to pass single file between its wheel and the wall. A man was washing a balcony with no concern for who walked beneath it. Two children waited to see whether the bar would produce something worth their money.

Lusette lifted the front board into its clips.

The nearer child read the name aloud. The other asked whether she had anything sweet.

“Tomorrow,” she told him.

He looked disappointed by her preparations.

Nerisse appeared in the doorway without the red dress. The buyer had taken it. She watched Lusette testing the handle, her expression difficult to read until she came forward and straightened the blue board where it sat crookedly in its clips.

“You can’t stand there. The onions will hide you.”

“I’m not opening here.”

“I can see that.”

Lusette moved the bar half a pace anyway. Nerisse smoothed her own skirt, noticed she was doing it and went back inside.

Briska followed. The afternoon had warmed her ear until it throbbed beneath the dressing. There was one curtain left to take down. Its pole would go with the house, but the cloth belonged to Nerisse. Pello fetched the short ladder and tested the bottom rung before climbing.

Briska gathered the hanging end so it would not drag through the dirt.

Near the stage Basile opened his instrument case. A loose fastening had caught one of the strings; he freed it, touched the note and winced. Nerisse turned at the sound.

“That was wrong last night as well.”

“Last night you had an orchestra.”

“I noticed.”

He adjusted the peg, tried the note again and played the few bars that followed it. Nerisse sat on the stage’s edge. She had the money from the dress in her hand. She laid it beside her and joined him halfway through the phrase.

It was the passage she had not finished beneath the carnival lamps.

Without the orchestra, the woman in the song seemed closer to the window. Briska could hear the little impatience in her complaint about its position. Nerisse reached the rise in the melody and stopped, breath gone before the note.

Basile rested his hand across the strings.

“Lower?” he asked.

She shook her head, waited and began the line again.

Pello had one curtain hook left. Briska held the cloth against her chest while he reached for it. Around the room people continued what they had been doing, but more slowly. The cushion buyers stood with the same cushion between them. Tovin stopped demonstrating the chair. Lusette came in from the street and remained beside the door.

Rosceline sat on a stack of folded cloth. Her mouth was closed. She had taken off the borrowed coat and laid it carefully beneath her, lining inward.

Nerisse finished the woman’s complaint. In the last line the lover returned with a mason, who looked at the window and told them the house would have to come down. The melody did not treat this as a tragedy. Basile let the last chord linger just long enough for the disagreement to begin again in the listener’s head.

Somebody laughed. Daska looked up from the curtain rings, smiling with the unguarded pleasure Briska had seen during the first song at the Finch.

The man buying the chair began to clap with it still hooked over one arm.

Pello let go of the last curtain hook, came down the two remaining rungs and caught the chair before its legs struck the woman beside him.

The cloth came free into Briska’s arms.
