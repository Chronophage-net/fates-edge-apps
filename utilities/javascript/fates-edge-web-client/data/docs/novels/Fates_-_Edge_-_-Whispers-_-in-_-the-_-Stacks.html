Chapter 1 — A Letter Before Supper

The window would stay open if Markus put his father’s book beneath it.

He tried a shoe first. The heel slipped on the wet stone and the sash came down, missing his fingers by rather less than he wished to consider. He retrieved the shoe, wiped the sill with yesterday’s shirt, and stood the Concordance on its fore edge. The leather took the weight. Outside, a woman on the opposite gallery was shaking crumbs from a cloth. She saw what he had done and pointed at the book.

“I know,” he said.

She shook the cloth again, this time at him.

The room had been tolerable before the rain. Now the plaster above the bed smelled damp and the brazier downstairs sent its smoke up through some inward defect in the building, so that his own small fire, which he had not lit, appeared to be troubling his eyes. He had complained to the woman who owned the rooms. She had told him to open the window. He was going to need another book.

He sat and began again.

Alayse, my heart—

There was no difficulty with that much. She had asked him to describe the place in which he spent his days, and he had discovered, reading back the first sheet, that he had described his tutor for nearly a page and his room not at all. Alayse would notice. She would also ask whether this remarkable man could be induced to obtain him a chair that did not leave bars across his thighs.

He turned the sheet over.

There is a stall below my window where they sell hot pears in wine, and another where a man repairs the cages used for carrying songbirds. He whistles at his work. I have heard the same unfinished tune since the beginning of the term. When I asked how it ended, he said he would tell me when I bought a cage.

That was better. He could see her reading it.

He told her about the pear woman, who kept a bowl of cold water in which to wet her fingers before handling the fruit, and about the new public lamps. On windy evenings the lamp-tender worked with his coat gathered under one arm and complained to every flame separately. The refectory had begun serving a soup with so much garlic that the disputations after supper were conducted at greater distances than formerly. Markus had taken to sitting near a door.

A knock came before he had finished the soup.

“Master Fenwood?”

“Yes.”

The door opened on a narrow woman carrying six wrapped books against her breast. She looked at his window, the Concordance, his ink, and lastly at him.

“Please don’t tell my father,” Markus said.

“I charge separately for correspondence.”

She put the books down on the chair he had vacated. Her sleeves were turned back above the elbow, and a strip of clean rag protected the outside edge of her right hand. He had seen her at the copying tables, bent over a page while men argued above her head.

“Phene,” she said, when he hesitated. “The returns. You borrowed the blue commentary through Master Aqyl.”

“I returned it.”

“You returned the other one.”

He looked at the books on the chair. There was a blue commentary on his shelf. He could see it now, above the washbowl, where it had been for three days.

“I thought that was mine.”

“So did the other gentleman. They usually do.”

He fetched it. Phene unwrapped one of her bundle and placed the two side by side. The cloth on his own was brighter. He had bought it less than a year before, when new books still looked to him like evidence of an improved life.

She opened the university copy and inspected the lower margins.

“The notes are in pencil,” he said.

“I can see that.”

“I was told pencil was permitted.”

“On the slips.”

There was a silence. Down below, the cage-maker whistled as far as he knew.

“How much?” Markus asked.

“I’ll have to lift these before it goes back. Two coppers, if they lift.”

She showed him a stroke which had bitten into the paper.

“That one may stay.”

He was embarrassed less by the charge than by the care with which she avoided appearing to rebuke him. His father had given him a considerable allowance for books. He had never included the work of removing himself from them.

Phene waited while he found the coins. He gave her three.

“That’s too much.”

“For the window,” he said. “If you know a better remedy.”

She considered it, took two coppers, and went to the door. When she returned she had a broken roof tile wrapped in a scrap of sacking.

“Under the right corner. That side of the frame isn’t rotten yet.”

Together they lifted the sash. Markus slid out the Concordance and she put the tile in its place. The wind entered freely. He could hear the rattle of a cart in the lane, wheels striking the channels cut across the paving for rainwater.

“There,” she said. “Keep the third.”

“Thank you.”

“Turn the book on its side while it dries. Don’t put it by a fire.”

After she left, he examined the fore edge. A faint grey stain had entered the paper. His father’s thumb had worn a smooth place on the front board, and a corner of the binding had been repaired with a different leather years before Markus was born. At the gate, when Braedyn handed it to him, he had smiled and called it the fourth legacy. Markus had wanted to ask what the fifth would be, but Lerris had been standing behind him, for once quiet, and he had let the moment pass.

He laid the book flat on a dry cloth.

In the letter he described the repair and admitted the cause. Alayse liked to be allowed to laugh at him. He had sent too many letters in which everything was progressing very well.

By the time he reached Aqyl the light was going.

He has a way of attending to a mistake which makes one wish to correct it rather than conceal it. I am trying to learn this. I asked whether he was descended from the Aqyl whose lectures Father owns. He said he was named for his father, and his father for his, and then asked me to finish the line I had abandoned in order to ask the question.

Markus smiled, reading it. That morning, after Markus corrected a total, Aqyl had made him write the wrong answer beside the right one and explain how he had arrived at it. The explanation took longer than the sum.

A bell rang from the court. A second answered before it finished, and a third, farther down towards the water, was slow enough that the last strokes fell into the quiet. Markus counted them without intention. Supper would be going onto the tables.

He wrote that he would like Alayse to see the city. He could show her the covered walks, the booksellers, the little garden where the white flowers opened in the late afternoon and made the whole enclosure smell sweet. He had found somewhere she might stay with better windows. He did not say he had asked the price twice and calculated which purchases he could forgo.

Then he signed, sanded the sheet, and went downstairs.

The pear woman was covering her pot. She lifted the cloth for him and let him choose the last undamaged fruit. The skin had split. Wine ran over his knuckles as he bit into it.

“You’ll spoil your dinner,” she told him.

“I’ve had the dinner.”

“Then you’ll spoil the memory.”

He laughed and bought the second pear, the one with the bruise, because he knew whom he would find still working.

Aqyl had a stall beside the north windows. His lamp was lit, although there was light enough to see by, and a little accumulation of ash lay beside the wick. He wore his usual dark coat with the fastening at the throat undone. One curl, more silver than black, had fallen across his forehead.

Markus set the pear on a clean slip.

“You missed the soup.”

“Did it improve?”

“I missed it too.”

“Then we must wait upon other testimony.”

Aqyl put his pen down and ate with evident pleasure. Markus had expected him to continue reading. Instead he closed the book and asked whether the room was less smoky.

“The window is mended.”

“Already?”

“A tile.”

“A sound beginning.”

Markus sat on the bench opposite. Outside the windows the last students were crossing the court in twos and threes, leaning together under cloaks because the rain had begun again. A boy lost his shoe in the channel and his friends waited until he had put his foot in the water before helping him recover it.

“I found the reference,” Markus said. “The toll account you couldn’t place.”

Aqyl swallowed. “Where?”

“In Lexicus. The early notebook. He cites a shelf that isn’t in the catalogue.”

“It is in an older catalogue.”

“I asked for that.”

“And?”

“The catalogue is on the shelf it would tell me how to find.”

Aqyl wiped his fingers. There was amusement at the corner of his mouth, but it went away when Markus put the copy before him.

“Ah,” he said.

Markus waited. He knew by now that prompting the tutor did not hurry him.

“Have you finished for the evening?” Aqyl asked.

“Yes.”

“Bring your coat. We shall need a lamp.”

They went through the public stalls while the attendants were closing the shutters. A woman called that the north walk was wet, and Aqyl thanked her. He took a hand-lantern from a hook and checked the oil before lighting it. Then he led Markus past the room in which the reference books were kept, down three shallow steps, and into a passage Markus had thought ended at the delivery yard.

It turned instead. The floor fell a little beneath their feet. On the wall, a line of iron hooks stopped where a partition had once stood; plaster filled the old mortises, pale against the stone. Aqyl lifted the lantern at the change in level.

“Mind the step.”

At the end was an oak door with a notice nailed across its upper panel.

CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS.

Under that, in a different hand, Condemned.

“Is the floor unsafe?” Markus asked.

“No.”

Aqyl took a thin piece of dark metal from inside his coat. It looked too slight for the lock. He inserted it, turned his wrist, and the door opened towards them.

“Then why—”

“The ordinary entrance is unsafe. This was retained.”

He held the door. Markus stepped past him into an air that tasted dry after the damp corridor. Dust lay thick on the nearest table. The lantern showed shelves, two chairs, a rolled carpet standing in a corner, and a dark patch where a picture had been taken from the wall.

Aqyl brushed a space with his sleeve and set down the light.

“Lexicus worked here.”

Markus put his hand on the back of one of the chairs. It was smaller than the chairs in the public stalls, made for sitting upright. One arm had split and been bound with wire.

“Are these his books?”

“Some. Some he was using. Some arrived afterwards.”

“After he left?”

Aqyl was reading a spine. “After these notes.”

Markus looked along the shelf nearest him. The labels were faded, and several bindings had been turned with their fore edges out. One volume, brown and swollen, lay flat beneath a smaller book. He recognized the abbreviated title from his morning’s search.

“May I?”

“That one, yes. Support the board.”

He opened it on the table with his hand beneath the binding. The page fell naturally to a place halfway through. There was a circle in the margin, a pale stain about the width of a cup, with loose grit caught at its lower edge.

He leaned closer. Beside the circle someone had written ford held. Three small triangles stood beneath the words.

Salt, he thought. It looked like the crystals left when a wet cloak dried near a brazier after a sea crossing.

He breathed out. The grit scattered a little across the margin.

Then it drew together.

He kept his face close to the page. A draught might have lifted the finest particles; the paper was bent; there were hollows in which dust would settle. He watched one bright grain move against the slope and come to rest beside the next. The ring was complete except where the ink crossed it.

“Aqyl.”

The tutor turned.

“What is this?”

He came over and looked at the page. For a moment neither spoke. Beyond the lamplight, one chair cast a long shadow through the legs of the other.

“Copy what you came for,” Aqyl said.

“The ring moved.”

“Did you touch it?”

“No.”

“Good. Keep your hands clear of it.”

Markus’s fingers tightened beneath the book. He could feel the rough leather of the spine.

“Should we close it?”

“Yes. When you have copied the reference.”

Aqyl stood beside him while he wrote. The scratch of the pen seemed unreasonably loud. Markus copied the shelf mark first, then the words ford held, then the three triangles. He began to draw the circle.

“Leave that,” Aqyl said.

Markus lifted the pen. A bead of ink swelled at its tip and fell on the slip.

The tutor placed a clean sheet over the open page. He closed the book without haste, keeping his hand flat on the board until it lay level.

On the way back Markus asked whether the stain had a name.

“Not one you need tonight.”

“But you know what it is.”

“I know that book.”

Aqyl lifted the lamp again at the steps. Above them a porter was dragging the great door of the public room along its worn track. He waited while they came through, then put his shoulder to the wood and drew it shut.

Markus went home by the street with the pear stall. Everything had been taken in except the brazier. Its wet ash gave off a little steam.

His letter lay where he had left it. He had written that the bright rooms were many and that he kept to them.

He read the sentence twice. Then he folded the letter, pressed the seal, and carried it downstairs to go with the morning’s post.

Chapter 2 — Levis Fenwood

Lerris arrived with a chest, two travelling bags, a case containing his father’s letters of introduction, and no idea where his brother lived.

The carrier knew where the university stood. He had never claimed to know where everyone inside it slept. He explained this distinction at some length while a mule behind them tried to eat the tassel from Lerris’s sleeve.

“I paid to be brought to Master Fenwood.”

“And here you are.”

“This is a gate.”

“A very good one.”

It was a very good gate, inlaid with strips of green stone and sheltered by a deep arch. On either side, vendors had spread books and cakes and penknives on cloths. Rainwater still shone between the paving stones. Beyond the arch Lerris could see a court with a fountain, and beyond that another gate through which scholars passed in coloured hoods, each apparently certain of a destination.

He did not wish to begin by appearing helpless.

“Find the porter,” he told the carrier.

The man pointed. A woman sat on a stool three paces away, mending the strap of a lantern.

Lerris had taken her for someone waiting to be admitted.

“Master Fenwood,” he said.

She looked up. “Which one?”

For a moment he was grateful enough to smile without calculation.

“Markus. I’m his brother.”

“Your name?”

“Lerris.”

She put the lantern between her feet and opened a book on the table behind her. The mule caught the tassel. Lerris pulled his arm away and left the end of the thread in its mouth.

“Levis,” the woman said.

“Also.”

“That is what’s here.”

“My father uses it.”

“Then you may use it at this gate.”

She turned the book to show him the line, as if there were some possibility that he might dispute her reading. It was indeed his father’s hand, or rather the hand of his father’s secretary, being clearer and less likely to leave the reader uncertain whether a flourish was a letter or an expression of impatience.

Levis Fenwood. Recommended for instruction—

He stopped reading there.

“Is my brother inside?”

“Not at present.”

“Do you know when he’ll return?”

“Yes.”

He waited.

“When his work is done.”

The carrier made a small sound. Lerris gave him a look that should have discouraged it and did not.

The woman closed the book. “You may leave the chest in the recess. Take the bags with you. The west court, second stair, room under the red gutter. If you reach the fish market, you’ve gone through the wrong arch.”

“Thank you. Mistress—”

“Doreta.”

“Thank you, Mistress Doreta.”

“I’ll want you to sign when you come back.”

The carrier carried the chest into the recess, accepted his remaining money, and departed with the speed of a man who had been paid in full. Lerris stood for a moment under the gate. His sleeve had begun to unravel.

He could have had it repaired at home. At home there would have been someone who saw it before he did, and someone else who knew where a matching thread was kept. He tucked the loose end inside his cuff and took both bags.

The city beyond the court was steeper than he had expected. Passages that appeared to join opened instead onto flights of steps; the sound of the fountain followed him through a covered walk, then vanished so completely that he turned to see whether he had walked through a door. He passed a room full of men arguing in a language he could follow only when they slowed down. One of them, white-bearded and red in the face, was holding a cabbage.

Lerris stopped.

The man pointed at it and said something which made the room erupt.

“Are you coming in?” asked a student at the door.

“Does he always bring his dinner?”

“He’s disproving a distinction.”

“With that?”

“It was a melon yesterday.”

“I see.”

“You don’t. Neither do we. Come back tomorrow.”

Lerris found himself grinning all the way to the second stair.

Markus opened the door in his shirtsleeves, with an ink mark near his mouth and a spoon in his hand. His expression changed so quickly that Lerris felt, unexpectedly, the full pleasure of having been missed.

“You’re late.”

“The mule was considering a different profession.”

“Come in.”

Markus took a bag from him, put it down, and embraced him. He held on long enough for Lerris to stop preparing his next remark.

“You look well,” Markus said.

“So do you. Apart from the beard.”

“There isn’t a beard.”

“That’s what makes it troubling.”

Markus touched his jaw and laughed. The room was small and full of books. There was a tile under the window and a bowl of something yellow on the table. A second bowl stood on a folded cloth beside the fire. Lerris could see his father’s Concordance beneath the window, its leather newly polished.

“Were you expecting me today?”

“Three days ago. Sit. I’ll fetch a spoon.”

Lerris looked away from the second bowl to give himself a moment in which not to make too much of it.

“Father says I am to improve.”

“He says that about the south road.”

“The south road receives more money.”

“Has he sent the letter?”

Lerris tapped the leather case.

“I have not opened it,” he said. “He seemed to consider that a virtue.”

Markus rummaged in a small drawer and found another spoon. “It will say you are of a scholarly disposition.”

“How do you know?”

“He wrote to me.”

“Did you answer?”

“Yes.”

“Unkind.”

The food was rice with cheese and the last of some eggs stirred into it. Lerris was hungry enough that he burned his tongue and continued eating. Below the window a man whistled half a tune and stopped.

“Has he forgotten the rest?”

“No one knows.”

“I could ask.”

“He’ll sell you a cage.”

“I don’t want a cage.”

“Then you’ll never know.”

They ate. Markus asked after the horses, their mother, the roof over the gallery, and a groom who had hurt his hand before Markus left. He asked after Alayse more carefully, trying to give the question the same place as the others. Lerris had seen her on the journey, and had a little packet which he had been instructed not to let anyone sit on.

“She says you describe too many buildings.”

“She asked about the city.”

“She wanted to know if you were happy in it.”

Markus held out his hand for the packet. Inside was a pair of soft cuffs, a folded letter, and a little bag which smelled of lavender. Lerris had not known about the cuffs. He watched his brother run a finger along the stitching and felt that he ought to be looking somewhere else.

“I’ll collect the chest,” he said.

“In a moment.”

“No, I should. The gate woman has it. She might decide it is also called Levis.”

Markus looked up. “What?”

“Nothing. Where do I sleep?”

“Next door for tonight. I spoke to the landlady.”

“Is she agreeable?”

“If you mean will she lend you money, no.”

“I meant will she introduce me to her daughters.”

“She has a husband who repairs locks.”

“Useful. Does he have daughters?”

“Lerris.”

“All right.”

Markus smiled at the letter in his hand. “Give me a little while. Then we’ll go together.”

Lerris took his boots off and lay across the bed. The mattress sagged pleasantly beneath him. He could hear his brother unfolding the sheet, then the light, regular sound of the spoon being moved away to keep the page clean. He meant to ask about the man with the cabbage. He had quite a number of things to ask.

When Markus touched his shoulder, the light had changed.

“You were tired.”

“I wasn’t asleep.”

“You told me that twice while you were.”

They went down to the gate. Markus was wearing the new cuffs.

Doreta had brought a lamp inside the arch. Beside her, a young man with one side of his face reddened from a recent shave was rubbing grease into the mended strap. He listened while she told Markus where she had sent his brother, then looked at Lerris’s sleeve.

“Mule?” he asked.

“An unusually perceptive one.”

“They prefer the wool with the oil still in it.”

“I’ll change my tailor.”

“You could change your distance.”

Doreta pushed the entry book across. Lerris signed beneath the name already entered. His pen hesitated over the first letters, then wrote Levis because he did not want another conversation about it.

Markus was lifting one end of the chest. The young man took the other without being asked.

“Where?”

“The room next to mine,” Markus said. “Thank you, Orest.”

Lerris reached for the handle. “That’s mine.”

“Then take this end.”

Orest let him. It was heavier than it had looked when the carrier carried it. By the second stair, Lerris understood why the man had wanted his money before attempting any further service.

On the landing they had to rest. Markus set his end down carefully and waited for Lerris to catch his breath.

“What have you put in this?”

“Books.”

“You haven’t that many.”

“Father does.”

Markus laughed, bent over the handle, and laughed harder. Lerris began too. He had a pain under his ribs and no room in his hands to hold it.

The landlady opened a door above them.

“If you drop that through my stair, your father will buy me another.”

“Certainly,” Lerris said.

“A stair,” she said. “Not a father.”

They carried the chest up the last flight.

Chapter 3 — The Changed Problem

The first thing Master Sothel did was spill a cup of water on the table.

Lerris, who had been expecting a lecture, moved his papers too late. The wet spread beneath them. Beside him a young man with a narrow black moustache snatched up his own sheets and gave Lerris a look of irritation, as if failing to anticipate the flood had been discourteous to everyone downstream.

“Dry it,” Sothel said.

Several students reached for cloths.

“Without those.”

There were seventeen in the room. Lerris had counted them because the room contained sixteen proper stools and he had arrived last. He was sitting on a box whose lid moved whenever he leaned forward. The master wore a plain coat, badly repaired under one arm, and had a small white scar across his upper lip which made his mouth appear to smile when it did not.

A woman at the far end raised her hand.

“Yes, Istren.”

“Dry the table or remove the water?”

“Tell me which you intend.”

“The table.”

“Proceed.”

She took a breath, laid her palms flat beside the wet, and drew them slowly apart. Lerris felt warmth against his face. The water thinned into shining patches, then vanished. The wood paled. His soaked paper curled at the corners.

A smell of old fish rose from somewhere beneath the table.

The man with the moustache covered his nose.

Sothel bent and looked at the underside. “Who brought food in here?”

No one answered.

“It was there before I came,” said someone at the back.

“A useful distinction. Istren, what have you changed?”

“The temperature of the table.”

“And?”

“The water has gone into the air.”

“And the fish has become warm. Open a window, someone.”

Orest’s voice came through the door. “It doesn’t open.”

He was carrying a bundle of rushes for the passage floor. Lerris recognized him and lifted a hand. Orest nodded towards the narrow gap above the shutter.

“There’s a catch at the top. Push it from the left. If you push from the right it falls out.”

The moustached student stood on his stool and did as instructed. Air entered. The smell diminished.

“Magic doesn’t solve problems,” Sothel said. “It changes them. Sometimes the change is all you wanted. This room is warmer, the table is dry, and we have discovered someone else’s breakfast. Istren, are you content?”

“With the table.”

“Good. Do the cup.”

She looked at him.

“The cup is empty.”

“And I was thirsty.”

This time the laughter was general. Istren smiled, but Lerris saw the small movement of her fingers against her skirt. She had held the working longer than had been comfortable.

He offered her the water bottle from his bag when the class finished. She accepted, drank, and returned it without thanking him until she had swallowed.

“You let him warm it too far,” the moustached student said.

“He asked for a dry table, Caldris.”

“He didn’t ask for a hot one.”

“He asked you last week. We had a wet table and a broken cup.”

“The cup had a flaw.”

“It has a larger one now.”

Lerris laughed. Caldris looked at him, then at the quality of his coat, and apparently decided to postpone a judgment.

“You’re Fenwood.”

“One of them.”

“The other one plays.”

“So do I.”

“Not music.”

“I was trying to give myself a chance.”

Istren fastened her bag. “He means the board. There’s a game after the afternoon reading.”

“Stakes?” Lerris asked.

“Caldris’s confidence,” she said. “We keep trying to reduce it.”

Caldris bowed to her with elaborate patience. He had fine hands and the nails were very clean. Lerris thought he might be one of those men who could dislike you courteously for years.

“I’d be pleased to play,” he said.

“Bring your brother.”

“I’ll ask him.”

Istren was already going. Lerris followed her into the court, where the fountain had been drained for cleaning. A workman stood inside the basin, scrubbing the green from the stone with a brush. Around him students ate, argued, and stepped over the hose which carried the last of the water into the channel.

“Have you been here long?” Lerris asked.

“Long enough to know that one.”

“I meant at the university.”

“So did I.”

She had a long stride. He had to hurry once to keep beside her, which he disliked because it suggested an intention to keep beside her.

“My brother knows a place for supper.”

“Most people do.”

“I thought you might like to—”

“I work in the evening.”

“Every evening?”

“Enough of them.”

He was about to make a remark about the cruelty of scholarship when she stopped at a door and took a key from her sleeve.

“I collate copies. Master Sothel teaches me in the morning because I can’t attend his afternoon course. If you need something copied, ask at the table. Don’t leave it with me in the court.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“People do.”

She let herself in. Lerris stood outside a moment, then looked into the court as though he had been intending to inspect the fountain all along.

At the far side, Markus was crossing with Aqyl. His brother had a folio tucked beneath one arm and was listening with his head slightly bent, the way he used to listen to their father when he wanted to disagree but had not yet found the place to begin.

Lerris went to meet them.

Aqyl greeted him by the name he had offered, without consulting a book. His hand was dry and warm. Up close he looked younger than Lerris had thought, until he looked older again when he turned towards the light.

“How are you finding Master Sothel?”

“He has a damp method.”

“Ah. The water.”

“Does he always do it?”

“Only when he has students.”

Markus smiled. He looked tired, though it was not yet noon.

“Will you play this afternoon?” Lerris asked him. “Caldris says there’s a game.”

“I have work.”

“You always have work.”

“And you have just arrived.”

“Precisely. Think how discouraging it would be if I lost.”

“You’re accustomed to it.”

Aqyl adjusted the folio under Markus’s arm before it could slip. “The reference will keep until tomorrow.”

“I wanted to finish the comparison.”

“It will keep.”

There was a small pause. Markus looked at his tutor, then at Lerris.

“One game.”

“Two if you lose.”

“One, then.”

They met in a room above a bookseller’s yard. The shutters stood open, and from below came the slap of wet sheets being laid on boards to dry. Someone had brought olives, bread, and a jug of wine diluted so generously that Lerris suspected the water of contributing most of the colour.

The board was good. The pieces were not. Several had been replaced with carved wood of a different shade, and one bore a chip that Caldris warned him not to mistake for a distinguishing mark.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Someone did.”

“He means himself,” Istren said from the window.

She had come after all. Lerris tried not to appear pleased. Phene sat beside her, sorting a sheaf of papers with a stone at each corner to keep the draught from lifting them. She did not look up when Lerris took his seat.

“You’re working here?” he asked.

“It’s quiet until you begin,” Phene said.

Caldris played well. Lerris discovered this soon enough to avoid a humiliating defeat and too late to arrange a graceful victory. He had learned from brothers who let him take back an occasional move so that the game would remain worth playing. Caldris permitted nothing of the kind. When Lerris put a piece down and hesitated, Caldris watched his hand until he removed it.

Markus stood behind him for a while, then went to the window. He and Istren spoke quietly about a passage she was collating. Lerris found this distracting. He had brought his brother to lend weight to his own performance, not to become engaged in another conversation.

“You needn’t be kind,” he told Caldris.

“I wasn’t aware that I had been.”

“No. I wanted to make certain we agreed.”

Phene laughed. Lerris was pleased enough by this that he made a move he regretted as soon as Caldris reached for his answering piece.

The game lasted another quarter of an hour. When it was over, Lerris leaned back and spread his hands.

“I have represented the family badly.”

“Your brother can restore it,” Caldris said.

Markus shook his head. “Not today.”

“You promised.”

“I promised to come.”

Lerris began to object, then saw his face. There was sweat beneath his hair, though the room was cool. He was looking at the board without appearing to look at any particular piece.

“Are you ill?”

“No. A headache.”

Istren moved the jug away from the edge of the table to give him room to sit. Markus did not sit. He put a hand on Lerris’s shoulder and pressed once.

“Stay. I’ll see you at supper.”

After he had gone, Caldris began resetting the pieces.

“He’s been working too late,” he said.

“Does he often leave like that?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then why did you ask him to play?”

Caldris looked up. “Because sometimes he plays.”

Lerris could find no answer to this which did not make him sound foolish. He took the other side of the board. The light through the shutters fell across Istren’s sleeve; Phene turned a page; a man in the yard below began singing to accompany the rhythm of his work.

He lost the second game more narrowly.

When he returned to the rooms, Markus was at his table with a cloth over his eyes. The supper bowls stood untouched.

“Shall I shut the window?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

He put down his bag quietly.

“Caldris is very good.”

“Yes.”

“I nearly had him.”

“No, you didn’t.”

Lerris smiled. He took one of the bowls and went downstairs to ask whether the food could be warmed.

Chapter 4 — From the East

Markus had copied the empty space incorrectly.

He saw it when he laid his sheet beside the ledger. Between next gate and reason for passage the original allowed room for two short lines. He had made one. It was the sort of compression he used automatically when copying for himself: there was nothing to record, so he had economized on paper.

Now the entries would not align.

He measured the gap with the edge of a slip and began again.

Across the public room, Phene was teaching a boy to hold a knife against a ruled edge. The boy pressed too hard. She moved his hand, made him try on waste paper, and watched until he had done it three times without tearing the corner. Markus liked working where she could be heard. The room had many sounds, but hers belonged to work being accomplished.

The ledger described a caravan out of Ash–Fenn. Twenty-three beasts, seven wagons. Duties paid at one gate, stamped at a ford, then nothing. The total was complete. The spaces where the next gate and the reason for passage should have been entered remained blank.

Lexicus had noticed it too. Markus turned to the older scholar’s copy, dated 648, and compared the words. The script was smaller than his own and impatient in places, with letters running up into the line above. It was good enough to read quickly and bad enough to make quick reading unwise.

He liked Lexicus. He had begun to feel that he knew when the man had been tired or amused, which observations were made to satisfy a tutor and which had kept him at his desk after the required work was done. On one leaf Lexicus had corrected a vowel three times, leaving the last version identical to the first. Markus had done the same thing that morning.

The toll-keeper’s loose note was bound into a later gathering. It described a game with the travellers while their animals drank. They had wagered dreams. The toll-keeper had taken it for a joke until his sleep changed.

Markus copied that passage slowly. The original writer had squeezed it into the space left beneath an account of fodder. There was no great announcement. The last line complained that he had woken tired for almost a month.

Someone set a cup beside him.

“Is this where you eat now?” Lerris asked.

“Sometimes.”

“It would explain the supper.”

Markus looked up. His brother was carrying bread folded around sliced meat and onions. He had bought enough for two and had bitten into one portion already.

“You needn’t bring me food.”

“I wanted some.”

“You could have eaten it downstairs.”

“And miss this?” Lerris looked at the ledger. “Beasts.”

“Twenty-three.”

“Any worth buying?”

“They’ve been dead for rather a long time.”

“Then Father will say I’ve overpaid.”

Markus covered the pages before taking the bread. The meat was heavily peppered. He had forgotten how hungry he was.

Lerris sat across from him and looked round the room with interest. He had begun collecting acquaintances. Already three people nodded as they passed, and a young woman near the door lifted two fingers in a gesture Markus did not understand and Lerris evidently did.

“Who is that?”

“I haven’t asked.”

“Yet.”

“It seemed premature.”

Markus laughed through a mouthful of bread. Lerris watched him with an expression that made him suspect this had been the purpose of the visit.

“What’s so interesting about the beasts?”

“The missing record.”

“Perhaps they went another way.”

“Perhaps.”

“Or the man forgot.”

“That is possible too.”

“You don’t look pleased by either possibility.”

Markus turned the covered sheet enough for Lerris to see the columns. “Three other entries have the same omission. The arithmetic is finished. The road stops.”

“Does it?”

“In the book.”

“Ah.”

Lerris leaned over it. A strand of onion fell from his bread onto the cloth. He picked it up quickly before Markus could object.

“What does the older man say?”

“Lexicus?”

“The one you like.”

“I don’t know that I like him.”

“You speak of him as though he owes you money.”

Markus showed him the note about the game. Lerris read it once, then again.

“Good wager.”

“Is it?”

“Easy to pay. Difficult to prove you’ve collected.”

“The man believed something had happened.”

“A month’s bad sleep after losing? Caldris could tell him about that.”

Markus was surprised by the irritation that rose in him. He had offered the passage as an interesting thing and found himself defending it against the very objection he would have made a few weeks before.

“You don’t have to believe it.”

“I didn’t say I disbelieved it.”

“You called it a joke.”

“No, I said—” Lerris stopped. “All right. What would make it certain?”

Markus looked down at the old writing. Nothing in the note could establish a cause. A man had played a game and slept badly. He knew that. He had copied enough testimony to know how often an account joined two events simply because the witness wanted the joining to be true.

“I don’t know,” he said.

Lerris nodded. They finished the bread. Markus wished he had not made the conversation unpleasant and did not know how to repair it without explaining why.

“There is another report,” he said finally. “About the caravan.”

“What happened?”

“It was burned.”

Lerris put down the last piece of crust.

“By whom?”

“The account says the charge was heresy.”

“All of them?”

“The account doesn’t count the people.”

Outside, a cart struck the lip of the drain with a heavy wooden crack. Both brothers glanced towards the windows.

“Perhaps you should leave that one until after dinner,” Lerris said.

Markus almost answered sharply. Instead he folded the cloth over the ledger and ate the last bite of meat.

They walked together into the court. The fountain was running again. The workman’s brush had left a bright band around the inside of the basin, and a little girl was trying to float a scrap of folded paper under the falling water. Each time it overturned she retrieved it and began again. Her mother watched from the shade of the arch.

Lerris knew somewhere that served better food than the refectory, and had undertaken to prove it. Markus let himself be led. They ate fish cooked with sharp green fruit in a room so crowded that their neighbour’s elbow rested intermittently on Lerris’s sleeve. The neighbour apologized the first three times and then offered them some of his bread.

Markus went back to the ledger the following morning.

He read the report of the burning. The copyist had preserved a change of ink halfway down the page, noting that the second part appeared to have been added later. The writer had heard voices; those voices had told him to bring his account to Aqyl. An elder and a younger had received him together.

He read it again.

Then he looked across the room at his tutor, who was explaining to Phene’s apprentice why a blade ought not to be sharpened on the face of the stone.

The boy held the knife out. Aqyl took it, examined the edge, and gave it back handle first.

Markus put a slip in the report. He would ask later, when there was time to discuss it properly.

That afternoon the oil in his lamp burned with a faint intermittent sound. It reminded him of someone beating a drum several rooms away. He trimmed the wick and the sound stopped.

When it began again, he took his books to the window.

Chapter 5 — The Use of a Key

Orest was replacing the leather on a door stop when Lerris found him. He sat on the floor with the door propped against his shoulder, one leg extended into the passage to discourage traffic.

“Can you let me into the upper room?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

“You haven’t asked which one.”

“You’re carrying a board.”

Lerris looked at the cloth bundle under his arm. “It might be a picture.”

“Is it?”

“No.”

“Then I haven’t needed to ask.”

He drove a small tack into the leather with three taps of a hammer. Lerris watched. The old covering lay beside him, worn through at the centre. Someone had carved a face in the exposed wood.

“They’ve closed the room over the yard,” Lerris said. “The ceiling is being repaired.”

“Yes.”

“We only need a table.”

“There are tables in the refectory.”

“Caldris won’t play there.”

“Then you don’t need one.”

Lerris leaned against the opposite wall. He was beginning to suspect that Orest enjoyed these conversations. There was a slight pause before each refusal, not quite long enough to be unkind.

“Have I offended you?”

“No.”

“Would it help if I did?”

Orest looked up and smiled in spite of himself. He had a gap beside one lower tooth. “The upper room is reserved.”

“For what?”

“People who reserved it.”

“I could reserve it.”

“Not for today.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Ask my mother.”

“I did.”

“What did she say?”

Lerris looked down the passage.

Orest laughed. He put the hammer aside and pushed the door with his back until the stop took its weight. It struck softly and remained still.

“There. If you want somewhere, the old cloakroom has a table. You’ll have to move the baskets. Put them back afterwards.”

“May we have a lamp?”

“One.”

“Wine?”

“Do you think I keep a cellar in my leg?”

“I am still discovering the university’s resources.”

“Bring your own cups.”

The cloakroom smelled of wool and dust. It had three barred windows too high to look through and a table with a crack across one end. Lerris moved the baskets, wiped the surface with his handkerchief, and arranged the pieces. By the time the others arrived he had acquired a second lamp from Phene, who required it back by the next morning, and a jug of wine from the shop beneath the pear woman’s rooms.

Istren came last, with ink on the side of her neck.

“You have something—” Lerris began.

“I know.”

“Just there.”

“I know where it is. I can’t see it.”

He offered his clean handkerchief, remembered what he had done with it, and withdrew it.

Phene wetted the end of a strip of cloth in her cup and wiped the mark off. Istren stood patiently with her head bent. Lerris found himself watching the brief exposure of her neck, then the easy way she let Phene turn her chin.

Caldris set the pieces correctly. Lerris had put two on the wrong side.

“You could have told me.”

“I am telling you.”

“Without moving them.”

“You’d only move them.”

They began.

For a while Lerris played carefully. He had spent the morning examining a position from his father’s book and wanted to see whether Caldris would recognize it. Caldris did, almost at once, and used the next three moves to make recognition expensive.

At the other end of the table, Istren and Phene were arguing about whether a customer who supplied bad paper could complain that the ink had spread. Phene said yes, because the customer did not necessarily know the paper was bad. Istren said that particular customer did know and had been told twice. Orest came in with a third cup, listened, and suggested they charge for telling him again.

“I’m losing,” Lerris announced.

“We can see,” Istren said.

“I thought you might offer consolation.”

“You haven’t lost yet.”

“I should like it in advance.”

Caldris touched the edge of one piece but did not move it. He was looking at something beyond the position. Lerris waited. The pause lengthened enough for Phene to stop speaking.

“Have you found a difficulty?” Lerris asked hopefully.

“Yes. Your brother made this move last winter.”

“Then I must be improving.”

“He intended something by it.”

“So do I.”

“What?”

“To discourage you.”

Caldris laughed, moved, and the brief prospect of rescue vanished.

They played again with Orest watching. He had never learned the formal names of the positions and referred to the pieces by where they had been at the beginning. Caldris corrected him once, then stopped when Orest asked why a piece that had moved still needed to be called by the name of a place it had left.

“Because that is its function,” Caldris said.

“Then say what it does.”

Lerris was grateful for the interruption. It gave him time to see the trap and avoid it. Caldris glanced at him, recognized the assistance, and let it pass.

When the game ended, they ate the bread Phene had brought. Someone had remembered cheese; someone else had brought a little jar of green paste which made Lerris cough and then reach for more. The room warmed. Rain began ticking at the high windows, and the lamps threw uneven shadows over the hanging pegs.

Markus had declined to come. Lerris had asked once and had not pressed him. He was pleased with himself for that until Phene asked how his brother was sleeping.

“Well enough, I think.”

“He was in before I was this morning.”

“He likes the quiet.”

“Yes.”

The answer left Lerris uncertain whether he had been contradicted.

“Is there something wrong?”

“I don’t know. He asked me yesterday whether I’d seen him on the stair.”

“Had you?”

“Yes. I’d spoken to him.”

“He forgets when he’s working.”

“I know.”

Again that little gap.

Lerris set his cup down. “I’ll ask him.”

“Don’t make a business of it. I only wondered.”

Caldris was examining the chipped piece. “He said something about a room. Last time we played. I thought he meant the room over the yard, but he said it was too quiet there.”

“He told you that?”

“No. I asked why he’d stopped going. He said he’d started going again.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“I know. That was why I remembered.”

Lerris wanted to ask more, but Istren reached past him for the board and began packing the pieces. She did it without ceremony, and the others took their cups to the water jar. He could either follow it or turn them all into people answering questions about his brother.

He put the baskets back himself.

Orest checked the window catches while Lerris wiped the table. Then they carried the lamps down to the gate. Doreta was recording something in the book. She did not look up until both lamps were set on the shelf.

“Your brother came through,” she said.

“When?”

“After the supper bell. He asked whether you’d left.”

“Did he say where he was going?”

“No.”

Lerris went back to the rooms. The table was empty. Markus’s coat was gone, but his hat remained on its peg and the rain had become heavy enough to patter through the mouth of the gutter.

He waited. He took off his wet shoes, changed his stockings, and considered beginning the exercise Sothel had given them. Outside, someone called for help with a cart stuck in the channel. He went to the window and saw three men pushing, their shoulders bent together. One slipped and swore. The others laughed only after the wheel came free.

He put his shoes back on.

At the gate, Doreta told him the public stalls were still open. Markus was not there. Phene’s table had been covered for the night, and a young attendant was gathering spent lamp-wicks into a little dish.

“Master Fenwood?” Lerris asked.

The attendant nodded towards the north passage.

Lerris went down the steps. The air changed, cooler against his damp face. He passed hooks with nothing hanging from them and a door bearing the wardens’ notice.

Light showed beneath it.

He knocked. A chair moved inside.

“Markus?”

There was no answer for a moment. Then the door opened and his brother stood in the gap, one hand still on the latch.

“What are you doing here?”

“Getting wet. You could have chosen a more convenient hobby.”

Markus looked behind him. On the table a book lay open beneath a lamp. There were loose slips, a cup, and a thin metal object beside the light. The room smelled of old dust and a sharper smell Lerris could not place.

“Is that your key?”

“It opens this door.”

“Apparently.”

“Aqyl knows I’m using the material.”

Lerris had not asked that. He looked at the notice.

“Does he know you’re here now?”

Markus rubbed his forehead. “I said I’d finish.”

“To whom?”

“To myself. Lerris, what is it?”

“I thought we might eat.”

“I have eaten.”

“Then you can sit and watch me.”

His brother looked tired enough to be angry. Lerris knew that expression from long journeys, when Markus would go silent for an hour before suddenly objecting to something no one had realized was troubling him.

“Please,” Lerris said. “I’ve come all this way.”

It was not a great distance. Markus looked at his soaked hair and the rain on his shoulders. Then his face softened.

“Give me a moment.”

He covered the open book, gathered his copies, and extinguished the lamp. Lerris waited in the passage while he locked the door. The key was an odd thing, no broader than a finger and dark enough that it seemed to disappear against his sleeve.

“Where did you get it?”

“I borrowed it.”

“From Aqyl?”

Markus was putting the key away.

“Shall we go?” he said.

They found a place still serving food. Markus ate after all. Lerris told him about Orest’s objection to the names of the pieces and was rewarded with a laugh.

On the way home his brother took his arm to avoid a puddle. He kept it until they reached the stair.

Lerris said nothing about Phene.

Chapter 6 — The Window Closed

The man at the ford was called Ionius.

Markus read the name in the translation, then found it in the older account. The scribe had marked it twice in red. Beneath it the ink became ordinary again and went on describing a practice.

He had been reading for nearly an hour. Rain moved against the shutters of the public stalls, a soft hiss interrupted by the knocks of larger drops shaken from the gutter. Aqyl was away at a disputation. Phene had taken her apprentice to collect a delivery, and the only other person near Markus was a sleeping student with his cheek on a closed book.

The account began harmlessly. A young man had attached himself to a teacher of the game. There were exercises in patience, silence during particular moves, a band of salt around the board. Markus had found the instruction interesting. Certain openings required restraint, and he could imagine how a physical custom might train a player to wait rather than fill an uncomfortable pause with action.

Then they took Ionius to the ford.

Markus reached for his pen before he had finished the paragraph. There was a relation here to the toll books. The phrase about a crossing being priced occurred in both. He wrote the first word of the reference in the margin of his own sheet.

His pen stopped.

He read the paragraph again, this time following what the people had done. Lanterns, rain, someone held in water while the others watched. The writer did not say whether Ionius struggled. He did say that the watchers were instructed to remain until dawn.

The space after that was enough for two fingers.

Markus put his hand over it.

Lexicus’s note admitted intrigue. The admission was careful, almost dutiful: he was recording a temptation so that he might rebuke it later. Markus had admired similar admissions in the earlier leaves. They had seemed proof that the writer could distinguish an intellectual attraction from an intention.

He looked at the beginning of the reference he had made.

He had not yet thought of Ionius as wet.

He closed the book.

The student opposite stirred. Markus sat still until the sound of the rain separated again into rain, gutter, shutter. Then he packed his own sheets and carried the borrowed volume to the returns table.

Phene was coming in with her apprentice and a bundle wrapped in oiled cloth. She set it down before taking the book.

“Finished?”

“For now.”

“There’s another gathering listed with this.”

“I don’t want it.”

She looked at him. “All right.”

“Would you keep my slips separately?”

“Here?”

“If you can. Until I ask.”

Phene held out her hand. He gave her the copies, including the sheet with the unfinished reference. She tied them with a strip of waste paper and wrote his name on the outside.

“Where are the originals?”

“Some in the north study.”

“Have you left them open?”

“No.”

“Good. I’ll note the return.”

She did not ask him to explain his face. He was grateful enough that he had to leave before gratitude became another thing to discuss.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The stones shone under a sudden break in the cloud. A dog lay asleep in the dry patch beneath the bookseller’s awning, one ear lifting at every passing cart. Markus walked all the way to the water without deciding to go there.

Boats stood against the quay. Men were moving baskets from one to another, calling across the narrow strips of water between hulls. The noise was abundant and welcome. He bought a piece of fried dough from a woman who took his money with flour on her fingers. It was too hot to hold comfortably. He ate it standing by the mooring posts and burned his tongue.

He would stay away for a week. Longer if necessary. He could work in the public room. There was a commentary Aqyl wanted compared against two earlier copies, and the comparison required no forbidden shelves. He had neglected it for days.

He went home and washed. The water in the basin darkened with the ordinary dirt of his hands. He took care over his clothes, changing the shirt he had worn too long and brushing the dust from his coat. One cuff Alayse had sent was stained at the edge. He laid it aside to be cleaned.

When Lerris came in, Markus was sitting by the window with the commentary.

“Is there food?” his brother asked.

“Not yet.”

“There could be.”

“Yes.”

“Are you agreeing to come out?”

“Yes.”

Lerris looked suspicious. “Have I done something?”

“Not that I know of.”

“We should go quickly, then.”

They went to a place Lerris had discovered near the dyers’ quarter. A narrow terrace overlooked a lane in which lengths of blue cloth hung from poles above their heads. The air smelled of wet wool, cooking oil, and the sharpness of the vats farther down. Someone was playing a flute inside, badly but with determination.

They ate slowly. Lerris talked about home. He had been saving a story about their father’s horse, which had stepped on the hem of a visiting gentleman’s robe at precisely the moment the man bowed. Markus laughed until his eyes watered. Lerris made the bow twice more to show how the gentleman had attempted to turn his predicament into ceremony.

For a while Markus thought he might tell him everything.

Then his brother began to speak of an exercise Sothel had set, and Markus listened instead. Lerris had understood more of it than he supposed. He was trying to describe a difficulty and kept making it into a joke before he reached the part he did not understand.

“Say it again,” Markus said.

“I just have.”

“Without the man falling into the basin.”

“That was the best part.”

“It was. Say the other part.”

They worked it out on the back of a bill with a bit of charcoal borrowed from the kitchen. When Lerris saw where his assumption failed, he struck his palm against the table hard enough to disturb the cups.

“Oh, that’s irritating.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t he say that?”

“He wanted you to find it.”

“He could have wanted it less expensively.”

They stayed until the terrace lamps were lit. Markus did not think of Ionius for nearly an hour. When he did, it hurt in a simpler way. He let the thought remain while his brother argued with the proprietor about whether the flute-player had reached a second tune or merely begun the first again.

At home he wrote one paragraph in his notebook.

I will not go back to the little room. I have returned the copies. If anything there must be examined, I will do it in daylight and with someone who knows what I am examining.

He read it, found nothing he wished to qualify, and put the book away.

He slept.

When he woke, his cheek was against a page.

At first he thought he had fallen asleep at his own table. There was a stiffness in his neck and a cold patch beneath his jaw where he had breathed moisture into the paper. He raised his head slowly. The lamp had burned almost to the end of its oil. The wick was bent, making a little black hook above the flame.

He recognized the wire on the chair arm.

His right hand lay beside a row of marks. Three, a space, then eight. Another space. He had written them in pencil, pressing hard enough to leave a shine.

The door was shut.

For several breaths he felt no fear at all. He was sitting where he ought to be sitting. The work was open. His hands were cold because he had been still too long, and he should finish before going home.

Then he remembered the terrace. Lerris, laughing. The paragraph in the notebook.

He stood so quickly that the chair struck the wall.

His knees failed to straighten at once. He caught the table and held on, making the lamp shake. Oil hissed against the wick. He could hear something slow inside the wall, a beat followed by a pause, then another.

He tried to hold his breath.

The sound ceased.

He waited until his chest hurt. When he breathed again, it resumed.

He did not test it a second time.

His coat hung on the chair. He put it on without looking into the sleeves, gathered nothing, and went to the door. The key was in the lock on the inside. He turned it the wrong way, tried again, and pulled the door open.

The passage was dark.

He had to return for the lamp. That was the worst of it: taking three steps back into the room, lifting the light, turning his back to the table. He did each thing separately. The flame gave just enough light to find the stairs.

At the door of the public room he met Doreta.

She carried a lamp of her own and wore a shawl over her nightclothes. Her expression sharpened when she saw him.

“Master Fenwood?”

“I need to go out.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No. I need—”

She put down her lamp and took his, which had begun to smoke. “Come here.”

“I can walk.”

“I can see that. Sit anyway.”

She brought him to the bench inside the gate. The outer doors were closed, the small wicket bolted. Through the gap at its foot he could see the wet street, silver where the moon reached it.

“What time is it?” he asked.

“Before dawn.”

“Did you let me in?”

“Orest did.”

“Did I say anything?”

“I wasn’t here.”

She moved her lamp closer. Markus looked down at his hands. There was graphite on his fingers and a pale grit beneath one nail.

“Shall I fetch your brother?”

He almost said no. The answer was ready, familiar, and would have saved him the humiliation of seeing Lerris’s face change.

“Yes,” he said.

Doreta opened the wicket and called to someone in the lodging opposite. Markus sat with his hands between his knees. She returned, put her shawl round his shoulders, and stood near the door until his brother came.

Chapter 7 — What Orest Remembered

Lerris had never seen his brother unable to explain himself.

Markus could fail to know an answer, or refuse to give one, or begin so far from the question that everyone lost patience before he arrived. This was different. He sat on the bench in Doreta’s shawl and said he had been asleep, then looked at Lerris as though Lerris might help him understand why that was insufficient.

“You walked there?”

“I must have.”

“From the room?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Were you dreaming?”

“I don’t remember.”

Lerris stopped asking.

He had come without fastening his coat properly. The street was cold and empty, and the man Doreta sent had refused to explain anything beyond saying his brother wanted him. Lerris had imagined a fall, a fever, an accusation. Now Markus seemed physically unharmed, and he did not know whether to be relieved.

Doreta brought a cup of water. Markus drank, holding it in both hands.

“Shall we go home?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

He stood. The shawl slipped and Lerris caught it before it fell. Markus thanked Doreta with painful formality. She said she would speak to Orest when he came on duty.

Outside, his brother took his arm. There was no puddle to avoid.

They climbed the stairs slowly. Markus’s room was as Lerris had last seen it: the commentary on the table, the washbowl by the bed, a shirt hanging over a chair. The bedclothes were thrown back. Markus looked at them for a long time.

“I was here,” he said.

“I know.”

“No, I mean—”

“I saw you.”

Markus sat on the bed. He was shaking now, a fine tremor in his hands that became visible when he tried to undo a button. Lerris knelt and pulled off his shoes. One heel was wet. There was a little mud on the sole.

“What should I do?” Lerris asked.

His brother looked at him.

“I don’t know,” Markus said.

Lerris put the shoes beside the bed. “Then I’ll stay.”

He took the chair by the window. For a while Markus remained sitting, elbows on his knees. At last he lay down without taking off the rest of his clothes. Lerris covered him and watched the light grow through the gap beneath the sash.

He had imagined, coming to Thepyrgos, that Markus would show him how to live there. He had expected to be annoyed by instructions, to disregard some, and to discover too late that his brother had been right. He had not expected the chair.

A cart passed below. The cage-maker opened his shutters. His unfinished tune began, stopped, and began again. Markus slept through it.

At the morning bell, Lerris went downstairs and asked the landlady to sit with him while he fetched someone. She listened without interrupting, then wiped her hands on her apron.

“My cousin used to walk in his sleep.”

“Did he go far?”

“To the well once.”

“What did you do?”

“Moved his bed downstairs. Put someone between him and the door.”

Lerris nodded. He was grateful for a thing that could be done with furniture.

Orest was at the gate with a bowl of porridge. He stood when he saw him.

“My mother told me.”

“When did Markus come in?”

“After the late bell.”

“Was he awake?”

Orest frowned. “He spoke to me.”

“What did he say?”

“That he’d left something. I asked if he wanted a lamp. He said he had one.”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

“Did he look—” Lerris could not find a word that did not already contain the answer he wanted. “Did he look like himself?”

“He looked tired.”

“He’s often tired.”

“I know.”

“Was there anything else?”

Orest set the bowl down.

“He asked whether his brother had gone out.”

“I was asleep.”

“I told him you hadn’t passed me.”

“And then?”

“He thanked me. Went in.”

Lerris looked through the arch towards the fountain. Everything was proceeding as it had yesterday. A student was bargaining for a used book. Someone carried a rolled mattress on his back. Water splashed steadily into the basin.

“You shouldn’t have let him,” he said.

Orest’s face closed.

“You didn’t tell us not to.”

“He was asleep.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You said he looked tired.”

“So do half the people who come through here.”

Lerris heard himself preparing another objection and hated Orest for making it possible. He wanted someone who had been awake to have noticed what he had missed while sleeping.

Orest picked up his bowl. “If you want us to stop him at night, tell my mother. Tell him too.”

He walked away before Lerris could apologize.

Aqyl was in the lower teaching room. Lerris waited until he had finished with a student, then spoke quickly, describing the night, the study, the marks. The tutor listened with his hands resting on the table.

“He came to you?” Aqyl asked.

“Doreta sent for me.”

“Good.”

“No. It wasn’t.”

Aqyl looked at him.

“I’m glad she did,” Lerris said. “That’s not what I mean.”

“I understand.”

“Do you? He had a key.”

“Yes.”

“Did you give it to him?”

“I let him use it.”

“And he kept it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Aqyl did not look away. “I expected him to return it.”

Lerris waited for more. None came.

“He says he decided not to go back.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

Aqyl rose. “Take me to him.”

The tutor walked without hurry, but Lerris had to lengthen his stride to stay beside him. At the rooms, the landlady let them in and withdrew. Markus was awake. He had taken off his coat and was sitting against the wall with a blanket over his knees.

“Where is the key?” Aqyl asked.

“In the study.”

“In the door?”

“Yes.”

“Did you bring any pages out?”

“No.”

“Did you leave anything of yours there?”

“My pencil. Some marks. I don’t know what else.”

Aqyl drew up the chair. “Tell me what you remember, from when you returned your copies.”

Markus did. He spoke in more detail than he had given Lerris, including the report of the ford. Lerris stood by the washbowl and listened. When Markus described the first moments of waking, the feeling that he was precisely where he should be, Aqyl lowered his eyes to his own hands.

“That is the part I dislike,” Markus said.

“I know.”

“I don’t mean the not remembering.”

“I know.”

Lerris wanted to ask what they both knew. He remained quiet because his brother had at last begun to speak without stopping.

Aqyl asked to see the notebook in which Markus had written his intention to stay away. He read the paragraph, closed the book, and returned it.

“For the present, no more of those materials,” he said.

“I had already decided that.”

“Yes.”

“What good did it do?”

The question came out louder than Markus intended. He looked away.

Aqyl waited.

“What do I do tonight?” Markus asked.

“Eat something warm. Sleep somewhere else. With someone nearby. I will speak to the wardens about the room and collect what you left.”

“Is that enough?” Lerris said.

“No,” Aqyl answered. “It is what we can arrange this morning.”

He asked permission before taking the notebook again. This time he examined the pages preceding the paragraph, then the blank pages after it. He did not read the folded letter from Alayse tucked inside the back cover.

“Will you tell me what you find?” Markus asked.

“Yes.”

“Before you decide what it means.”

Aqyl inclined his head. “Yes.”

After he left, Lerris moved the chair nearer the bed. Markus watched him.

“You don’t have to spend the day here.”

“I have nowhere better to be.”

“You have a class.”

“It will survive.”

“Your understanding may not.”

Lerris smiled, too quickly. Markus saw it and shut his eyes.

“Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Laugh because I’m managing to sound like myself.”

The room was very quiet. Lerris sat down properly.

“All right.”

Markus opened his eyes. “I’m sorry.”

“No. You’re right.”

“I don’t want you to sit there being frightened of me.”

“I’m not frightened of you.”

“Then stop watching my hands.”

Lerris looked at the window. On the sill a small fly was struggling through the damp dust. He moved the tile a little, widening the gap, and the fly found the air.

“I blamed Orest,” he said.

“For what?”

“Letting you in.”

“What did he say?”

“That I hadn’t told him not to.”

Markus was silent for a moment. “You should apologize.”

“I know.”

“Not with a joke.”

“I know.”

His brother lay down. Lerris took up the commentary and began reading it badly. He had to go back over the first paragraph three times. By the fourth, Markus was asleep.

That afternoon he found Orest in the yard and apologized. He managed it without explaining how frightened he had been. Orest listened, nodded, and handed him the end of a roll of matting.

“Hold that.”

They carried it under cover before the rain began.

Chapter 8 — The Parcel

Constano’s parcel arrived five days after the night in the study.

By then Markus was sleeping in Lerris’s room. They had moved the second bed against the wall and put a bell on the latch, an arrangement Markus had accepted after removing the more elaborate device Lerris first proposed, which involved a chair, a cord, and almost certainly an injury to whoever needed the privy.

“You could just wake me,” Lerris had said.

“If I know I’m getting up, I will.”

They had looked at each other, and Lerris had taken away the chair.

The first two nights were dreadful. Nothing happened, and the anticipation exhausted them both. Each movement made Lerris open his eyes. Each time Markus saw him awake, he lay still a little longer before changing position. By the third night they were sufficiently tired to sleep, and by the fourth the small bell rang only when the landlady came to remind them that two men occupying one room did not make the other room free.

Aqyl returned Markus’s pencil and the sheet on which he had been marking. The marks were incomplete and the tutor would not guess aloud at their purpose. Aqyl kept the key. He said the study was closed, including the retained entrance, and that the wardens knew someone had entered without recollection of doing so.

“Do they believe that?” Markus asked.

“They have agreed not to rely on disbelief.”

He had also retrieved a cup. It was not Markus’s. Aqyl set it aside when Markus said so, wrapped it again, and took it away.

Lerris did not ask why a cup should trouble him more than the pencil marks. He remembered the cup on the table when he went to fetch his brother. It might have been there for years.

Markus resumed the ordinary commentary in the public room. He worked shorter hours. He complained about the interruption more often as his strength returned, which relieved Lerris even when the complaint was addressed to him.

Then the parcel came.

The wrapper was worn at the corners and smelled faintly of cedar. Constano had used too much cord, as he always did when packing something he would not have to open himself. Markus cut it with a small knife. Lerris leaned against the window and watched.

“Money?”

“Paper.”

“Perhaps money inside the paper.”

“Letters.”

“Money in the letters?”

Markus handed him a sheet. “Read that and stop.”

Constano wrote that the bundle had come from the black trunk in the east room. There were old letters, copies, two pieces which looked like their father’s working pages. If they belonged with Markus’s materials he should keep them; otherwise return them to the trunk. Their mother had asked whether Lerris needed more linen. Constano advised him to answer before she sent it, because the linen would arrive whether he replied or not and a reply might influence how much.

Lerris read the last lines aloud. Markus smiled without looking up.

“Do you?”

“I always need more linen.”

“You need to send what you have downstairs.”

“That requires someone to remember it.”

“Try yourself.”

The two leaves were brittle, shaved from a larger sheet. One bore a heading which had been struck through. Markus angled it towards the window.

“Twin–Oases,” he said.

Lerris came over.

There was a diagram beneath the title. He recognized enough of it to see two opposed edges being treated differently from the rest. In the margin their father had written a note in the cramped hand he used when no secretary would be expected to make a fair copy.

This feels like a rite, not an opening. Disturbing. It teaches the crossing rather than the play.

Lerris read it twice.

“Perhaps he lost.”

Markus looked up sharply.

“It was a joke,” Lerris said.

“Yes.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“I know.”

Markus laid the leaf flat. His hands were steady. The diagram did not look especially remarkable to Lerris; he had seen their father study positions stranger than this, and abandon them with much stronger language. But Markus had gone pale.

“Shall we put it away?”

“In a moment.”

“Markus.”

“I am reading what Father wrote.”

The irritation was justified. Lerris had heard his own voice assume the tone used with a child reaching towards a fire. He stepped back.

Markus read the second leaf, then returned to the first. After a while he folded the wrapper over both.

“I should show Aqyl.”

“Will you?”

“Yes.”

Lerris nodded. He did not ask when. He had made that mistake twice already.

They went out separately. Lerris had promised Orest help with moving a bench, having learned that the easiest way to stop an apology from occupying every conversation was to acquire something else to do. The bench was heavier than either expected. They took it through a door sideways, discovered they had chosen the wrong door, and had to reverse while a group of students offered advice from the passage.

“Could one of you lift?” Orest asked.

The advice diminished.

Afterwards they drank water at the fountain. Orest splashed his face and let it dry without using his sleeve. He asked after Markus, and Lerris told him he was sleeping better.

“Good.”

“He hates the bell.”

“I would.”

“It was his idea to keep it.”

“That doesn’t mean he has to like it.”

Lerris sat on the edge of the basin. Across the court, Caldris was arguing with a bookseller over the condition of a binding. He lifted a loose board as evidence. The bookseller lifted both hands as if Caldris had personally caused the damage by noticing it.

“Do you ever get used to all this?” Lerris asked.

“What?”

“People spending a day on whether a word means something.”

Orest considered. “They pay me to move the benches while they do it.”

“You could attend the lectures.”

“Some of them.”

“Would you like to?”

“Some of them.”

“I could ask—”

“My mother already asked. The one I want is while I’m on duty.”

“Oh.”

“It’ll be offered again.”

Lerris looked at his hands. He had a little blood beneath one nail from the bench.

“What is it?”

“Bridges.”

“Why bridges?”

“Because ours keeps needing work.”

He pointed through the arch towards the lower street. Lerris had crossed the little bridge almost every day without thinking of it as anything other than a rise in the road.

“I could cover the gate,” he said.

Orest laughed.

“I could.”

“You don’t know who to let in.”

“I could ask.”

“That’s how they get in.”

Lerris laughed too, and for a while neither spoke.

He returned to the rooms late in the afternoon. Markus was writing a letter. The parcel lay tied on the shelf, the severed cord replaced with a strip of cloth.

“Did you see him?” Lerris asked.

“Aqyl? Yes.”

“And?”

“He wants to examine the originals with me tomorrow.”

“Here?”

“In the public room.”

Lerris took off his coat. He had mud on one sleeve and tried to brush it away before it could dry into the cloth.

“Constano should have warned us.”

“How?” Markus asked.

“He might have—” Lerris stopped. Constano had found some paper in a trunk. He would have thought he was doing a useful thing.

Markus sanded the letter. “I’m asking him to keep the originals if anything else turns up. Send a description first.”

“Good.”

“Would you like to inspect the letter?”

Lerris looked at him. His brother’s face was composed, which was worse than anger.

“No.”

“You keep asking me whether I have done what I said I would do.”

“I know.”

“I may forget an ordinary errand. That happened before.”

“I know.”

Markus put down the sand.

“I cannot spend all day being grateful for your concern.”

The sentence hurt more than Lerris expected. He had spent the afternoon carrying a bench and had blood under one nail from it. It seemed unfair to return from such reasonable activities and be accused of doing too much.

“Then don’t,” he said.

Markus looked tired. “Lerris—”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No. You didn’t.”

Lerris went to his own room. The little bell on the latch rang as he entered. He took it off, stood with it in his hand, and put it back.

At supper they spoke of the weather. The landlady told them both to stop looking as though they had been denied a deathbed bequest. Lerris laughed, and Markus did too, but they did not look at each other.

That night his brother said, in the dark, “I am glad you came.”

Lerris lay still.

“I don’t mean tonight,” Markus said. “To Thepyrgos.”

“I know.”

He could hear his brother breathing. It sounded ordinary, a little congested from the damp.

“I am too,” Lerris said.

Neither added anything. After a while Markus turned over, and the beds settled into their different creaks.

Chapter 9 — A Public Table

Aqyl spread a clean cloth over the table before unwrapping the leaves.

Markus had arrived early. He had chosen a place by the windows, away from his usual stall, and asked Phene whether she would object to remaining nearby while they examined some material. She had said she would be there anyway. When he began to explain, she said he could tell her what he needed her to notice without reading the pages to her.

He had thanked her, and felt foolish for not having thought of that.

Now she worked at the next table. Lerris was elsewhere. Markus had asked him not to attend, then spent the walk to the university wondering whether this had been unnecessarily harsh. He could not find a way to ask for help that did not eventually make him resent its presence.

Aqyl read Braedyn’s note without touching the diagram.

“Have you seen this before?” Markus asked.

“Similar arrangements.”

“This one?”

“No.”

“Is Father right?”

“About what he felt? Evidently.”

“About what it does.”

“We have two leaves.”

Markus looked at the tutor’s hands. They lay flat beside the cloth. One finger had an old ink stain near the nail.

“I understand the opening,” he said. “I can see why someone would use it.”

“On a board?”

“Yes.”

“Then tell me.”

Markus explained. At first he spoke too quickly, anticipating objections Aqyl did not make. He slowed when he realized he was trying to demonstrate that he had not been frightened into stupidity. The tutor listened, asked him to repeat one step, and pointed out an alternative which Markus had overlooked.

It was familiar work. He felt his shoulders ease.

“Now,” Aqyl said, “what does the leaf require that the position does not?”

Markus read the small instructions around the edge. There was a direction concerning silence. A material to be laid down. A pause whose duration was measured in breath rather than the opponent’s move.

“These.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I would need the rest of the material to answer that.”

“Will you look for it?”

Aqyl did not answer at once.

“I am asking whether you will,” Markus said. “Not whether I should.”

“Yes. I will inquire.”

“And tell me what you find.”

“Where I can.”

“That is less than you promised.”

The tutor lifted his eyes. There was no anger in them. His hands stayed flat beside the cloth.

“I promised to tell you what I found in the room,” Aqyl said. “The cup belonged to neither of us. The sheet contained marks you do not remember making. I found no one else there. I have told you these things.”

“Do you know whose cup it is?”

“No.”

“Was it there when you first took me?”

“I don’t remember seeing it.”

Markus looked towards Phene. Her knife scraped softly across a patch of dried paste.

“You took me there.”

“Yes.”

“Did you know this could happen?”

Aqyl’s face changed. It was slight, and Markus could not have described it afterwards, but for once the tutor did not seem to have been waiting patiently for the question to arrive.

“I knew there were risks in the material.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No.”

The room went on around them. Someone dropped a book; someone else protested. Rainwater tapped into a vessel placed beneath a leak.

“I did not expect you to return in your sleep,” Aqyl said.

“Would you have taken me if you had?”

“No.”

Markus believed him. The relief made him angry.

“Why did you leave me the key?”

“I should have asked for it back.”

There was no qualification. Markus had prepared for one, and found himself unable to proceed.

Aqyl drew the cloth over the diagram.

“We need not finish today.”

“I would like to finish what we can.”

“Very well.”

They worked for another half-hour. Markus described the relation between the leaves and the older notes without reproducing the full sequence. Aqyl asked him to separate what he had copied from what he inferred. Several connections became less certain when stated aloud. One became more troubling.

“The same words,” Markus said. “The same order. Lexicus and I were both told to eat something warm.”

“I have said it to many students.”

“And asked whether we were sleeping.”

“You were not.”

“Neither was he.”

Aqyl waited.

Markus could hear how easily the resemblance could be explained. Tutors had been telling students to eat and sleep for as long as there had been students determined to do neither. Yet the words remained in his mind beside the pencil marks on his sheet: three, a space, then eight.

“Did you know him?” he asked.

“Through his work.”

“Only?”

Aqyl folded the cloth more carefully. “You have had a difficult week, Markus.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

“No,” the tutor said. “It does not.”

Markus sat back.

He had never before understood how silence could be courteous and still make him want to strike someone.

At the next table Phene set down her knife. She did not look at them.

“We’re about to close the shutters on this side,” she said. “If you need more light, take the end table.”

Markus looked out. A dark bank of cloud had gathered above the roofs. The room was colder than it had been when they began.

“We’re finished,” he said.

He let Aqyl wrap the leaves. The tutor gave them back, advised him to keep them out of his sleeping room, and offered to arrange storage if he wished.

“I’ll ask Phene,” Markus said.

Aqyl inclined his head. “Of course.”

After he left, Markus remained at the table. Phene came over with a length of clean cord.

“Do you want these with the others?”

“Yes. If you don’t mind.”

“Sealed?”

“Yes.”

She let him fold the wrapper and tie it. He pressed his seal into the wax while she wrote the date on a separate slip. Nothing moved under his hands except what he moved himself.

“Did you hear?” he asked.

“Some.”

“Am I being unreasonable?”

“I don’t know what was in the room.”

“Neither do I.”

She waited while the wax cooled.

“My apprentice cut himself last month,” she said. “I had told him where to put his hand. I had also given him the knife. I thought about both things afterwards.”

Markus looked at her.

“He’s all right?”

“Yes. Cross with me for making him practise on waste paper again.”

She lifted the packet. “You can collect these when you want them. I’ll want your signature.”

“Even if you recognize me?”

“Especially then. It’s easier to forget who took a thing when you were pleased to see them.”

He signed. She put the packet away without reading his label twice or treating it as though it might bite.

Outside, the rain had begun. Markus stood under the arch until it eased. He was tired, but there was enough of the day left to write to Alayse.

He would tell her about the study. Not everything at once; he did not yet know everything. But he could tell her that he had been frightened, that Lerris had helped him, and that he wanted to hear from her before the next post was due.

When he reached the rooms, his brother had gone out. On the table lay Sothel’s exercise, abandoned halfway through, with a drawing of a man falling into a basin in the margin.

Alayse’s reply arrived with a stain across the outer fold and a complaint about the carrier before she had answered anything Markus wrote.

He swears it is rain. I have told him that rain does not ordinarily smell of lamp oil. If you receive this in a condition that requires interpretation, begin by asking him what else he carries in that bag.

Markus read the opening aloud. Lerris was on the floor repairing the buckle of a travelling strap. He looked up and smiled.

“Did she ask after me?”

“She says you are not to be allowed to prescribe anything.”

“Unjust.”

“Especially spirits.”

“I offered them once.”

“She knows you.”

The rest Markus read silently. Lerris bent over the strap again, trying to push a piece of stiff leather through a slot made for a thinner piece. It would have been simple to take it to someone who did such work. He had begun himself because he wanted the pleasure of mending it, and now pride obliged him to continue.

Outside, the late sun reached into the room. The long spell of wet weather had broken. The gallery opposite was hung with washing, and the people passing below moved slowly enough to be accused of having nowhere to go.

Markus folded the letter.

“She wants me to come for a visit.”

“Will you?”

“Yes.”

Lerris looked down at the buckle. “Good.”

“You could come part of the way.”

“I’ve only just got here.”

“I know.”

“And Sothel would say I’ve escaped before learning anything.”

“He might.”

Markus set the letter beside him. He seemed easier than he had for weeks. The bell had come off the door three nights earlier, after they spoke to the landlady and agreed that Markus would use the room farther from the stair until he went away. Nothing had happened. That ought to have been enough to make Lerris easy too.

He pushed too hard and scraped his thumb on the buckle.

“Damn.”

“Let me see.”

“It’s nothing.”

“The strap.”

Markus took it. He examined the slot, turned the leather end sideways, and worked it through with a small patient motion. Lerris watched him succeed in less than a minute at what had occupied most of the afternoon.

“I had nearly done that,” he said.

“Of course.”

They went out for supper. Istren had arranged a meal in the bookseller’s yard to mark the return of his repaired upper room, although the bookseller insisted there had been no need to mark it and then brought out a larger table. Phene arrived with a pie. Caldris had obtained wine worth recognizing as such. Orest came after the gate changed hands and sat with his sleeves rolled up, a pale band showing where the sun had not reached him.

Lerris had expected the evening to become a game. The board remained wrapped. They ate, and the bookseller told them how the ceiling had first begun to fail, which involved a former tenant, a hanging lamp, and a woman whose name he would not give although everyone but Lerris appeared to know it.

Phene’s apprentice was present. His name was Dasken, and he was tall enough to look older than he behaved. He ate as if someone might remove the dish. When Phene told him there was more, he slowed for two bites and then forgot.

Markus spoke to him about his work. Lerris watched his brother listen to an account of ruined paper without attempting to turn it into instruction. Dasken had cut a whole gathering too short. The pages were being used for exercises now, and he had to look at them whenever a student asked for waste.

“I’ve done that,” Markus said.

“With a whole gathering?”

“No. With a coat.”

Dasken looked up.

“I thought the sleeves were too long.”

“They were,” Lerris said. “While he was wearing my coat.”

Markus laughed. He had forgotten that part, or had hoped everyone else had. Dasken was delighted. For the rest of the meal he kept looking at Lerris’s cuffs.

The daylight faded from the upper windows. Someone brought lamps and placed them on the wall out of reach of the elbows. From a neighbouring house came the sound of an instrument being tuned, then a woman’s voice singing a song none of them knew. They quieted to hear it. At the end Orest tapped the table softly with his fingers and the others joined him.

Lerris did not make a remark. He had one ready, affectionate enough, but Istren was still listening to the silence after the song, and he liked her face as it was.

Later he found himself beside her at the water jar.

“Will you be here next term?” he asked.

“If the work holds.”

“What would you do otherwise?”

“Find other work.”

“Here?”

“I hope so.”

He wanted to offer help and remembered how the offer had gone with Orest. Instead he held her cup while she lifted the jar.

“I like Sothel,” he said.

“Yes.”

“He thinks I’m lazy.”

“You told him you were.”

“I was being amusing.”

“He has a large class. You may have saved him time.”

Lerris laughed, then looked at her. “Do you think I am?”

“I think you like being forgiven before you’ve done a thing.”

It was said without solemnity. She took her cup back and drank. He could have answered with a joke, and she probably expected him to. The yard was warm; he had eaten well; he did not want to spend the evening discovering reasons to dislike himself.

“I’ll try doing it first,” he said.

“Doing what?”

“The work.”

“Oh. Yes. Try that.”

They returned to the table. Markus was explaining some small dispute to Caldris, who disagreed so vigorously that his wine moved dangerously near the edge. Phene moved it back without looking.

On the way home, Lerris asked whether his brother wanted company to the carriage stage in the morning.

“I can find it.”

“I know.”

“Then yes.”

They walked under the covered passage. At the north turning Markus slowed. The passage was dark beyond the first lamp, and a new strip of wood had been fixed across the door in addition to the old notice. Lerris knew Aqyl had arranged it. He had not gone down to see.

“Do you want—”

“No,” Markus said.

They went on.

“Tell her I was helpful,” Lerris said.

“I already have.”

“Write,” Markus said.

“I will.”

“Something besides a request for money.”

“I’ll put the request at the end.”

When he returned to the rooms, the bell lay on the table beside his unfinished exercise.

He put it in the drawer, fetched a clean sheet, and sat down.

Chapter 11 — An Old Room

By the time Lerris returned that autumn, the woman who sold pears had acquired a granddaughter, the cage-maker had died, and Doreta had begun using a higher stool because she found it harder to get up from the low one.

She recognized him before he reached the gate.

“No horse inside.”

“I haven’t brought one.”

“You would say that while somebody else was bringing it round.”

“I’ve become trustworthy.”

“That will be expensive.”

He bent to embrace her and she allowed it, patting his shoulder once before asking him to move because he was obstructing a delivery.

The court looked smaller. He knew it was not. The fountain had acquired a new basin after the old one cracked in a winter frost, and the statue above it had been cleaned badly enough to leave one side of its face lighter than the other. Students moved past him with the impatient certainty he remembered having. One struck his bag against Lerris’s leg and apologized without stopping.

Orest came out of the gate room carrying a child.

Lerris stared.

“Not mine,” Orest said.

“You could have let me congratulate you.”

“You can congratulate me on giving her back.”

He handed the child to a woman emerging from the inner court. She thanked him and adjusted the little girl’s cap. The child retained a fistful of Orest’s hair until her mother opened each finger separately.

“She likes him,” Doreta said.

“She likes pulling.”

Orest had grown broader through the shoulders. There were pale marks along two fingers of his left hand, and a bunch of keys hung from his belt in a leather cover to keep them from striking his leg.

“Still here?” Lerris asked.

“Frequently.”

“The bridges?”

“I work on those too. Where’s your luggage?”

“Behind me, if the carrier hasn’t judged it more valuable than my custom.”

Orest looked past him. “Same room?”

“If it’s available.”

“It isn’t.”

“Markus’s?”

“He has it.”

“I know. I wrote.”

“He didn’t say.”

“He may not have received it.”

Orest looked at him for a moment, then pointed towards the old stair. “Ask the house. There was a room free last week.”

The stair had been repaired. Lerris noticed because it no longer complained beneath the third tread. The landlady’s son answered the door, a man now, with the same distrust of visiting baggage. His mother had gone to live with a sister outside the city. He would rent Lerris the room for the term, paid in advance, and if his noble family chose to send more trunks the floor would not bear them free of charge.

“Your mother said much the same.”

“She taught me.”

Markus was out. Lerris left his bag, washed his face, and went down to the copying room.

Phene had her own table by the east window. Two younger workers sat beneath it sorting scraps, and a narrow shelf above held folded packets labelled in her hand. Her hair was bound back more severely than he remembered. She looked up, smiled, and continued the stroke she had begun before putting down her pen.

“You’re late.”

“Everyone says that.”

“Then perhaps you are.”

He kissed her cheek. She smelled of soap and the faint sharpness of paste. There were new lines beside her mouth, visible when she smiled again.

“Have you seen Markus?”

“This morning.”

“Is he well?”

“You’ll have to ask him.”

He knew better now than to press her for a diagnosis of someone else’s day.

“How are you?”

“Busy. We are moving half the north returns before the roof work begins.”

“All of them?”

“Half. You haven’t improved at listening.”

“I’m preserving a familiar quality.”

She handed him a tied bundle. “Preserve this one on the shelf behind you.”

He obeyed. The label bore a student’s name and a sum. The next bundle was larger and had a note attached in another hand. Lerris recognized the handwriting before he read it.

“Istren?”

“Upstairs. She has a room now.”

“Teaching?”

“Some. Working. Complaining.”

“She was always good at the last.”

“So are you. She also gets the work done.”

He put up the second bundle and carried three more while Phene explained where they belonged. The work was pleasant because he could be useful without understanding it. She told him who had married, who had left, and who had been removed from the teaching list after selling the same commentary to two patrons who unexpectedly met.

Caldris had a post on the other side of the city. He still played. He had recently lost to a woman who repaired instruments and had spent a fortnight finding ways to invite her back without admitting why.

Lerris laughed. “Tell him I sympathize.”

“Oh, we heard about your fall.”

“Everyone heard about it.”

“You helped.”

“I thought the correct account should travel first.”

“Did it?”

“No. But it travelled.”

Phene smiled and passed him another packet. He had told the story of being unhorsed often enough that he could now omit the instant of fear when he hit the ground, before breath returned and he discovered that nothing important was broken. He was less certain which version had reached his brother.

“Do you still have those papers?” he asked.

Phene’s hands stopped for a moment.

“Which?”

“Markus’s. The old ones.”

“Yes.”

“Has he asked for them?”

“Sometimes. With Master Aqyl.”

“Recently?”

“Lerris.”

He looked at her.

“Ask him.”

“I will.”

“You asked me that last time you came.”

He had. He remembered only after she said it.

“I know.”

She gave him one final bundle. “You can put that down. Go and see Istren before her class.”

Istren’s room contained two chairs, a table, a shelf of books and a plant that appeared to have survived years of argument with its owner. She was on her knees fastening something beneath the table when he knocked.

“If that is the notice, put it under the door.”

“It is an old friend.”

“Will you fit?”

She came out from under the table with a length of cord in her hand. He offered to help her up. She accepted, then drew him into a quick embrace which left him absurdly pleased.

“You look prosperous,” she said.

“I look dressed.”

“Here that can be misleading.”

He looked round. “A room.”

“Yes.”

“Congratulations.”

“The ceiling leaks.”

“Then you’ve arrived.”

She laughed and sat. The room suited her. He could see which objects were continually used and which had been put there to stop someone else putting something worse in their place. On the sill stood a cup with a broken handle, full of pens. He remembered it from the cloakroom.

“Are you staying?”

“For the term.”

“Studying?”

“I thought I might attempt it.”

“Good.”

There was no correction or joke attached. He was grateful for that.

“How is Markus?” he asked.

“Tired.”

“He often is.”

“Yes.”

He looked towards the window. Ten years had not improved that conversation.

Istren put the cord aside. “He is helping with the north returns. He knows the older marks better than anyone who can be persuaded to do the work. I think he likes being asked.”

“Is that wise?”

“Which part?”

“The north.”

“We aren’t opening his old room.”

“Still.”

“It’s a large building. Most of it contains books.”

He heard the impatience and knew it had been earned elsewhere, in conversations he had not attended. He could arrive carrying an old fear. They had been living beside his brother through ordinary weeks.

“I’ll ask him,” he said.

“Yes.”

She stood because her class was due. At the door she touched his sleeve.

“I’m glad you’re here.”

“So am I.”

“Come to supper on the sixth evening. No excuses.”

“I don’t yet have any.”

“You’ll find one.”

He went downstairs smiling, and found Markus waiting under the arch.

His brother looked older. Not dramatically; the change was in the hollows beside his eyes, the way he held his left shoulder a little higher under the weight of a bag. His coat had been repaired. He wore the cuffs Alayse had sent years before, or another pair made to the same pattern.

“Did you get my letter?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

“I thought you might be at the rooms.”

“I was working.”

“So everyone tells me.”

Markus put down the bag and embraced him.

Then Markus stepped back and looked at his coat.

“You’ll ruin that here.”

“Eventually.”

“Soon.”

“Then we had better go somewhere worth ruining it.”

They went to eat. Lerris did not ask about the north until they had finished.

Chapter 12 — A Useful Explanation

The roof work was to begin on the first dry morning after the returns had been moved.

That was the arrangement. The men employed to strip the roof wanted a date, the copying workers wanted more time, and the steward wanted both without paying for the interval. Lerris heard three accounts of the same difficulty before he had spent two days back in the city.

Markus said the work was progressing. Phene said the labels were wrong. Istren said both were true and wished everyone would stop bringing the matter to her room while she was teaching.

Lerris offered to help.

Markus looked at him across the supper table. “With what?”

“Moving things.”

“You hate moving things.”

“I dislike continuing to move them. Beginning is agreeable.”

“You can carry the light bundles.”

“An insult, but a practical one.”

For two mornings he worked beside Dasken, who had grown into his height and wore a beard which did little to disguise his delight when he remembered the cut coat. Dasken knew the delivery routes, the marks used by different rooms, and how much strain an old binding could tolerate before the stitching began to pull. He also had a new pair of boots and talked about them continually because the right one hurt.

“Take it off,” Lerris suggested.

“Then I won’t get it on again.”

“Wear the old ones.”

“I gave them to my brother.”

“Take them back.”

“He’s gone.”

“Where?”

“To sea. So unless you’re prepared to fetch him—”

They carried the bundles down a short stair and through a covered yard. Each was checked against a list before being placed on the new shelves. The work smelled of dust, leather, and the sour cloth used to wipe mould from the outer boards. Lerris sneezed until Phene told him to stop shaking his head over the open books.

Markus worked farther inside, comparing the old shelf marks to the new list. He was good at it. Lerris could see the pleasure in his face when a confusing entry resolved into something definite. Twice men came to him with questions they had been arguing over for an hour, and he answered without making them feel that the hour had been wasted.

It was easy, watching him, to forget the bench at the gate.

On the third afternoon rain entered beneath a temporary covering and wet a stack of empty cases. The cases had to be moved. Two workers were sent to help the roof men, Dasken was asked to fetch another cart, and Markus remained in the returns room to finish a group of entries that could not safely be left separated.

Lerris had promised to meet Caldris. He had also promised to bring Markus, though he had not yet asked him.

“Come and eat,” he said.

“In a while.”

“Caldris is waiting.”

“Then he will continue.”

“You said you wanted to see him.”

“I do.”

Markus did not look up. He was following a line with a clean slip, moving it down carefully so as not to lose his place. Lerris waited until the slip reached the bottom.

“How long?”

“Half an hour.”

“You said that an hour ago.”

“No. You asked me an hour ago and I said I didn’t know.”

The distinction was correct and irritating.

“Dasken will bring the cart,” Markus said. “We’ll finish this row, and I’ll come.”

“All right.”

Lerris went out. In the passage Orest was speaking to one of the roof workers. He turned when he saw him.

“Is your brother done?”

“Nearly.”

“We’re closing the outer return after the cart leaves. The covering won’t hold another night. If he needs longer, tell him to move to the public side.”

Lerris looked back towards the room. Through the open door he could see Markus bending over the list, shoulders drawn up. If he went back and repeated the warning, his brother would hear another instruction from someone who had spent less than three days doing a job Markus had been doing for weeks.

“He’ll be out,” Lerris said.

“Has he finished?”

“Dasken’s getting the last cart. He’s only waiting for that.”

Orest nodded. “Good.”

It was close enough to what Markus had said. Lerris walked on.

Caldris had obtained a room above a wine shop with a view of the lower roofs. He was there with two colleagues and a woman Lerris recognized from the instrument-maker’s stall. She had a narrow face, strong fingers, and no apparent concern about Caldris’s reputation. He introduced her as Veleth and offered Lerris wine before asking where Markus was.

“Coming.”

“He said that last time.”

“He is working.”

“So am I,” Caldris said, pointing at the board.

“Then you should be paid less.”

They settled. Veleth was repairing the strap of a case while the others arranged a position for discussion. Lerris drank slowly, still thirsty from carrying books, and felt the dust in his throat ease.

He told them about home. Caldris asked about the tournament and received the shortened version. Veleth asked whether being unhorsed hurt as much as it looked. Lerris began his usual answer, then said yes. She nodded, satisfied, and returned to the strap.

Markus did not come.

After a while Lerris stopped glancing at the door. He had learned not to arrange his evening around his brother’s willingness to leave a page. He had also learned that this could sound like virtue when he wanted to remain where he was.

The wine shop served a dish of beans with crisped onions. They ordered another. Outside, the rain grew heavy enough to hide the farther roofs. Water spilled from a broken gutter onto an awning and was shaken off with a great flapping noise by the proprietor’s son.

At the second bell Lerris stood.

“I should go.”

“Bring him next time,” Caldris said.

“I tried.”

“Try less. He may come.”

Lerris left irritated with both brothers, which he considered fair.

The gate was still open. Orest was carrying a shutter across the court with another man. He set it down when he saw Lerris.

“Your brother found you?”

“No.”

Orest’s hand remained on the shutter.

“He went home?” Lerris asked.

“I haven’t seen him.”

“Then he’s still working.”

“The return is closed.”

“You said after the cart.”

“It went.”

“With Dasken?”

“With the cart man. Dasken stayed to fetch the straps.”

Lerris felt the evening separate abruptly from the courtyard in which he stood. The beans, the game, Veleth’s hands: all of it continued somewhere else, with no way to bring the time back.

“Where is Dasken?”

“I thought he was with you.”

“Why would he be with me?”

“He said he was taking Master Fenwood’s bag out.”

“Markus’s?”

“Yes.”

They looked at each other. Orest called to the man beside him to fetch his mother, then took a lamp from the wall.

“Come on.”

They went through the public stalls. Lerris could hear his wet soles squeaking on the stone. The returns door had been fastened with a bar and the wardens’ cord. Orest lifted the lamp to examine the seal.

“Who closed this?” Lerris asked.

“I did.”

“Did you look inside?”

“Yes.”

“Did you call?”

“Yes.”

Orest’s voice was becoming flat. Lerris heard the old quarrel approaching and tried to stop it.

“I’m sorry. I don’t mean—”

“Hold the lamp.”

He gave it to Lerris, cut the cord with his working knife, and lifted the bar. The door opened easily. Beyond it, the return room was dark.

They stepped inside.

The table stood where it had stood that afternoon. The row of books Markus was checking had been covered with cloth. A cart strap lay on the floor. One corner of the temporary roof covering tapped against the outside wall.

“Markus?” Lerris called.

No answer.

Orest took the lamp back and went towards the inner shelves. Lerris followed, lifting the cloth on the table as he passed. The list lay beneath it, weighted with Markus’s ruler. The last entry was unfinished.

His brother’s bag was gone.

“Dasken?” Orest called.

The name travelled farther than it should have. Lerris heard it return from the passage beyond the shelves, thin and almost conversational.

Orest stopped.

“What?” Lerris whispered.

“That passage is shut.”

Lerris looked past him. At the far end of the room a narrow opening showed between two cases. He could see the edge of a step. There had been a stack of empty crates there in the afternoon.

“Perhaps they moved them.”

“Yes.”

Orest lifted the lamp.

From below came a sound of leather dragging on stone.

“Dasken?” Lerris called.

This time a voice answered.

“Mind the strap.”

It was Markus’s voice. Lerris started forward. Orest caught his sleeve, not hard, just enough to stop him.

“Wait.”

“My brother—”

“I heard.”

The dragging sound came again, then three short knocks. Someone might have been hauling a loaded case up a stair, resting it at each step.

Lerris pulled his arm free and went to the opening. The stair descended farther than the lamplight reached. There was a smell of damp cloth and something dry beneath it, a smell he remembered before he remembered where he had smelled it.

“Markus. We’re here.”

Silence.

Orest moved beside him. Together they looked down.

On the second step lay a new boot, the right one. Its laces were still tied.

Chapter 13 — Someone Below

Lerris recognized the boot by the little cut Dasken had made in the leather above the heel. He had watched him make it that morning, using the point of a knife and a folded scrap of cloth to protect his hand.

“That’ll let it ease,” Dasken had said.

Now it lay on its side with the toe pointing up the stair. Rainwater dripped somewhere below. Lerris listened for breathing and heard his own.

“Markus.”

He did not call loudly. The stair seemed close enough that whoever had answered ought to hear him without difficulty.

Orest put a hand on the stone beside the opening. His fingers moved along a line in the mortar.

“This is wrong.”

“I can see that.”

“No. This should be filled.”

“It’s a stair.”

“It was a stair. There should be a wall across it.”

Lerris looked at him. Orest was not frightened in the way Lerris wanted him to be. He was examining the stone with absorbed anger, as if someone had made a dangerous alteration and neglected to tell him.

“How far down?”

“The old level. If that’s where it goes.”

“You don’t know?”

“I know where it went.”

The dragging sound resumed. It came from below and to the left, not straight down the stair. Lerris could picture the case catching at a turn.

“We can’t leave them.”

“We aren’t.”

Orest took the boot off the step and set it in the room behind them. He put the lamp down beside it, went to a shelf, and returned with the long strap they had found on the floor.

“Hold this end.”

“You’re going?”

“To the turn. Stay where I can see the light.”

He wound the strap round his wrist, not tying it, and descended. His head dropped below the level of the floor. Lerris held the free end and lifted the lamp. It threw Orest’s shadow down the stair before him.

The first steps were dry. Farther down they shone with moisture. Orest went sideways, testing each with his foot before shifting his weight. At the turn he stopped.

“What can you see?” Lerris asked.

“Boxes. Something covering the wall.”

“Anyone?”

“No.”

“Call.”

Orest looked up at him. “I can hear you.”

Lerris tightened his hand on the strap.

“Dasken,” Orest called. “It’s Orest. If you’re hurt, make a sound.”

They waited.

From the room behind Lerris someone said, “Mind the strap.”

He turned so sharply that the lamp struck the door case. Oil jumped inside the vessel. The room was empty.

Orest came up quickly, his hand moving along the strap, his face lifted towards the light. Lerris backed away from the opening to give him room. Neither spoke until Orest stood beside him.

“You heard that?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

“It was behind me.”

“Yes.”

They looked at the room. The cloth on the table had not moved. Rain tapped at the covering outside.

A door opened in the public side and Doreta entered with the man who had been carrying the shutter. She took in the open passage, the boot, the strap between their hands.

“Where are they?”

“We don’t know,” Orest said.

“Then send for Thorne.”

The man went at once. Doreta came forward, looked into the stair, and stopped short of the top step.

“That was bricked.”

“I know,” Orest said.

“When?” Lerris asked.

“Before he was born.”

She pointed at Orest without looking away from the opening.

Lerris wanted to dispute it. There had to be some more recent work, a passage opened during a repair and forgotten by people with too many passages to remember. He could see where the crates had stood. Someone had moved them. Someone might have taken down a partition. It was possible to make a thing less frightening by placing workmen between it and the person who found it.

Then he remembered the voice behind him.

“Don’t go down,” Doreta said.

“My brother is—”

“We don’t know where your brother is.”

“He answered.”

Orest looked at him. Lerris hated the look because he knew what it meant.

“He answered the first time,” he said.

Doreta took the lamp from his hand and set it on the table. “Sit if you’re shaking.”

“I’m not.”

She did not argue. She moved a chair closer with her foot and turned to Orest.

“Who was last through?”

“The cart man. He said Dasken was fetching the straps. Master Fenwood was finished.”

Lerris opened his mouth, then closed it.

Doreta looked at him.

“I said he was finishing,” he told her.

“To whom?”

“Orest.”

“Had he told you that?”

“He said he’d finish the row.”

Orest was staring at the table.

“He wasn’t only waiting for the cart?”

“He was working.”

“You said—”

“I know what I said.”

The words came out hard enough to stop the conversation. Doreta’s face did not change. That made it worse.

“Tell Captain Thorne exactly,” she said. “When he comes.”

Lerris looked down. He could see a grain of dust on the sleeve of his fine coat, left from lifting the cloth. He rubbed it and made a pale streak.

Thorne arrived with two guards, a hooded lantern, and the expression of a man summoned to a difficulty just when he had been promised an end to his day. The expression altered when he saw the stair.

“Who opened that?”

“No one here,” Doreta said.

He listened to their accounts separately. Lerris found that telling his own took longer than he wanted. Thorne asked him to repeat what Markus had said and then what he had told Orest. He did not comment on the difference. He made one of the guards write it down.

“Two missing,” he said. “Anyone else working inside?”

“No,” Orest answered.

“Anyone could have entered after you left?”

“The gate was open.”

“This room.”

“Yes.”

Thorne sent one guard to check the lodgings and the other to fetch more lamps, rope, and Aqyl. Lerris objected to the first errand before he could stop himself.

“They’re not at home.”

“Then we will know that.”

Thorne crouched beside the opening. He put his hand on the same mortar line Orest had examined and swore quietly.

“Do you know it?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

“Where does it go?”

The captain looked at him. “Below.”

He did not intend to offer more until he could be sure of it. Lerris understood this and still wanted to shake him.

More men arrived. A rope was tied to the base of a shelving frame after Orest tested the frame and rejected the first knot. Thorne gave instructions about who would descend and who would remain. Doreta fetched a blanket. No one had asked for it, and Lerris wanted to believe this meant she expected someone to be brought up alive.

Aqyl came last, his coat buttoned incorrectly at the throat.

He looked first at Lerris, then at the opening. He did not approach it at once.

“What did you hear?” he asked.

Lerris told him.

“The same words both times?”

“Yes.”

“The same voice?”

“Yes.”

Aqyl asked Orest. The answer was the same.

“Did either of you answer the second voice?”

“No,” Orest said.

Lerris tried to remember. He had turned. Orest had come up. He had asked whether Orest heard it. Had he called his brother’s name again?

“I don’t think so.”

“Don’t guess.”

“I don’t remember.”

Aqyl nodded. He went to the table and examined Markus’s unfinished list without touching it. Then he turned to Thorne.

“I’ll go with you.”

“No,” the captain said.

“You will need—”

“I will need someone here who can answer when I call. You can do that from here.”

Aqyl looked at the stair again. For a moment Lerris thought he would insist. Instead he stepped back.

Thorne descended with Orest and one guard. They carried two lamps, leaving the brighter light above. Lerris stood beside Aqyl and watched the rope pay out through another guard’s hands.

He could hear their feet, then Thorne’s voice describing the turn. Orest answered. There was a pause, the scrape of something being moved, and a sharp cough.

“Who’s there?” Thorne called.

The rope stopped moving.

Lerris leaned forward.

“Markus?”

Aqyl caught his arm. This time the grip hurt.

“Let them work.”

From below came Orest’s voice. “We’ve got someone.”

“Who?” Lerris called.

“Give us room.”

He stepped back. Doreta had already unfolded the blanket. The guard at the rope took in the slack, feeding it behind his feet.

First came the light. Then Thorne’s shoulder appeared at the turn, and the guard behind him, lifting someone between them. Markus’s head hung forward. His coat had caught under one arm and his shirt was torn at the shoulder. Orest came behind, carrying the second lamp.

Lerris moved to help. Thorne told him where to put his hands, and he obeyed. His brother was heavier than he expected. Together they brought him onto the level floor and lowered him beside the table.

Markus opened his eyes.

“Where’s Dasken?” he said.

No one answered at once.

“He was behind me.”

Aqyl knelt. “Markus. Look at me.”

“He was carrying the other end.”

“What other end?”

“The case. I couldn’t—” He tried to raise himself, then made a sound Lerris had never heard from him before. His right hand went to his ribs.

“Stay down,” Thorne said.

Lerris held his shoulder. The cloth was wet. He could not tell whether the dark on it was water or blood until it reached his fingers.

Markus looked at him.

“You went,” he said.

“I came back.”

“You said you’d tell them.”

Lerris stared.

“What?”

Markus shut his eyes. “I said we needed longer.”

“No. You said—”

Aqyl lifted a hand, stopping him.

Lerris looked at Orest. Orest was setting down the lamp. His face was white beneath the dust.

“I didn’t hear that,” Lerris said.

No one told him whether they believed him.

Thorne sent the guard for a healer and two men to carry Markus. Then he and Orest prepared to go below again. Aqyl remained kneeling beside the injured man, asking small questions, leaving time for each answer.

Markus could remember the cart arriving. He could remember a case too heavy for Dasken to lift alone. He remembered moving the empty crates to clear the space behind them. After that the account became confused. There had been another door. Dasken had said the route was shorter. Or Markus had said it. He could not tell.

“I knew it,” he whispered.

“The route?” Aqyl asked.

Markus opened his eyes and looked towards the stair.

“It was familiar.”

Below, Thorne called to the men above. The rope moved farther this time. Orest answered once, his voice muffled by the turn.

Lerris stayed with his brother. He wanted to go down and wanted to remain beside him and could not endure the thought that either desire might be cowardice. He wiped the blood from his fingers onto his own coat because there was nowhere else to put it.

Doreta brought water. Markus could drink only a little.

The rope stopped. Voices rose below, too low to distinguish. Then Thorne called that they were coming up.

“Have they got him?” Markus asked.

Lerris watched the opening.

Orest appeared first. He carried something in both hands. Behind him came Thorne and the guard. No one else.

Orest put Dasken’s bag on the table. The shoulder strap had been severed, not cleanly. One side was dark with water. The other looked dry enough to have been lying there for days.

“Further passage,” Thorne said to Aqyl. “Not safe with this light. We need men who know the lower plan.”

Markus tried to sit again.

“We’re not leaving him,” the captain said.

“You are.”

“We’re getting what we need.”

Markus turned his face away. Lerris kept a hand against his shoulder, feeling the effort of each breath.

Orest stood beside the table. He took the right boot and set it beneath Dasken’s bag. His hands remained there for a moment after he had put it down.

The healer arrived with two attendants and a carrying chair. While they examined Markus, Thorne asked the guard to repeat his instructions back to him. Two men at the opening. No one below without leave. No answering a voice that could not be located. The outer room kept open until the search resumed.

Lerris listened to every word. When the attendants raised his brother, he took one side of the chair and carried him out through the public stalls.

People moved away to let them pass. At Phene’s table the cloth was still folded over the unfinished work. A scrap of paper had slipped to the floor, and Lerris stepped on it because his hands were occupied and his brother was looking at him.

Chapter 14 — The Empty Place

Phene came while the healer was cutting Markus’s shirt.

She stopped inside the infirmary door. Lerris saw her look at the bed, the bloodied cloth, and the second bed beside it, which was empty.

“Where is he?”

“They’re searching.”

She put her bag down. It fell sideways and a small packet slid out. She did not pick it up.

“He was with your brother.”

“Yes.”

“Where did they find him?”

Lerris described the stair. He kept his voice low because Markus’s eyes were closed and the healer had asked for quiet. Phene listened without moving. When he reached the part about the boot, she turned and went out.

He followed her into the passage.

“Thorne is sending more men.”

“I’m going there.”

“He won’t let you down.”

“I didn’t ask you what he’d let me do.”

Her anger was so immediate that Lerris stepped aside. He watched her walk away, one hand pressed flat against her skirt. Then he went back to the bed.

The healer was a small man who introduced himself as Pellant only after he had finished examining the wound. Markus had struck something under his arm. The cut was ugly but not deep; the greater difficulty was the pain when he breathed. Pellant said two ribs might be cracked and ordered him to remain still while he cleaned the wound.

“Can you mend them?” Lerris asked.

“I can do something for the cut tonight. The rest needs examination in better light.”

“We can bring another lamp.”

“I mean morning.”

Markus opened his eyes. “Leave him, Lerris.”

Lerris sat.

The healer warmed a basin of water and washed his hands, then the skin around the wound. His assistant held the lamp. There was nothing grand about the working. Pellant placed two fingers beside the torn skin and remained so still that Lerris began to hear the small movements of his own clothes. The bleeding diminished. Markus took a careful breath and winced.

“That is enough,” Pellant said.

He sat back, wiped his face, and asked the assistant for a cup. She had it ready. The wound still needed dressing. The shirt was ruined. The second bed was still empty.

When the healer left to examine another patient, Lerris pulled his chair closer.

“What do you remember?”

His brother looked at him. “You’ve heard.”

“Before the stair.”

“I’m tired.”

“I know.”

“Then let me be tired.”

Lerris sat back. Through the window he could hear the rain striking a tin gutter. He had not eaten since the wine shop. The thought of the beans made him feel sick.

After a while Markus spoke.

“He went back for it.”

“What?”

“The bag. I left it on the case. He said he’d get it.”

“Dasken?”

“Yes.”

“Where were you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Was that before you fell?”

Markus shook his head a little, then stopped because it hurt.

“There was a light. I thought it was the room. I told him to hurry.”

His hand moved over the blanket, seeking a place to rest. Lerris put his own hand near it without taking hold.

“You don’t have to remember now.”

“You just asked.”

“I know.”

Markus closed his eyes. Lerris remained until his breathing settled into sleep, then asked the assistant to fetch him if he woke. She pointed out the door through which he could return after the infirmary shut its main entrance. He repeated the route back to her, making certain he had heard it correctly.

The public stalls were lit more brightly than he had ever seen them. Lamps stood on tables usually kept clear of flame. Thorne had placed a guard at the returns door. Men carried rope, a short ladder, a basket of wedges, and a folded sheet whose corners were held together with clips.

Istren was looking at the sheet with Orest. When Lerris approached, she moved enough to let him see. It was a plan, or several plans copied onto one page. The lines did not always agree.

“Where are they?” he asked.

Orest pointed. “This should be the landing. We found your brother here.”

“And Dasken?”

No one answered.

Phene stood farther down the room beside Dasken’s bag. She had opened it. Inside were his dinner cloth, a spare shirt, a small wooden box, and half a heel of bread. The bread had been flattened beneath the box. She put each thing back carefully, leaving the shirt aside.

“Why do you have that?” Lerris asked Istren, pointing at the plan.

“Because they asked me to find it.”

She sounded exhausted already. He had the absurd feeling that he was the only person who had not been given a useful task.

“I can go.”

Thorne, coming through the door, heard him.

“Where?”

“Below.”

“You don’t know it.”

“Neither did Dasken.”

The captain looked at him. Lerris wished he could retrieve the words.

“I mean I can carry. Hold a light. Whatever you need.”

“You can stay here until someone needs you to carry.”

He went to the plan. Lerris remained beside the table, uselessly conscious of his hands.

The next party went down with two ropes and three lamps. Aqyl accompanied them this time. Before descending, he spoke to Phene. Lerris could not hear what he said. She answered with a short movement of her head. He went without touching her shoulder or making any further attempt to comfort her.

Lerris sat on a bench near the doorway. Orest remained at the rope, passing occasional reports to Thorne’s guard. The words were practical: turn, obstruction, water, more slack. Each one permitted Lerris to imagine men making progress through a place that would eventually lead to the missing worker.

Then the reports stopped for a while.

Phene came and sat beside him. They watched the rope.

“He hated those boots,” she said.

“I know.”

“His brother said the good ones would last longer. He bought them because he wanted to give the old pair away.”

Lerris looked at the floor. He had thought the complaint about the boots tedious by the second morning.

“He told me.”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

She nodded. Her hands lay open on her knees.

Lerris wanted to tell her that he had not known, that Markus had said half an hour, that everyone had misunderstood an ordinary thing. Beside him Phene sat listening for her former apprentice’s voice.

“I told Orest Markus was only waiting for the cart,” he said.

She turned.

“He was still working. I thought he’d finish before the door closed.”

Phene looked at him for long enough that he wanted her to speak, even angrily.

“Does Thorne know?”

“Yes.”

“Then tell Dasken when he comes up.”

She turned back towards the rope.

An hour passed. A man brought bread and cups of something hot from the kitchen. Lerris drank without tasting it. Phene kept hers between her hands until it cooled.

When the search party returned, Aqyl was wet to the waist. One guard had a cut across his cheek. They had found a storeroom, a blocked arch, water beyond it, and no Dasken. The passage on the plan did not join where it ought to join. Thorne questioned them in low voices. Orest watched their mouths while they spoke, as if he might catch some useful difference between the words and the speaking.

Phene stood.

“Again?” she asked.

Aqyl looked at Thorne.

“After we’ve changed the lamps,” the captain said.

Lerris rose before anyone could ask. He took the empty oil vessels to the lamp table, found the filled ones, and brought them back. His hands shook less when there was something in them.

Outside, the rain stopped. Water continued dripping from the eaves, each drop landing clearly in the quiet court. Somewhere a door opened onto the first preparations for morning.

Phene folded the dry shirt over her arm and waited beside the stair.

Chapter 15 — The Water in the Wall

The third search came back sooner than the second.

Lerris was trimming a wick when the rope tightened in Orest’s hands. Orest called down, listened, and called for another man. The guard beside the table crossed to him. Together they began taking in the rope.

“Have they found him?” Phene asked.

“Someone’s coming up.”

That was all Orest would say. He braced one foot against the shelving frame. The rope left a wet line across the floor as it gathered behind him.

Aqyl appeared first, bent almost double beneath the lintel. Water streamed from his coat. Behind him two guards were lifting a third man, who was trying to use his feet and failing. Thorne came last, carrying both lamps.

The injured man’s eyes were open. He wore a leather apron over a short coat, and a little collection of nails had spilled from a pocket into the fold at his waist. Lerris had seen him on the roof the previous afternoon, handing lengths of timber up to another worker.

“Where was he?” Orest asked.

“In the water,” Thorne said. “Move that bench.”

Lerris and the guard pulled it clear. They laid the man on the floor. He coughed water onto his own sleeve, then rolled towards the warmth of the nearest lamp. Phene knelt and took away the wet cloth pressed against his mouth.

“Easy,” she said. “Breathe first.”

“I was—”

“After.”

His apron was caught beneath him. Lerris cut the fastening when it would not untie and helped pull it free. The nails scattered, bright against the floor. One of the guards began gathering them automatically, then stopped.

The man gripped Lerris’s wrist.

“My mate.”

“Where?”

“Up there.”

He pointed towards the ceiling.

“On the roof?”

The man nodded, coughed again, and could say nothing for a while.

Thorne sent someone to look. Doreta went to the infirmary. Orest fetched dry cloths and knelt by the man’s boots, working at the buckles with fingers made clumsy by the wet rope.

Lerris kept his wrist still until the man released it. He wanted to ask about Dasken. The question was so urgent that it seemed almost cruel to let the man breathe without asking it.

Aqyl stood beside the table, wringing water from his sleeve into a bowl. His hands shook. Lerris had never seen that before. The tutor noticed him looking and set the bowl down.

“What happened?”

“The water rose.”

“How far?”

“Far enough.”

Thorne answered more fully. Beyond the blocked arch they had found a narrow gap. One guard went through on a rope and reached a level floor. The water there was ankle-deep. While he called back, it climbed his legs. They pulled him out before it reached his chest and saw another man caught against the far side of the arch.

“No current until we moved him,” Thorne said. “Then it tried to take all three.”

“Could there be another way in?” Orest asked.

“There is another way in. He came from somewhere.”

The roof worker had begun shivering violently. Phene laid Dasken’s shirt over his chest and tucked it beneath the blanket. She did it quickly, before Lerris could offer something else.

By the time the guard returned from above, the man’s mate had come down the outside ladder and was arguing at the gate. He had been left holding a timber while his companion went to fetch wedges. He said he had waited half an hour. Doreta said no one had come through the court. Both men were brought to the returns room and separated before they could improve one another’s recollection.

The man from the water remembered a door. He had opened it because it stood where the tool cupboard should be. He could not remember any stair.

“Was it dark?” Thorne asked.

“No.”

“What light?”

“Window.”

“Where?”

The man’s face twisted. “Behind me, I think. I don’t know. I was looking for the wedges.”

He began to cry, angrily, wiping his eyes with the back of his hand. His mate made a movement towards him. Thorne let him go. The two sat together on the bench while the attendants prepared the carrying chair again.

Phene stood near the table. Her hands were empty now. Lerris looked at the shirt beneath the blanket and could not think what to say to her.

“Did you see anyone else?” she asked the rescued man.

He shook his head.

“A young man. Dark beard. One boot missing.”

“I didn’t see.”

“Did you hear him?”

The man looked at his mate, then at her.

“There were people talking.”

“What were they saying?”

“I couldn’t—”

“Anything. One word.”

Thorne touched the back of the bench. “He needs to go.”

Phene stepped aside at once. Lerris watched her make room for the attendants and knew she would ask again as soon as the man could answer.

The captain ordered the roof cleared. He closed the northern passage at both ends, left guards at the returns door, and sent the remaining workers to report every route they had used that morning. Men who had expected to be paid for a day’s work stood in the court with their tools, asking whether they should wait or go home. No one yet knew what to tell them.

Orest took the plan and went out to the yard. Lerris followed.

Morning had reached the upper windows. The air smelled fresh after the rain, almost indecently pleasant. A strip of loose roof cloth lifted and fell in the breeze. Water ran steadily from a spout into the channel below it.

Orest held the plan against the wall.

“That drain,” he said. “It goes down the outside. The water below shouldn’t be getting in from here.”

“From the old roof?”

“Some. Not that much.”

He folded the plan to expose another line. Then he lowered it and looked at the building itself.

Lerris waited.

“You think someone altered it?”

“People have been altering it for two hundred years.”

“Recently.”

Orest shook his head. “I think I need to see where that pipe comes out.”

He climbed onto a low coping and leaned across to the inspection opening. Lerris held the back of his belt when he reached too far. The wood cover came away with a little shower of rotten fibres.

Inside, something knocked against the pipe.

Orest drew back.

The sound came again. Three knocks, with a pause between the second and third.

Lerris looked up at him.

“Someone below?”

“Could be debris.”

Orest put his hand against the pipe. Its vibration was visible in his fingers. He listened for a long time, then climbed down.

“Fetch the captain.”

“Do you think—”

“Fetch him.”

When Thorne came, the knocking had stopped. They opened a second inspection point nearer the ground and found water, a broken piece of lath, and a strip of cloth caught round the inside bend. Orest pulled the lath free. The cloth would not come. It had been wound tightly about something beyond the reach of his fingers.

Thorne looked at the width of the pipe.

“No person through there.”

“No,” Orest said.

Phene had followed them out. She knelt beside the opening and looked at the cloth without touching it.

“What is it?” Lerris asked.

“Binding linen.”

She showed him the straight edge and the little regular punctures where stitching had passed through it. He could see no reason for it to be in a roof drain. That no longer seemed the most difficult thing about the morning.

Orest fetched a hook. They drew out a few more inches before the fabric tore. Phene held the wet fragment over her palm.

“There are cases wrapped in this,” she said. “In the north returns.”

“Can you tell which?” Thorne asked.

“Not from this.”

She rose with difficulty, one hand pressed to the wall. Lerris offered his arm. She took it without looking at him, steadied herself, and let go.

Orest set the inspection cover back in place. The water resumed its small, continuous sound.

Chapter 16 — The Room Above the Sick

Markus woke while someone was being sick behind the screen.

For a moment he believed he was on a boat. The narrow bed, the water sounds, the pain when he tried to turn: he had slept badly on crossings before. Then the smell of the dressing brought back the infirmary, and with it the stair.

He kept his eyes closed.

The other man retched again. An attendant spoke to him quietly. Markus recognized the voice as the woman who had held the lamp while Pellant worked. He tried to recall what she had been called and could not. This seemed an important failure until he remembered that no one had told him.

He opened his eyes.

A cup stood on a stool beside the bed. For one instant he saw the cup from the study, the one Aqyl had taken away ten years earlier. Then he looked properly and it was nothing like it. This one was red clay with a broad handle, chipped at the rim. The study cup had been pale and thin.

He reached for the red one. His ribs stopped him.

The attendant came round the screen, saw what he wanted, and held it while he drank.

“Slowly.”

He obeyed. The water tasted of the vessel, and his mouth was dry enough that he did not mind.

“Has anyone come?”

“Your brother went to the search.”

“Before that.”

“Master Aqyl asked after you. You were sleeping.”

“Did they find Dasken?”

“No word yet.”

She put the cup down where he could reach it more easily. He thanked her and asked her name.

“Lessa.”

“I’m sorry. I should have—”

“You’ve had other things to attend to.”

She went back behind the screen. Markus listened to her move a basin. He wondered what she thought he had been attending to. Most of the night was missing, and the parts he could remember arrived without any certainty about the spaces between them.

He had carried one end of a case. Dasken had told him to mind the strap. There had been a light ahead, the colour of afternoon through old glass. He remembered feeling annoyed that someone had left a route so cluttered when it could have been useful all week.

He did not remember deciding to use it.

The screen shifted. The rescued roof worker asked for his mate, and Lessa told him the man was outside. The answer satisfied him for a few breaths. Then he asked again.

Markus understood the need to hear the answer more than once. He hated understanding it.

When Istren arrived, she carried a small slate and no flowers, food, or other object that would require him to look pleased.

“Can you talk?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Pellant says ten minutes.”

“He hasn’t asked me.”

“He looked at you.”

She sat beside the bed, holding the slate on her knees. There was chalk on the edge of her sleeve. Markus had spent enough time working with her to recognize the particular impatience in her face: she had a question and was trying not to make the asking itself another burden.

“Go on,” he said.

“The case you were moving. Which one?”

He shut his eyes and tried to place it. Grey wrapping, older straps, one board warped at the corner. There had been a mark beneath his hand.

“The returns from the long room. I think.”

“Do you remember the mark?”

“An old one. Before the shelves were renumbered.”

She turned the slate. She had copied three marks, leaving space beside each.

He looked at them. The middle one seemed familiar, but he could not tell whether that was because he remembered seeing it or because he had spent weeks reading similar marks.

“I won’t guess.”

“Good.”

“The lower stroke should be longer,” he said, pointing to the first.

“In the copy?”

“Yes. It distinguishes the inner room from the stair cupboard.”

She altered it. He watched the line lengthen and felt a small relief at doing something he could still do.

“We found binding linen in the drain,” she said. “Phene thinks it may be from one of the cases.”

“Which drain?”

“The yard pipe.”

He tried to picture the yard against the return room and felt the familiar plan resist him. It was not a complete failure. He knew where the windows stood, where the table had been, how many steps he had descended with the case. He could not place those things beside one another without moving one through a wall.

“I can’t help with that.”

“You’ve helped with the mark.”

She did not say more. He was too tired to be congratulated for being useful.

“Did Lerris tell them?” he asked.

“Tell them what?”

“That I wanted longer.”

Istren held the slate still.

“He told Thorne what he remembers.”

“What does he remember?”

“You saying half an hour.”

Markus looked at the cup.

“I went to the door,” he said. “Afterwards.”

“Did you see him?”

“I thought I did.”

“Where?”

“In the passage.”

“His face?”

He tried to recover the face and found instead the back of a coat. A sleeve. The sense of someone listening, already impatient to be away.

“I don’t know.”

Istren did not write anything down. He wanted her to, then was glad she had not.

“Will you tell him?” he asked.

“If you want me to.”

“No. I’ll tell him.”

She nodded.

He was remembering something else now. His own voice saying that he would come out when the row was done. Then a reply, perhaps. He could not distinguish speech from what he had expected the other person to say.

“Don’t let them use my account to place the passage,” he said.

“We aren’t.”

“It feels clear until I try to put it in order.”

“Then we’ll keep it as it is.”

From behind the screen came a small groan. Istren waited until Lessa had spoken to the other patient, then asked whether Markus wanted anything brought from his room.

“My notebook.”

She hesitated.

“The ordinary one,” he said. “With Alayse’s letter.”

“I know which.”

“And a clean shirt. Lerris will bring something impossible.”

She smiled. “I’ll ask Phene to choose.”

When she stood, he caught her sleeve before he could decide whether to ask.

“What are they doing now?”

“Looking for another way to the flooded room.”

“Not stopping?”

“No.”

He let go. Istren remained a moment longer, as though giving him time to ask the same thing again. He did not.

Later, Lerris came with the shirt Istren had promised. It was Markus’s own. He laid it on the stool and sat without speaking.

“You look terrible,” Markus said.

“I’ve been making an effort.”

He wanted to laugh. His ribs permitted only the beginning of it.

“Don’t.”

“Sorry.”

Lerris looked towards the screen. “That’s the man from the roof.”

“I gathered.”

“He went for wedges.”

“Yes.”

“They found him below.”

Markus watched his brother’s hands. There was dried blood beneath one nail. He recognized it as his own with a strange, small shock.

“I may not have spoken to you,” he said.

Lerris went still.

“In the passage. I remember a coat. I don’t know.”

“You told Istren?”

“Yes.”

His brother looked down. Relief passed over his face and was followed so quickly by shame that Markus wished he had said nothing.

“I still told Orest you were done,” Lerris said.

“Was I?”

“No.”

“Then tell him that.”

“I have.”

Markus nodded. He was tired again. The conversation had required more of him than Istren’s slate.

Lerris reached towards the cup, saw that it was full, and withdrew his hand.

“Read to me,” Markus said.

“What?”

“Anything from the table. Something dull.”

Lerris fetched a little book from the end of the room. It was an account of fevers, with several remedies crossed out in different inks.

“Not that,” Markus said.

“It was your instruction.”

“Something else.”

His brother found a worn volume beneath it, opened it, and looked doubtful.

“Gardens.”

“Perfect.”

Lerris began. He read too quickly at first, then settled into a slower pace when he saw Markus close his eyes. The author disliked planting trees near a wall and spent a considerable time explaining several instances in which other people had done so against his advice.

For a while Markus listened to the failures of those absent gardeners. The bed hurt less when he stopped trying to find a better way to lie in it. His brother stumbled over a word, corrected himself, and went on.

Chapter 17 — The People Outside

By midday the court had filled with people who wanted to pass through it.

Some wanted their books. Some had work inside. A woman carrying a basket of bread wanted to know whether she was still expected to deliver it to the north hall or whether someone would come and take it from her. Two students argued that a closed room did not excuse them from a disputation at which the books they needed were expected to be cited accurately.

Doreta dealt with the bread first.

“Here,” she said, pointing to the covered bench. “Someone will pay you.”

“Who?”

“Me, if no one else does.”

The woman put down the basket. The students waited until she had finished counting the loaves, then resumed their argument.

Lerris came out of the infirmary passage into this ordinary impatience and felt, absurdly, that everyone ought to have known. Dasken had been missing since the previous evening. Markus could not turn in bed. Yet one of the students was complaining about damp entering a binding.

He almost said something.

Then he saw Phene at the edge of the crowd. She was listening to the student and asking which book he meant.

“You’re helping him?” Lerris said when she came away.

“I’m finding out what is where.”

“He can wait.”

“He is waiting.”

She handed him a list. “Take that to Istren. The upper cupboards are still accessible from the south walk. Thorne wants to know which ones have been opened since yesterday.”

Lerris took it. He had slept for less than an hour in the infirmary chair. The words moved a little until he held the page farther away.

“Have you eaten?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He did not believe her, but she was already speaking to the bread woman.

Istren was in a small room off the court with Thorne, Aqyl, Orest, and a steward Lerris knew only by sight. The steward had an anxious habit of turning his ring round his finger. On the table lay the lower plan, the revised returns list, and a clean sheet which no one had yet marked.

“I’m not asking you to abandon him,” the steward was saying. “I’m asking which rooms we can safely use.”

“I can’t tell you that yet,” Thorne said.

“The eastern lecture room?”

“I can’t tell you.”

“It’s two courts away.”

“The man we pulled out went through a cupboard on the roof.”

The steward’s hand stopped on the ring. Lerris had heard that fact several times now. It did not become less difficult with repetition.

Aqyl took the list from him and passed it to Istren.

“The south walk has remained ordinary so far,” the tutor said. “The cupboards can be checked from there without entering the returns.”

“So far,” Thorne repeated.

“Yes.”

“And yesterday?”

“Yesterday it was ordinary too.”

The captain pushed his chair back. He looked as though he had slept no more than Lerris. His beard had begun to show grey along the jaw, and his uniform was drying in stiff patches where the water had reached it.

“Four men on the lower stair,” he said. “Two at the yard. Another party checking the outside drains. I cannot watch every door in this building.”

“You could close it,” the steward said.

“To whom? The people who sleep here? The sick? Your kitchen?”

The steward opened his mouth, then shut it.

Orest leaned over the plan. “There is a drying room above the old store. The floor has service openings. If we can get to those, we may see the flooded room without entering the stair.”

“May,” Thorne said.

“I haven’t been in that room for years.”

“Who has?”

Istren looked at the list. “Dasken. This week.”

No one moved for a moment.

Then Aqyl pointed to a mark near the corner. “I have used the passage outside it. It joins the south walk.”

“How recently?”

“Last winter.”

Thorne rubbed his eyes with the heels of his hands.

Lerris said, “I can carry what they need.”

The captain lowered his hands.

“You’ve told me.”

“And you’re short of men.”

“You’re short of sleep.”

“So are you.”

Orest looked up. Lerris heard how the answer sounded and restrained the next one.

“I can carry,” he said again. “I can follow an instruction. If you need someone here, I’ll stay here. But give me something.”

Thorne looked at him for a long time. Then he turned to Orest.

“What would you take?”

“A lamp on a line. A hook. Wedges, if the boards have swelled. Someone who can read the old room marks.”

“I can,” Istren said.

“And someone who knows what Dasken moved.”

“Phene,” Lerris said.

Thorne shook his head. “No.”

“She knows.”

“She has been awake all night waiting for him.”

“So have we.”

“She will hear him in every room.”

Lerris thought of the voice behind his own shoulder and had no answer.

The door opened. Phene stood there with the bread woman behind her.

“I may,” she said. “You can tell me when I do.”

The steward rose automatically to take the basket. The bread woman waited until he had counted out payment, then went away. Phene remained.

Thorne gestured towards the chair. She did not sit.

“The wrapping came from a case,” she said. “There are three kinds in the north return. I can tell you which rooms used which. If you find another piece, I can look at it. If you find him, I can speak to him.”

“We would bring him out.”

“If he will come.”

Aqyl looked at Phene more attentively.

Thorne took the clean sheet and began writing names. He chose one guard, Orest, Istren, Aqyl, and Phene. Then he added Lerris.

“You stay with Orest,” he told him. “You carry what he gives you. You don’t go towards a voice until someone else has seen who is speaking.”

“I understand.”

“Tell me again when you’re down there.”

The captain gave them an hour to eat and rest while the outside party checked the corresponding wall.

They ate in Istren’s room. Phene took the better chair because no one asked her whether she wanted it; Istren put the plate in her hands and sat on the window ledge. Lerris stood until Orest pointed at the floor.

“There.”

He sat. The bread was fresh enough to pull in warm strands. Someone had brought cheese and a bowl of olives. For several minutes no one spoke.

Then Istren said, “My plant has survived.”

Lerris looked up at the dry leaves beside her elbow.

“Has it?”

“Don’t start. I have no patience left.”

Orest examined it. “Too much water.”

“It has had none.”

“Then too little.”

Phene made a small sound, almost a laugh. She looked surprised by it. The others continued eating without turning to watch her.

When the hour was nearly gone, Lerris went to see Markus. His brother was awake and had the garden book open against his knees.

“They’re going above the store,” Lerris said. “I’m going with them.”

“Who?”

He named the party.

Markus looked at the page for a moment. “Don’t let them follow the numbers on the doors.”

“Istren has the marks.”

“The numbers were changed twice. The little cuts on the lower jamb are older. Tell her to look there.”

“I will.”

“And Lerris—”

He waited.

Markus shut the book. “Don’t go because of what you told Orest.”

“I can be useful.”

“I know.”

It was unexpected enough that Lerris had to look away.

His brother put the book on the stool. “Bring my bag if you see it. The brown one. Not at the expense of anything else.”

“Dasken had it.”

“I know.”

Lerris went to the door, then came back and put his hand briefly over Markus’s. His brother squeezed once and let go.

Chapter 18 — The Drying Room

The south walk smelled of bread.

That was the first thing Lerris noticed, and he disliked being comforted by it. Somewhere below, the kitchen ovens were being opened. Warm air found its way through the old service passages and out beneath the shutters. Orest paused at one of them, held up a hand, and felt the draught.

“Remember that,” he said.

“The kitchen?”

“The way the air moves.”

Lerris nodded. He carried the folded ladder on one shoulder, its weight pressing through his coat. Orest had wrapped the upper end in cloth to keep it from catching on door frames. It made Lerris look less like a gentleman accompanying an investigation and more like someone moving a ladder, which was a relief until he struck his elbow against a pillar.

The guard took the rear. Thorne remained with the lower search, expecting them back within the hour. At the entrance to the walk he had made each person answer when named, then told the man stationed there to remain until he saw all six return.

Aqyl led them through a small room in which empty drying frames leaned against the wall. Phene recognized one and touched the repaired corner as she passed.

“We used that in the wet winter,” she said.

“Which one?” Orest asked.

“The one we still mean when we say it.”

They went up five steps, crossed a landing, and came to a door with a new number painted over an older one. Istren knelt to examine the jamb. Beneath the paint were two shallow cuts and a third slanting across them.

“Here,” she said.

Lerris put down the ladder carefully. Aqyl tried the latch. It lifted, but the door did not move.

“Swelled,” Orest said.

He passed Lerris a wedge, worked another into the gap near the floor, and tapped it with the heel of his hand. The wood gave with a small cracking sound. Dust fell from the upper edge.

The room beyond was long and low, with shutters along one side and racks on the other. Strips of cloth hung from several of the rails. Some were stiff with paste; others stirred in the draught as the door opened. At the far end a chair stood facing the wall.

Orest remained on the threshold.

“No one move yet.”

He set down his lamp, knelt, and pressed the nearest floorboard with both hands. Then he shifted his weight onto it. It creaked but held.

They entered one at a time. Lerris kept the ladder low. He had begun to feel that the room was waiting for them to drop something.

Phene looked along the racks. “These haven’t been used lately.”

“How can you tell?”

“The cloth would have been taken down. It sets like this if you leave it.”

She touched one edge and a little dried paste broke off against her nail.

Istren opened the inner cover over the first service hole. Beneath it was a second board, swollen tight. Orest used the wedges again. Lerris held the lamp while he worked. The room warmed with their breath and effort. Aqyl stood near the door, listening.

When the board came free, a smell rose through the opening: wet leather, stale water, and the cold mineral scent of a place that had been closed too long.

Orest tied the lamp to its line and lowered it.

Light spread below them. Lerris could see the top of a shelving case, a narrow strip of water, and the corner of a table. The lamp turned slowly, showing one part of the room and then another.

Phene knelt beside him.

“That case,” she said. “The grey wrapping.”

It leaned against a wall. One strap had come loose and lay in the water. Lerris thought of Markus and Dasken carrying opposite ends.

“Can you see anyone?” Istren asked.

He bent lower. His shoulder pressed against Phene’s. Across the room below, something pale lay on a bench.

“There.”

Orest steadied the line. The light stopped turning.

It was a hand.

For a moment Lerris could see nothing else. The fingers rested over the edge of the bench, slightly curled. Then the light shifted and showed a sleeve, a bent knee, the side of a face turned towards the wall.

Phene drew in a breath so sharply that he felt it through her shoulder.

“Dasken.”

The hand moved.

Orest looked at the opening, then at the ladder.

“We can reach.”

The guard came forward. “Call first.”

“Dasken,” Phene said, louder. “It’s Phene. Look up.”

The man on the bench moved his head. Lerris saw the beard. He could not see the eyes.

“Dasken.”

“I heard.”

The voice was hoarse. Phene put a hand over her mouth, then lowered it at once.

“We’re coming down.”

“No.”

Orest stopped unfolding the ladder.

“What is it?” Phene asked.

“Don’t move the light.”

They held still.

From somewhere outside the room below came a scrape, then a soft thump. It might have been a case being set down. Dasken raised one hand, palm towards them, and kept it there until the sound ceased.

“There’s someone bringing the returns,” he whispered.

Lerris looked at Aqyl. The tutor had come to the opening. He listened without leaning into the light.

“Can you stand?” Phene asked.

Dasken nodded, then shook his head.

“My foot.”

“Which one?”

He looked down as though he needed to inspect them before answering.

“The one without the boot.”

Orest and the guard lowered the ladder. Its feet found the floor beneath the water. Orest tested it, shifted it a little, and nodded.

“I’ll go.”

“So will I,” Phene said.

“One first.”

Lerris held the top while Orest descended. The weight pulled steadily against his hands. He braced his knees on either side of the opening and looked down at the back of Orest’s head.

The water reached his ankles. He went to the bench without splashing if he could avoid it. Dasken watched him approach.

“Can I touch you?” Orest asked.

Dasken nodded.

Orest put a hand on his shoulder. Nothing happened. Lerris realized he had expected something and was ashamed of the relief when the missing man merely winced.

Phene descended next. At the bottom she went straight to Dasken and knelt in the water. He leaned forward until his forehead rested against her shoulder. She held him with both arms.

No one spoke for a while.

Then Orest examined the bare foot. It was swollen, the ankle dark beneath the dirt. He looked up.

“We’ll need the sling.”

The guard unwound the carrying cloth from his pack. Lerris helped knot the corners as instructed. His fingers were slow, and the guard checked each knot before lowering it.

Dasken made a small cry when they moved him. Phene spoke close to his ear. The sound from outside the lower room came again: scrape, thump, then a pause.

Aqyl turned towards the drying-room door.

“What?” Lerris asked.

“Keep working.”

They brought Dasken to the foot of the ladder. Orest climbed two steps to guide him while Phene supported him from below. The guard hauled on the line. Lerris reached down, caught the cloth, and pulled until the injured man’s shoulders cleared the opening.

Dasken was lighter than Markus. That ought to have made it easier. It did not. He clutched at the edge and cried out again when his ankle struck the board. Istren took his hands, telling him where to put them, and together they drew him onto the floor.

He lay on his side, shaking. Lerris knelt beside him and said his name.

Dasken looked at him without recognition.

“It’s Lerris.”

“I know.”

His eyes were fixed on the opening.

“Get her up.”

Orest was already coming. He reached down for Phene’s hand while the guard held the ladder. Aqyl stood at the door. Lerris could see only the side of his face.

The dragging sound was louder now.

Phene climbed quickly, her skirt wet and heavy against her legs. At the top her foot slipped. Orest caught her under the arm and pulled. She struck her knee hard against the edge but made no sound.

“Leave it,” Aqyl said.

The guard had begun lifting the ladder. He stopped.

“The lamp too?” Orest asked.

“Yes.”

They left both. Istren replaced the upper board over the service hole while Orest and Lerris lifted Dasken between them. The guard took the injured foot. Phene carried his bag, recovered from the bench below, then looked down at it in surprise.

“That isn’t his,” she said.

It was Markus’s brown bag.

“Bring it,” Lerris said.

They crossed the drying room. The cloths on the racks moved as they passed, touching Lerris’s sleeve, his cheek. He kept his eyes on Aqyl and the door.

Behind them something struck the underside of the floor.

The sound followed them to the landing.

Chapter 19 — The Guard at the Door

Dasken was too cold to stop shaking.

They rested him on a bench in the room of empty frames. Phene wrapped her coat round him, although its lower half was wet, and Istren put her own over that. The guard checked the ankle again, then told them they would have to carry him the rest of the way.

“We know,” Orest said.

“I mean without letting it hang.”

“I know.”

Neither man looked at the other. Lerris could feel his own exhaustion turning every instruction into a rebuke. He was grateful that someone else had said it first and then ashamed of that too.

Dasken caught his sleeve.

“Your brother?”

“In the infirmary. Alive.”

“Did he get out?”

“Yes.”

“He fell.”

“I know.”

“I went back for the bag.”

“We’ve got it.”

Dasken looked at the brown bag in Phene’s hands. His face crumpled for a moment, then tightened.

“I thought he was still there.”

Lerris put a hand on his shoulder. He had wanted to apologize, to explain exactly what he had done. None of those things belonged in the next breath.

“We’re taking you out.”

Aqyl opened the door to the south walk.

The smell of bread had gone.

Lerris noticed before he saw anything different. The air was cold and still, with none of the kitchen warmth Orest had told him to remember. He looked down the walk. The windows remained on the same side. The painted beam above the passage was familiar. At the far end stood the guard Thorne had left to count them back.

The guard lifted a hand.

“Wait there.”

They stopped.

Aqyl said, “We have the missing man.”

“I can see you’ve brought someone.”

“Fetch the carrying chair.”

The guard did not move. He was young, with a large nose and a small cut on his chin where he had shaved too hastily. Lerris remembered the cut. He had noticed it when the man checked their names before they entered.

“Captain said no one in until he’d come back.”

“We’re coming out,” Orest said.

The guard looked past them into the room of frames.

“You just went in.”

No one answered immediately.

“An hour ago,” Istren said.

“No.”

The guard’s hand moved towards his sword. It hung at his hip, the grip dark from use. He had not drawn it. Lerris watched the hand and thought, with an unpleasant clarity, that he might.

“What is your name?” Aqyl asked.

“You know my name.”

“Tell me.”

The guard’s face reddened. “You spoke to me coming through.”

“What did I say?”

“To stay here.”

“And you have.”

“Yes.”

“Good. Stay while we pass.”

The guard looked at each of them. His eyes stopped at the injured man, the wet clothes, the bag.

“Put him down,” he said.

Dasken’s fingers tightened on Lerris’s sleeve.

“We need a chair,” Lerris said. “He can’t walk.”

“I said put him down.”

Orest lowered his end first. They settled Dasken against the wall. The stone was cold through Lerris’s trousers when he knelt beside him. Phene stood between the injured man and the guard without seeming to decide to do so.

Their own guard came forward.

“You saw me go in.”

“Yes.”

“Then look at me. I was dry.”

“I can see you’re wet.”

“Fetch Thorne.”

“I can’t leave.”

“Call him.”

The young man swallowed. He looked towards the far arch and called the captain’s name.

The sound went away along the passage.

There was no answer.

He called again, louder. Lerris could see his throat working. This man was real. That did not tell Lerris where they were.

Aqyl moved one step closer.

“Don’t,” the guard said.

The tutor stopped.

“What can you see beyond that arch?” he asked.

“The court.”

“Who is in it?”

The guard glanced back.

His hand left the sword.

Lerris could not see past him clearly enough to know what had changed. He heard water, slower and heavier than the fountain’s, moving beneath a low roof.

Orest turned his head towards the windows.

“Open one,” he told Istren.

She lifted the catch on the nearest shutter and pulled. It did not move. She tried the second. The same.

“They’re fastened outside.”

“No,” Orest said. “They open inward.”

“I know how they open.”

He went to help her. Together they worked the catch free. The shutter gave a little, then stopped against something solid.

Stone filled the gap.

Not loose rubble. A wall, close enough that Orest’s fingers could not fit between it and the shutter. He tapped it with his knuckle. The sound was dull.

The guard at the arch began to back towards them.

“There was a court,” he said.

Aqyl held out a hand, low, as he might have approached a frightened animal. “Come here.”

“I haven’t moved.”

“Come here now.”

The young man came. When he was close enough, their own guard took his arm and turned him away from the arch. No one mentioned the sword.

Lerris looked past them.

The passage beyond the arch sloped down. He could see the reflection of their lamps on water at the bottom. There was no court, no daylight, no sound of people waiting to be admitted.

Dasken began to shake harder.

“I told you,” he whispered.

Phene knelt beside him. “You told us to keep the light still.”

“There’s someone bringing them.”

“We’ve stopped.”

“They don’t stop.”

The words emerged so softly that Lerris had to bend to hear them. Behind him Orest was testing another shutter, slower now. Istren had taken out her slate. Aqyl stood facing the arch with one hand against the wall.

Lerris looked back towards the room of frames. It was still there. The bench, the racks, the empty place where they had rested Dasken: all unchanged. He wanted to go back to it because he could remember being there without knowing this.

“Do we go back?” he asked.

No one answered at once.

Then Orest said, “Not carrying him like this.”

He pointed to a long frame leaning inside the room.

“We make something to carry him on. Then we decide.”

It was work. Lerris stood and went to help.

Chapter 20 — The Frame

The first frame split when Orest put his weight on it.

He sat down abruptly among the broken rails. No one laughed. Lerris wished someone had; the sharp report of the wood had left them all listening for an answer.

“Better now,” Orest said.

He got up and began examining the next one.

The room contained five frames large enough to carry a man. Two had rot near the feet. Another had been repaired with pegs too short for the joints. Orest chose the last, laid it flat, and made Lerris stand on the crosspiece while he watched the corners.

Lerris stood. The wood flexed beneath him. He felt absurdly conscious of his expensive boots.

“Move a little.”

“How much?”

“As if you were alive.”

He shifted his weight. One corner creaked, but the joint held.

They used the carrying cloth for the middle and their belts to fasten it. The guard who had accompanied them lent his, removing his sword and passing it to the young man from the entrance before he unbuckled it. He introduced himself as Tassir when Orest asked whom the belt belonged to.

“Do you want it marked?” Lerris asked.

“I want it back.”

The younger guard stood by the door holding two swords. He seemed uncertain what to do with the extra one. Tassir took it from him and laid it on the bench within reach.

“Vedran,” he said. “Come here.”

The young man came.

“Hold this while I pull.”

Vedran seized the end of the cloth. His hands were steady now that the work required both of them. When Orest asked for more tension, he pulled hard enough to bring Tassir forward onto one knee.

“Enough.”

“Sorry.”

“Enough is good.”

At the other end of the room Phene had persuaded Dasken to drink. He was sitting against the wall with his injured leg extended, his bare foot wrapped in a strip of cloth from Istren’s underskirt. The swelling had spread above the ankle. He kept looking towards the door through which they had come from the drying room.

Lerris wondered how many times he had heard someone approaching before they found him.

“Does the water taste right?” he asked.

Phene looked at the bottle in her hand. “It’s ours.”

“I know. I only—”

He stopped. She gave Dasken another mouthful. He swallowed and closed his eyes.

Orest tested the finished carrier. Then he and Lerris lifted it while Tassir lay across it, his knees bent to fit. The cloth sagged but did not slip. The frame was too short for Dasken’s legs. They would have to carry him half sitting, the injured foot supported beyond the rail.

“It’ll go through the door,” Orest said.

“The turn?” Istren asked.

“We’ll find out.”

She looked at the doorway and shook her head. “Measure it first.”

Orest opened his mouth, then closed it. He took the frame with Lerris and tried it empty. At the turn they had to raise one end and tilt the other. It could be done, but Dasken would feel it.

When they returned, Phene had begun talking about his brother.

“He wrote from the coast?”

“Before they left.”

“What did he say?”

“That the ship smelled.”

“Of what?”

“Everything.”

Dasken’s mouth moved a little. Phene waited.

“He said he’d get used to it. Or they’d wash him in the harbour.”

“He would be difficult to clean.”

“Yes.”

His eyes remained shut, but the hand at his throat had relaxed.

Lerris helped move him. Dasken cried out when they lifted the leg, then apologized so quickly that Phene told him not to waste breath on it. They settled him against a roll made from her coat. Istren supported the ankle until Tassir had fastened a loop beneath it.

The work took longer than Lerris expected. He had imagined a man being put on something and carried. There were folds beneath Dasken’s back to smooth, a fastening pressing into his thigh, a place where the rail might strike the injured foot. Each adjustment required hands which were already holding something else.

When they finished, his own back hurt. He was grateful for the pain because it was easy to account for.

Aqyl had remained near the outer door. Now he came to the carrier and asked Dasken whether he could remember how the cases entered the room below.

“Through the far door.”

“Did you see anyone carrying them?”

Dasken opened his eyes.

“I saw the cases.”

“Did they arrive while you were awake?”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I closed my eyes.”

Aqyl said nothing for a moment.

“When the light went away,” Dasken added. “I closed them. There were more when I looked.”

Lerris thought of the hand over the edge of the bench. He had been relieved when it moved. He had not thought what opening his eyes might have cost Dasken.

“Was the room always flooded?” Aqyl asked.

“No. There was a woman sweeping.”

Phene looked at him.

“When?”

“Before.”

“Before the water?”

“Yes.”

“Did you speak to her?”

“She told me to lift my feet.”

Dasken looked down at the wrapped ankle, puzzled. “I think I did.”

Aqyl turned away from him. Lerris followed him to the door.

“Do you know who she was?”

“No.”

“Have you heard of her?”

“I have heard accounts of people in rooms that were believed empty.”

“Here?”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

Aqyl looked at him. “Would a number help you decide where to put your feet?”

“No. It might help me decide whether to follow you.”

For a moment the tutor’s face was still. Lerris thought he had gone too far, then realized he had been waiting years to ask some version of that question.

“You should follow Orest on the floor,” Aqyl said. “I will tell you when I recognize something. I do not recognize this room as it now stands.”

“Did you before?”

“Yes.”

“Then something has happened to it.”

“Yes.”

Lerris almost laughed. The answers were so exact and so little use.

From the bench, Tassir said, “We can argue while we walk.”

They chose the narrow service door at the other end of the frame room. Orest had found air moving beneath it. It was faint and smelled of old ash, but it was warm. The flooded slope beyond the guard’s arch offered nothing they could use with the carrier.

Before opening the service door, Istren scratched an arrow into the wood beside it. Then she put the slate back in her bag and used her knife to make the mark deeper.

“Chalk comes off,” she said when Lerris looked at her.

Tassir secured his sheathed sword at his side with a short carrying sling, since his belt now held the frame together.

Vedran wanted to leave a lamp in the room. Tassir said they could not spare it. They had two working lamps left, and the young guard’s smaller lantern, whose oil he had not replenished before taking his post. Orest trimmed that wick and gave it to Phene.

“What if someone comes looking?” Vedran asked.

“They’ll have a light,” Tassir said.

“But if they don’t?”

No one answered. Vedran looked at the room, at the broken frame, the little pile of wood Orest had swept aside with his foot.

Lerris and Orest took the carrier first. Tassir went ahead with Aqyl. Istren carried the tools. Phene walked beside Dasken, and Vedran came last, looking back until the turn took the room from view.

The service passage was narrow enough to scrape Lerris’s knuckles. At the first corner they had to tip the carrier as they had practised. Dasken gripped the side rail. Phene kept one hand behind his head.

“Nearly,” Orest said.

He had said it too soon. They remained caught for several breaths while Lerris raised his end higher and tried to turn his wrist without letting go.

Then they were through.

Ahead, Tassir lifted the lamp. A shallow flight of steps rose towards a closed door. Beneath it lay a thin band of yellow light.

Someone on the other side was laughing.

Chapter 21 — The Wrong Hour

Markus could tell from the way Lessa set down the basin that she had been told not to tell him something.

She was ordinarily direct. Now she asked whether his side hurt before looking at it, and whether he wanted water while moving the cup to a place she could see he could reach. When he asked after the search, she said the captain was arranging matters.

“What matters?”

“I haven’t been there.”

“Has my brother come back?”

“Not yet.”

“How long?”

She folded the cloth. “I don’t know when he went.”

He did. Lerris had left before the kitchen sent up the midday bowls. Those had been taken away. Light from the high window had moved across the foot of the next bed and was now on the wall.

“Would you fetch Captain Thorne?”

“He is busy.”

“So you said.”

Lessa looked at him, and he regretted the sharpness. She was not the person who had decided what he could bear to hear.

“Please,” he said. “Tell him I may be able to help.”

She went. Markus lay still until the sound of her steps faded, then tried to sit up.

The pain was immediate. He had expected it and still caught his breath. He found that if he rolled towards the injured side first and pushed with the other arm, he could raise himself without the worst of it. By the time his feet touched the floor, sweat had gathered above his lip.

His shoes were under the bed. He looked at them for a long time.

Thorne arrived before he had decided how to reach them.

“Where do you intend to go?”

“Where is Lerris?”

“Not back.”

“Is he late?”

“Yes.”

The captain drew up the chair. He looked older than he had during the night. A little dried mud had cracked across the back of one hand.

“Don’t spare me the sequence,” Markus said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

Thorne told him. The party had entered by the south walk. A guard remained at its entrance. When the hour passed, another man went to ask for a report and found the post empty. The walk beyond it was open. The room of frames contained the broken frame, one sound frame missing, and no people. The drying-room floor opening was shut. Beneath it, there was no light.

“You looked down?”

“With a lamp on a line.”

“What did you see?”

“Shelves. Water. An empty bench.”

“Dasken?”

“No.”

“Then they brought him up.”

“Possibly.”

“The frame.”

“Yes.”

Markus put a hand on the bed to steady himself. He wanted the possibility to become a report. The captain would not oblige him.

“Did you find a mark?” he asked.

“An arrow beside a service door. Freshly cut.”

“Istren.”

“Probably.”

“Where does it lead?”

“A stair. A locked cupboard.”

Markus looked at him.

“We opened it,” Thorne said. “It is a cupboard. We have checked the back and the sides.”

“Nothing beyond?”

“Stone.”

The room seemed to contract around the bed. He could not stand, go there, and see what had been misunderstood.

“Bring me the list from the returns table.”

“Why?”

“I was working on it. The last case may have come from a different room.”

“We know the case.”

“You know the wrapping. Bring the list.”

Thorne studied him, then stood and gave the request to the guard outside. When he returned, he remained standing.

“If you know a way to follow them, tell me before trying it.”

“I can’t put on my shoes.”

“That did not answer me.”

“No,” Markus said. “It didn’t.”

The captain sat again.

They waited. The roof worker slept behind the screen. His breathing had become quieter, though a cough still caught at him now and then. From outside came the ordinary noise of a delivery: wheels, a shouted instruction, the thud of a sack being put down.

“My men want to close the inner doors,” Thorne said.

“Will you?”

“Some.”

“Not the south walk.”

“Not yet.”

Markus looked at the window. He had spent years learning which fear meant he should stop and which was only the memory of stopping. Now his brother was somewhere beyond a cupboard and he could not tell whether any of that experience was useful.

The guard returned with two sheets and a strip of cloth. He put them on the bed. Markus recognized the cloth from his table. There was a brown water mark at one edge which had not been there when he worked.

He began with the last completed entry. His handwriting was ordinary: smaller towards the end of the line, a correction where the old room mark had been misread, a short stroke through the rejected number. The following entry was unfinished. He remembered that.

Below it were three more lines.

He read them once. Then he bent closer, despite the pain.

“What?” Thorne asked.

“Who wrote these?”

“You?”

“No.”

The captain leaned over. “It looks like the same hand.”

“It does.”

The added lines completed the sequence. The first assigned the case to a shelf. The second gave an old room mark. The third directed a return by the lower drying passage.

Markus touched the first line without letting his finger rest on the ink.

“I couldn’t have put this here.”

“You don’t remember the later part of the night.”

“I don’t mean that I don’t remember.”

He pointed to the unresolved mark above. “I had not identified this. Without it, the shelf number could be one of two. Whoever finished the line chose the one that makes the next instruction possible.”

“Could you have identified it later?”

“I would have corrected the mark first.”

“Perhaps you forgot.”

Markus shut his eyes. He could hear how it sounded: a man insisting his habits were stronger than whatever had injured him. He had no right to ask the captain to trust that merely because he was frightened.

He opened them and found another discrepancy.

“The cupboard,” he said. “Does it have a painted number?”

“Thirty-six.”

“And the old mark?”

“I didn’t look.”

“Ask them to look. Low on the jamb. A stroke with two cuts across it, or three.”

Thorne called the guard back.

While they waited, Markus compared the added letters to the rest. Whoever had made them knew how he formed a long tail, where he crowded words, even the little upward turn when his pen was beginning to dry.

The answer came: two cuts.

Markus let out a breath.

“Then the instruction sends them to the wrong side.”

“Are you certain?”

“About the mark, yes. Not about what’s there now.”

Thorne took the sheet. “We’ll send no one on this.”

“Keep it away from the other list.”

“I will.”

“And don’t let someone copy it as mine.”

The captain looked at him, then folded the added portion underneath without creasing the ink.

Markus watched until he could no longer see the three lines. He had wanted to be useful. Now he wanted to wash his hands.

Chapter 22 — The Supper Room

The laughter stopped before they reached the door.

Tassir lifted the latch with the tip of his knife. He pushed the door inward, keeping his body behind the jamb. Light spread across the top steps.

The room beyond contained a table set for supper.

There were bowls, a loaf cut into thick pieces, a dish of something pale beneath a cloth. A lamp stood at the centre. The chairs had been pushed back at different angles, as if people had risen without troubling to put them in. One cup lay on its side. Nothing moved except the flame.

“Wait,” Aqyl said.

Tassir remained on the threshold. Orest and Lerris held Dasken’s carrier on the stair, one end higher than the other. Phene had her hand behind his shoulders to keep him from sliding.

“We can’t stay like this,” Orest said.

Aqyl went in.

He crossed to the table, looked beneath it, and then examined the second door. He did not touch the food. At last he nodded.

They carried Dasken through and set him down beside the wall. Lerris’s hands would not open immediately. He flexed them against his thighs and felt the small cuts where the wood had pressed into the skin.

Vedran stood near the first door, looking at the table.

“I know this room.”

“From where?” Tassir asked.

“The west lodging. My uncle ate here.”

“Does it look the same?”

Vedran moved his eyes slowly across the furnishings. “The table. The crack there. I remember putting my nail in it.”

He took a step forward. Tassir caught his elbow.

“When?”

“When I was small.”

Aqyl looked towards the window. It was shuttered, like the windows on the walk. He did not attempt to open it.

“Has anyone else been here?” he asked.

No one answered.

Lerris smelled onions cooked in fat. His stomach tightened. He had eaten in Istren’s room, but that seemed a long time ago. Beside him Orest wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and looked angry with himself.

Phene opened their own bottle. She drank and passed it to Istren.

“Don’t take theirs,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to,” Orest answered.

“I was speaking to myself.”

He looked at her, then took the bottle when it reached him.

Vedran had gone very pale. He said his uncle was dead. No one asked when. The answer seemed to have arrived only after he recognized the room.

Lerris wanted to tell him that it was only a likeness. He had no basis for saying so.

Instead he asked whether the uncle had worked at the university.

“In the stores.”

“What was his name?”

Vedran told him. Lerris repeated it because he had not quite heard.

From behind the second door, a man answered, “What?”

Vedran turned.

Tassir put himself between him and the door. No one drew a weapon. The room was too small for a sensible fight, and the voice had sounded entirely unprepared to be dangerous.

“Don’t answer,” Aqyl said.

The man behind the door made a sound of impatience. A chair scraped.

Vedran’s mouth had opened. Tassir held his shoulder now, not roughly.

“Look at me.”

“That was—”

“Look at me.”

The young guard did. His eyes were wet.

Aqyl went to the door they had entered by and looked out. Then he returned to the table and extinguished its lamp with the snuffer beside it.

The room changed very little. Their own lights still showed the food and the chairs. The smell remained.

“What are you doing?” Istren asked.

“Removing a light we did not bring.”

“Will that help?”

“I don’t know.”

For several breaths nothing happened. Then a hand struck the other side of the second door, flat against the wood.

“I’m eating.”

Vedran flinched.

Lerris could imagine the man annoyed at being interrupted, wiping his mouth, preparing to come and see why no one would let him finish.

Dasken raised himself on one elbow.

“There’s a third door,” he said.

They looked at him.

“Behind the cloth.”

A long piece of faded fabric hung beside the shelves. Phene lifted it. Behind was a low door, its lower edge rubbed pale by use.

“How do you know?” she asked.

Dasken swallowed. “I don’t. I thought—”

He looked at the table, then shut his eyes.

“I’ve been through.”

Orest examined the door. It opened onto a short landing, empty except for a broken basket. Beyond it rose another stair.

Warm air moved down it.

“Kitchen again,” he said quietly.

“No,” Phene said. “Hot cloth.”

She was right. It was damp wool near heat. Lerris could distinguish it once she named it.

Behind them the second door rattled. Vedran made a small involuntary movement towards it, then caught himself and took hold of Dasken’s carrier instead.

“I can carry,” he said.

Tassir let him take Lerris’s end. Lerris moved beside the injured foot, supporting it through the turn. They went out beneath the hanging cloth while Aqyl remained by the supper-room table.

He followed last and pulled the low door shut.

At the top of the stair they found a linen cupboard large enough to stand in. Sheets lay folded on shelves, yellow with age along the exposed edges. There was a crack in the wall through which warm air entered. Orest held the lamp near it and watched the flame lean.

“This way.”

“We can’t fit through that,” Istren said.

“No. Whatever is warm is on the other side. We look for the way round.”

They rested Dasken while Orest and Tassir examined the adjoining passage. Lerris sat beside him on the floor. His legs had begun trembling from carrying, and he was grateful that everyone else was occupied.

“I need to tell you something,” he said.

Dasken looked at him.

“Before the room closed. I told Orest Markus was finished. He wasn’t.”

The younger man watched his face.

“Why?”

“I wanted him to come out. I thought he would.”

“That isn’t why you told Orest.”

“No.”

Lerris looked down at his hands. “I didn’t want to go back and ask him again.”

Dasken closed his eyes.

Lerris waited. He had not expected forgiveness. He had expected something more bearable than the silence.

At last Dasken said, “Help me move my leg.”

He did. They adjusted the loop beneath the ankle, lifted the heel a little, and settled it on a folded piece of cloth Phene passed to them. Dasken breathed through his mouth until the pain eased.

“That’s better,” he said.

Lerris kept his hands where they were until Dasken no longer needed them.

Chapter 23 — A Question Through Stone

They found the heat behind a narrow iron grille.

Orest had to clear a layer of dust from it before he could see the fastenings. Beyond was a shaft, warm enough that sweat rose on his face when he leaned close. A faint orange light showed far below.

“Oven flue,” he said. “Or a drying furnace.”

“Can we call?” Phene asked.

He listened first. The shaft carried small noises: a scrape, a dull impact, a distant metallic clatter. He put his mouth near the grille and shouted.

The answer was immediate and furious.

“Keep your head out of there!”

Orest pulled back. For a moment everyone looked at everyone else.

Then he laughed, one sharp breath.

“Kitchen,” he said.

Aqyl moved beside him. “Don’t tell them to open anything yet.”

Orest nodded and called again, naming himself and asking for Captain Thorne.

The voice below replied that the captain had enough to do without fools shouting into hot shafts. Orest repeated his name, louder, and said Doreta’s son. That produced a pause.

“What are you doing up there?”

“Lost.”

“You always were.”

Orest put his forehead against the cool stone beside the grille. He was smiling and looked close to weeping. Lerris had never heard the other voice, but he would have liked to embrace its owner.

“Fetch Thorne,” Orest said. “Please.”

They waited.

No one spoke much. They brought Dasken nearer the warmth without putting him against it. Phene loosened the wet cloth around his foot and let the air reach his skin. Istren marked the route on her slate, stopped, and drew a question beside the stair from the supper room.

Lerris watched the orange light below. He wanted to lower something into it, a message, a hand, anything that would make the connection more substantial. Aqyl said the shaft might narrow and that they should wait until someone could look from the other end.

When the captain’s voice came, it sounded unlike itself through the stone.

“Who is there?”

They gave their names. Thorne made each speak separately, including Dasken and Vedran. The young guard’s voice broke when he said he had not left his post. The captain told him they would discuss it when he came out.

Lerris could hear other voices below. Someone asked for a pole. Someone else objected to standing on the hearth.

“Can you see our light?” Thorne called.

Orest lowered his face to the grille. “No change.”

“We’re beneath the western drying furnace.”

Orest looked at Istren. She turned the slate round, then shook her head.

“That should be below the linen room,” she said.

“It is,” Thorne answered. He had heard her.

“Which linen room?”

“The one above the old kitchen.”

“There are shelves here. A crack in the wall. No kitchen stair.”

A long pause followed. Lerris imagined the captain looking at a plan and discovering that the people he could hear had no place on it.

“Stay there,” Thorne said.

They did.

The warmth began to feel oppressive. Dasken slept in brief, startled intervals. Each time he woke, Phene told him they were still beside the furnace and the captain knew where to find them. After the third time, she changed it to the captain could hear them. Lerris noticed the correction and wished he had not.

Vedran sat apart with Tassir. They spoke too quietly for him to hear. Once the younger man covered his face and Tassir looked away until he was ready to continue.

Aqyl examined the grille. He did not touch its fastenings. He ran his finger along the mortar above it and brought away a little black dust.

“Is it old?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

“How old?”

“Older than that repair.”

He pointed to a patch beside the stone. Lerris could see it now, a different grit in the mortar.

“What does that tell you?”

“That someone wanted this opening kept.”

“Or repaired it because it was broken.”

“Yes.”

Lerris almost smiled. He preferred the answer to the certainty he had been hoping for.

Below, Thorne called them back to the grille.

“There is a room here. Empty. Same shelves.”

“Can you see the frame?” Orest asked.

“What frame?”

“The carrier.”

“No.”

“Put a light at the grille.”

They waited. Nothing changed above. The orange glow remained far below them.

“I have a man standing where you say you are,” Thorne said.

No one answered.

Dasken had woken. He watched their faces.

“Can you hear us from there?” Istren asked.

“No. Only from the furnace.”

Orest sat back against the wall.

Then another voice came through the shaft.

“Lerris.”

He moved before he could think.

“Markus?”

“Yes.”

“What are you doing there?”

“Sitting very uncomfortably. Don’t make me explain it twice.”

Lerris laughed. The sound escaped him and went down the shaft. He heard his brother cough and tried to imagine what the journey from the infirmary had cost him.

“We’ve found Dasken,” he said.

“I heard.”

“He’s hurt.”

“I heard that too. Dasken, can you speak?”

The injured man answered. Markus asked about the case, the room below, the door through which it arrived. The questions were gentle and irritatingly particular. Dasken answered some and refused others. His refusal seemed to satisfy Markus more than his guesses.

Then Markus asked Istren to describe the route she had drawn.

She did so. At the end there was silence below, broken by a low conversation Lerris could not distinguish.

“Have you found writing?” Markus asked.

“Marks on the doors,” Istren said.

“Instructions. A list.”

“No.”

“If you do, don’t assume it’s ours.”

Lerris looked at Aqyl. The tutor had become very still.

“What happened?” he called.

“Something finished my work.”

Markus described the added lines and the incorrect room mark. Istren copied what he said, then struck through it so heavily that the chalk broke.

“Do you know where we are?” she asked.

“I know which rooms these resemble.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“No.”

Lerris was glad his brother had not dressed it as something better.

Thorne returned to the shaft. He would keep a man there, he said. Another party would check the adjacent service rooms. They were not to move until he called again unless something forced them to.

“What if we can’t hear you?” Orest asked.

“Then don’t wait for an order you can’t receive.”

Orest nodded, though the captain could not see him.

They settled beside the grille. Phene had begun rubbing warmth back into Dasken’s bare foot with both hands, careful of the ankle. Vedran offered his spare stocking. It was too small, so she cut the toe open and drew it over the heel.

Lerris sat with his shoulder against the wall. From below came the scrape of a chair and his brother’s restrained breath as someone helped him shift. He knew that sound now.

“Markus?”

“Yes.”

“Stay a little.”

“I am.”

Lerris closed his eyes. He could hear the kitchen around his brother: people working, a vessel being set down, someone asking whether the bread should be kept warm. He listened until he could separate each sound from the next.

Chapter 24 — Bread on a Line

Lerris slept with his mouth open and woke because his neck hurt.

For a little while he did not remember where he was. There was warmth against his left side, stone against his back, and the comfortable murmur of people doing something beyond a wall. He might have been waiting for a carriage in a crowded posting house. Then he saw the carrier and Dasken’s bandaged foot, and the room returned around him.

Phene was asleep too. She sat with one hand on the carrier, her head tilted at an angle which could not be comfortable. Istren had put a folded cloth between her cheek and the wall. No one else seemed inclined to wake her.

Orest crouched beside the grille. He had taken off his coat and was using its sleeve to protect his hand while he worked at one of the fastenings. Tassir held the lamp. Aqyl sat a little way apart, eyes open, with his hands resting loosely on his knees.

“What are you doing?” Lerris asked.

“Making a larger place to shout through.”

“Will it help?”

“I’m hoping to do something besides shout.”

Orest eased a strip of iron out of the stone. Dust trickled into the shaft. From below came a protest, then Thorne’s voice asking what had fallen.

“Mortar,” Orest called. “Keep clear.”

The grille moved. One fastening remained sound, so Orest left it in place and swung the iron aside. The opening was still too small for a person. Lerris put his hand near it and felt hot air pass between his fingers.

They had a length of fine cord in the tool roll. Orest tied his smallest wedge to the end and lowered it, paying out the cord slowly. Lerris watched it descend through the dark until it vanished. For several breaths nothing happened.

Then the cord moved against Orest’s fingers.

“Got it,” someone called.

Orest looked at Lerris and grinned.

The expression made him look young again. Lerris had forgotten, watching him test floors and arrange the carrying frame, that he had once been a man sitting in a doorway making jokes about a wine cellar in his leg.

They asked for water first.

There was a delay below while someone found a vessel narrow enough to pass. It rose wrapped in cloth, swinging gently against the stone. Orest guided it through the opening and set it down. The outside was warm; the water inside was cool.

Phene woke as he poured. She watched him give the first cup to Dasken and then held out her own hand.

“From below?”

“Yes.”

She drank half, stopped herself, and passed it to Istren. Orest told her there was more coming. She nodded but did not take it back.

A second vessel followed. Then a little parcel containing bread, cheese, and a heel of cooked meat. The cloth was tied with a knot Lerris could not undo until Vedran lent him the point of his knife.

“Not the edge,” Tassir told the younger man.

“I know.”

“Then stop giving people the edge.”

Vedran turned the knife round. It was such an ordinary rebuke that Lerris nearly laughed.

The bread smelled of the kitchen. They ate sitting on the floor, passing the cheese from hand to hand and cutting the meat into uneven pieces. Dasken wanted very little at first. Phene did not press him. She left a piece on the cloth beside his hand, and after a while he reached for it.

Lerris chewed slowly. The food did not feel like proof that they could get out. It did make it possible to imagine standing up and carrying again.

Below, Markus asked whether everyone had eaten.

“Yes,” Lerris called.

“All of you?”

“Yes.”

A pause.

“Phene?”

She leaned towards the shaft. “Mind your own dinner.”

His brother laughed, then coughed. The cough went on long enough that Lerris stopped eating.

When it ended, Thorne said Markus was being taken back to the infirmary for a while. Markus objected in a voice too low for the words to carry. The captain waited him out.

“I’ll come back,” Markus called at last.

“Rest,” Lerris said.

“So should you.”

“I have.”

“How much?”

“An excellent amount. I was insufferable throughout.”

He heard his brother’s breath, almost a laugh this time, then the sounds of men helping him rise. Lerris kept looking into the shaft until the voices moved away.

They sent for oil next. Tassir filled the lamps while Orest checked the cord for wear. One section had begun to fray. He shortened it and retied the wedge, leaving a piece of coloured thread at the place where the cord passed the opening. If it moved again, he wanted to know how far.

Aqyl watched without speaking. Lerris offered him the last piece of meat. He took it, thanked him, and ate as though he had only just remembered that he could.

“Could they widen it from below?” Lerris asked.

Orest shook his head. “There’s a bend. We can pass a bottle. We can’t pass him.”

He nodded towards Dasken.

“Any of us?”

“I wouldn’t put Vedran through it.”

Vedran looked up quickly. “I could try.”

“No,” Tassir said.

“I only meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

The younger guard put down his bread. Lerris recognized the look on his face: the hope of being given one difficult thing to do which might settle everything else. He was glad Tassir had answered before he could encourage it.

They waited for the next report. The oil had steadied the light. Dasken’s shaking diminished. Istren took out the slate, looked at what she had drawn, and put it away without changing anything.

Phene asked Orest whether his mother knew they had found Dasken.

“I told the kitchen to tell her.”

“She’ll want to hear it herself.”

“So will yours,” Orest said.

Phene looked at him. “Mine has been dead twelve years.”

He shut his eyes briefly. “I know. I’m sorry.”

“That’s all right.”

Orest opened his mouth, then closed it. Phene reached out and touched his sleeve.

“You’re tired.”

“Yes.”

“Sit down for a little.”

He did. Tassir took his place by the shaft.

A while later the cord twitched.

“Something coming?” Tassir called.

“No,” said the voice below.

The cord moved again, sliding a finger’s width through his hand. He held it fast. The coloured thread drew tight against the edge of the stone.

“What have you tied on?”

“Nothing.”

Tassir looked at Orest. Orest was already up.

“Let me.”

He took the cord and pulled gently. It came a little way, then stopped. He slackened it and tried again. The line drew downward with a steady pressure.

“Unfasten your end,” he called.

A confused answer came back. Their end was free. It was lying beside the furnace. No one was holding it.

Aqyl moved closer.

“Cut it.”

Orest looked at him.

“Now.”

The knife was still in Lerris’s hand from the bread. He passed it to Orest handle first. Orest severed the cord below the coloured thread.

The lower length vanished.

There was a sharp sound far below, then a cry. Tassir put his face to the opening.

“Who is hurt?”

No answer came for several breaths. Phene held Dasken’s hand. The lamp flames leaned towards the shaft.

At last Thorne called up. A kitchen boy had been struck across the fingers by the whipping line. He was hurt, but not badly. The loose cord had disappeared into the flue.

“Did you pull?” the captain asked.

“No,” Orest said.

“Neither did we.”

They looked at the little length remaining in Orest’s hand. He set it on the floor.

The air grew hotter.

It was gradual enough that Lerris first noticed sweat beneath his collar. Then Dasken turned his face away and said he couldn’t breathe properly. Phene moved the carrier farther from the opening.

“Have you fed the furnace?” Orest called.

“No.”

“Shut the draught.”

“We have.”

Aqyl put his hand against the stone, withdrew it, and examined his palm.

“We should move.”

“Wait,” Orest said. “They can bank it.”

“They are banking it.”

The tutor’s voice was quiet. Lerris looked at his reddening hand.

Below, men were shouting. Something heavy scraped across the hearth. The orange light diminished and the heat continued to rise.

Thorne called their names, asking whether they could still hear him. Orest answered. The captain told them to take the passage away from the furnace if they could, mark their route, and keep the water.

“We’ll find another place to speak,” he said.

No one promised they would.

They packed the food, the oil, the filled bottles. Tassir and Vedran lifted the carrier this time. Orest remained by the shaft until everyone was ready, then called to his mother, though she was not there to hear him.

“Tell her we’re moving.”

The answer came faintly through the hot air.

Lerris went last with Aqyl. The tutor swung the grille back into place using the folded sleeve of his coat. It did not fit as it had before. He left it hanging from the one sound fastening.

At the doorway, Lerris looked back. The coloured thread lay on the floor beside the cut cord. Beyond it, the warm stone had begun to darken.

Chapter 25 — The Hand in the Margin

Markus was still in the carrying chair when the kitchen boy was brought past him.

The boy held one hand against his chest. Two fingers had swollen and a narrow red line crossed the knuckles. He was crying with anger, repeatedly saying he had not touched anything. Lessa stopped the men carrying Markus and went to him.

Markus listened while she asked him to open his hand.

“It caught me,” the boy said. “I was only standing there.”

“I know.”

“You don’t. You weren’t—”

“Open it as far as you can.”

He tried. She nodded, put a folded cloth beneath it, and took him towards the infirmary. Markus asked the men to carry him back to the furnace.

They refused.

It was not an argument he could win by speaking more precisely. One of the men had been told by Pellant to put him in bed. The other was watching the wet patch beginning to show through Markus’s dressing.

“Your side,” he said.

“I can feel it.”

“Then help us carry you without making it worse.”

Markus leaned back. The humiliation was sharp and childish. He wanted to be sufficiently necessary that people would let him do what he chose. Instead they carried him to the bed and helped him lie down while Lessa fetched the healer.

Thorne came before Pellant had finished the dressing.

“They’ve moved,” he said.

“Why?”

The captain told him. Markus looked at the ceiling while Pellant worked. He could hear his own breathing become faster and made himself slow it.

“Was the line burned?”

“No.”

“The part that struck the boy.”

“It vanished. The piece they cut off is with them.”

“Was there a weight attached?”

“Not when we sent the last bottle.”

Pellant pressed a clean cloth against the wound. Markus caught the edge of the mattress and waited for the pain to ease.

“You can question each other after I’ve finished,” the healer said.

Thorne stood silently until he had. When the curtain was drawn back again, the captain held a narrow folded paper in his hand.

“Doreta found this in the old gate book.”

Markus recognized the wrapper before he saw the writing. It was a repair account, folded twice so it could be carried in a coat. The date belonged to a term before his enrollment.

Thorne opened it on the stool.

Most of the entries were ordinary: a damaged shutter, a replacement latch, wages for clearing plaster from the west walk. At the bottom another hand had added a warning about the furnace speaking hole. Do not stop it with mortar. Fit iron. Keep the opening clear.

Below that was a name and a short description of a missing labourer.

Markus read it again.

“Why is this with the repairs?”

“That’s where Doreta remembered seeing it.”

“Was he found?”

“Not in that book.”

The missing man had been hired for three days. There was no family address, only the name of the lodging from which he came. A later entry recorded that the lodging had been paid for the room through the end of the week.

“Bring the other accounts,” Markus said.

“We’re looking.”

“No. The ordinary ones. Wages, meals, lamps. They may say where people stopped going.”

Thorne folded the paper carefully.

“Can you read them here?”

“Yes.”

The captain nodded. It was a small concession, but Markus accepted it before pride could make him stupid.

They brought him three bundles. He worked with the sheets supported on a board across his knees. The first account contained nothing he could use. The second gave a different name for a passage already on Istren’s plan. The third included a payment for moving twelve drying frames out of a room because the workers would no longer enter it.

There was no explanation. The steward at the time had paid to have the work done elsewhere. Two pages later he had objected to the increased cost of carrying wet cloth farther.

Markus could imagine the argument. He disliked how easy that was.

Doreta came herself with a fourth bundle. She stood at the end of the bed, her hands clasped together so tightly that the skin over her knuckles shone.

“Has Orest spoken again?” she asked.

“No.”

She nodded once. “These are the older keys.”

The bundle was a list of locks and the people entitled to use them. Several entries had been crossed out. Beside one was Aqyl’s name.

Markus looked at the date.

He did not ask whether it was the same man. Doreta would have an answer which might be accurate and would not help him bring anyone back.

“This door,” he said. “Does it still exist?”

“Which?”

“The cloth stair. Under the outer gallery.”

“Yes. It goes to the old stores.”

“Can you get there from the west drying room?”

“You could. I don’t know what has been done since.”

“Who would?”

She looked at him.

“Orest.”

He lowered his eyes to the paper.

Doreta sat in the chair beside the bed. For a while she said nothing. Markus continued reading, aware that she was waiting for him to find something in which her son’s knowledge was not on the inaccessible side of the wall.

At last he put down the sheet.

“He knows the building.”

“Yes.”

“He was careful before we went into the return.”

“Yes.”

Doreta rubbed one thumb across the other.

“When he was little,” she said, “he got himself into the space behind the gatehouse stairs. He’d dropped a penny. He couldn’t turn round.”

Markus waited.

“He shouted until I came. Then he told me not to call anyone because they’d take the boards off.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

She looked towards the window.

“He was furious. I had to sit with him afterwards while he told me where he’d meant to put his foot.”

Markus could see the child more clearly than he wanted. He turned a page to give her time and found an entry in a different ink.

The cloth stair had been closed from the lower end. The upper door remained in use for inspections. Beside the closure was a sketch of the fastening.

He traced it with his finger. He had seen that fastening.

Not in a book. On a door beyond the table in the old study, half hidden by shelving. Aqyl had told him it was unused. Markus had taken that to mean it led nowhere useful.

“Fetch Thorne,” he said.

Doreta rose so quickly that the chair scraped against the floor.

Markus caught her hand before she went.

“It may be nothing.”

“I heard you.”

She left. He remained looking at the sketch, trying to remember the exact position of the door and whether he had ever heard anyone on the other side.

Chapter 26 — The Weight of the Shelves

The passage away from the furnace narrowed, then opened without warning into a room so large that their lamps could not find its far wall.

They stopped at the threshold. The air was cool. Lerris felt it against his wet collar and shivered.

Shelves stood in long parallel rows. Some held books. Others contained tied packets, rolls of cloth, small wooden boxes, objects wrapped so carefully that their shapes had disappeared beneath the coverings. A high rail ran across the ends of the rows, with hooks hanging from it at regular intervals.

Phene lifted her lamp.

“Returns,” she said.

“All of them?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

She went to the nearest shelf. A label hung from a cord around a packet. She read it, turned it over, and put it down.

“What?”

“Work kept back. Things that couldn’t be returned to the owner.”

“Why?”

“Dead. Gone. Disputed payment. It depends.”

Orest looked up at the rail. “They moved the heavier boxes along that.”

There was a small trolley at one end, its wheels held in an iron channel. The lifting rope had rotted. Below it, a table stood on thick legs with a groove worn across the top.

“We can rest him there,” Tassir said.

They cleared a space. Phene stopped Lerris before he put a packet on the floor and showed him an empty shelf instead.

Dasken lay with his eyes open, following the movement of their lights. He had become quieter since they left the furnace. Lerris asked whether he was cold.

“A little.”

They had no dry coat left. Aqyl took off his own and laid it over him. The tutor’s shirt was damp at the cuffs. Without the coat he looked slight and unprotected, a man who had come out without expecting to be long.

Istren examined the marks on the ends of the shelves. “These aren’t in room order.”

“No,” Phene said. “They’re in the order they arrived.”

“Then how would anyone find a thing?”

“There would be a book.”

No one moved towards the dark beyond the lamps.

Lerris said, “We need a door.”

“Yes,” Phene answered. “We do.”

Orest and Tassir went along the left wall, keeping within call. Lerris remained near Dasken while Istren and Phene examined the next row. Vedran stood where he could see both the entrance and the table. Aqyl walked slowly beneath the high rail, looking at the hooks.

One of them moved.

Lerris watched it until he could be certain. It swung a little, stopped, then swung again. There was no draught strong enough to move the hanging labels.

“Aqyl.”

The tutor had seen it. He held up a hand.

The trolley at the far end made a small metallic click.

Orest stopped along the wall. Everyone listened.

Nothing followed. Aqyl lowered his hand and walked back to the table.

“We shouldn’t stay long.”

“We haven’t found a way out,” Istren said.

“I know.”

Phene was holding a narrow packet. She had not untied it. Her thumb rested on the name written across the wrapping.

“What is it?” Lerris asked.

“Someone I worked with.”

“Is it hers?”

“It has her name.”

Phene looked at the shelf from which she had taken it. There was a space there, clean enough to show that the packet had been moved recently.

“She left,” she said. “Years ago. She thought I’d said something about her work.”

“Had you?”

“Yes.”

The answer surprised him. Phene put the packet back, then adjusted it so the label faced outward.

“I didn’t say it to the person she thought.”

Lerris wanted to ask whether that made a difference. He did not know enough about it, and she had not offered him the account in order to receive a judgment.

At the other end of the row, Istren made a sharp sound.

She stood before a shallow box. Its lid was open. Inside lay a bundle of pages tied with a blue thread.

“No,” she said.

Aqyl crossed to her. “What have you touched?”

“The lid.”

“Nothing inside?”

“No.”

He looked into the box without leaning close.

Lerris could read the upper page. It bore Istren’s name and, beneath it, a heading he did not recognize. The hand was hers, tighter and more careful than the notes she made now.

“My first submission,” she said. “It was returned.”

“Did you leave it here?”

“No.”

“Do you still have it?”

“I burned it.”

Lerris watched her face. He had known her for ten years and had never heard of the submission. He could see how much work had gone into making the letters regular.

“Close it,” Aqyl said.

She did not move.

“Istren.”

“The answer was attached.”

“Leave it.”

“You haven’t read it.”

“No.”

“Then don’t speak as though you know what it is.”

Aqyl stepped back. Istren looked at him, then down at the box, and closed the lid herself.

Orest called from the wall. He had found a door.

They prepared to move Dasken. Lerris reached for the carrier and discovered that the nearest shelf had shifted closer to the table. Not much: a hand’s breadth, perhaps. Enough that the rail no longer cleared the end.

“Was this here?” he asked.

Tassir looked, then put his shoulder against the shelving frame. It did not move.

“It was,” Vedran said.

“There was room.”

“There was.”

Orest came back. He examined the feet of the shelf. They were mounted on small iron rollers sunk into channels in the floor. He crouched and found a wedge beneath one.

“Compacting shelves,” he said. “To make room.”

“Who’s moving them?”

He did not answer. He took the wedge out, put it on the other side of the wheel, and pressed it home with his heel.

The rail above them clicked again.

This time they all heard the wheels.

A row farther into the room began to move. Packets trembled. A loose roll fell from a shelf and struck the floor. The movement was slow, almost gentle, until the end of the row touched the next and stopped with a weighty thud.

“Up,” Tassir said.

They lifted Dasken. Lerris took the front with Orest while Tassir supported the injured leg. Phene carried the water and Markus’s bag. Istren held her lamp high enough to show the channel in the floor.

The route to the door was narrowing.

Aqyl went ahead and tried to put another wedge beneath a wheel. It was too small. The wheel climbed it, crushing the wood with a dry crack.

He pulled his hand clear.

“Faster.”

Lerris wanted to run and could not. The carrier struck the shelf at his right. Dasken cried out. They turned it, raised one end, and squeezed through while packets brushed Lerris’s shoulder.

Behind him Vedran shouted.

His sleeve had caught on a projecting hook. He pulled, tore the cloth, and stumbled into the path of the moving row. Tassir let go of the supported foot and caught him by the collar. For a moment the younger man’s body was stretched between the two, one arm trapped behind him.

Orest and Lerris set the carrier down hard enough to make Dasken gasp.

Lerris went back.

He could see Vedran’s hand. The fingers had closed round the hook. He was trying to free the sleeve without letting go, though letting go was the first thing he needed to do.

“Open your hand.”

Vedran stared at him.

“Your hand.”

Lerris struck his wrist with the heel of his palm, not hard enough to injure, hard enough to interrupt the grip. The fingers opened. Tassir pulled him through as the shelf struck the next.

The hook tore along Vedran’s forearm. Blood ran into his cuff.

They moved again. No one tried to inspect the wound. Orest reached the door, put his back against it, and shoved. It opened outward into a narrow stone passage.

They carried Dasken through. Istren followed with the light. Aqyl came last, holding the door while Tassir pulled Vedran across the threshold.

A shelf struck the inside face.

The door leaped in Aqyl’s hands. He released it and stepped back. It slammed shut, driving a small cloud of dust from the jamb.

For a while the only sound was their breathing.

Then, beyond the wood, something heavy continued moving.

Chapter 27 — The Cloth Stair

Vedran would not look at his arm.

He held it out while Phene cut away the sleeve. The wound was long and ragged but had not reached deeply into the muscle. Blood welled along it whenever he moved his fingers.

“Keep still,” she said.

“I am.”

“Your hand.”

He looked at it as though it belonged to someone who had embarrassed him. Tassir crouched beside him and held the wrist while Phene cleaned the cut with the last of the water from the first vessel.

Lerris watched the floor. He could still feel the impact of Vedran’s wrist against his palm.

“Did I hurt you?” he asked.

The younger guard shook his head.

“You can say.”

“The shelf hurt me.”

Lerris nodded and let the subject go.

They were in a stair passage. Above them the steps ended at a closed door. Below, they turned out of sight. There was no heat here, no sound of water. The air smelled of lime and something faintly sweet which Lerris could not identify.

Aqyl examined the upper door. He found a fastening recessed into the stone and touched it with two fingers.

“I know this.”

Everyone looked up.

“The cloth stair,” he said.

“Does it go out?” Orest asked.

“It joins a room I have used.”

“Which room?”

Aqyl did not answer immediately. Lerris knew before he did.

“The study.”

“Yes.”

For a moment Lerris was back outside the wardens’ notice, waiting while Markus gathered his papers. He remembered the lamp, the two chairs, the thin key. It was a small room. He could not make it contain the distance they had travelled.

“Can you open it?”

“Not from this side.”

Orest stood and came to look. He ran his hands along the jamb, tested the lower hinge, and examined the recessed ironwork.

“There’s a pin through it.”

“Yes.”

“We could drive it.”

“It is secured beyond the stone.”

“Then we could call.”

Aqyl stepped aside.

Orest struck the door with the wooden handle of a tool. He waited, then struck again. The sound was strong enough that Lerris felt it through the stair beneath his feet.

No answer came.

He tried calling Thorne, then Doreta, then anyone in the room beyond. The silence remained complete.

Istren sat beside Dasken. Her hands were black with dust from the shelves. She had tucked the slate inside her coat to protect it and kept touching the place where it lay.

“Were they all things we’d lost?” she asked.

Phene looked up from Vedran’s bandage.

“In the shelves.”

“No,” Phene said. “Most were ordinary returns.”

“Did you read them all?”

“No.”

Istren nodded. She seemed relieved by the failure to know.

Dasken said, “There was a room like that before.”

Aqyl turned from the door.

“Before the flooded room?”

“Yes.”

“What did you see?”

“My mother’s box.”

No one asked what was in it. He went on after a while.

“I sent it home when she died. I didn’t keep it.”

“Yes.”

He was looking at his hands.

“It was empty. I thought someone had taken the things.”

Lerris remembered the small wooden box in Dasken’s recovered bag above. He did not know whether it was the same one and would not ask him to decide now.

Aqyl crouched beside the carrier. “Did you go looking?”

Dasken nodded.

“That’s when you lost Markus?”

“I don’t know.”

The tutor waited, then rose without pressing him.

Lerris went to the door. The wood was old and dry beneath his palm. Somewhere beyond it there might be the room in which his brother had learned to be afraid of sleep. There might be nothing.

He knocked with his fist.

“Markus.”

The name sounded foolish in the closed passage. His brother was in the infirmary. He knew that.

He knocked again anyway.

From the other side came a scrape.

Aqyl caught his shoulder. Lerris did not move. He listened so hard that he could hear the blood in his own ears.

“Who is there?”

It was Thorne.

Lerris shut his eyes.

“Lerris. All of us. Dasken too.”

The captain asked the names again. They answered, each one, speaking towards the wood. When Vedran spoke, his voice shook. Thorne told him he had done well to remain with the party.

The young man looked down at his bandaged arm.

“Can you open it?” Orest called.

“We’re looking at the fastening.”

Aqyl described the pin. Thorne answered with another question. There was a sound of metal against stone, a muttered instruction, then silence.

Markus spoke from farther away.

“The lower fitting. It’s under the shelf.”

Lerris looked at Aqyl. The tutor’s face had become difficult to read.

“You told him this door was unused,” Lerris said quietly.

“It was.”

“Not where it went.”

“I did not know it would lead here.”

The answer might be true. Lerris could not decide how much that excused.

Beyond the door, something heavy was dragged across the floor. Markus coughed. Lerris put both hands on the wood as though he could hold the room steady for him.

Thorne called that they were lifting the pin.

Orest moved everyone back from the top step. Tassir freed his sheathed sword from the carrying sling at his side and drew it, holding it low beside his leg. No one objected. Aqyl stood to one side of the door, his wet coat still covering Dasken.

The fastening scraped.

A line of light appeared at the edge.

It widened slowly. Lerris saw a hand, then Thorne’s face, then the familiar brown table in the study beyond. The captain held the door open with his shoulder and stared at them.

Behind him Markus sat on a chair with one hand pressed to his side.

For a moment no one spoke.

Then Lerris laughed, a cracked, astonished sound, and went up the last step.

Thorne put a hand against his chest.

“Wait.”

He pointed down.

Between the threshold and the floor of the study was a gap no wider than a man’s foot. Lerris had taken it for a shadow. Now he could see that the edge of the study floor ended cleanly, and beyond it there was nothing their light reached.

Orest knelt. He held a loose splinter over the gap and let it fall.

They heard no impact.

“Board,” he said.

Thorne called for one. Markus remained in the chair, staring at his brother.

“We’re here,” Lerris said.

“I can see.”

“I know. I just—”

“I can see you.”

A plank was passed across. Orest tested the end on their side. Thorne set his boot on the other. It spanned the gap easily.

Vedran went first, because Tassir told him to and because his injured arm needed attention. He stepped onto the plank, crossed, and turned at once to look back. He was in the room. Lerris could see his boots on its floor.

Then Dasken made a small sound behind them.

Phene had turned to lift the carrier. The lower stair was no longer empty.

Far below, beyond the reach of the lamps, something was coming up with the slow scrape and thump of a heavy case carried badly.

Chapter 28 — Across

“Don’t look down there,” Tassir said.

Lerris had already looked. He saw only the turn of the stair, the curve of stone catching a little light, and something which might have been the corner of a case. It moved out of sight before he could be sure.

“Take his feet.”

Orest had the upper end of the carrier. Lerris bent, found the rail, and lifted. Dasken gripped the cloth beneath him. Phene walked backwards up the steps, steadying his head and telling Orest how much room remained.

“Another. Now stop.”

They stopped with the carrier level against the top landing. The plank was too narrow to take them side by side. Thorne leaned across and caught the front rail while Orest shifted his hands.

“We’ll pass him.”

“No,” Orest said. “You won’t hold that weight leaning.”

“I have someone behind me.”

Vedran had put down his sword and taken the captain’s belt with his good hand. Behind him a second guard braced against the table. Lerris could see the effort in the captain’s shoulders.

“We need the frame resting,” Orest said. “Here, then slide.”

They set the forward end on the plank. The wood bowed. Dasken shut his eyes.

The scraping below ceased.

That was worse. Lerris had been using the sound to judge the distance.

Tassir stood at the lower edge of the landing. Aqyl had taken one lamp and held it past him. The tutor said something too softly for Lerris to hear.

“Again,” Phene said.

They pushed. The carrier moved along the plank, its lower rail rasping over the wood. Thorne took more of the weight. Vedran’s face had gone white. Blood showed through his bandage.

Istren squeezed past Lerris and stepped across, keeping one hand on the door frame. She caught the carrier beside Thorne. Together they drew Dasken into the room.

His bare heel struck the edge. He cried out. Phene said his name, then went over after him, still holding Markus’s bag.

Orest followed. Lerris remained by the threshold, waiting for the others.

“Go,” Tassir said.

“I’m holding the—”

“Go.”

He obeyed. The plank flexed beneath his weight. For one instant he was suspended over the dark with nothing in his hands, and his stomach dropped as though he were already falling. Then his foot reached the study floor.

Markus caught his sleeve.

It was a weak grip. Lerris covered it with his own hand and held on while Tassir crossed behind him.

Aqyl remained on the landing.

He was looking down the stair. The lamp in his hand lit his face from below, making his eyes seem hollow. He said something again. Lerris could not make out the words.

“Aqyl,” Thorne called.

The tutor moved backwards onto the plank.

From the stair came a man’s voice, breathless with effort.

“Where do you want this?”

Lerris knew the voice. He could not place it. He almost answered.

Aqyl crossed. Thorne pulled the plank after him and pushed the door shut. For a moment something resisted it from the other side. The captain put his shoulder against the wood. Tassir joined him.

It closed.

Orest drove the pin down with the butt of a tool. The sound rang through the room. They waited for a blow from beyond the door.

None came.

Dasken lay on the carrier beside the table. Phene was kneeling over him, checking the ankle. Istren stood with both hands pressed against her face. Vedran had sat down on the floor and was holding his arm against his chest.

Lerris turned to his brother.

“You came back.”

Markus looked at him. “So did you.”

He began to say something else and could not. Lerris knelt beside the chair. His brother put a hand on the back of his neck, drew him close, and held him. Lerris pressed his face against the clean shoulder of Markus’s shirt, careful of the injured side. For a while he could do nothing else.

When Lerris drew back, Markus wiped his own face impatiently.

“You smell terrible.”

“I’ve been industrious.”

“I can tell.”

The joke was poor. Neither tried to improve it.

Thorne went to the outer door. The guard who had braced him remained beside Vedran. Everyone else seemed occupied with a task: bandaging, checking lamps, putting down what had been carried too long.

The captain lifted the latch.

It did not move.

He tried again, then put a hand flat against the door and pushed. Lerris watched the muscles tighten along his jaw.

“Orest.”

The quietness of the call drew more attention than a shout would have.

Orest came over. He examined the latch, lifted it while Thorne pushed, then crouched to look at the gap beneath the door.

“Barred outside.”

“No one had instructions to bar it.”

Thorne struck the wood and called to the man he had left in the passage. No answer came.

Lerris felt Markus’s hand close on his sleeve again.

“We were here,” his brother said. “The door was open.”

“I know.”

“I watched them come through it.”

Across the room Aqyl was looking at the table.

The lamp stood where Lerris remembered it from ten years earlier. There were books, a wooden rest, a little dish used for spent wicks. Against the wall were the two chairs, one of them the chair in which Markus sat. Wire held its split arm together.

Lerris had not noticed that when he embraced him.

“Was that chair here when you arrived?” he asked.

Markus followed his eyes. “Yes.”

“Was it this one?”

His brother looked down at the wire. He touched it once and withdrew his hand.

Thorne called again. The room swallowed the sound in a way the passage should not have permitted. Lerris remembered hearing the porter drag the great public door shut from inside here. Now he heard only the captain and the movement of clothes behind him.

Aqyl crossed to the table and moved a closed book away from the lamp.

“Don’t open anything,” he said.

“What have you seen?” Thorne asked.

“Something that was removed.”

He pointed to a pale cup beside the book.

Markus tried to stand. Lerris helped him, then guided him back when the pain caught. His brother was staring at the cup.

“You took that away.”

“Yes,” Aqyl said.

“Where did you put it?”

“In a locked cabinet.”

“Here?”

“No.”

Thorne turned from the door. “Can it hurt us?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then leave it alone.”

He took the lamp and examined the outer wall. There was a narrow shutter high above the shelves, too small for a man but large enough to call through. Orest climbed onto the table, avoiding the cup, and worked the fastening loose.

The shutter opened.

Beyond was the public room.

Lerris saw a table, a row of empty stalls, and a man standing with his back to them. The light was grey and ordinary. He could hear footsteps. Relief rose in him so suddenly that he almost called before Orest did.

“Here!”

The man did not turn.

Orest shouted again. The man moved away between the tables.

Thorne climbed beside him. He struck the stone around the opening with the handle of his knife. The sound was sharp inside the study. Nothing in the public room responded.

Lerris moved closer. Through the opening he could now see Doreta. She was speaking to a guard. He recognized the way she held her hands together, one thumb working across the other.

“Orest,” he said.

“I see her.”

The porter called to his mother. She continued speaking, then shook her head and walked out of view.

Orest remained on the table after she had gone.

Tassir asked whether they could break the opening larger. The captain looked at the stone, then at the empty air beyond it.

“Not until we’ve tested what goes through.”

He took a loose slip from his pocket, folded it, and pushed it through the opening. It fell into the public room, turning once before it reached the floor.

A guard passed close enough to step on it. His boot came down where the paper lay. When he lifted his foot, the paper remained unmarked.

“No one climbs through.”

He stepped down from the table. Orest stayed another moment, his hand on the fastening, then followed.

Lerris looked at his brother. Markus had closed his eyes. His lips moved once, silently, and stopped.

“What?”

“I was counting.”

“Why?”

“To hear something I had begun myself.”

Lerris sat on the floor beside the chair. He could not think of anything to say that would not make the room smaller.

At the table, Phene had finished wrapping Vedran’s arm. She tore the remaining cloth into strips and folded them for later use. Istren took out her slate, erased a section with her sleeve, and began drawing the study.

Thorne put his lamp beside hers.

“All right,” he said. “Who is hurt badly enough that we must move them first?”

Pellant was not there to answer. Lerris looked at the people who were, and began with his brother.

Chapter 29 — Eleven in a Small Room

The study had been crowded with two men in it. With eleven, there was nowhere to stand without obstructing someone who needed to move.

They put Dasken’s carrier on the table after clearing the books to a shelf. Markus kept the chair near the inner door because moving him hurt, and because he refused to sit beside the pale cup. Vedran took the floor beneath the shutter. The guard who had come in with Thorne stood over him for a while, not knowing where else to go, until the captain told him to sit too.

“Your name?” Phene asked the man.

“Arvel.”

“Arvel, take his hand. Keep it still while I tie this.”

He obeyed. He was older than Tassir, with a thick neck and a burn scar just below one ear. His hands were unexpectedly gentle. Vedran watched them rather than the wound.

Lerris passed the water. There was enough for drinking and cleaning if they used it carefully. He had begun to hate the word carefully. Everyone was being careful. His brother had been careful for ten years.

Markus saw him looking.

“Sit down.”

“I am doing something.”

“So am I.”

“What?”

“Trying not to watch you fall over.”

Lerris sat on the edge of the rolled carpet. Dust rose from it. He sneezed, then sneezed again so violently that Dasken opened his eyes.

“Sorry.”

“Don’t apologize for that,” Dasken said.

Lerris looked at him. The injured man had already closed his eyes again.

Aqyl had begun examining the books moved from the table.

“Leave them,” Thorne said.

“I need to know which are here.”

“Why?”

“Because some should not be.”

“That applies to us too.”

The captain returned to the latch. Aqyl kept his hands clear of the books, but he continued reading their spines.

Lerris watched him. Without his coat, which still lay over Dasken, the tutor looked cold. A sleeve had torn near the elbow. There was nothing in his appearance to justify the expectation that he would know how to get them out. Lerris could not stop expecting it.

“Have you been shut in here before?” he asked.

Aqyl turned.

“No.”

“Anywhere like it?”

“Yes.”

The room quieted. Even Orest stopped working at the latch.

“What happened?”

“We found another way out.”

“Through what?”

“A workroom.”

“Where?”

“Not here.”

Lerris stood up again. “Then tell us something we can use.”

Markus said his name, quietly. He ignored it.

“You’ve recognized doors. You’ve told us not to touch things. You knew enough to leave the ladder. What are you waiting for?”

Aqyl looked towards Dasken. “For a thing I know to remain true long enough to act upon it.”

“That is a very good answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

Lerris felt the old pleasure of finding the next cutting remark. He had an audience, he was frightened, and Aqyl would not defend himself in any way that risked seeming unkind. It would have been easy.

“Fenwood. Hold the light.”

He gave Lerris a lamp and pointed to the latch. Lerris held it. The captain slid a thin blade into the gap above the iron tongue and worked it down. Metal clicked. The latch lifted freely.

“No bar,” Orest said.

“You said there was.”

“It felt like one.”

“The frame will go first.”

“Flue?” Arvel asked.

“Maybe.”

“Don’t open it blind,” Aqyl said.

Orest glanced at him. “I was going to look.”

“Tool,” he said.

Chapter 30 — The Copy He Kept

Sothel would not strike the wall where Orest asked him to.

“The arch is carrying weight,” he said. “I can take the facing off. I will not have the north floor on my head to prove a point.”

“Then take the facing off.”

“We are.”

“The workroom Aqyl mentioned.”

“I heard.”

The passage concerned a blocked opening and the movement of a collection to another room. It was dry work. Lerris read it twice before he understood what troubled his brother.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“I don’t remember.”

Lerris looked at the ink. It was as old as the lines around it.

“Could it be like the list?”

“I don’t know.”

“I copied this from a marginal note. I didn’t mark the source.”

“Would you usually?”

“Yes.”

“Then perhaps—”

“Or perhaps I was tired.”

His brother’s voice was sharp. Lerris stopped.

The hammering paused. Beyond the wall, Sothel asked for a different tool. Someone answered. Markus listened until the work resumed, then called Aqyl over.

“Do you recognize it?” Markus asked.

“Yes.”

“From where?”

“A set of instructions that should not have been kept with the ordinary records.”

“Were they kept here?”

“Yes.”

Markus looked down at the page. He seemed less angry now, which made Lerris uneasy.

“What happens if someone follows them?”

“I have seen someone arrange the material and become unable to leave it.”

“They would put it right again whenever we moved it. They would not eat until it was right.”

“We did.”

“Did they recover?”

Aqyl did not answer at once.

“Some habits remained.”

Lerris looked at his brother’s fingers on the book. Markus removed them from the line.

“What did you do with the instructions?”

“We separated the copies. Sealed the room.”

“And left a key.”

“Yes.”

Beyond the wall a tool struck metal, and Sothel swore.

“It has before.”

“Would burning them help us?”

“I cannot promise that.”

“Have you tried?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“It stopped one thing. It did not stop everything.”

Markus closed the notebook. His face was grey with fatigue.

“How much of this did you know when you took me here?”

“Enough that I should have told you more.”

“That isn’t an amount.”

“No.”

Lerris wanted to strike him. The impulse was sudden and physical; he imagined the tutor’s head turning, the satisfaction of producing an answer that was not measured. Then he saw Markus looking at him.

His brother shook his head once.

Aqyl stepped away when Markus asked him to. He did not offer to help decide which pages mattered.

There were years in it. Lerris could see that now: the changes of ink, a hand growing smaller as paper became scarce, a hurried drawing of a window, a note about a conversation whose speaker had since left the university. A pressed leaf had stained one page. Markus lifted it carefully and put it with Alayse’s letter.

“You don’t have to burn all of it,” Lerris said.

“I know.”

“You could take out the line.”

“I know.”

His brother remained still for a long time. Then he took his small knife from the bag and began cutting through the thread that held the first gathering.

Lerris watched the blade move. It was ordinary work, done badly because Markus’s hands hurt and his position made the angle difficult.

“Let me.”

“No. Hold the light.”

He did.

Chapter 31 — What Would Burn

They used the dish for spent wicks.

It was too small. Orest found a wider metal tray beneath the shelf and brought it over, first scraping away the old wax and dust. They set it on the bare patch of floor where the carpet had been rolled back. Tassir stood beside it with water ready.

Markus had removed two gatherings. He had left the newer inventories bound in place. Aqyl looked at the loose pages when asked, pointed to one passage, and said it repeated material from the first gathering. Markus cut that leaf free too.

The first page caught slowly. Damp along one edge made it curl and hiss. Markus held it by a dry corner until the flame approached his fingers, then laid it in the tray.

Lerris could read a few words as the paper darkened. A place name. A date. Part of the phrase about the ford. Then the centre collapsed.

The cup on the table made a sound.

It was small, no more than the click of a cup set down too firmly. Everyone looked at it. It had not moved as far as Lerris could tell.

Markus fed in the second sheet.

From the other side of the inner door came someone clearing his throat.

Markus’s hand stopped.

Lerris knew that sound. Their father used to make it when a room had talked long enough to discover what it did not know. No word followed. None was needed for the old response to rise in him: sit straighter, stop smiling, prepare to be asked something specific.

He looked at Markus. His brother had heard it too.

The loose paper trembled between his fingers.

“Do you want me to take it?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

Markus put it into the flame. He missed the tray’s centre and Lerris pushed the burning corner inward with a piece of wood.

The throat cleared again, more impatiently.

At the outer wall, the hammering stopped.

“Keep going,” Thorne called to Sothel.

“We’ve met iron.”

“Then work round it.”

“We are.”

The voices helped. They were cross, occupied, and did not concern themselves with the fire. Lerris held on to them while Markus separated the next leaves.

One would not come free. Damp had joined it to another. Markus tried to pull them apart and tore the edge. He made a sound of distress which embarrassed him at once.

“Leave them together,” Lerris said.

“I need the other.”

“What is it?”

“A comparison. Three copies. I can’t do it again.”

Lerris looked. He understood nothing of the page beyond the care with which its columns had been aligned. His brother had probably spent weeks on it. The fire was burning low while they waited.

“You could keep that one separate.”

Markus shook his head.

He put both leaves in.

For a moment Lerris hated Aqyl more than he had when the tutor admitted withholding knowledge. Then the upper page whitened, broke, and fell through the one beneath it. He looked at his brother instead.

Markus was crying without much sound. He kept feeding the pages one at a time because the tray would not hold more. Lerris moved the lamp away so it did not shine directly in his face.

The throat-clearing stopped.

The room did not release them. The door remained closed. Dasken still lay injured on the table, Vedran still held his arm against his chest, and Orest was still trying to extract a piece of iron from the wall. Smoke gathered beneath the ceiling.

But when Lerris glanced at the pale cup, he could see a crack across its rim which had not been there before.

Markus laid down the last leaf. He watched it burn, then held out the surviving notebook to Lerris.

“Put it away.”

Lerris wrapped it in the dry part of the cloth and returned it to the bag. He did not tell his brother that he could remember some of the lost pages.

Sothel called through the wall.

“There is a space behind the facing. Can you see light?”

Orest lifted the lamp away from the crack. A thin yellow point appeared in the darkness beyond it.

“Yes.”

“Does it move?”

“Up. Left. Down.”

“Good.”

For the first time, Orest put his fingers into the opening without hesitation. A tool touched them from the other side. He withdrew his hand, grinning, then looked back at Markus and the tray.

They worked faster. Plaster fell in chunks. Tassir carried it away from the injured so it would not gather beneath their feet. Arvel wedged the table to keep it from shifting when Dasken flinched at the blows. Phene covered the young man’s face loosely with a clean cloth and spoke to him beneath the noise.

At last the opening was wide enough for Orest to see through.

“It’s the passage,” he said.

“What part?” Thorne asked.

“The one with the hooks. Sothel’s here.”

He moved aside. Lerris saw a piece of the teacher’s face, dust in the eyebrows and irritation in the eye nearest the gap.

“Someone has built the jamb into an older flue,” Sothel said. “Badly.”

“Can you pull it out?”

“Not without dropping the stone above it.”

Orest pointed to the remaining iron. “We can take that across the load.”

“If it’s sound.”

“We can test it.”

They began discussing how. Lerris followed only half the words, but the space beyond the wall remained visible while they spoke. A woman passed behind Sothel carrying a basket. She stopped, looked at the hole, and asked whether the people inside needed water.

Lerris laughed. He could not help it.

“Yes,” he said. “Yes, please.”

She went to fetch it.

Thorne did not laugh. He watched the damaged jamb, the old iron, the people who would have to pass beneath it. Then he asked Orest how many men he needed to hold the temporary support.

“Two. Perhaps three.”

“Can they come after?”

Orest looked at the stone above the door.

“We’ll arrange it so they can.”

The captain waited until Orest met his eyes.

“Arrange it first.”

Chapter 32 — The Last Man at the Door

The support came through the opening in pieces.

Sothel sent a length of timber, two short blocks, and wedges cut from a harder wood. Orest and Arvel set them beneath the old iron, lifting only enough to take its weight. Every blow made dust sift down. Lerris stood beside Dasken’s carrier and watched a little heap grow on the floor.

Outside, there were more people now. He heard Doreta once, sharply ordering someone to move back. He heard the bread woman, or someone with a voice like hers, asking where to put a bucket. They were so close that he wanted to apologize for how long it was taking.

Thorne put Tassir and Arvel at the support. He remained beside Orest while the porter loosened the last section of the jamb.

“Slowly.”

“I know.”

The wood came free an inch at a time. A nail tore through rotten grain with a sound like cloth ripping. The door sagged. Orest caught its edge and stepped aside while Thorne pulled it inward.

The passage opened before them.

It was narrow, badly lit, and crowded with people trying not to crowd it. Lerris had never seen anything more welcome.

Doreta stood behind Sothel. She saw Orest and put a hand over her mouth. He nodded at her, once, because he was holding a piece of the frame and could not go to her yet.

Thorne sent Vedran first. The young guard crossed beneath the support with his injured arm held close. Doreta caught his good hand and drew him away from the opening. He turned to protest, then let himself be taken.

Dasken followed on the frame. Orest and Lerris carried him to the threshold, where two men outside took the forward end. Phene went with him. His eyes remained open throughout, fixed on the lintel above his face. When they set him down in the passage, he asked whether they were really out.

Phene said she could feel the draught from the court.

Istren crossed next with the tools and the bag. Aqyl waited beside Markus while Lerris helped his brother stand. He could feel Markus trying not to lean on him and wished, fiercely, that he would stop trying.

“Use me.”

“I am.”

“More.”

His brother’s weight settled against his shoulder. Together they reached the opening. Markus bent his head beneath the timber, gasped, and stopped.

“What?”

“The step.”

It was only a little change in level. With his side injured, it might as well have been a wall. Lerris moved his foot closer and told him where to put his own. Sothel took Markus’s other arm from outside. They brought him over.

Once he was clear, Lerris turned back.

Aqyl was still inside. He had gone to the shelf beside the table and was lifting a wrapped packet from beneath a book.

“Leave it,” Lerris said.

The tutor looked up.

“It should not remain here.”

“Then bring it.”

Aqyl hesitated. The hesitation frightened Lerris more than the packet.

“What is it?”

“An account of the room.”

“Yours?”

“No.”

Behind him, Tassir shifted beneath the iron. The timber gave a little creak.

“Come out,” Thorne said.

Aqyl set the packet down.

He crossed. Orest followed, carrying the hammer. Thorne remained with the two guards. They had arranged wedges to take the pressure while the men withdrew, but the stone above had begun to settle unevenly. Arvel was pushing upward with both hands, his shoulders tight beneath his coat.

“Left one,” Orest said from the passage.

Thorne drove the wedge. The support steadied.

“Tassir.”

The guard stepped back slowly, testing the weight before he released it. He ducked through. Arvel remained.

Lerris watched the man’s hands. They were the same hands that had held Vedran’s wrist while Phene bandaged him. He knew very little else about him.

“Now you,” Thorne said.

Arvel shook his head. “It’s moving.”

“Another wedge.”

Orest passed one through. Thorne set it. The wood split.

A thin line opened in the plaster above Arvel’s head.

Sothel ordered everyone back except the men at the support. Lerris remained where he was. He could see the wrapped packet on the shelf behind Arvel, close enough that one step would reach it. For a moment he thought of Markus’s burned pages and everything they still did not know.

Then Arvel’s knee bent.

Lerris went under the timber and caught him round the chest.

“Let go.”

“I can’t—”

“Let go.”

Thorne seized the back of the guard’s coat. Together they pulled. Arvel’s hands came free. The iron dropped against the wedges with a blow that shook the doorway.

They fell into the passage.

A stone struck Lerris’s calf. He barely felt it then. He crawled backwards, pulling Arvel with him, while Thorne rolled aside and Sothel shoved a long pole beneath the descending timber.

For one breath the opening remained clear.

Lerris saw the study table. The packet. The wire-bound chair. On the far side of the room the inner door stood open, though Orest had pinned it shut.

A person was standing there.

He could not see the face. He saw a grey sleeve, one hand held out as though asking to be helped across, and something dark at the feet.

“Wait,” he said.

The pole bowed.

Sothel shouted for the men to get clear. Thorne caught Lerris’s collar and hauled him back. The stone came down.

Dust filled the passage. People coughed and called names. Somewhere Doreta was telling Orest not to move until she had looked at his hands. Lerris lay on his side with Arvel’s coat still twisted in his fist.

When he could see again, the opening was gone.

Not sealed neatly. Broken stone, timber, plaster, and the bent length of iron filled the place where the door had been. Through a small gap near the floor he could see darkness. No hand reached from it.

Thorne knelt beside Arvel, who was breathing hard but answering. Sothel had blood on his forehead. Istren came back through the dust and put a hand on Lerris’s shoulder.

“Can you stand?”

“There was someone.”

“I know. Arvel’s here.”

“No. Inside.”

She looked at the fallen stone.

Lerris tried to explain the sleeve, the hand. He could hear how little it was. He had seen familiar things in rooms that could not contain them. He had heard the dead answer from behind a door.

But he had also seen a roof worker brought out of water where no one believed a man could be.

Thorne came to him.

“What did you see?”

He told him. The captain listened without deciding aloud.

“We’ll inspect it when the stone is held,” he said.

“Now.”

“Not under that.”

Lerris looked at the ruin of the doorway. Somewhere beneath the dust his calf had begun to hurt. Istren kept her hand on his shoulder until he stopped trying to rise towards the blocked opening.

From the court came the sound of the fountain.

He had wanted to hear it for so long that he could not bear it at first.

Chapter 33 — The Beds by the Window

The infirmary had run out of stools.

Lerris learned this from a woman who wanted him to get off one. She carried a basin in both hands and had a folded cloth beneath her chin. He stood too quickly, put his weight on the struck leg, and sat down again with such violence that water leapt over the basin’s edge.

“Oh, stay there,” she said.

She put the basin on the floor and went away to find another. Lerris watched the wet patch spread through the dust on his hose. There was a little stone inside his shoe. He could feel it beneath the outer edge of his foot, but reaching down made his calf tighten, and he could not yet persuade himself that taking off the shoe was worth the effort.

Across the room, Pellant was cutting Dasken’s stocking.

Phene stood beside the bed holding the recovered right boot. Someone had brought it from the porter’s room with his bag. She had opened the lacing all the way down; the tongue hung forward like an exhausted animal’s. Dasken watched her rather than Pellant.

“You needn’t cut that too,” he said.

“I won’t.”

“I’ve hardly had them.”

“I know.”

Pellant asked him to look at the ceiling. Dasken did, obediently, and then jerked his head sideways with a cry that stopped the conversations nearest his bed.

Phene put the boot down.

Lerris found that he had gripped the stool beneath him with both hands. A moment later the young man began swearing. The words came in an unbroken, furious stream, some of them unfamiliar to Lerris, and Phene said, with considerable feeling, “Yes.”

Pellant waited until Dasken could hear him again.

“The joint is where it should be. Don’t try it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You will in a little while.”

He called for the shaped boards. Lessa brought them from a cupboard, kneeling to choose among lengths that had clearly served many different legs. The first pair were too short. She set them aside without making Dasken move again.

Markus lay two beds farther on, beneath a window whose shutters had been tied open. His eyes were closed. Each time someone crossed between him and the light, his brow contracted. Lerris had already asked twice whether he wanted the shutters closed. The second time Markus had said that if he asked again he would throw something, provided somebody first supplied him with an object he could lift.

There was nothing on the table beside him except a cup.

Lerris had left it there.

A boy came through the door carrying bread on a board. He wore a bandage round his hand, and used his forearm to support the load. The furnace cord. Lerris recognized him only when the boy shifted the board and winced.

“Should you be doing that?”

The boy looked at the bread, then at the beds, as though uncertain which part of his work was in question.

“It’s my other hand.”

Lessa took the board from him before Lerris could answer. She sent him to sit by the door and eat two pieces. He chose the largest and the smallest, settled on the threshold, and began with the small one.

Somewhere beyond the infirmary wall, a hammer struck twice. Lerris turned towards it. A pause, then four blows in quick succession. He had been trying to follow the work since they brought him in. The strokes ceased whenever he thought he had learned what the men were doing.

“They’re fixing the outer prop,” Istren said.

She stood behind his stool with Markus’s brown bag slung over her shoulder. Her hair had come loose from its fastening. A band of pale skin showed at the edge of the dust on her neck.

“How do you know?”

“I asked.”

He could have done that. The thought irritated him more than it should have.

“Thorne said he’d send.”

“He will. The passage is still standing.”

She set the bag beneath Markus’s table, where he could reach it without getting out of bed. Then she sat on the floor beside Lerris and began removing her own shoes.

One heel was raw. She examined it without touching it, let her head rest against the wall, and closed her eyes.

“Did you see anyone?” he asked.

“When?”

“In the study. After Aqyl came through.”

“No. I was out in the passage with the bag.”

“Of course.”

She kept her eyes closed. He wished she had asked him to describe the figure. He wished just as strongly that nobody would ask again.

The woman with the basin returned. It was empty now. She set it before him and looked at his leg.

“Can you get the shoe off?”

“Yes.”

He bent. Halfway down, the pain caught. Istren opened her eyes, watched him for a moment, and took his ankle in both hands.

“Leave it,” she said.

She eased the heel free. The little stone dropped into the basin with an absurdly clear sound.

His hose had stuck to the broken skin. They soaked the cloth before drawing it away. Beneath, the calf was already swollen, a darkening patch around a shallow split. Pellant came to look once Dasken’s boards were bound. He made Lerris move his foot, pressed along the bone, and told him to keep the leg raised.

“How long?”

“Until I look again.”

“I mean before I can—”

“I know what you mean.”

He moved on to Vedran, who was trying to hold his wounded arm high without appearing to do so.

They found a folded blanket for Lerris’s leg. Istren stayed on the floor while the woman washed the dust out of the cut. Lerris watched the water turn grey. He had imagined that once he was out he would want to tell somebody everything. Now the order of it seemed to have gone from him. He could remember Phene cutting a stocking more clearly than the passage in which they had nearly lost Vedran. Markus’s voice through the furnace was close enough to hear. The way from the furnace to the shelves would not come at all until he shut his eyes, and then it came too quickly.

“Levis.”

Orest stood at the door.

He had washed his face but missed the dust in his eyebrows. His mother followed with a bundle of shirts over her arm. Before he could speak she pushed one against his chest.

“That one first. The seam’s gone in yours.”

“I know.”

“You’ll know better when you put your hand through it.”

He took the shirt. She continued among the beds, finding people who needed things she had already brought.

Orest remained near the door, working a bit of plaster out from beneath his thumbnail.

“They’ve reached the floor,” he said. “There’s a space behind the fall. No answer yet.”

Lerris began to lower his leg.

“Thorne sent me to tell you. He didn’t send for you.”

“I could recognize—”

“What? A sleeve?”

Istren sat up.

Orest looked towards Dasken, then back at Lerris. His mouth moved as if he had something caught between his teeth.

“They’re looking,” he said.

He left with the clean shirt under his arm.

For a little while Lerris heard every movement in the infirmary. The torn bread, water poured from one vessel to another, Dasken asking whether somebody had his left boot. He had thought Orest might be angry when there was time. He had not expected to be so ashamed of wanting the anger postponed.

Markus opened his eyes.

“Is there any bread left?”

Lerris looked at him. His brother’s face was pale, the lines beside his mouth deepened by the way he was holding himself.

“Yes.”

“With something on it?”

“I’ll find out.”

“Ask from there.”

Lerris called to the boy by the door. The boy held up the large piece he had saved, considered it, and broke it in half.

Chapter 34 — Behind the Fall

By dusk, Thorne had a view of the table.

He sent for Lerris only after Pellant had looked at the leg again. The healer permitted the walk with a stick, on the condition that somebody accompanied him and brought him back. Istren volunteered. She had acquired different shoes, too large at the toe, which made a soft slap against the passage stones.

Outside, the air smelled of wet timber. The masons had watered the dust before clearing it. A lamp hung from a new crosspiece, its light touching fresh cuts in the wood, and two men crouched beside a hole little larger than a serving hatch. One held a mirror on a short handle. The other kept a lamp low enough to light what the mirror showed.

Lerris had expected the doorway to be recognizable. Most of the jamb had come away. The iron that had held them looked much thinner from this side.

Thorne stopped him beyond the props.

“Here.”

He indicated a place where Lerris could lean against the sound wall. Sothel stood beside him, a square of dressing at his hairline. There was mortar on the knees of his robe.

“We’ve called through from here and from the old flue,” the captain said. “No answer. There’s no movement we can see.”

“Can you see the far door?”

“Part of it.”

The man with the mirror adjusted it. Lerris bent, awkwardly, and found a small reversed room in the glass. The table lay slantwise. One leg had broken. The wire-bound chair was on its side, and the inner door showed as a dark strip behind it.

“Is it open?”

“We can’t tell how far,” Sothel said. “The chair cuts across it.”

“The person was there.”

“Standing?”

“Yes.”

“Facing you?”

“I think so.”

Thorne asked him to point. Lerris did. The mason moved the mirror until the dark strip filled more of it, but there was nothing to be gained from making the same darkness larger.

“Could the sleeve have been cloth hanging from the door?” Sothel asked.

Lerris felt himself stiffen.

“The hand moved.”

“You didn’t say that before.”

He looked away from the mirror.

Had it moved? He had seen it held towards him. In remembering, the figure had begun to reach. He tried to return to the instant before Thorne caught his collar, to hold the arm where it had been without allowing it to continue.

“I saw a hand,” he said. “I don’t know if it moved.”

The captain told the mason to hold the lamp still. Nobody remarked on the correction. Lerris was grateful, and disliked being grateful for it.

From the other end of the passage came the sound of an argument conducted in low voices. A man wanted a locked chest brought from the adjoining room. The guard was explaining that the adjoining room was now part of the closed section. The man said there were papers in it needed for a morning examination.

Thorne went to speak to him.

Sothel remained beside the hole. He rubbed mortar from one knuckle with the thumb of his other hand.

“If there is someone behind that chair, why aren’t we going in?” Lerris asked.

“Because this is carrying that.”

He laid his palm against the nearest prop, then pointed to a fractured stone above it. Only when Lerris followed the line did he see how much of the wall bore down on the new wood.

“We need a second support from the passage beyond. The stair to it has separated from the wall. I’ve sent for longer beams.”

“How long?”

“They’re coming from the quay.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No. It’s where they are.”

The sharpness went out of Sothel’s voice almost at once. He bent to take the mirror from the mason, relieving the man’s cramped wrist.

Lerris stood against the wall. Istren did not offer to take him back yet.

When Thorne returned, he brought Doreta with him. She carried a thin board and a sheet on which names had been entered in several hands.

“Everyone who was signed into the north rooms,” she said. “The workers as well as readers. We’ve checked the people who left before the closure. Some twice.”

Her finger went down the sheet. Markus, under his proper name. Dasken. The roof worker. The search parties had their own list on the reverse. No one currently recorded was unaccounted for.

“That doesn’t include somebody who came through a service door,” she said before Lerris could object. “Or somebody who wasn’t supposed to be here.”

“Or somebody older,” he said.

Doreta’s finger stopped.

He wished he had put it differently. There was a repair account in the infirmary, a missing labourer named beneath a warning about the speaking hole. He had never met the man. Already it was easier to make him into an explanation than to imagine him standing in the dark for all those years.

“We need the old account,” Thorne said.

“I know where it is,” Istren told him.

She did not move until Lerris said he would wait where he was.

After she left, Doreta sat on an unused block of wood. She had found a shawl, but it kept slipping off one shoulder. Her hands rested on the list.

“Did Orest tell you about the space?”

“Yes.”

“He said you wanted to come.”

“I did.”

She looked at his stick. He could not tell what she was thinking, and he had relied on being able to tell for so many years that the uncertainty felt like being kept outside her door.

“Will they question him?” he asked.

“They’ll question everyone.”

“He took my word.”

“Yes.”

The man with the examination papers returned with someone more important. Thorne met them at the turn before they reached the props. Their voices fell. Doreta folded the list once, carefully, keeping the writing inward.

“You should tell them that,” she said.

“I intend to.”

“I mean before they ask him why he didn’t check.”

He looked down the passage for Thorne. The captain had taken the two men aside and was pointing towards the damaged ceiling. Lerris could not hear his words. One of the men looked up and stepped back.

“I’ll tell him now.”

Doreta caught the edge of her shawl before it fell again.

When Istren returned, she found Lerris dictating to the guard at the table by the passage entrance. Thorne stood beside them. The account was short. Lerris had said that Markus was waiting for a cart. He had not seen him waiting. He had known he might still be working inside.

The guard read it back.

“Not might,” Lerris said.

Thorne looked at him.

“I knew he hadn’t finished. I didn’t know which room he was in.”

They changed the sentence. He gave the name by which the university knew him, then his full name when Thorne requested it. The guard paused only long enough to ask how Fenwood was spelled.

Behind them, the mason called into the broken room once more.

Chapter 35 — A Room for the Term

Lerris slept until someone dropped a pan.

He came upright with both hands searching the blanket. His leg caught against the end of the bed. For a moment the infirmary door stood where the inner door of the study should have been, and he could not understand why nobody was holding it.

Then Lessa swore from the little room beyond. The pan rang again, less loudly, as she picked it up.

Markus watched him from the next bed.

“I was awake,” Lerris said.

“I wasn’t going to ask.”

Morning light had reached the wall above the cupboard. Somebody had hung a clean shirt over a chair. Lerris could smell porridge and the bitter preparation Pellant used to wash wounds.

He lay back. His heart took some time to settle. Markus turned his head towards the window, allowing him to do it without conversation.

They were still in the infirmary when the porter brought the notice.

It had been folded without a seal. Lerris opened it expecting a summons to give the longer account Thorne had asked for. Instead he found that his permission to enter the closed collections had been suspended. He was to return the borrowing counters he held for two workrooms and obtain written permission before visiting the north range on any business.

At the bottom, in a different hand, someone had added that his testimony was required at the second afternoon bell.

He read it twice.

“What is it?” Markus asked.

“Nothing that prevents me having breakfast.”

“Then put it down and have breakfast.”

He did not. The suspension was reasonable. He could have written a persuasive defence of it himself. What occupied him was the way the paper had reached him in a room full of people, by a porter who had once fetched him keys without needing to be told where he meant to go.

Markus held out a hand.

Lerris gave him the notice. His brother read it, folded it along its existing creases, and returned it.

“They’ll need those counters.”

“I can read.”

“I know. You keep doing it.”

Lerris pushed the notice beneath his cup.

After breakfast, Istren brought news of the beams. They had arrived during the night. The workmen had secured the next passage and enlarged the opening enough to move the broken table. The study was empty. Its inner door opened onto a short stair ending in masonry, which Thorne had ordered left untouched until it could be examined from both sides. There was no visible blood, no clothing, and no answer from beyond it.

“Did they find the packet?” Markus asked.

“Yes.”

“Where is it?”

“With the other things taken out. Nobody’s opened it.”

Lerris turned towards him. Markus was looking at Istren, waiting for the rest.

“The room is guarded,” she said. “They’re examining the repair accounts. Doreta has sent enquiries about the missing labourer. That’s all I know.”

Markus nodded. His hand had come to rest on the edge of his blanket, thumb pressing into the weave.

Istren set a bundle on Lerris’s bed.

“Your things from my room.”

He recognized the corner of a book, his writing case, a comb he had accused her of losing. She had tied them in an old tablecloth.

“Are you moving?”

“I’m taking the room above the east court for my pupils. For the rest of the term.”

“That’s a better room.”

“Yes.”

He waited. She stood with her hands empty at her sides.

“I need the counter,” she said.

He reached into the pocket of his discarded coat. There were three counters on the ring, worn smooth at their holes. He had ceased thinking of them as things that belonged to anyone. Istren identified hers without touching it.

“That one.”

He worked it off. The ring had bent, and the counter caught at the join. For several seconds they both watched him struggle with it.

“You could have asked me to bring these,” he said.

“I was bringing them anyway.”

When he held it out, she took it.

“Is this because of the notice?”

“No.”

She looked towards the beds nearest them. Dasken was asleep. Phene had gone home to wash and promised to return with his other clothes. Vedran was eating from a bowl held awkwardly against his chest.

Istren lowered her voice.

“I have pupils who wait outside while you talk to me. You come in because you know you can. Half the time I enjoy it. Then you go away and I’m behind with the work.”

“You should have said.”

“I have said.”

He remembered occasions on which she had asked him to leave. They had mostly been amusing. He could remember making them amusing.

“And yesterday?” he asked.

“Yesterday I went with you because Dasken was missing.”

“I know that.”

“I want you to know it when you tell this.”

He looked at the bundle. She had put a piece of scrap paper between his comb and the book so the teeth would not scratch the cover.

“I wouldn’t say—”

She waited.

He had already spoken of them as his friends, the people who had come with him. He had thought nothing of it. Even now he wanted her to acknowledge that she was his friend, as if obtaining the word would undo the rest of the conversation.

“I’ll come at the visiting hour,” he said.

“Write first. I may be teaching somewhere else.”

“Very well.”

She touched the end of his bed, briefly, and went out with the counter in her hand.

Markus said nothing.

Lerris untied the tablecloth. His comb was clean. A small curl of somebody else’s dark hair had caught beneath the writing-case clasp; he pulled it free and let it fall. He arranged the things on the table, then found there was no room for his cup and had to move them again.

By the second afternoon bell he had borrowed a better stick.

The room in which they took the statements was ordinarily used to examine disputes over rents. Lerris had been there once with a friend who claimed his landlord charged separately for the rats. He remembered the window sticking, and the relief when somebody finally laughed. Today it stood open. There were three people at the table, Thorne near the wall, and a clerk with a sheaf already half filled.

They asked him to begin before the closing of the north rooms.

He described his conversation with Orest. They asked what he had known, what he had seen, and what he had intended Orest to understand. The last question was the worst because he could answer it without difficulty.

“That he needn’t trouble Markus.”

“Was Markus in danger of disciplinary action?”

“I thought he might be questioned about remaining.”

“Had anyone threatened such action?”

“No.”

The clerk’s pen made a soft, hurried noise.

They asked about Dasken, about the room, about the routes he could remember. He described the supplies through the furnace and made sure the injured boy appeared in the account. He could not give the boy’s name. The clerk left a space for it.

When they came to the working notebook, Lerris paused.

Markus had asked about the packet. He could see his brother’s hand on the blanket. He could leave out the burning until someone else mentioned it; it was not as though he understood what had happened well enough to explain it.

“Markus recognized something he’d copied,” he said. “He cut out the pages.”

“On whose instruction?”

“His own.”

“Master Aqyl’s?”

“No. My brother’s.”

He told them about the tray and the water, the smoke that had made Phene cover Dasken’s face, the simultaneous work at the door. He could not say that destroying the pages had opened it. When asked whether he believed it had helped, he said yes.

“Why?”

He thought of the pale cup, and the small sound it had made.

“I can’t establish that.”

Thorne shifted against the wall. One of the people at the table drew a line beside something in the clerk’s earlier account.

They kept Lerris until his calf throbbed. By the time they finished, the open window had become cold. He asked to hear the statement read back before signing it, and found three things he had said badly and one that had been written differently from what he meant. The clerk corrected them with increasing stiffness.

Outside, Orest waited on the bench.

Lerris stopped. His first impulse was to explain that he had told them. He managed not to.

“They’re ready for you,” he said.

Orest stood. His clean shirt had a collar too tight for him, and he had left it unfastened.

“Did they ask about the side door?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

He went in.

Lerris lowered himself onto the bench. There was no reason to wait; Orest might come out by the other passage, or prefer to walk back alone. He shifted the stick until it would stay upright between his knees and settled against the wall.

Chapter 36 — The East Court

Orest came out by the other passage.

Lerris saw him through the open end of the gallery, walking with Thorne. The captain was speaking; Orest listened with his head turned a little, his hands pushed into his sleeves. Neither saw Lerris. He remained on the bench until they had crossed the court, then struggled upright and went back to the infirmary.

He was annoyed with himself for having waited. By the time he reached the beds he was annoyed with Orest, which was worse.

Markus had visitors. Two men stood beside his table with a rolled plan, and a woman was asking him about a room which, from her description, Lerris doubted Markus had ever entered. His brother looked exhausted. Lerris opened his mouth to dismiss them.

“Under the west sill,” Markus said. “Not the cupboard. You have to lift the sill board.”

The woman put a finger on the plan.

“Here?”

“Yes. If it hasn’t been moved.”

She thanked him. One of the men began another question, but she touched his arm and they left together. Markus watched them through the door.

“What was that?” Lerris asked.

“An old valuation. They’re trying to find which tables can be moved.”

“Couldn’t someone else do it?”

“Apparently not.”

“You need to rest.”

“I’ve been lying down all day.”

Lerris sat. The clean shirt he had been given pulled tight across one shoulder. Nothing fitted; nothing stayed where he wanted it; and Markus was looking at him with the patient expression that had infuriated him long before either of them came to Thepyrgos.

“Did Orest come back with you?” his brother asked.

“No.”

Markus reached for his water. Lerris passed it before he could manage, spilling a little on the table.

Three mornings later, the east court filled with furniture.

Lerris watched from the infirmary steps. He had permission to walk that far without someone beside him, and had made it into an expedition by bringing a book. There was nowhere comfortable to put his leg. The stone had held the night’s cold. He stayed because the alternative was another morning listening to someone breathe painfully behind him while he pretended to read.

The tables came first, carried upside down. Then the stools, tied in bundles, and a desk that would not pass through the arch until its drawer had been removed. The drawer contained a nest of old papers, a spoon, and three coins. A brief and lively dispute followed over the coins. Someone claimed the spoon. Nobody wanted the papers.

Classes were to resume in the southern rooms the following day. The valuation Markus had found allowed several pieces of furniture to be identified as university property rather than a master’s private purchase. This had not pleased everyone. A thin man followed his desk across the court protesting that ownership of the wood did not confer ownership of the arrangement of his drawers.

Lerris laughed. The man heard and gave him a look so wounded that he turned the laugh into a cough, too late.

Phene arrived with Dasken’s bag over one shoulder. She stopped at the bottom of the steps and regarded the procession.

“They’ll put the copying tables under that roof,” she said.

“Is that bad?”

“It leaks on the left.”

“I could tell someone.”

“I have.”

She put the bag down. It made a heavier sound than Lerris expected.

“What’s in it?”

“His work. What was out here. He wants to see what can still be delivered.”

“Surely they aren’t asking him—”

“He asked me.”

She rubbed the mark the strap had left on her shoulder. Lerris shifted his book, making a place beside him. She looked at the stone and remained standing.

“They’ve promised to pay him for the week,” she said. “That’s something. But there are people waiting for things he was copying before he went in. They aren’t all rich. Some of them have paid for lodging while they wait.”

“Can somebody finish them?”

“Yes. If somebody knows where he stopped.”

He watched a stool roll loose from its bundle. A boy chased it towards the fountain and caught it by one leg.

Phene picked up the bag. “If you’re coming, bring the papers on the bench. Those are his too.”

Lerris put his book beneath his arm and took the little stack. The top sheet contained a beautifully drawn initial, followed by a line that had been crossed out so thoroughly the paper had begun to tear. He kept his thumb off it.

Dasken received the work with a concentration that made him look less ill. He found two sheets belonging to a different commission, sent Phene for a piece of charcoal, and began making marks on the wrappers. When he reached the damaged initial, his expression changed.

“That was before,” he said.

Phene nodded.

“Before what?” Lerris asked.

“Before I went in.” Dasken held the paper farther away. “I was making a mess of it then as well.”

He put it aside. Lerris had no answer, and was relieved when Phene asked about another sheet.

They worked for nearly an hour. Lerris fetched water, held a board, and eventually read aloud from one copy while Dasken checked another. The passage concerned the dimensions of a granary. There were no hidden meanings in it that he could see. Dasken corrected him twice on the same unit and, the third time, began saying it before Lerris reached the word.

“I’m not entirely incapable,” Lerris said.

“Then surprise me.”

Phene laughed into her sleeve.

When they finished, Dasken was trembling with fatigue. He had kept the damaged initial on his blanket. Phene took the other work away but left that sheet where it was.

In the court, somebody had found a cloth large enough to cover the copying tables. Its edge was weighted with stones. Lerris paused to watch the workers shake the folds straight. Rain had begun, so fine that he could see it only against the dark arch beyond them.

Chapter 37 — The Pages Between

Markus wanted his knife.

Lerris had put it in the drawer beneath the washstand, where it would not fall off the crowded bedside table. He remembered doing this. He could picture his hand placing it beside a twist of unused cord. When he opened the drawer, there was only the cord.

“Have you moved it?” he asked.

“If I’d moved it, I would have it.”

“It was here.”

Markus waited.

They had been given a small room off the infirmary for two more nights. Its beds were narrower than the ones they had left, but there was a door they could close. Lerris had welcomed the privacy until he discovered that sharing a room without interruptions gave his brother far more opportunity to object to him.

He searched beneath the washstand. Then the shelf. Then his own writing case, where he found the knife tucked into a fold of cloth.

“Here.”

Markus held out his hand.

“I was keeping it dry.”

“It was dry.”

“The basin—”

“Lerris.”

He put it on the table.

His brother picked it up, tested the edge with his thumb, and began trimming a pen. It was an ordinary little knife, the handle darkened by years of use. Lerris watched him turn it awkwardly to spare his injured side.

“You could have asked me to do that.”

“I asked you to give it to me.”

The shaving fell onto the blanket. Markus lifted it carefully and set it with the others.

Lerris went to the window. Across the court, a woman was airing bedding over a rail. She slapped a pillow twice and shook it with an energy he envied.

“You might remember I’ve been trying to help.”

“I haven’t forgotten.”

“It doesn’t sound like it.”

Markus put down the pen.

“For three days you’ve moved everything I put within reach. The cup is too near the edge. The bag is where someone might tread on it. The lamp will trouble my eyes. I wake and have to ask you where my own things are.”

“The cup nearly fell.”

“Then let me spill the water.”

Lerris turned back. “And when it’s something worse?”

The room went quiet.

Markus looked at him for a long time. Then he began brushing the pen shavings from the blanket into his palm.

Lerris wished he had said it more gently. He also wanted an answer. That want remained, ugly and stubborn, while Markus gathered the tiny curls one at a time.

“I don’t know,” his brother said.

He emptied his hand into the firebox, where there was no fire, and picked up the pen.

Lerris sat on his own bed. Outside, the pillow struck the rail again.

After a while Markus asked him to open the ink. The stopper had stuck. Lerris loosened it and returned the bottle to the place beside his brother’s right hand.

“Thank you.”

The letter was for Alayse. Markus had begun it twice, abandoned both openings, and now appeared determined to proceed with the third whatever its faults. He wrote slowly. Lerris could hear the pauses in the movement of the nib.

“Does she know?” he asked.

“I sent a short letter from the infirmary.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That I’d been hurt. That I was out.”

“Will she come?”

“I asked her not to set off before I could tell her where I’d be.”

Lerris nodded. He had wanted to send for her himself. He had even considered which messenger could be trusted with the road. Now he was glad he had done neither, though he suspected he would have felt equally justified if he had.

Markus wrote another line, frowned at it, and left it alone.

“Will you read something?”

“Of course.”

“Not this.”

He reached for the brown bag. Lerris remained seated while he drew it closer with his foot, then lifted it onto his knees. The surviving notebook came out wrapped in a cloth. Its spine had collapsed where the gatherings were missing.

Markus opened it at an ordinary inventory.

“I need to know if this account makes sense without the comparison.”

Lerris took the book. Near the gutter, cut threads protruded like pale hairs. He remembered the leaves separating under the knife, smoke gathering over Dasken’s face. Here the empty place looked small.

The inventory described pieces of a damaged table. A note referred to dimensions measured elsewhere. That reference led into the gap.

“No,” he said.

Markus nodded.

“Could you make the measurement again?”

“The table was broken up six years ago.”

“Oh.”

“There’s another on the next page.”

They went through five entries. Two stood on their own. One could be checked against a valuation in the public collection. The other two no longer had enough behind them to be used. Markus marked those lightly in charcoal. He did not cross them out.

Lerris found himself lingering over the marks. He had spoken of the burning in his statement as a decision, something his brother had done. He had not expected to sit with him afterwards over a table that no longer existed, unable to supply the width of one leg.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

Markus looked up.

“About the book.”

“Yes.” His brother touched the cut edge. “So am I.”

There was nothing hurried in the answer. Lerris let him turn the page.

They stopped when the light became too poor. Markus set the notebook beside his cup, in the place he had chosen for it, and asked Lerris to read back the portion of the statement concerning the rescue. Thorne had allowed him a copy of his own account. He fetched it from his case.

At the furnace, Markus interrupted.

“I was sitting on a sack.”

“What?”

“You’ve said I stood by the opening. I couldn’t stand that long. They put me on a sack of something very uncomfortable.”

Lerris changed it.

“And I didn’t know the mark was wrong. I suspected it. Thorne checked.”

“That’s farther down.”

“Good.”

They continued. Markus did not object to the burning, or to Lerris’s inability to explain what it had achieved. At the broken door he asked where Arvel had been standing. Lerris showed him with the cup and the ink bottle.

His brother moved the cup a little.

“Here?”

“Yes.”

Markus considered the space between them. “You shouldn’t have gone back under it.”

“Probably not.”

He expected more. Markus only touched the edge of the paper, holding it flat while Lerris made the correction about the sack.

Later they ate supper from the same tray because there was nowhere else to put it. The fish had bones in it. Markus complained that Lerris was taking all the sauce, and Lerris told him he was welcome to the skin. They divided the last piece of bread with the little knife.

Chapter 38 — The Man in the Account

The missing labourer had a sister.

Doreta learned this from a carter who remembered carrying lime to the university when the west rooms were repaired. The carter had not known the man well. He remembered his sister because she had come twice to ask whether her brother had gone away with a work party, and because the second time she had brought him food, apparently believing he might be more willing to remember after eating.

He had eaten. He had remembered nothing useful.

The sister had since left the city. A neighbour thought she had gone to live with a married daughter along the coast. There was no address, only the name of a harbour and a description of the daughter’s husband, who had repaired nets and walked with a shortened step.

Lerris listened to this in the porter’s room. Doreta had laid the replies beside the old repair account. The missing man’s name appeared there in an abbreviated hand; two readers had supplied different endings. She had left both versions on the enquiry.

“Thorne has sent to the harbour,” she said. “Someone may know.”

“What if she comes?”

“Then we’ll have to tell her what we found.”

“We haven’t found him.”

“I know.”

A kettle breathed beside the fire. Lerris had not been invited to sit, but after a moment Doreta pushed a stool towards him with her foot. He lowered himself carefully. His calf was better until he forgot it.

The room had acquired a second rack for keys. Each hook bore a little tag. The north-range keys hung separately inside a locked box, and the box itself was fastened to the wall. Orest had done the fastening. Lerris recognized the neat countersunk heads, though he could not have said when he had begun noticing such things.

“Has Orest gone back to the bridges?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Is he well enough?”

“He says so. He’ll be checking stonework, not carrying it.”

Lerris watched the kettle. He had come partly to ask about the labourer and partly because he hoped to meet Orest without having to send a message. Doreta probably knew. He was tired of being easy to read to everyone except himself.

“The university will pay the messenger?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“And if she needs to travel?”

“That hasn’t been settled.”

He reached for his purse, then stopped with his hand inside his coat. Doreta noticed the movement.

“You can offer,” she said. “Don’t put money down as if we’ve agreed.”

“I’ll offer to Thorne.”

“Good.”

She took the kettle off before it boiled over.

On the way out he met Aqyl beneath the covered walk.

The tutor carried nothing. Lerris had become accustomed to seeing him with a book, a key, something that appeared to account for his presence. Empty-handed, he looked almost like a man waiting for a friend.

“They’ve found a possible relative,” Lerris said.

“So I understand.”

“Did you know him?”

“No.”

“Were you here?”

Aqyl looked towards the porter’s door. “I did not see him brought in. I did not see him leave.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No.”

Lerris felt an anger so fresh that for a moment it was almost pleasurable.

“You could answer one ordinary question.”

“I have answered the captain’s questions.”

“Then answer mine.”

People passed at the end of the walk. A pair of students were carrying a board between them, debating which way to turn it at the stair. Aqyl waited until they had gone.

“I knew of the account before your brother found it.”

“How long before?”

“Long enough that I should have had it brought out.”

Lerris gripped his stick. “And did you tell Thorne that?”

“Yes.”

He wanted to disbelieve him. It would have made the conversation easier to continue.

“Why didn’t you?”

“I thought keeping people away from the room was sufficient.”

“You left the key.”

“Yes.”

Aqyl’s face gave him little. Lerris remembered a cup on a table, the composure with which this man had watched Markus, the occasional precise attention that had seemed worth any discomfort required to obtain it. He could not decide how much of that judgment had been his brother’s and how much he had borrowed because it pleased him to belong near it.

“Are you leaving?” he asked.

“My access has been withdrawn.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No. I have other work here.”

A bell sounded from the southern rooms. Aqyl turned his head towards it, then back.

“Your brother did well.”

“Tell him.”

“I intend to.”

“Ask first.”

The tutor inclined his head. Lerris went past him before he could think of something else to say.

At the foot of the infirmary stair, Orest was lifting a small chest onto a handcart. He saw Lerris and continued fastening it. There was room to pass. Lerris stopped anyway.

“Your mother said you’re going tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“Will you be gone long?”

“A month. Perhaps more.”

Orest pulled the strap through its buckle. The leather creaked.

“I waited outside,” Lerris said.

“After the statement? Thorne told me.”

“Oh.”

“I had to show him the fastening.”

“I thought you might have gone another way because—”

“I did go another way.”

They looked at one another. Lerris heard a laugh start somewhere in his own throat and suppressed it. Orest’s expression altered slightly, not enough to make the exchange easy.

“I shouldn’t have given you my word about Markus,” Lerris said.

“No.”

“I wanted him left alone.”

“You had him left alone.”

Lerris looked at the chest, at the rolled blanket tied above it. Orest would be sleeping somewhere with different noises tomorrow night. He envied him until he remembered the bridges.

“Will you write?”

“To my mother.”

“Of course.”

Orest tested the strap once more.

“If you want to know how I am, you can write to me.”

“Where?”

“I’ll leave the direction with her.”

Lerris nodded. Orest took the cart handles, lifted, and winced.

“Your hand?”

“Still stiff.”

“Shall I take one side?”

Orest considered him, including the stick.

“You can open the gate.”

Lerris went ahead. The latch lifted cleanly. He held the gate wide while Orest manoeuvred the cart over the raised stone, one wheel and then the other, taking his time.

Chapter 39 — The Things Returned

They laid the recovered things on the tables in the east court.

There were fewer than Lerris expected. The guarded room had seemed crowded when they were trapped in it; here, with each object separated from its neighbour, much of the space remained bare. A chair with a broken stretcher. A tray discoloured by fire. The pale cup, cracked from rim to foot but still in one piece. Several packets had been put inside plain wooden boxes, their original wrappers visible through the open lids.

The guard checked Lerris’s written permission before letting him approach. He had obtained it from Thorne that morning. Markus’s was folded with it, though Markus had chosen not to come.

“I know what my bag looks like,” his brother had said.

“The bag’s with you.”

“Then I have even less reason.”

Lerris had almost argued. Now he was glad to be alone. He wanted time to look at the things without having to pretend he had come only to identify them.

Sothel stood at the far table with Phene. She was examining the wrapped account Aqyl had nearly taken. A clerk had opened the outer binding in their presence; beneath it were ordinary folded sheets, some brittle at the edges, one patched with a scrap of different paper.

“That’s ours,” she said.

“Your hand?” Sothel asked.

“My predecessor’s. Those are the payments for completed copies.”

Lerris came closer.

Phene turned a leaf by sliding a flat piece of wood beneath it. Against the entries ran little marks, some strokes and some circles. One page had been totalled twice, the second sum smaller.

“Why was it in there?” he asked.

“No idea.”

“Does it say anything about the missing man?”

“This is copying work.”

“I know. But if there were payments—”

“I’ll look.”

She did not say it unkindly. That made him hear the impatience in his own question. He stepped back enough to give her light.

For several minutes there was only the sound of paper lifted and laid down. Someone on the other side of the court was trying a newly hung door. Each time it struck the frame he shaved a little wood from the edge and tried again. Phene stopped at an entry, smiled faintly, and went on.

“What?” Lerris asked.

“A man who never paid until we threatened to keep his copy. He’s marked paid here. I would have liked to see that.”

He looked at the little circle. A person had waited for money, received it, and made the mark. He wanted to ask whether the person was still alive. Instead he watched Phene examine the next page.

There was no mention of the labourer. There were no instructions. Sothel had the clerk note the result without declaring the packet harmless. It would remain apart from the usable records until they knew more about where it had been.

Lerris went to the chair.

The wire round its joint was dark with old handling. Markus had remembered it before anything else. He rested two fingers on the back, then took them away. The chair had no particular effect on him. He felt foolish for having tested it, and still did not want to sit in it.

“Do you recognize everything?” Thorne asked.

The captain had arrived behind him without making the guard stand aside. His sleeve had been mended at the shoulder with thread that did not quite match.

“I think so.”

“Think, or do?”

“The objects. I can’t speak for what’s inside all the wrappers.”

Thorne marked his list.

“What will you do with them?”

“Keep them where people can’t borrow them.”

“And the room?”

“Keep it shut.”

Lerris looked towards the north range. From the court its upper windows appeared unchanged. A bird landed on one sill and began working at something between the stones.

“Is that enough?”

“No one has told me how to do more safely.”

The captain followed his gaze.

“We’ve blocked the service approaches. The men doing it stayed in sight of one another. Sothel has the measurements. If anything shifts, I want to know before someone discovers a new shortcut.”

“What about the figure?”

“Your account is with the enquiry.”

That answer was too small for the thing Lerris remembered. He could hear how often he had asked the same question, altering the words just enough to allow himself to ask it again.

Thorne put the list down.

“I’ve no body to recover. No living person I can reach. I have men who have been here through two nights and have other duties. I’m leaving a watch. I’m not sending them through that masonry on a guess.”

“I could go.”

“No.”

“I’ve been there.”

“So have I.”

The captain picked up his list. Lerris stayed beside the chair.

At the end of the table stood the tray in which Markus had burned his pages. A curl of blackened paper remained caught beneath the rim. There was no writing visible on it. Lerris leaned close enough to be certain, then straightened without touching it.

When he left, Phene was laughing at another entry. Sothel bent to see what she had found. Lerris could have gone back and asked. He continued through the arch instead.

Chapter 40 — An Unfinished Game

Caldris brought fruit and a board.

The fruit was bruised. He defended it before either brother objected, explaining that ripe fruit could not reasonably be expected to survive being dropped by a porter, and that the porter had had no business insisting on taking it from him in the first place. He set the basket on the table and began laying out the pieces.

Markus examined a pear.

“Did he drop the board?”

“No.”

“A pity. You could have blamed the last game.”

Caldris looked at him. Then he smiled, slowly, and took the pear away to cut out the damaged part.

They were back in Markus’s rooms. Pellant had allowed the short journey on the condition that they used a cart, which Lerris had hired without asking whether his brother preferred to walk. Markus had objected to the cost rather than the decision. His landlord had moved a chair nearer the window and charged nothing for doing it, a kindness Markus appeared to find more difficult to discuss than the cart.

Istren arrived after her teaching. She had brought a cushion she said she did not want returned until she asked for it. Lerris thanked her without promising to call the next day. She put it behind Markus herself, after asking where he wanted it, and sat near the window with a piece of fruit.

For a while the four of them occupied the room as they once had. Caldris described a pupil who had attempted to conceal a lost argument by translating it into a language his opponent did not know. Istren supplied the opponent’s subsequent remarks. Markus laughed and hurt himself doing it. They waited while he recovered, and then Caldris repeated the least respectable part because he had not heard it properly.

Lerris caught Istren watching his brother. The concern in her face was plain. It passed when she saw him looking, but she did not apologize for it or offer him reassurance.

“Will you play?” Caldris asked Markus.

“Not well.”

“That will be a change for one of us.”

They began.

Lerris had forgotten the small sounds of a game: a piece put down firmly, another slid rather than lifted, the edge of a sleeve brushing wood. Outside, the street was busy with an ordinary late delivery. Men called to one another beneath the window; a wheel caught in a rut, was freed, and went on. Markus rested between moves with his eyes on the board.

There was nothing wrong with the pieces. Lerris nevertheless counted them when Caldris turned to answer Istren. He hated discovering himself doing it. He finished the count before he could stop.

Markus made a poor move.

Caldris looked at him, then took the offered piece. No one said anything. Two moves later Markus saw what he had done and swore softly.

“Do you want it back?” Caldris asked.

“No.”

“Good.”

The game continued. Lerris relaxed enough to eat the piece of pear that had been softening in his hand.

Later, Markus lost track of a position he had understood a moment before. He bent towards the board, frowned, and sat back. Caldris waited. Istren began talking to Lerris about the new room, asking whether he remembered a bookseller who had once offered a set of cheap stools. He answered too quickly.

Markus moved at last. Caldris did not answer it immediately. He studied the position with the same absorption he had given it before, and Lerris could not tell whether he was sparing his opponent or had found a difficulty of his own.

After another few turns Markus put a hand flat on the table.

“I’ve had enough.”

“We can leave it,” Caldris said.

“I may not remember it tomorrow.”

“I will.”

He began sketching the position on a scrap of paper, adding the damaged corner of the board so it could not be read the wrong way round. Markus watched, then corrected one piece. Caldris erased it with his thumb and drew it again.

They ate what remained of the fruit. Istren left first, with an appointment to keep and no invitation for Lerris to accompany her. At the door she asked Markus whether he wanted a visitor the following week.

“I hope to be away.”

“Then write.”

“I will.”

She touched his shoulder and went down the stairs.

Caldris wrapped the pieces, leaving the board against the wall. Before he left he handed Lerris the drawing.

“Don’t improve it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You looked tempted.”

He went out smiling.

Lerris set the drawing beside Markus’s notebook. His brother had closed his eyes, but he was not asleep.

“Did you enjoy it?” Lerris asked.

“Yes.”

He waited for a qualification. None came.

After a little while he began clearing the fruit skins. Markus asked him to leave the window open. The air was cooling and would soon be too cold, but Lerris left it as it was.

Chapter 41 — Before the Cart Came

The reply from the coast arrived on the morning Markus was to leave.

The woman they had sought was dead. Her daughter still lived near the harbour, and had answered the enquiry in a hand steadier than the messenger’s covering note. She remembered her uncle, though she had been young when he disappeared. Her mother had believed for years that he might return. There had been a shirt kept for him. The daughter did not know what had become of it.

Doreta brought the letter to Lerris at the gate. He had gone there to make certain the hired cart would be admitted to the courtyard, and had found himself explaining its expected arrival to Orest’s replacement with a precision that made the young man impatient.

“It’s a cart,” the porter said. “If it comes for your brother, I’ll send it through.”

Then Doreta appeared with the folded sheet.

The daughter wanted to know why the university was asking now.

Lerris read that sentence twice. Beneath it she had written the name in full, settling the uncertainty in the repair account. There were questions about whether anything had been found and whether somebody should come. She did not ask for money.

“Has Thorne seen this?”

“He sent it over.”

“Who will answer?”

“He will. With the copy of the old account.”

“And what I saw?”

“He’ll say what you saw.”

Lerris folded the sheet along its creases. The person at the far door remained grey sleeve and hand, less substantial in daylight than the daughter’s careful writing. He had wanted a name. Now there was one, and somebody would have to receive a letter that did not tell her where her uncle was.

“I offered to pay for a journey,” he said.

“I told him.”

“If she wants to come.”

“Yes.”

Doreta took the letter back. Before she went she asked whether Markus had something to eat on the road.

“He says he has.”

“Look.”

Lerris laughed, unexpectedly. She smiled at him, tiredly but without reserve, and went to speak to the new porter.

Markus had packed too much.

Two bundles stood by the door and a third lay open on the bed. Lerris recognized the garden book, a spare pair of shoes, letters folded inside a cloth, the notebook with its missing gatherings. The little knife lay beside them. Markus was trying to fit a shirt around the shoes without bending the book.

“Put that in mine,” Lerris said.

“You aren’t coming all the way.”

“I can carry it to the cart.”

Markus handed him the book.

He would travel the first stretch with hired company, then take the road on which he expected to meet Alayse. The arrangements had been made by letters which Lerris had not read. He knew where his brother meant to stop and who had been asked to meet him. He did not know whether all of it would happen as planned. Markus appeared to regard that as an ordinary feature of travel.

Lerris found the food in a covered bowl. There was bread, cheese, and a pear that had escaped Caldris’s visit.

“Enough?” Markus asked.

“Yes.”

“Did Doreta send you?”

“Yes.”

“Tell her I submitted to inspection.”

They finished the bundles together. The notebook went into the brown bag, where Markus could reach it. Lerris put the game drawing inside the garden book and told him where it was.

From below came the scrape of wheels. Neither moved at once.

“Will you stay here?” Markus asked.

“For a little while.”

“Doing what?”

Lerris looked at the empty space on the table.

“I haven’t decided.”

His brother fastened the bag.

“Don’t spend all of it in the north court.”

“I can’t get in.”

“You can stand outside.”

He could. He had already done so more often than he wished Markus to know.

“I’ll find something.”

Markus stood carefully. They went down slowly, Lerris carrying the heavier bundle and stopping when his brother stopped, without asking each time whether he needed to. In the courtyard the driver had put a folded blanket on the seat. Markus thanked him. Lerris checked that the step was sound, then held the bundle while his brother climbed.

There was no suitable way to embrace him without hurting his side. Lerris settled for his hand, and found himself holding it too tightly.

“Write when you arrive.”

“I will.”

“And if—”

“I will.”

He let go.

The cart went through the gate. Its wheel jolted over the raised stone; Markus put a hand against the side and looked back with an expression of mild accusation, as though Lerris had arranged the bump. Lerris grinned at him. Then the cart turned and there was only the sound of it among the other wheels.

He remained long enough to earn a glance from the new porter, then went upstairs for his own things.

The room looked larger. There was a pale rectangle on the table where Markus’s papers had kept off the dust, and a shaving from the penknife beneath the chair. Lerris picked it up, rolled it between his fingers, and dropped it into the cold firebox.

His own writing case was still packed. He opened it and took out a sheet.

Orest’s direction lay inside the lid. Lerris copied it onto the outside fold before beginning. He wrote about the university tables, the leaking roof, Dasken’s impatience with his reading. He told him Markus had left. He did not describe the conversation at the cart.

Halfway down the page he began a sentence about the study, crossed it out, and asked about the bridges instead. He wanted to know whether the stonework had survived the rains. He remembered Orest saying something about the way water worked behind a facing, but not enough to ask intelligently, and admitted that.

There was space left. He described Caldris dropping the fruit himself, which he had begun to suspect was what had actually happened. That was unfair and probably true. Orest would decide.

When the ink had dried, he folded the letter and set it ready to send.

Beneath it in the case lay his copy of the rescue account. He drew it out and found the passage about Markus sitting beside the furnace. The correction was plainly visible. He read on through the old cupboard mark, the pages burning, his brother crossing the narrow gap with one arm held against his side. At the end there was the figure. He had written no more than he had seen.

From the courtyard came the new porter’s voice, asking someone to wait while he checked a room.

Lerris read the last paragraph again.

Then he put the account beneath the letter, closed the case, and took his letter down to find someone who could carry it to the bridges.
