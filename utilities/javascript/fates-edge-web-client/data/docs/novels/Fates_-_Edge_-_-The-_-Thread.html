The Thread

Being the second account of Riarhin, sometime scout of the Fenwood army, from the hills above Cair to the edge of the Red Wastes; with the usual difficulty about what she was actually looking for.

Sequel to The Last Blade, and in one particular to No Bruises.

The soup is always free. The price never is. — An ancient proverb, said at the banking of the fires

Prologue: The Huntress

Farrick prayed for wind. He prayed every day for a stout breeze. It was late autumn, the cold was wet and went through you like knives, down to the bone. He had been cold, and miserable, and wet for a fortnight, and still he prayed for wind.

They were held up in an abandoned mill. Its gray, weathered wood was covered in rose vines and thorns that looked black in the moonlight. Six boys left out of twenty. Young, frightened, cold, each of them praying for wind.

It was a week after the hamlet. It hadn’t been burned when they found it. The Rothari wanted to send a message. The hamlet hadn’t paid its dues in two months. Normally Farrick would drown a bridge or burn the wheat and let them starve slowly. But it had gone on too long, and the country had gotten lax, and a message was to be sent.

And Farrick was ever the faithful messenger.

Twenty boys, green, given a purpose in the Rothari they never had in whatever mudflat they crawled out of. They pillaged. They took what they wanted, and whoever they wanted. Laughter, fire, whiskey, and piss-poor ale off the brewer’s widow. That was the last joy they had.

The first one died in the river valley. They had been following the shore—the river was low, and the round rocks were easier walking than the game trails in the hills. That was a mistake. The valley was wooded, and steep.

The arrow took him in the throat. There was no wind; the air was still and cool. There was no sound until the choke and the wet gurgle. His legs faltered and he dropped like a doll. Dead soon enough. It was a cruel shot.

They formed a circle. Two more boys: an eye, a leg. Farrick sent six into the hills to find whoever it was. He found their bodies that night by torchlight. In the morning, three more were dead—throats cut in their sleep. Nobody saw anything but “perhaps a shadow.”

Perhaps a shadow.

They found a wheelhouse that night that had turned lumber, once. It was old; the valley had been emptied by plague two centuries ago. It should not have been standing. The slatted walls were failing, but it was shelter. Or so he thought.

He woke to smoke. Someone had fired the thatch. They fled. They should have found buckets and made a try of it, but they fled. He tried to pull them into some kind of order; they scattered anyway, shadows in the dark. Two fewer shadows came back, and one was left screaming somewhere behind them.

And then silence.

The rules were simple. Low fires. No prattle. Move when the wind rises.

They moved.

Shack to shack, hamlet to hamlet, tracing the river. The shadow took a man a day. Just one. And maimed another. They began leaving the maimed ones behind—alone and scared, to the shadow’s mercy.

Now they were out of shelter, backed into a lazy eddy at the foot of a bluff. In an old mill that had quit turning when the river changed course.

No fires.

No sound.

Just the smell of piss and fear.

“Farrick,” a voice called from the hills. A woman’s voice. Close. Too close. He did not answer.

“I’d promise to spare your boys, but that’s a lie.”

Anger rose in him, and rage took what sense he had.

“Who are you? What do you want?” he called. Brigands? Why send a woman?

“It’s obvious,” the voice said, not kindly. Then silence.

Then he knew. Not a woman. The woman. She had been haunting these hills for months. A flash of dull auburn, a glimpse of green cloth. Elf-touched, and carrying fire from some distant hell.

“If your quarrel is with me, I’ll face you. Leave my boys.”

“How noble.” The voice mocked him, closer still. “I’ll come to you.”

“Fire! Torches!” he cried, but it was too late. The sound of broken thatch. A shadow dropped into the room. A knife took a boy in the shoulder; a thin dull sword arched and took another. Three rushed her. Two shrank back.

Green fools.

The three went down like wheat before a scythe. He didn’t see it clearly. Then it was him, two boys who’d wet themselves, and her between them and the door. And she took her time.

“I yield!” he cried, lifting his sword. She stopped, and took a stance. “Mercy!” He threw it aside.

“Pick it up. You are beyond my mercy.” She waited.

He reached. The world went black as he lunged for the blade. And then—cold.

Act I

Chapter One: Half in Advance

The tavern was loud enough to hide in.

Riarhin sat at the bar, in the shadow of the stair, with a whiskey she hadn’t paid for yet and a wet sack on the floor beside her stool. She had been there an hour. She would be there another.

She did not look at the door. She had learned a long time ago that looking at the door is a thing people notice.

Kolmar was neutral ground. That was the whole of its economy — a town on the Acasi side of the old border, where the Rothari kept a house and the Viterran interests kept a factor and neither of them kept a garrison. Neutral ground in Acasia meant you could sit in a tavern with a Rothari’s head in a sack and nobody would touch you for it until you were outside the walls.

In Acasia, that was as close to law as law got.

She intended to be outside the walls by morning.

The three came in the way Rothari men came in anywhere — loud, and slow, and certain the room would make way. It did.

Riarhin watched them in the mirror behind the bar, which was cracked and flyspecked and did the work well enough. At the edge of the mirror, in the shadow of the doorframe, a very large man leaned against the jamb with his arms crossed and did not move.

Na’ar had been mistaken for the tavern’s muscle three times since they sat down. He had not corrected anybody. He found it funny, and Riarhin found it useful, and neither of them had discussed it.

The Rothari took a table. They took a bottle. They looked for someone to bully, because that was the order of the evening, and found her.

One of them noticed her first. Elbow, nod, grin. The other two turned.

They saw the hair first — dull auburn, red enough to catch the lamplight and wrong for the room. Then the cloth, green, country-cut, nothing a woman in Acasia would wear unless she wanted to be looked at.

Then the eyes.

Green, or gray, or something between, depending on the light and the angle. Hazel at the edge, if you were close enough to see the edge. And near the iris, where the color should have gone quiet, it went the other way — a thin flare of gold, like a coal breathed on, like something behind the eye had turned its head and looked back.

They saw it. She watched them see it.

The big one — rings, good boots, the kind of man who has never once been told no by somebody smaller — put his hand on the bar beside her cup and leaned in.

“Evening.”

She hated Acasi. Silkstrand had given it a music and the hills had taken the music back out, left it flat and blunt and Vilikari in the bones, and she had never once heard it spoken without hearing both places at once.

She drank her whiskey and did not answer.

“Wet sack.” He nodded at the floor. “What’s in it?”

She set the cup down.

“Farrick. Or at least his head.”

The man went still. Then his face did the thing she had seen before and always enjoyed — the half-second where the body hasn’t caught up to the news yet, and the rage arrives just behind the words.

“He was my kin,” he hissed through his teeth.

One of the others leaned in and said something in his ear. The big man shrugged him off like a coat.

At the doorframe, Na’ar shifted his weight. It was a small movement. It was not a nervous movement. It was the movement of a man deciding he was not needed and hoping that changed.

Riarhin lifted two fingers at the barmaid.

“Round. On me.”

The maid went. The men did not sit — not yet — but they did not leave either, and that was the tell. They stood with their hands loose and their weight forward and no notion of what they were waiting for.

Riarhin picked up her cup and drank. She looked at the big one over the rim. She did not blink.

It took about a minute. She watched it happen. Rage, then doubt, then the slow cold arrival of the arithmetic: she was sitting, she was calm, she had a sack on the floor with a Rothari’s head in it, and she had just bought them a drink.

By the time the barmaid came back with the bottle and three cups, his face had gone from rage to unease and had settled there.

The maid set the bottle down and got out of the way. She had done this before, or she was smarter than she looked, or both.

“Drink.”

The big man looked at her. He looked at the sack. Something moved in his face — stunned, then confused, then angry all over again, and this time it was the kind of angry that does something.

He shifted his weight.

The man beside him put a hand on his shoulder.

Riarhin smiled.

“Drink.”

The smile went away.

She kicked the sack. It moved, heavy, and settled, and something inside it knocked against the floorboards.

“Or join your kin.”

They drank.

They poured with shaking hands and they drank, all three, and she watched them do it, and she did not touch her own cup.

The door opened.

Aryka Everblood came in like a woman who had bought the building and was considering whether to keep it.

She was tall, gray at the temple, nearer fifty than forty and carrying it the way a soldier carries a scar — without comment. She wore good plain clothes cut by someone who knew her measurements, and a sword she had used, and a signet on her right hand that she did not bother to hide.

The purse was on her, of course. It was always on her. Riarhin had known Aryka Everblood for two years and had never once seen her hand a purse to a servant or a factor or a guard. The woman carried her own money the way other people carried a weapon: because the moment you let someone else hold it, you had given something away.

She walked in, took three steps, and stood where she could see the whole room. The four men with her spread out behind her without being told. They were not her guards. Riarhin knew the look of guards. These were men who worked for her and knew what happened to men who did not.

The room noticed. The room had been noticing Aryka Everblood for eleven years.

Her eyes found Riarhin first. Then the three Rothari. Then the sack. Then Na’ar, at the doorframe, and something flickered across her face — recognition, and a dry, private amusement, and something else that Riarhin did not bother to name.

She had followed the bodies. Riarhin could see it in the way she stood. Aryka Everblood had walked into Kolmar behind a trail of dead Rothari boys, and she had done it with a smile, because that was what she did. Cold, mechanical, unhurried. The hatred was not hot in her. It had not been hot in years. It had been filed down and oiled and put to work.

“What’s the affair?”

The Rothari men had gone rigid. The big one’s hand had drifted halfway to his hip and stopped there, like a man who has just remembered where he is.

Riarhin did not look at her.

She lifted her cup.

“To family.”

They hesitated. Then they drank, because their hands were already on the cups, and because the alternative was on the floor.

Riarhin set her cup down and turned on the stool.

Then she switched.

“Aryka,” she said, in Vhasian — hill Vhasian, the vowels flat where the hills flatten them and the r rolled back in the throat. “You’ll need to buy your own.”

The three Rothari men looked at each other. They had not understood a word of it and they knew they hadn’t, and that was its own kind of message.

Aryka’s eyebrows went up a fraction of an inch.

And she answered in court Vhasian.

Not the same tongue at all, when you heard them side by side. Hers came out round and unhurried, every vowel finished, the r soft as a courtier’s bow — Vhasian the way it was spoken in a nursery in Aberiel, with a tutor in the room and a queen listening from the passage. “And if I don’t?”

Riarhin heard it and had to work to keep her face still.

Twenty years in the hills and she had forgotten that was a thing people could do. That a woman could stand in a tavern in Acasia with a sack of Rothari head on the floor and ask and if I don’t like she was declining a second cup of tea.

It was ridiculous. It was also, and she hated this, faintly charming.

Riarhin stood, slowly, the way a person stands when she has nowhere to be and wants everyone to know it. She reached down and took the sack by the neck, and lifted it, and held it at shoulder height so Aryka could see it properly — the wet dark of it, the weight, the shape.

And switched back.

“After the bounty.”

Aryka Everblood looked at the sack.

She looked at the three Rothari, still holding their cups, still not breathing right. They had become furniture. They knew they had become furniture. There was nothing they could do about it, and Aryka was enjoying that fact so much that she did not bother to hide it.

She looked at Riarhin, and at the gold in her eyes, and at the whiskey she still hadn’t paid for.

And she laughed.

Not a court laugh. Not a polite one. A real one, short and surprised and genuinely pleased — the laugh of a woman who has been in a lot of bad rooms and has just found something in this one worth the trip.

“You,” she said.

Still court Vhasian. Still round. Still a princess in a tavern, and either she couldn’t turn it off or she didn’t want to, and Riarhin genuinely could not tell which.

Then Acasi again, for the bar, as she pulled out the stool two down from Riarhin’s.

“Barkeep. Whiskey. And put it on my tab.”

Riarhin sat back down. She set the sack on the floor between their stools.

“Generous.”

“I’m paying for the round. I’m not paying for your whiskey.”

“You’re paying for the head. The whiskey’s a courtesy.”

“I don’t do courtesies.”

“No,” Riarhin said. “You don’t.”

Aryka took the whiskey the barmaid brought and drank it without ceremony. She did not look at the three Rothari, who were still standing at the bar with their cups, still not sure whether the evening was over, still furniture.

“Rulf,” she said, without turning. “You may go.”

The big one — Rulf — opened his mouth.

“Go,” Aryka said.

He looked at her. He looked at Riarhin. He looked at the sack on the floor.

He set his cup down and went. The other two followed him like the tide going out, and the door closed behind them, and the room got its noise back.

At the doorframe, Na’ar uncrossed his arms and settled against the jamb again. He had not moved during any of it. He had simply been there, and now he was still there, and Riarhin could feel him being amused from twelve feet away.

Aryka drank.

“You took Farrick,” she said. Not a question.

“I took Farrick.”

“Good.”

Riarhin shrugged. “He was in my way.”

Aryka raised an eyebrow this time.

Riarhin let a brief smirk touch her face before it went still again.

Aryka looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed again — shorter this time, more private, the laugh of a woman conceding a point she had already decided not to fight over.

“You’re a difficult woman, Riarhin.”

“I’ve been told.”

“By people who are still alive?”

“By people who are still alive.”

The fire cracked. Somewhere behind them a man was losing badly at dice and complaining about it in Acasi, and a woman was laughing at him, and the room went on being a room.

Aryka refilled both cups. She did it herself, which told Riarhin something — either she trusted the barmaid not to water it or she wanted her hands busy — and then she sat back and looked at the sack on the floor.

“How long did you carry that?”

“Six days.”

“And nobody stopped you.”

“Nobody would.”

Aryka nodded slowly. She was looking at Riarhin the way a woman looks at a tool she has just discovered is sharper than she thought.

“You know what he was,” she said. “Farrick. What he did at the hamlet.”

“I know.”

“And you know whose order he was acting on.”

“I know that too.”

“Then you know why I’m here.”

Riarhin drank. “You’re here to pay me.”

“Yes. And I’m here to pay you for the rest of them.”

Aryka leaned back. She had the look of a woman who had been waiting for this conversation for a while and was enjoying finally having it.

“There are four more,” she said. “The man who gave the order, and three hands who were at the hamlet with Farrick. I have a writ on all four. I have had the writ for eight months and I have spent those eight months discovering that my men are too loud, my name is too well known, and the hills above Cair do not care about either.”

“So you need someone quiet.”

“I need someone who can walk into those hills and come out with four heads.”

“Go on.”

“And I’ll pay well for it.”

Riarhin turned the cup in her fingers. She did not look at Aryka. She looked at the whiskey, and at the fire behind the bar, and at nothing at all.

“What’s the figure?”

“Two hundred crowns for the man. Fifty each for the hands. Paid on delivery, here, in Kolmar, where the money means something.”

Riarhin said nothing.

She did not need the money. That was the thing Aryka did not know, or had not yet decided to notice. Riarhin had little need for silver and Na’ar had silver enough from his last job. They had been living lean in the hills for two years because there was nothing in the hills worth buying. She did not need two hundred crowns. She did not need fifty.

But Aryka Everblood had asked her for something. And Aryka Everblood had asked her for something in Acasia, in the Acasi way, with a figure, like a woman buying a horse.

“Three hundred for the man,” she said. “Seventy-five for the hands. Half in advance.”

Aryka’s eyebrows went up. “Half in advance is not how this works.”

“Then just the four.” Riarhin made to stand, slowly. “And we’ll be on our way.”

Aryka gracefully made an idle gesture. Riarhin sat. Ever the princess.

“You don’t trust me.” she said.

“I don’t trust anybody,” Riarhin said. “You’re not special.”

Aryka looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached inside her coat and pulled out the purse, and counted coins onto the bar — not all of them, but enough, and the sound they made was a sound Riarhin had not heard in a while and had not missed.

“Half,” Aryka said. “In advance. And you bring me the man’s head first, before the others, because he’s the one I want, and I want him while he’s still frightened that the hands are dying.”

“Those weren’t the terms offered.”

“I’m changing the terms.”

Riarhin looked at the coins and sighed.

She took them.

“North road,” she said. “Three days. Bring your own horse and don’t bring your men.”

“I don’t take orders.”

“You’re not taking orders. You’re being given an itinerary.”

Aryka smiled. It was a real smile and it reached her eyes, which Riarhin had not expected, and she did not know what to do with it.

“I like you,” Aryka said. “That’s inconvenient.”

“It is.”

Riarhin stood, slowly, and picked up the sack, and slung it over her shoulder like a bag of meal. At the doorframe, Na’ar straightened and fell in behind her, and Aryka watched him do it — watched the way he moved, big and fast and unhurried, a man who had been doing this for a long time and would do it for a long time more.

“Ykrul,” Aryka said.

Na’ar looked at her.

“I’ve been to the Violet Steppe,” Aryka said.

“I’ve been to Aberiel,” Na’ar smirked. His voice was rough.

“Did you like it?”

“No.”

“Neither did I,” Aryka said, and it was a real answer and it landed, and Na’ar let out a deep laugh from his belly.

Riarhin looked back over her shoulder. The gold in her eyes caught the lamplight for just a moment.

“Three days,” she said. “The ford above Cair. Don’t be late. I don’t wait.”

“I know,” Aryka said. “That’s what I’m paying for.”

Riarhin walked out with Na’ar. The door closed behind them.

Aryka Everblood sat alone at the bar for a moment, with two cups and a bottle and the noise of the room coming back around her. She looked at the empty stool where Riarhin had been sitting. She looked at the coins still on the bar, and the ones that had gone into a coat pocket, and she did the arithmetic she had been doing all evening and arrived at the same answer she had started with.

She was grinning. She knew she was grinning. She had been grinning for the last ten minutes and had not been able to stop, and she was fifty years old and a princess of the blood and she had spent thirty years learning to control her face, and she could not stop.

“Barkeep,” she said. “Another.”

She did not pay for it. She put it on her own tab, and the barkeep, who had been keeping tabs in Kolmar for thirty years and knew exactly what that meant, wrote it down without a word.

Acasia, she thought. A head in a sack, a warlord buying drinks for the woman who delivered it. Oh, and a trail of dead. It was going to be a fun ride in the morning.

The fire cracked. The dice game went on. Somebody opened the door and let in the cold, and somebody else shouted at them to close it, and the night kept going the way nights do in a town that belongs to no one.

Chapter Two: The Ford Above Cair

Aryka came alone and she came early.

Riarhin had been on the rise above the ford since the middle of the afternoon, and she had chosen the rise because it gave her the road for two miles in the direction Aryka would come from and one mile in the direction she would not. She had watched a carter water four mules, a boy drive geese across and lose one, a man walk south with a door on his back. None of them had looked up.

Aryka rode into the water at the shallow end, let the horse drink, and then sat in the saddle looking directly at the place where Riarhin was lying.

“I can see your boot,” she said.

Riarhin looked at her boot. It was where she had put it, behind a stone, in shade.

“No you can’t.”

“No,” Aryka agreed. “But you’ve moved now.”

Riarhin came down the slope. She had been three days above Cair and had not spoken to anyone but Na’ar, who did not require it, and the sound of her own voice in Vhasian was briefly unfamiliar.

Aryka had brought one horse, a spare, a bedroll, and a bag that did not look large enough to hold what a woman of her sort would consider necessary. No men. She had done exactly as she had been told and had contrived to look as though she had thought of it herself.

“Where’s the Ykrul?”

“Behind you.”

Aryka did not turn around to check, which Riarhin marked.

Na’ar came down off the far bank a few minutes later, leading his horse because the stones were bad, and Aryka watched him the whole way in.

He was somewhere past forty-five and had been large for the whole of it. The grey had got into the beard and had not yet got into the arms. He moved like a man who had never once needed to hurry and who had learned a long time ago exactly how much of himself to spend.

Riarhin had made an honest estimate of him in the first tenday and had not revised it. With a sword, in the open, with room, she would lose. Not certainly and not quickly, but the arithmetic was against her, and the arithmetic was his feet and his reach. He knew where his weight was at every moment, which most large men never trouble to learn because they have never had to, and he could hold a blade a foot and a half further out than she could reach past it, and a foot and a half is the whole of it.

He knew all of that. It had never once come up.

They rode north until the light went, and then Aryka kept riding.

Riarhin had expected to stop. She had chosen a place to stop, a fold below a stand of thorn with water at the bottom of it, and she had been carrying the place in her head since noon the way she carried such places. At dusk she said so.

“There’s water at the bottom of the next fold.”

“Is there water at the top of the pass?”

“Yes.”

“Then we’ll use that.”

They came over the pass at full dark with the horses walking and the stones moving under them, and Aryka got down and unsaddled and lay in her cloak on the ground like a woman who had been doing it for thirty years, because she had.

Na’ar looked at Riarhin. Riarhin looked back at him. Neither of them said anything, and in the morning Aryka was up before either of them, sitting on a rock with her boots off, going over her feet with her thumbs for hot places before they became blisters.

It was a small thing. Riarhin had seen perhaps six people in her life do it without being told to.

“You’ve done this,” she said.

“Thirty-one years.” Aryka pulled the boot on. “Not lately. The knees have an opinion.”

“And the men you left in Kolmar?”

“Will be drunk for two days and useful on the third.” She stood, tested the foot, and found it acceptable. “They’re not for the hills. I told you that and I meant it and I would rather you believed me the first time, because we’re going to be doing this for a month and I dislike repeating myself.”

“So do I.”

“Good.”

The country above Cair was not empty; it was emptied.

Riarhin knew the difference and did not explain it. Empty ground has no paths in it. This ground had paths, and stock walls with the stone robbed out of the gaps, and one hamlet with the thatch fallen in and a plum tree still bearing beside the door. Somebody had taken the fruit. There were stones under the tree where they had thrown at the high branches.

She got down and looked at the stones.

“Four days,” she said. “Perhaps five. Two of them, one small.”

Aryka came up beside her and looked at the same stones and saw, Riarhin could tell, stones.

“You can have that from thrown rock?”

“I can have it from the grass where they stood and the grass where they didn’t.” She pointed, and Aryka followed the finger, and then did something Riarhin had not expected: she squatted down, put her hand flat where the grass had been pressed, and left it there until she had understood it.

“Say the rest.”

“They came from the south and left north and they weren’t afraid. They ate the plums here, in the open, in daylight.” Riarhin stood. “Which means nobody is living within a half-day of us who would have taken the tree first.”

“Which means the hamlet below is gone as well.”

“It means nobody has been hungry here since the spring.”

Aryka thought about it. She did not say good and she did not say how terrible. She said, “Then the men we want are being fed from somewhere that isn’t here,” and got back on her horse.

Riarhin found that she had been waiting to be complimented, and that not being complimented was better, and she was annoyed about both.

On the fourth day she left them.

She did not announce it. She had said, at the water, that the road bent east around a shoulder of rock and that she wanted to see the other side of the shoulder, and Aryka had said fine, and then Riarhin had not come back for a day and a half.

Na’ar built a fire, which he was not supposed to do, and cooked, which he was very good at, and answered Aryka’s questions about the Violet Steppe in his own time, and did not once suggest going to look.

When Riarhin came down off the rock at the second dusk, Aryka was sitting with her back against a stone and her sword across her knees, and she waited until Riarhin had eaten before she spoke.

“Don’t do that again.”

“I found the house.”

“I know you found the house. You have the look.” Aryka turned the sword over. “Don’t do it again without telling me how long. Not because I need to give you permission. Because if you are three days gone I have to decide whether you are dead, and I will decide wrong, and then I will do something expensive.”

Riarhin considered several answers. She had used all of them before on people with more right to hear them.

“Two days,” she said. “If I’m longer than two days, I’m not coming.”

“Thank you.”

“That isn’t an agreement about permission.”

“No,” Aryka said. “It’s an itinerary.”

Na’ar laughed into the fire.

The house was a stone tower with a hall built onto it, forty years newer than the tower and worse made, and it had eleven men in it and a woman who cooked and a boy who took the horses.

Riarhin lay on the slope above it for six hours and did not move.

This was the part nobody understood, and she had long ago stopped explaining it. Waiting was not the absence of the work. The work was the six hours. In the first hour she had the walls and the doors. In the third she had the rotation — two men, changed at what she judged to be the fourth hour of the watch, one of them always the same man, which meant he was either trusted or being punished. In the fifth hour the boy came out and emptied something and went back, and that told her where the kitchen drain ran, and where the ground would be soft, and that the boy was allowed out after dark.

In the sixth hour a man came out onto the step, looked at the hills for a while, and spat.

She had his face. She had his gait and the set of his shoulders and the particular way he put his weight on the outside of the left foot. She had the ring on his hand, and she would have it in a year, and in ten.

Then she came down off the slope and told Aryka that the man who had given the order was in the tower, and that it would take four days.

“Four days for one man?”

“Four days for one man and no fighting.”

Aryka looked at the hills, and at the dark where the tower was, and at Riarhin.

“And if I said I wanted it tomorrow?”

“Then you’d have eleven men and a boy,” Riarhin said, “and one of them would get out, and we’d spend the rest of the month on the hands.”

Aryka smiled slowly. It was the smile of a woman putting something away in a place she would be able to find it again.

“Four days,” she said.

Chapter Three: Under the Plum Tree

It took five.

On the second day the boy came out after dark to empty the slops and Riarhin was standing at the end of the drain where the ground was soft.

He was perhaps eleven. He had a bucket in both hands and he saw her and did not drop it, which was interesting, and did not shout, which was more interesting still.

“Whose are you?” she asked in Acasi.

He looked at the hills behind her. He was working out whether there were more of her.

“Tacen’s.”

“Tacen is the one with the ring.”

“Tacen is the one with the ring.”

“Are you his?”

The boy took a moment over it. Then he said, “I’m the horses’,” and Riarhin decided she was not going to have to do anything about the boy.

“Where do the horses’ hay come from?”

“Sedris. Cart every tenday, on Road.”

“And when did the cart last come?”

“Road before last.”

She gave him a copper, which he did not want and took anyway, because a boy who refuses money has to explain himself later.

“You didn’t see me.”

“I didn’t see you.”

“You did,” she said. “Tell them on Road. Not before.”

He thought about that and then he understood it, and she watched him understand it, and it was the last easy thing that happened at the tower.

The cart came on Road, which was the fifth day of the four she had promised, and Riarhin was under it.

She had walked six miles to Sedris, found the yard, found the carter, and established in one conversation about axle grease that he was paid by the load and not by the hour and that he stopped twice on the road up because the second hill was cruel to the mule.

Between the first stop and the second she got under the bed and put her back against the boards and her boots against the axle tree and rode up to the tower that way, with the sword left in the thorn above Sedris, because a sword on the back does not fit under a cart, and Toothpick in her boot where it always was.

The gate opened. The mule went through. The boy came for the mule.

Nobody looks under a cart they have been expecting for nine days.

She let the yard empty. Then she came out on the far side, into the space between the cart and the hall wall, and stood up, and walked in through the kitchen door like a woman who lived there.

The woman who cooked was at the fire. She turned around with a spoon in her hand and looked at Riarhin, and Riarhin put a finger against her own lips, and the woman looked at her for a long moment and then turned back to the fire and went on stirring.

Riarhin went up the stair.

Tacen was writing.

That was the thing she had not expected and would remember: a man at a table with a pen, in a cold room, doing sums in a ledger with his coat over his shoulders and his tongue between his teeth.

He looked up.

He had time to put his hand on the table. He did not have time to do anything else, because she had not come up the stair to talk to him, and Toothpick was already out of her boot and moving before he had finished turning his head.

He went over backwards with the chair.

She stood and listened. The house went on being a house. Downstairs a man laughed at something, and somewhere a door closed, and nobody came.

Riarhin looked at the ledger for a while.

It was a household book. Grain, hay, wages, a dispute about a horse. On the ninth page there was a list of eleven names with figures beside them and four of the figures had been struck through and written again higher, and the date beside the change was two weeks after the hamlet.

He had paid them more afterwards.

She tore out the page and put it inside her coat. Then she did the work she had come for, and wrapped it, and went out through the kitchen, and the woman at the fire did not turn round.

Riarhin stopped in the doorway.

“There’s a boy in the stable.”

The spoon kept moving.

“He’ll be blamed,” Riarhin said.

The woman stopped stirring. She did not look up, and she spoke to the pot.

“Then take him to the Sedris road and put him on the cart, and I’ll say the both of you went over the wall, and there’ll be nothing to blame him for.”

Riarhin went to the stable.

Aryka was standing when she came down out of the trees, which meant she had been standing for some time.

Riarhin put the wrapped thing on the ground between them and did not make a performance of it.

Aryka looked at it. Then she looked past Riarhin at the boy, who had stopped at the edge of the firelight and was holding the mule.

“What’s that?”

“That’s the horses’.”

Aryka’s eyebrows went up. Riarhin waited for the argument, and had three answers ready, and discovered that she needed none of them, because Aryka simply said, “Na’ar. Feed him,” and squatted down and began to unwrap the head to be certain of the face.

She was certain of it. She had been carrying the face for eight months.

Then she sat back on her heels and stayed there a while longer than the work required.

“Five days,” she said.

“Five days.”

“You said four.”

“The cart came on Road.”

Aryka laughed once, without much in it, and wrapped the head again.

“I have wanted this,” she said, “since the fourth of Maiora, and I find I have nowhere to put it.”

Riarhin sat down across the fire from her. The boy was eating with both hands and Na’ar was watching him eat with the satisfaction of a man watching weather improve.

“Put it in the sack,” she said. “It travels better.”

Chapter Four: The Hands

Aryka wanted them frightened first, and Riarhin gave her that, and afterwards was not sure it had been a kindness to anybody.

The three hands were at Vethric’s yard, eleven miles east, and they knew by the fourth day that Tacen was dead because a man came up from Sedris with the news and the news had a shape to it: no fight, no siege, nobody heard anything, the cook says two of them went over the wall. By the fifth day they had moved into the yard’s loft and set a watch. By the seventh they had begun to disagree about whether to go south.

Riarhin did not touch them for nine days.

What she did was smaller. She took a horse out of the paddock on the second night and left the gate open, and the horse came back on its own, and the men spent a morning deciding whether it had ever been gone. On the fourth night she cut one strap on each of four saddles, halfway through, from the inside. On the sixth she left a fire on the ridge where they could see it and sat a hundred yards from the fire watching the man who came to look at it.

On the eighth night one of the three walked out of the yard in the dark with a bag and did not tell the others.

Riarhin let him get four miles.

He was called Parrin and he had a wife in Sedris and he was crying before she asked him anything, which made it slower rather than faster.

She sat him down against a rock with his own knife on the ground between them where he could reach it, which is a thing that stops a man reaching for it.

“You were at the hamlet.”

“I was at the hamlet.”

“What did you do there?”

He told her. He took a long time about it and he did not make himself better than he had been, which she had not expected, and twice he stopped and said I don’t know why in a way that she believed.

When he had finished she asked him where the other two slept and which of them held the watch after midnight, and he told her that too.

Then he asked whether he was going to die.

“Yes.”

He put his face in his hands. After a while he asked if she would tell his wife it had been quick.

“I’m not going to Sedris.”

“Then—”

“I’ll tell the boy at the yard to say it was quick,” Riarhin said. “He’ll say it. And she’ll know he was told to say it, because everybody is always told to say it, and she’ll believe it anyway, because people do.”

Parrin thought about this. He seemed to find it better than nothing.

She let him say what he wanted to say first. It took a while. Then she drew Toothpick and did it behind the ear, from the side, while he was still talking, because a man who has finished talking has to be waited on and she had decided years ago that this was a cruelty she would not perform.

The other two she did in the loft on the tenth night, and it was not clean, and one of them got to the ladder.

He got to the ladder because the second one was awake when he should not have been, sitting in the dark with a bow across his knees and no fire and no light, which was better practice than either of them had shown in nine days. He put an arrow into the roof beam eight inches from Riarhin’s head and then she was across the boards and inside the length of the bow with Toothpick, and close work in a loft with hay on the floor is not a thing anyone plans.

She took a broken tooth out of it and a cut along the ribs that she did not notice until the morning.

The one at the ladder she shot from the loft door as he crossed the yard, which was a poor shot and took him through the hip, and she had to go down and finish it in the mud with the knife, with the dogs barking and a light going on in the farmhouse fifty yards away.

She was on the ridge before anyone came out.

Aryka looked at the ribs and then at Riarhin’s mouth.

“Open.”

“It’s a tooth.”

“Open.”

Riarhin opened her mouth. Aryka put a thumb on her jaw and turned her head towards the fire and looked in there with the frank disinterest of a woman looking at a horse.

“Back left. You’ll want that out before it goes bad.”

“It’ll grow.”

Aryka’s hand stopped.

She did not say anything. She let go of Riarhin’s jaw, sat back, and reached for the cloth and the flask, and began cleaning the cut along the ribs as though nothing had been said, and Riarhin let her, and neither of them came back to it that night or ever.

Na’ar put more wood on the fire.

The heads went into the sack and the sack went onto the spare horse, and they came down out of the hills on the second of Verrus with the rain coming sideways.

“Nine days,” Aryka said, at the ford.

“Nine days of nothing. One night of it.”

“Yes. I watched you do the nothing.” Aryka wiped her face with the back of her wrist. “That’s the part I paid for and I didn’t know it when I paid.”

Riarhin did not answer. Across the water Na’ar had got down and was leading his horse, because the stones were bad and he was the heaviest of them, and he was singing something in Ykrul with two notes in it.

“You could have taken the three in one night,” Aryka said. “The first night. Before they knew about Tacen.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“You asked for them frightened.”

Aryka was quiet for a stride or two.

“I did,” she said. “I’d forgotten I’d said it.”

“I hadn’t.”

Chapter Five: Paid in Full

Kolmar had not improved in a month.

They came in on the north road with the sack on the spare horse and the whole town knew before they reached the square, because a boy had run ahead, because a boy always runs ahead.

The Rothari house on the east side of the square had its shutters closed. Riarhin looked at the shutters on the way past and did not slow down.

Aryka paid her in the same tavern, at the same bar, in front of the same barkeep, and she did it loudly enough that the room could hear it and slowly enough that the room could count it. Three hundred for Tacen. Seventy-five each for the hands. Less the half already paid.

She laid the last of it out in stacks and pushed them across.

“Count it.”

“I don’t need to count it.”

“Count it anyway. I want the barkeep to see you count it.”

Riarhin counted it. It came to what it should have come to, which she had known from the sound.

“Done,” Aryka said.

“Done.”

And that was the commission finished, and the writ discharged, and the eight months of Aryka Everblood’s business in the hills above Cair concluded, and there was nothing further between them.

Aryka bought a bottle and did not leave.

The Rothari came on the second night, later than Riarhin had expected and earlier than Aryka had.

There were nine and they did it properly, for Rothari: two at the front, two into the yard, the rest up the lane from the east side of the square with the shutters they had been sitting behind still closed, so that nobody could say the house had sent anybody.

Riarhin was on the stair, which was where she had been sitting every evening since they arrived, for this reason.

She did not get to do anything.

Na’ar had been at the end of the bar for two hours with a cup and an expression of profound absence, and he had been mistaken for the tavern’s muscle eleven times in four days, and when the first man came through the door with a hatchet he set the cup down.

What happened then took perhaps forty seconds. Riarhin watched all of it from the stair with her bow across her knees and never put an arrow on the string.

He did not roar. That was the thing people got wrong afterwards in the telling, because a big man is supposed to roar. He was quiet, and he was fast in a way that had nothing to do with hurry, and he was delighted — she could see it plainly; the whole of his face had opened — and he used the room. He used the doorframe to narrow them and a bench to break the second man’s line and his own reach to keep three of them a foot and a half further out than they wanted to be.

Two got out of it. One of those was carried.

When there was nothing left to do he stood in the middle of the floor and breathed, with a hatchet cut along the outside of his forearm that he had not noticed yet, and looked round the room for more, and found none, and the disappointment on his face was entirely without irony.

Then he picked his cup up off the bar and found it had not been spilled, and was pleased about that as well.

The barkeep had got under the bar at the appropriate moment and came out from under it at the appropriate moment.

Aryka Everblood had not moved from her stool and had drawn nothing. She put money down for the furniture without being asked, and then sat looking at Na’ar for a while.

“Where did you find him?”

“In a tavern.”

“Doing that?”

“Drinking,” Riarhin said. “And then that.”

Na’ar came and sat two stools along and let her look at the arm.

“She did not find me,” he said. “I found her.”

“He did not.”

“I was drinking. She came and talked to me. I do not know why. I was not being interesting.” He turned the arm over so she could reach the far side of the cut, which was the only cooperation he had ever offered in these situations. “And I said: nice to meet you, I am Na’ar, you seem interesting. I am going to follow you.”

Aryka waited.

“That is all of it,” Na’ar said.

“And she said?”

“Nothing. She left.” He drank. “So I followed her.”

Riarhin tied off the bandage harder than the wound required.

Riarhin had expected the leaving to happen the next morning.

It did not happen the next morning. It did not happen on the day after, when Aryka spent four hours with a factor about something that was not explained, or on the day after that, when she paid off two of her four men and sent them south with a letter and kept the other two.

On the fourth morning Riarhin found her in the stable yard having a shoe looked at.

“You’ve somewhere to be,” Riarhin said.

“I have somewhere I’m not going.”

“That’s a different thing.”

“It is.” Aryka watched the farrier work. “Where are you going?”

“South. Eventually.”

“Eventually.”

“I have a thing to find first and I don’t know where it is.”

Aryka nodded as though that were an ordinary answer, and Riarhin realised with some irritation that to a woman who had spent eight months in a country that did not care who she was, it probably was.

“Well,” Aryka said. “I’m not doing anything.”

“You don’t have to come.”

“I know.”

Riarhin heard loneliness in it, and filed it, and was wrong.

Na’ar was pleased, which required no investigation.

He had been pleased in the hills, and he was pleased in Kolmar, and he would be pleased on any road that had an end further away than this one. Riarhin had been travelling with him for a year and four months and had never once had to ask him whether he minded.

She found him at the horse trough with Aryka’s two remaining men, teaching them a game with three stones and no rules that either of them could establish.

“She’s coming,” Riarhin said.

Na’ar moved a stone. One of the men made a sound of protest.

“Good,” he said.

“You don’t know why.”

“No.” He moved another stone, and the second man laughed, and the first man swore. “Do you?”

Riarhin thought about it on the way back across the yard and had not arrived anywhere by the time she got there.

Chapter Six: What He Was Looking For

Riarhin had four pieces of paper and she had been carrying them for two years.

She laid them out on the table in the room above the tavern because Aryka had asked what she was looking for, and because after a month in the hills the question had been asked in a way that made refusing it more trouble than answering.

The first was a copy in her own hand of an entry in a clerk’s catalogue: an account of the Himadri temple, two leaves inserted in another hand, a drawing of green glass in a brass frame, and a dispute about whether the leaves were old enough to justify the price. The second was a dealer’s name and the name of a forwarding house on the southern coast. The third was a letter from that forwarding house, eighteen months old, regretting that the volume described had been consigned onward and that their instructions did not permit them to say to whom. The fourth was a list of six names in Oshiira, of which four were confirmed dead and one had never existed.

Aryka read all four. She took her time.

“This is nothing,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You’ve had two years.”

“I’ve had two years and a dealer who died in Verrus of the year before last, and a forwarding house that keeps its instructions, and a correspondent in Oshiira who may be a man or may be a way of signing letters.” Riarhin put her hand flat on the four sheets. “And a word.”

“Kalrantha.”

“Kalrantha.”

Aryka sat back. “What is it?”

“I don’t know.”

“What do you think it is?”

“I don’t know that either. It’s in three northern books and in all three it’s the same paragraph, copied. Green glass in a brass frame, out of the Himadri, and a wish with a clause folded into it.” She could have said all three word for word and did not. “Which is a rumour wearing a description.”

“That’s somebody else’s line.”

Riarhin looked up.

“You said it the way a person says a thing they were told,” Aryka said. “Whose?”

“A man who spent money on it for years and never got it.”

“And where is he?”

“In the ground above a dry well, with his brothers’ knives.”

Aryka was quiet for a while. She turned the forwarding house’s letter over and looked at the back of it, which was blank, as Riarhin had looked at the back of it perhaps forty times.

“So he wanted it, and couldn’t have it, and that’s your reason.”

“He was careful about what he wanted.”

“That’s not a reason. That’s a recommendation.”

“It’s enough,” Riarhin said. “He’s dead. What he was interested in didn’t die with him. Better I find it before the next careful man does.”

It was an excellent reason. She had been using it for two years and it had never once let her down, and she noticed, saying it aloud to a woman who had been paying attention for a month, that it did not sound quite the way it sounded in her head.

Aryka did not pick at it. That was worse.

Later, when Aryka had gone down, Riarhin sat with the four sheets and did not put them away.

She had been moving since the crossing. Work, road, town, another problem, another man who wanted killing, another fire, another morning. She had become very good at leaving, and she could remember the order of it: leaving had been freedom for a while, and then it had been staying alive, and then it had been a habit, and habits do not announce the day they change over.

What she had noticed lately — not thought about; noticed, the way she noticed a ford she did not intend to use — was that the road had begun to feel like standing still.

Kalrantha was not a road.

It was a thing to find.

She folded the four sheets and put them inside her coat and did not examine why that mattered.

There was a fifth piece of paper and it was not on the table.

It had come to her at Vethric two years before, forwarded four times, and it was three lines in a clerk’s hand telling her that the Duke of the Belworth’s condition was unchanged and that the young gentleman had arrived and been received.

Unchanged, in the spring of that year, had meant alive.

She had not written back. There had been nothing to write that was not a question, and the honest form of the question was is he dead yet, and she had not been able to find a way to put that on paper to a household that would have to read it aloud to somebody.

Two years was long enough that the answer had stopped being news and become a fact she did not have.

She knew exactly how many days it had been. She had never once counted them on purpose.

Chapter Seven: Have Some Soup

The witch was in a hamlet called Ossen Fold, and she was dying, and she had sent for Riarhin by name.

That was the part that got Riarhin up the valley. Not the sending — people sent for her; it was how she was paid. The name. She had been in Acasia under four arrangements of her own name and none of them had reached this valley, and the woman at the bottom of the track had said she’s expecting the Lethai woman with the green cloth in the flat tone of somebody repeating an instruction she did not enjoy, in a word she had plainly been made to learn and had got a little wrong.

Aryka wanted to come in. Riarhin told her to wait at the wall.

“Why?”

“Because there’s one door.”

Aryka looked at the house. It was stone to the window-line and turf above, with a bush of something grown up over the south side and a thread of smoke coming out of the roof rather than a chimney.

“And if I hear a noise?”

“Then I’ll be making it.”

Inside it was dark and warm and smelled of wet wool, mutton fat, and the flowering herb that Riarhin had last smelled in a room above a courtyard in Silkstrand where a woman had been made comfortable enough to die slowly.

The witch was on a box bed against the far wall with her legs under a horse blanket. She was old in the way that has nothing to do with years: skin gone papery at the wrists, the whole architecture of her face resettled around the bones. Her eyes were entirely present.

There was a pot over the fire, and a bowl beside it, and a stool.

“Sit down,” the witch said in Acasi. “You’re standing where the draught is.”

Riarhin did not sit down. She looked at the room first: the one door, the window shuttered and pegged from inside, a hook, a broom, a rack of drying stalks, a box under the bed with the lid not quite closed. Then she sat on the stool, which put her back to the door, and she moved the stool until it did not.

The witch watched her do all of it without comment.

“You know my name,” Riarhin said.

“I know four of them.”

“Who from?”

“A woman in Sedris whose sister is married in Cair, who had it from a carter who had it from a boy who was taken out of a stable on a hay cart in Verrus.” The witch’s mouth moved. “You want it to have been sorcery.”

“No,” Riarhin said. “That would have been less trouble to fix.”

“Ha.”

The old woman shifted herself higher against the wall. It cost her, and she did it without asking for help, and Riarhin did not offer any.

“I’m dying.”

“Yes.”

“Say it back to me properly. People go soft at this part and then I have to do it twice.”

“You’ll be dead in two days,” Riarhin said. “Three if the weather turns and you’re stubborn about it.”

The witch smiled with real pleasure.

“Good. Then we can trade.”

“I want you to save my daughter,” the witch said.

Riarhin did not answer at once. She had come up the valley for a name and a rumour and she had expected to be sold something.

“Where is she?”

“Ah.” The witch’s finger came up. “That’s a different question and it costs more.”

“What do you have that I want?”

“I know where Kalrantha is not.”

Riarhin sat very still. It was, she thought afterwards, the most skilful thing anybody had said to her in two years, because a woman who says I know where it is is selling and a woman who says I know where it is not has already read your papers.

“Go on.”

“Not in the north. Not in the Himadri, whatever your three books say. And not in any hand that would answer a letter.” The witch let that sit. “And I know the name of the house that had the account, and the city it stands in, and I’ll give you the wording exactly, because the wording is the whole of it and it will do you no good at all if you get it wrong.”

“Tell me how to find Kalrantha,” Riarhin said, “and I’ll find the girl.”

The witch looked at her for a long moment.

Then she said: “Have some soup.”

Riarhin looked at the pot.

She knew what a pot on a fire in a witch’s house was worth and she knew four ways to be certain of it and she had used all four, in other rooms, on other nights, and she was tired in a way that had nothing to do with the last nine days.

She got up, and took the bowl, and filled it, and ate it standing.

It was mutton and barley and something sour she could not place, and it was very good, and there were three pieces of meat in it, which for a woman two days from dying was not a small thing to put in a pot for a stranger.

The witch watched her finish. She watched the way a woman watches a seam go in.

When Riarhin set the bowl down, the old woman said:

“Say you’ll find the girl.”

“You haven’t given me the wording.”

“Say it.”

Riarhin looked at the bowl. Then at the fire. Then at the door, which was where the draught was.

“I will find the girl,” she said.

The witch smiled.

Riarhin did not.

She got the wording. She got it four times, and made the old woman say it four times, and the fourth time it came out the same as the first, which was how Riarhin knew it had been learned and not composed.

The counting house, in Silkstrand. But you must go by the counting house.

“By it.”

“By it.”

“Not into it.”

“I have said the words,” the witch said, “and I will not improve them, and if you go into it you will find clerks and a ledger and a man who is sorry he cannot help, and that will be the end of your two years.”

“And the girl?”

“Is at the shieling above the burnt wood. Four hours north, and then up.”

“How old?”

The witch’s face did something then that Riarhin would think about for a long time afterwards, and could not have named, and did not try to.

“Nine,” she said. “Twenty-two years ago.”

She died in the night, before the two days, which Riarhin had allowed for.

Riarhin was sitting on the stool with her back not to the door when it happened, and there was no last word and no instruction, only the breath going wrong and then going out, and the room being the same room afterwards with one less thing in it.

She sat for a while.

Then she felt it: a pressure, quite specific, closing around her throat, not tight enough to interfere with breathing and not loose enough to be imagined. She put two fingers against her neck. There was nothing there.

She had felt a rope and she had felt a hand and she had felt a bowstring and this was none of those.

It was finer than that.

She went out to the wall and told Aryka the old woman was dead, and did not mention her throat, and did not touch it again where anyone could see.

Chapter Eight: Wyllmont’s Shadow

They went north because Riarhin had said a sentence in a dark room and could not now get out of it.

Four hours north and then up meant two days in that country, and the two days were how she learned what Aryka Everblood actually was, which was not what the hills had told her and not what Kolmar had told her either.

It came out in pieces, the way it does when nobody is asking.

The first piece came at a wall, where they stopped because the horses had had enough of the stones.

Aryka sat down on the wall and put her hands on her knees, and out of nothing she said, “I could have taken her when she was twenty.”

“Who?”

“Veralyn.”

Riarhin waited.

“She had the height on me. She has always had the height on me and there is nothing to be done about height.” Aryka turned her hand over and looked at the palm. “But at twenty I was the stronger. I know that. She knows it. It never mattered, because there was no room in Viterra for a woman to be the stronger in a way anybody had to write down.”

“Vhasia would have written it down.”

“Vhasia would have put me in a saddle at fifteen and given me a company and made a song about it.” She said it without much bitterness, which made it worse. “Viterra gave me a good master-at-arms, in a courtyard, with the door shut, and a great deal of praise for how well I managed it, and then went on arranging the succession as though I had been a tapestry.”

The second piece came about her brothers.

Wyllmont, who had stormed a fortress because he had grown tired of standing outside it being looked at, and had died doing it, and had been right that it could be taken and wrong about almost everything else. Wyllmont, who had denied her Aberiel and had later found in himself, for Veralyn, an amount of forgiveness and opportunity that he had never once located for his sister.

“He wasn’t cruel to me,” Aryka said. “That’s the part people get wrong when they tell it. He simply never thought of it. There was a shape a thing was supposed to have and I was not that shape, and he was not a man who could hold two shapes in his head at once.”

And Arryk, the other brother, the ally, who history had settled on as a drunk.

“He was,” Aryka said. “He was that. I’m not going to argue about the cup.”

“Then what’s the argument?”

“That he was a man of thirty living in Wyllmont’s shadow after Charau, carrying things nobody around him would let him put a name to, and being told every year of his life that one day the whole of it would come to him from the man who had done it.” She looked at the hills. “You can drink at that. I’ve watched better people drink at less.”

Arryk’s son was Arwen, whom history remembered as little better, and who had been struck out of the succession by Brightstride for an offence that Aryka described in one sentence and did not elaborate.

“He touched the Fenwood interest.”

Riarhin kept her face where it was.

“You know what the Fenwoods are,” Aryka said. It was not a question, and she had not looked over, and Riarhin understood that she had been carrying whatever she knew about Riarhin’s history for a month and had chosen this wall to put it down on. “They hold enough of Viterra and enough of the river that the Crown goes on existing partly by agreeing not to notice. Everybody signs the same word — vassal — and everybody knows what the word is doing. That fiction is the settlement. It has held for a hundred and forty years because nobody has ever been stupid enough to say it out loud in a room where it would have to be answered.”

“And Arwen said it out loud.”

“Arwen made it impossible not to answer.” Aryka got off the wall. “So Brightstride removed him, which was expedient, and everyone agreed it was justice. And then Brightstride died the year after, and nobody wanted to reopen anything, and the expedient became the settlement, and it has been the settlement ever since.”

She picked up her reins.

“I carried that for years,” she said. “Then decades. I am finished with it.”

Riarhin looked at the road ahead of them.

“Vhasia,” Aryka said. “Viterra. My cousins. Veralyn. Arwen. The throne. All of it. Done.”

Riarhin heard wounded pride.

She heard it clearly and she was not being stupid about it. She had watched a great many people decide, in her hearing, that the thing they had not been given had never been worth having, and they all said it the same way, and they all said done about three times more often than a person says a word they mean.

“You’re not done,” she said.

Aryka did not argue.

That was the part Riarhin got wrong, and she got it wrong for the most reasonable reason available: a woman who is telling the truth and a woman who has given up arguing look identical from the outside.

Na’ar rode behind them all that afternoon and said nothing about any of it.

At the top of the second climb he brought his horse up alongside Riarhin’s and looked at the burnt wood below them — forty acres of black stalk on a north slope, six or seven years dead, with green coming up through it in the wet places.

“Fire from above,” he said.

“Lightning?”

“Lightning takes one tree and then the wind takes the rest.” He pointed with his chin at the shape of the burn, which was a wedge, narrow at the top. “That started at the top of a hill in still air.”

Riarhin looked at it for a while.

“Say the rest.”

“I have said it,” Na’ar said, and went back to riding.

Chapter Nine: The Girl Who Was Not

The shieling above the burnt wood had one room and a stone bench and a child sitting on the bench with her hands in her lap.

She was small and she was clean, and that was the first thing wrong. Her dress had been washed. Her hair had been combed and parted and the parting was exact. There was no water within a quarter-mile of the shieling, uphill, and there had been nobody on that hill in six years.

“You came,” the child said.

“Yes.”

“She said you would. She was very certain and I told her that certainty is a thing people spend.” The child smiled. She had a child’s teeth. “Will you sit down? You’re standing where the draught is.”

Riarhin did not sit down.

Behind her in the doorway Aryka had stopped with her hand on the jamb, and Na’ar had not come in at all, and Riarhin could hear him outside, not moving.

“What are you?” Riarhin asked.

“I am what was asked for.”

“By whom?”

“By a woman who wanted her sister back.” The child folded her hands more comfortably. “Not that woman. An older one, a long time ago, in a room with the shutters pegged. She said she would give anything, which is a sentence people say without hearing it, and she said it out loud, which is different from thinking it, and she said it in a place where saying it is an opening.”

“And something answered.”

“Something is always answering,” the child said. “That is the whole of its work. It is not clever and it is not cruel. It is attentive.”

“And the girl?”

“Died of a fever in her ninth year and has been dead twenty-two years and is not in this room.” The child looked down at her own hands with an interest that was not a child’s interest. “This is a shape. It was given to me and I keep it well. You will notice that I have not once told you a lie.”

“No,” Riarhin said. “You’ve been very careful.”

“Thank you.”

“It wasn’t praise.”

“It was accurate,” the child said, “which I prefer.”

“The old woman sent me,” Riarhin said.

“She sent you to save me.”

“She sent me to find you.”

Something happened in the child’s face then — not a flinch, nothing so useful. A small adjustment, like a clerk turning a page and finding the column already added.

“Yes,” it said. “She did say find. She was a careful woman too. We understood each other for a long time.”

“Twenty-two years.”

“She fed me,” the child said. “I do not require it. She required it.” It tilted its head. “She gave you soup.”

“Yes.”

“And you ate it, and then she asked you to say the words, and you said them.” The smile came back. “You will have felt something afterwards. At the throat. Not tight. Fine.”

Riarhin did not touch her neck.

“Yes,” she said.

“That is not mine,” the child said. “I want you to know that, because you will blame me for it later and I would rather be blamed for my own work. She spun that herself. It is the last thing she made and she made it out of a bowl of soup and eleven words, and it will hold, and you are going to keep your promise.” It looked genuinely pleased. “She was very good.”

“Tell me about the counting house,” Riarhin said.

“You have the wording.”

“Tell me why.”

“Because what you want is not in the counting house, and the man in the counting house is not lying when he says so, and if you go in and ask him he will tell you the truth and the truth will close the road.” The child swung its feet, once, and Riarhin’s hand did not move but everything behind her hand did. “Go by it. Not in. Watch who comes out of it and decide which of them is interesting. One of them will be.”

“And that’s the whole of it?”

“That’s the whole of it that I have.” A pause. “I know less than you think and more than you would like. Kalrantha is a word that has had a great deal of trouble taken over it. I do not know what it is either. I have wondered. Wondering is most of what I do.”

Riarhin believed it, which was the worst thing that had happened all day.

“Then we’re finished,” she said.

“Are we?”

“I was asked to find you. I’ve found you.”

“Yes,” the child said. “You have.”

Then it looked past her, at the doorway, and its whole attention moved.

“And you,” it said.

Aryka took her hand off the jamb.

“I did not see you properly,” the child said. “Forgive me. You were standing behind something I was interested in.” It leaned forward very slightly. “I smell the hunger in you.”

Aryka went still. Riarhin had seen her still at the ford and still at the tower and this was a different stillness.

“Riches?” the child asked.

Nothing.

“Glory,” it said, and there was something almost warm in it, almost coaxing, the voice of a thing that has done this a great many times and still enjoys it. “Glory? No. Not that either.”

It sat back.

“No. You seek pow—”

Riarhin drew Toothpick and put it through the base of the skull, upward, from behind the ear, the way she had done it to a weeping man against a rock nine days before, and the child-shaped thing stopped in the middle of the word and did not finish it.

There was very little blood. The body went sideways off the bench and lay on the stone with its hands still folded, because the hands had been part of the shape and the shape had been kept well.

At Riarhin’s throat, something gave.

Not a snap. A slackening, the way a line goes slack when whoever was holding the other end has let go of it, which is not the same as being free of the line.

Aryka had not moved from the doorway.

“What,” she said, “was that.”

“A promise being kept.”

“That was a child.”

“It was not a child, and you know it wasn’t, and you were about to let it finish a sentence about you in front of two other people.” Riarhin wiped Toothpick on her thigh and put it away. “It doesn’t lie. That’s not a virtue. It means everything it says arrives true and lands where it was aimed.”

Aryka looked at the thing on the floor. She was breathing the way people breathe when they have been holding it.

“The old woman asked you to save her.”

“The old woman asked me to save her,” Riarhin said. “I said I’d find the girl. I never said I’d save her.”

Aryka’s head came round.

And she was furious. Riarhin had expected that and had been ready for it, and had the answer prepared about promises and wording and what the old woman had actually bought, and none of it was necessary, because what came out of Aryka Everblood’s mouth was not about the child at all.

“I wanted to hear the rest.”

Outside, Na’ar had built a fire without asking anybody.

He looked at Riarhin’s face, and at Aryka’s back going down the slope alone, and he put another stick on.

“The child?” he said.

“Wasn’t.”

Na’ar nodded, as though that settled the whole of it, which for him it did.

Chapter Ten: The Roads South

They buried the witch on the third day, because it took them two days to come back down and one more to do it properly in ground that did not want to be dug.

Aryka dug. She did not offer and she was not asked and she took the spade off Na’ar after an hour without a word about it, and she worked until her hands were in a state, and Riarhin understood that this was what Aryka Everblood did instead of saying things, and filed it correctly this time.

They put her in with the horse blanket, because there was no one to leave it to.

The hamlet came up the track afterwards, eleven of them, and stood at the wall and did not come in. Riarhin told them the old woman was dead and that she had been sent for and had gone up the valley and had done what she was asked. Nobody asked what that was. One woman asked whether the shieling above the burnt wood was empty now.

“Yes,” Riarhin said.

The woman put her hand over her mouth, and then took it away, and said thank you, and went down the track.

Riarhin rubbed the house out afterwards, in the way she had: stool back where it had been, bowl washed, shutter pegged, the box under the bed left exactly as unclosed as she had found it. She did not know why she did it and she did not stop to find out.

On the way out she stopped at the fire, which was cold.

Eleven words. She could say them in the old woman’s voice and in the old woman’s Acasi, with the catch in the third one where the breath had gone wrong.

She did not say them.

They rode south for nine days and Aryka talked about it twice.

The first time was at a ford, where she said, without preamble, “I meant it,” and Riarhin said, “I know,” and neither of them believed the other.

The second time was at an inn in a town whose name Riarhin forgot on purpose, where Aryka drank two cups and said, “You think I am going to turn round.”

“I think you’ve been turning round for thirty years.”

“And now?”

“And now you’ve got two men, a purse you carry yourself, and nothing to do.”

Aryka looked into the cup and smiled, and it was not the smile from the tavern in Kolmar. It was the other one — the one she put things away with.

“Yes,” she said. “Nothing to do.”

The country changed four days out from the coast and Riarhin’s body knew before she did.

The shutters went thin. She noticed it at an inn where the shutter did not meet the frame and the light came through in a line, and she was up and across the room and had her hand on the peg before she had decided to move, and the peg was where her hand expected it to be.

Then there was jasmine in a courtyard, laid over a drain, and the smell of the two together did something to her that the smell of either alone did not.

And on the eighth day a factor’s man bowed to Aryka in the street, and Riarhin caught herself counting the rings on his hand and estimating, quite precisely and without having asked herself to, how easily each of them would come off.

She stopped. She had not done that in twenty years.

Nobody had taught her that on the road.

Na’ar found her on the sea wall the night before they went down into the city.

He did not ask her what was wrong. He had never once asked her what was wrong, in a year and four months, which was most of why she was still travelling with him.

He stood beside her and looked at the water, which was flat and black and going nowhere, and after a while he said:

“I had four brothers.”

Riarhin did not turn her head. In a year and four months he had told her nothing about himself that was not about the next forty miles.

“Kargath?”

“Kargath’s rise.” He said it the way a man says a season. “Not him. Men who were becoming his. It is the same thing, and I am told that it is not.”

“And now he’s dead.”

“Now he is dead.” Na’ar put his hands on the wall. There was a hatchet scar going white along the outside of one forearm. “So there is nothing to be done about it. That is good. A man with nothing to be done about it is free.”

The water went on doing nothing. Somewhere below them a boat knocked against a stone, and knocked again, and a man swore at it without any real feeling.

Riarhin knew the sentence. She had several of her own in that shape, built to the same tolerance, and she had been carrying one of them down this road for two years and calling it an excellent reason.

She did not say so. He had not asked her anything.

“Free,” she said.

“Free,” said Na’ar, and went back to the fire.

Riarhin stayed on the wall until the tide turned, and did not examine it, and in the morning they went down into Silkstrand.

Act II

Chapter Eleven: By the Counting House

Silkstrand is not built on ground. It is built across water: a hundred and some islands stitched together with arched bridges, and the bridges are the whole of the city’s argument with itself. Every crossing belongs to somebody. Every canal carries something nobody will name. Above the tide there are guild charters and the Archivolt’s clerks and the Matron’s velvet justice on her palazzo stair; below the bridges there are night-boats that carry no cargo, and the Ragman owns the steps the patricians climb, and none of that has changed in the fifty years since Riarhin last stood on a plank and felt it move.

The dyewater had not changed either. It never washes out. The lower canals run a colour that is not any colour, and on the day they came in it was running red, which meant somebody in Vhasia was burning barns.

The counting house stood on Threadneedle with a brass plate and a boot-scraper and eleven steps up from the water, and Riarhin sat across from it for four days and did not go in.

This was not difficult. She had sat across from worse things for longer. What was difficult was that the coffee house she sat in had a woman at the back who sang in the afternoons, and the woman sang in Silkstrandi, and on the second day she sang something that Riarhin had last heard through a floor.

Riarhin paid and moved to a different table and went on watching the steps.

“You could go in,” Aryka said, on the third day. “Ask the clerk. Worst he says is no.”

“The worst he says is the truth.”

“Which would be what?”

“That there’s nothing there, and he’s sorry, and he’d be glad to check the older books.” Riarhin did not take her eyes off the plate. “And then it’s finished. Two years and four sheets of paper and a dead woman’s soup, and a polite man in an apron has closed the road, and there is nowhere further to stand.”

Aryka thought about it.

“So we watch the door.”

“We go by the door.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“No,” Riarhin said. “It isn’t. And if it were, she’d have said the other thing, because she said it four times and she said it the same way four times.”

What she wanted was a man who was wrong.

That was the whole of the method and she had never had to explain it to anybody who mattered. In four days, eleven men and three women went up the eleven steps and came down them again, and all of them behaved exactly as a person behaves who has been to a counting house: they went in at a walk and came out at a walk and put their papers away on the step or in the street.

On the fourth afternoon a man came out and put his papers away before he opened the door.

He did it inside, in the dark of the hall, where nobody in the street could see what he was carrying, and then he came out with his hands empty and his coat flat and he turned inward, away from the wharves and the Exchange, which is the wrong way for a factor and the right way for a man who has somewhere to be that is not his office.

Riarhin was off the bench before she had finished thinking about it.

He crossed four bridges in half a mile, and on the fourth he stopped being a man walking and became a man arriving.

She knew the transition. There is a change in the shoulders about sixty paces out. He put a hand to his collar, and looked at a window that had nothing in it but shutters, and went in under an arch that had jasmine over it and a drain underneath.

Riarhin stood in the street and smelled the two of them together and did not move for a moment.

Then she went back to the quay and found the others and said, “Tonight,” and Na’ar, who had been leaning against a wall for an hour and a half being a large man near an alley, straightened up and looked almost interested.

Aryka said, “What’s the house?”

“It has no name on it.”

“Everything here has a name on it.”

“Not that one,” Riarhin said. “That one has jasmine on it.”

Chapter Twelve: The Velvet Court

They were expected, which Riarhin had assumed, and they were expected by name, which she had not.

The woman at the inner door was forty and beautifully dressed and asked, in Vhasian, whether the Lady Aryka Everblood would care to wait in the blue room, and whether the Ykrul gentleman would be more comfortable in the yard where there was a bench, and whether Riarhin would come up.

Riarhin looked at her for a moment.

“In that order?”

“In that order,” the woman said, and did not smile, and was not unkind about it either, and Riarhin understood that she had just been shown the whole arrangement of the house in a single sentence and that she was meant to have understood it.

Aryka’s jaw moved. She said, “The blue room,” pleasantly, and went where she was put.

The room upstairs had silk on the walls and red glass in the lamps and a bowl of fruit that had been changed before anything in it spoiled, and Riarhin counted four doors before she counted the woman.

Madam Serafine sat in a dark dress with a velvet veil fitted to a comb, and she had arranged the room so well that Riarhin’s own preference — back to the wall, sight of the entrance — put her in the chair that faced the light.

Riarhin looked at the chair. Then she moved it eight inches and sat in it anyway, which was the only correction available and cost her nothing and was noticed.

“You moved my chair,” Serafine said.

“You put it there.”

“I did.” The veil turned very slightly. “Somebody taught you rooms.”

“Somebody taught me this room.”

“Yes,” Serafine said. “I rather thought so. You came over Three-Queens like a woman who had crossed it before and did not enjoy the memory, and you stopped under my arch for four breaths, and you have not once looked at the fruit.” A pause. “Your mother was in the houses.”

Riarhin did not answer.

“I am not going to ask which,” Serafine said. “It is nearly always a cruelty and it is never information.” The veil came up a degree. “You are very young to have got this far on four sheets of paper.”

Riarhin let it stand.

It was the only thing the woman had been wrong about in six minutes, and it was the only advantage available anywhere in that room, and she had learned before Serafine was born that you do not spend an advantage correcting somebody’s manners.

“Sit still,” Serafine said, “drink nothing you did not see poured, and tell me what you want. I have eleven minutes and then a Censor’s brother.”

Riarhin told her. Not all of it: the word, the counting house, the account she wanted, the two years.

Serafine listened without moving at all, which was worse than being interrupted.

“Kalrantha,” she said, when it was finished.

“You know it.”

“I know the word. I have known the word for nineteen years and it has never once been useful.” The veil moved. “Shall I tell you the useful part, or the part you came for? They are not the same and you will only believe one of them.”

“Both.”

“The part you came for: yes, there was an account, and yes, some of it passed through this house, because in this city almost everything passes through this house, which is not a boast, it is a description of drainage.” Serafine turned her hand over. “The useful part: I cannot give it to you. Not will not. Cannot. It is in three places, none of them mine to open, and two of the three belong to people who would ask me why I wanted it, and I have spent thirty years being a woman nobody asks that question of.”

“Then you’ll tell me where the three places are.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because you would go and get it,” Serafine said, “and you would be caught, and you would say my name, and then I would have to be disappointed in a way that is expensive for everybody.”

Riarhin sat with that for a moment.

“You’ve just told me it’s in three places and that one of them is yours.”

“I have.”

“That’s more than nothing.”

“It is exactly as much as I intended,” Serafine said, “and you may have it, and you may not have the rest, and now I am going to be pleasant to you for four minutes because I like you, and then Celise will show you out.”

She was pleasant for four minutes. It was the most information Riarhin had gathered from anybody in two years, and nearly all of it was about the woman giving it.

Aryka was in the blue room with her boots on a footstool and a glass of something she had not touched.

“Well?”

“She likes me.”

“That’s inconvenient,” Aryka said, which was the second time in a month that one of them had said it, and Riarhin noted that it had stopped being funny.

“She’s given us a wall to stand against. It’s in three places and one of them is hers.”

“And she’ll open it?”

“She’ll do nothing. She has arranged it so that anything I do next is something she has already thought about.” Riarhin stood in the doorway and looked back up the stair. “I’ve met two people like her. The other one is dead and I put her in the ground myself.”

In the yard Na’ar was sitting on the bench with a cup, being looked at by two of the house’s men, and enjoying it.

Chapter Thirteen: To Whom It May Concern

The plan that worked was the fourth one.

The first had been to buy a clerk. Riarhin spent two days establishing which of the counting house’s four clerks had debts, and found that the one with debts had them to Serafine, which meant buying him was a way of sending Serafine a letter.

The second had been to go in at night. She got as far as the roof of the building opposite and lay there for five hours and came down and said nothing about it, because what she had seen was that the counting house was not the problem and never had been.

The third had been to follow the man with the flat coat again, which produced a second visit to the house with the jasmine, and a third, and a clear understanding that he was a courier and that couriers know routes and not contents.

The fourth was a note.

“We’re going to be robbed,” Riarhin said.

Aryka looked up from her boot. “By whom?”

“That’s the question, isn’t it.”

They went down to the Dye Yards in the afternoon, which was the correct hour, and walked four streets that Riarhin had picked for the density of children, and she carried her purse where a purse should not be carried.

“What are we doing?”

“Watching.”

“Just watching?”

“And one other thing.”

Aryka waited. Riarhin let her wait, because Aryka Everblood had made her wait on a stair in a pillow house for half an hour and Riarhin was not made of stone.

“We’re looking for a thief.”

Aryka looked at the street — at the porters, the dye vats, the women carrying skeins on poles, the boys running with nothing in their hands and everywhere to be.

“Seems like if we can spot a thief out in the street, they’re not going to be much good.”

“True. But we have an advantage.”

“Oh really?” Aryka said. “Is it you?”

“No. Bait.”

“What bait?”

A child came past between them, at the speed a child comes past, and Riarhin’s hand came down on a small wrist half an inch from her purse and threw it off.

The child bolted.

“Bold little bastard,” Aryka said.

“Yes,” said Riarhin. “You should never try to steal from two people at once.”

There was a pause of a length Riarhin enjoyed very much.

Aryka’s hand went to her belt.

Then Aryka Everblood, princess of the blood, veteran of thirty-one years, was running down a street in the Dye Yards shouting in court Vhasian at a child who had already gone over a wall.

She did not catch her.

Riarhin had known she would not catch her, and had known it in the way she knew where water ran, and had said nothing, and stood in the street for the four minutes it took Aryka to come back with her hair out of its pin and her face carefully arranged.

“Nine crowns,” Aryka said.

“Mm.”

“You knew.”

“I planted it on her during the brush.”

“You planted nine of my crowns—”

“I planted a note,” Riarhin said. “The crowns were yours and she took those on her own initiative. I’d have warned you but you’d have held the purse and then she wouldn’t have taken it, and I need her to have taken something, or the note is just a note.”

Aryka put her hair back up.

“What does it say?”

“To whom it may concern.”

“And?”

“And the rest of it is for the person who is concerned,” Riarhin said, “and not for the child, and not for the four people the child reports to, and not for you. It’s addressed upward. It’ll go upward. That’s all a note has to do.”

Aryka finished with her hair.

“You understand,” she said, “that I am going to make you tell me the rest eventually.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Chapter Fourteen: Quick Lena

The note took two days to climb and it came back down in the form of a girl sitting on Riarhin’s bed.

Riarhin had taken a room above a cookshop with one window, a bolt she had improved, and a hatch to the roof space that she had checked on the first night and satisfied herself was nailed. It was not nailed. It had been nailed and was now hinged, and it had been done well enough that she had missed it, which she would think about later and at length.

The girl was perhaps nineteen and sitting cross-legged with Riarhin’s note open on her knee and Riarhin’s whetstone in her hand, turning it over.

“This is a good stone,” she said in Silkstrandi.

“It was a gift.”

“From somebody who liked you or somebody who owed you?”

“Neither.”

“Then it’s a good stone,” Lena said, and put it back exactly where it had been, which Riarhin also noticed.

She was small and brown and had a broken nose that had been set by someone competent, and she wore four rings that were all worth about the same, which is a thing a person does who expects to lose one.

“You wrote up,” she said. “Nobody writes up. People come down and ask for the best thief in Silkstrand and get sold a fool with a reputation.”

“I didn’t want the best thief in Silkstrand.”

“No. You wanted whoever the letter got to.” Lena tapped the note against her knee. “That’s not flattering but it’s honest. What’s the job?”

“Three places, one house, one account. Papers, not coin.”

“Whose house?”

“Serafine’s.”

Lena went on tapping the note against her knee, and her face did not do anything at all, and Riarhin — who had spent four days reading a veil — found that she could not read a nineteen-year-old with a broken nose.

“Well,” Lena said. “That’s a price.”

They argued for an hour and the argument was not about money.

“Half in advance,” Riarhin said.

“No.”

“Then all at the end.”

“No.”

Riarhin put her cup down. “Then what do you want?”

“Paid at the end for the work, which is ordinary, and nothing in advance, which is also ordinary, and no favour either way when it’s finished.” Lena counted it off on three fingers. “You don’t owe me and I don’t owe you. When it’s done, we’re done. Nobody carries anything.”

“That isn’t how anyone does this.”

“It’s how I do this.”

“Why?”

Lena looked at her for a while. She had a way of going still that was not Serafine’s stillness and not Aryka’s; it was the stillness of somebody who has learned that most questions answer themselves if you decline to help.

“Because in this city everybody holds a piece of everybody,” she said. “That’s the trade. Serafine has four hundred people and she doesn’t own one of them outright, she owns a piece, and the pieces are why nothing here ever moves. A clean theft leaves nobody holding anything. In and gone and quits.” She shrugged. “Luck’s a loan. You get given it and you give it back and then it’s nobody’s. If you start keeping it you’re a bank, and banks get robbed.”

“Who told you that?”

“A man who was hanged,” Lena said, “which he’d say proves it.”

Riarhin sat with that for a moment, because she had heard the other half of it before she was twenty, in this city, from a woman who dressed her hair.

Never accept a favour without speaking its terms. An unspoken kindness is a thread, and a thread can be pulled from either end, and the other end is always held by somebody who has been playing longer than you.

She had been taught it as a rule for surviving rooms. Lena had arrived at the same place from underneath and had turned it into a trade: speak every term, take wages, hold nothing, be owed nothing. It was the only defence anybody had ever devised against this city and it required you to be poor.

She would not take a retainer, she would not take a bed in the same house, and she would not tell Riarhin where she slept.

She would take a list of what was wanted, and she took it, and she read it twice and gave it back rather than keep it, having learned it.

“You don’t write anything down,” Riarhin said.

“Do you?”

“No.”

“Then we’ll get on,” said Lena.

At the door — the hatch, not the door — she stopped with one hand up on the frame.

“Your big man,” she said. “Does he do stairs?”

“He does anything he’s pointed at.”

“Good. Keep him pointed at nothing for a while.” Lena went up through the hatch and out, and her feet made no sound on the boards above, and then there was one soft knock from directly over Riarhin’s head, and then nothing.

Riarhin lay awake a long time, and it was not the hatch she was thinking about.

It was that in an hour and a half the girl had not once asked her why she wanted the papers.

Serafine had asked inside eleven minutes. Aryka had asked at a wall in the hills. Everybody asked; it was the only thing anybody ever wanted, because the why was where the leverage lived.

Lena had not asked, and had not forgotten to ask.

She had simply declined to pick it up.

Chapter Fifteen: What Needs Be Done

Aryka began going out on the ninth day.

She did not sneak. She came down in the morning with her coat on and said she had something to see to, and when Riarhin asked what, she said, “What needs be done,” in the tone of a woman answering a question she considered already answered, and came back at dusk with dust on her boots and her purse where it always was.

Riarhin let it go the first time because she was busy.

She let it go the second time because Aryka had been useless in the blue room and useless on the stair and useless in a street in the Dye Yards, and a woman who has been made to feel useless for a fortnight will find somewhere to be, and it is a kindness not to ask where.

By the fifth time it had become a shape, and Riarhin looked at the shape and put a name on it, and the name was she is finding something to do.

The observations were correct. Every one of them.

There were mercenaries in Silkstrand. There are always mercenaries in Silkstrand: the Brass Coast companies wintered in the lower city and sold themselves by the month to anybody with a warehouse and an opinion, and a woman could stand on the Threadneedle steps and count eleven armed men who belonged to nobody in particular.

There were carts riding low. Silkstrand moved fortunes every day of the year, and a cart with its springs flat is a cart with metal in it, and metal in this city meant almost anything.

On the fourteenth day Riarhin came round the corner of a cooper’s yard and saw a man touch two fingers to his brow at Aryka — not a bow, nothing a servant does; the small economical gesture of a man acknowledging an officer in a street where he has been told not to acknowledge her.

Aryka saw Riarhin see it.

Neither of them said anything. Aryka’s face did not change and she did not explain, and Riarhin filed it, and what she filed it under was she has hired a bodyguard and does not want to be laughed at.

Which was reasonable. That was the trouble with all of it afterwards. Every single reading she made was reasonable.

“You don’t have to stay,” she said that night.

Aryka was eating, and went on eating.

“You’ve got two men, a purse, and nothing to do in a city where nobody can be hit,” Riarhin said. “Acasia’s four weeks that way. You said you were building something.”

“I did say that.”

“So go and build it.”

Aryka put her spoon down and looked at her, and there was something in her face that Riarhin would afterwards be able to reconstruct exactly, and would spend a long time turning over, and would never entirely forgive herself for.

It was patience.

“Soon,” Aryka said.

Chapter Sixteen: The Shape of the House

The Velvet Court had four ways in and Lena knew three of them before Riarhin asked.

“Front, for patrons. Kitchen court, for deliveries and for women coming off shift. Laundry stair, which is not a stair, it’s a slope with a rail, and it comes up inside the linen rooms.” Lena drew none of it. She said it twice instead, so that Riarhin would have it twice. “Fourth is the roofs, and the roofs are mine, and I’m not selling you the roofs.”

“I’m not buying the roofs.”

“Good, because they’re not for sale, and also they don’t get you where you’re going. Whatever’s in that house that’s worth papers isn’t up. Papers are down.”

They cased it for eleven days.

Riarhin had done this in eight countries and had never done it with somebody better. Lena had children — nine that Riarhin ever saw, none of them introduced, all of them apparently doing something else. Bobbin-runners: the city is full of them, and they cross it faster than any grown person because they know which planks creak, and respectable Silkstrand looks at them the way it looks at gulls. Over eleven days they produced the delivery hours, the laundry cycle, the name of the man who came for the night soil, the two evenings of the tenday — Market and Still — when the Censor’s brother was in the house and the whole rhythm changed, and the fact that the room Riarhin wanted was not a room.

“It’s a press,” Lena said. “Under the stair off the counting room. Not a strongroom. A press with three locks and a man who sits opposite it doing accounts, and he’s there from the second hour to the ninth, and he is never not there, because he lives in the house and he has nowhere to be.”

“Then we go at the ninth hour.”

“At the ninth hour Celise locks the counting room and takes the key up, and the key goes on a hook in a room with Serafine in it.”

Riarhin looked at the wall for a while.

“Then we don’t take the key.”

The first plan was clean and Riarhin loved it.

It went like this: the clerk lived in the house because his sister was in Bell Street and could not be moved, and a man who cannot leave can be made to want to leave for one hour. Give him a reason — a message about the sister, not a false one, a true one, arranged — and he would go, because he was the sort of man who would go, and Riarhin had watched him for eleven days and knew exactly what sort of man he was.

That was the part she liked. You did not have to make people do things. You had to understand them well enough that the thing they were always going to do happened at an hour of your choosing.

On the nineteenth day the clerk did not go to Bell Street.

He read the message and put it in his coat and went on doing accounts, and at the ninth hour he locked up as usual, and Riarhin watched him through a gap in a shutter and understood that she had been wrong about him, or that somebody had spoken to him, or both.

Lena found her on the roof afterwards.

“Well,” Lena said.

“He should have gone.”

“People should do all sorts.”

“No,” Riarhin said. “Not should like that. I had him. I had eleven days of him. He goes to Bell Street on the eleventh and the twenty-third of every month and he pays a woman there and he sits with his sister for forty minutes and he is late back and he lies about why.” She was angrier than she wanted to be and could hear it. “He was always going to go.”

“And he didn’t.”

“And he didn’t.”

Lena sat down on the ridge tiles with her arms round her knees and looked out at the harbour, where the lamps had come on along the wall.

“You like it when they do it themselves,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Because then it isn’t you.”

Riarhin did not answer that, and Lena, who did not pick things up, let it lie.

Chapter Seventeen: Speed

The second plan lasted nine days and died of a seal.

The seal was a good idea and it was Lena’s: a copied impression off a delivery docket, good enough to put a cart through the kitchen court on a day when the regular carter was drunk, which he could be arranged to be. It worked as far as the yard. Then it stopped working, because the house had begun countersigning dockets in a second hand, and nobody had told anybody, and there was no reason to countersign dockets, and Riarhin sat in a cookshop with the ruined seal in front of her and understood what was actually happening.

“She’s not watching us,” she said.

Lena ate.

“She’s not watching us. She hasn’t got a man on me. If she had a man on me I’d have him by now.” Riarhin turned the ruined seal over. “She’s broken the count. That’s the whole of it. Everything in this city runs on the Old Count, because everything in this city is work. Linen goes out on Hearth. Carts come on Road. Accounts are read on Tally and nobody distrains on Still because nobody in Silkstrand can.”

“And she doesn’t.”

“She does her linen on Stone. She reads her books on Yoking, or on Water, or on whichever morning she got out of bed wanting to. She takes deliveries on Still, which is not unlawful and is very nearly obscene.” Riarhin put the seal down. “Her own people can’t tell you what day a thing happens on. They wait to be told. Nineteen years of that.”

“Because of you?”

“Because of anybody.” Riarhin put the seal down. “She’s arranged a house that nobody can make a plan against, and she has been arranging it for nineteen years, and I have had thirty-one days, and the reason my plans keep dying is that she is not answering them. There’s nothing to answer. I’m not playing her. I’m walking into weather.”

She had spent four years in a house that had heard its accounts on Tally for four hundred of them, and had never once thought of that as a decision anybody had made.

Lena thought about that.

“Then stop making plans.”

“That’s not—”

“I mean it.” Lena pushed her bowl away. “You keep building a thing that needs everybody to be who they are for three days running. Nobody’s who they are for three days running. Build one that needs them to be who they are for a minute, and then build another one, and then another one, and go when the minute comes.”

So that is what they did, and Riarhin hated it, and it worked.

The third plan was four plans. It had a way in for each of five evenings and a different way out for each way in, and it had two hours that were good and one that was better, and it had nothing in it that required anybody to behave for longer than the time it takes to cross a yard.

It made her feel like a woman running down a hill.

She had a name for what she preferred and it was not a flattering name and she had never said it aloud: she liked to have already won before she arrived. She liked to have understood everyone so thoroughly that the evening simply arrived at the conclusion she had built for it, and nobody had to be fought, and nothing had to be improvised, and the whole thing had the shape of a thing that had happened by itself.

What she got instead, in Silkstrand, for twenty-nine days, was: a thing failing, and eleven seconds to decide, and a new thing.

And here was the part she did not look at directly.

She was better at it.

She had always been better at it. Everything she had ever done that had worked — the receiving room at the registry, the bolt in the split wood, the cart under the tower, the nine days of nothing above Cair — had been a thing she had built out of the wreck of something else while it was still falling.

She simply did not enjoy it, and she had spent forty years arranging her life so that she would not have to notice that the thing she was worst at wanting was the thing she was best at.

Aryka came in on the twenty-sixth day while they were laying out the fourth version and looked at it for a while.

“How many men does she have in that house?”

“Fourteen, and none of them soldiers,” Lena said.

“Then I have a suggestion.”

Riarhin waited.

“Twenty men through the kitchen court at the second hour,” Aryka said. “Hold the yard, hold the two doors, put four on the stair, and take your press apart with a maul.”

There was a silence of the kind that happens when somebody who has been quiet for a fortnight says something ridiculous.

“Twenty armed men,” Riarhin said, “inside a hundred yards of that house, would be noticed before they reached the corner. By everybody. By the Watch, who are paid; by the Censor’s brother, who is in the house on Market and on Still; by four hundred people who owe her something. We would be fighting the whole city by the third hour and we would not have the papers.”

“Mm,” Aryka said.

She did not argue. She looked at the fourth version on the table for a moment longer, and then she nodded once, and went to see about her boots.

Riarhin watched her go and thought: she has finally understood why the obvious thing doesn’t work.

Lena, who had been watching Aryka rather than the table, said nothing at all.

Chapter Eighteen: Eight Who Got Out

The Dye Yards do not burn any more.

Riarhin learned this from a woman selling fried dough at the foot of Cutler’s Bridge, who said it the way people say a thing they are proud of and cannot explain, and then said that they had burned once, nineteen years ago, and that it had been bad.

She asked what had been in the Dye Yards nineteen years ago.

“A guildhouse,” the woman said, and turned the dough, and did not look up. “Thieves. Nice ones. There was a fire.”

“Who set it?”

“There was a fire,” the woman said again, and that was the whole of the conversation.

Riarhin did not ask Lena.

She watched instead, which was cheaper and truer. And over eleven days of roofs and delivery hours and children she was never introduced to, she assembled it the way she assembled everything, out of things nobody had said.

Lena knew the Dye Yards the way a person knows a place they were small in. She went round the site of the old guildhouse rather than across it, and she did it without appearing to, the way Riarhin went round a ford she did not intend to use. Her crew were children and she fed them and she did not own them; there was no debt anywhere in that arrangement, and it was a lot of trouble to keep it that way, and she kept it.

And once, when Serafine’s name came up over a bowl, Lena had gone on eating in a manner so precisely ordinary that Riarhin had put her spoon down.

She was about the right age. She would have been nine or ten.

Riarhin did the arithmetic and then declined to say any part of it out loud, and found, with some surprise, that she was doing for Lena the thing that Na’ar did for her.

What she did say was this, on the thirty-first evening, on the ridge tiles, with the harbour lamps going on below.

“When it’s finished, you should get out of the city.”

Lena laughed. “That’s my line.”

“I mean it. When we take this, she’ll know it was local. She’ll know within a day. It doesn’t matter what we leave behind; there’s no version of this where she doesn’t work out that somebody who knows the laundry hour was involved.”

“And?”

“And she’s a woman who settles things properly and then keeps a list of everyone who saw her do it.” Riarhin looked at the side of Lena’s face. “You don’t want to be on a list in this city.”

Lena was quiet for a while.

“I’d need a reason to go,” she said. “And money.”

“You’ll have money.”

“I’ll have wages,” Lena said. “That’s the arrangement and I’m not changing it. You don’t owe me and I don’t owe you.” She stood up on the tiles, and balanced there without thinking about it, which was the most frightening thing Riarhin had seen her do. “But I’ll take the reason, if one turns up.”

By the last tenday Riarhin had stopped consulting Aryka at all.

It was not a decision. It happened the way a path happens. Lena had the house, the hours, the roofs and nine children; Lena argued with her properly and was right about a third of the time, which is a very high proportion; Lena had told her to stop making plans and had been correct.

Aryka had a purse, two men, a suggestion about twenty soldiers, and somewhere to be in the afternoons.

On the Tally before they went, Riarhin went over the whole of the fourth version with Lena at a table in a cookshop for three hours, and Aryka sat at the end of the same table and cleaned her sword and did not contribute anything, and Riarhin noticed at the end of the three hours that she had not once looked up.

Na’ar noticed.

Na’ar sat against the wall with a cup and looked at Aryka Everblood cleaning a sword she had not drawn in six weeks, in a city where no sword was any use, and then he looked at Riarhin, and then he looked back at Aryka, and he did not say anything, because nobody had pointed at anybody yet.

Chapter Nineteen: The Night Before

They went on the twelfth of Solaris, which in the house’s own reckoning was Still, because Still was the better of the two hours.

The last evening was spent on the things that are always spent on: where the horses would be, which of the nine children were to be nowhere near it, what was to happen if the laundry slope was wet, who would say what if they were stopped in the kitchen court, and the fact that nobody was to carry anything written, including the list of what they had come for, which Riarhin had said aloud to Lena twice in the afternoon.

At the end of it Lena said, “Say the last part again.”

“If it goes wrong, out through whichever door is nearest and nobody waits for anybody.”

“Right.”

“That includes me.”

“I know,” Lena said. “That part I believed the first time.”

Aryka came in late, with dust on her boots.

“Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Good.” She put her coat over the chair. “Do you want me in it?”

Riarhin had been dreading the question for a tenday and had four answers prepared, all of them courteous, and what came out was, “No.”

Aryka nodded.

“Right,” she said. “Then I’ll be about.”

And that was all. No argument, no wounded silence, no request to be given something small to hold. She ate, and went up, and Riarhin sat at the table with the fourth version in her head and felt the particular guilt of having been let off.

One thing happened that night and Riarhin got it wrong.

She went out at the eleventh hour to walk the two lanes below the Court, which she had done every night for a tenday and more.

On the second bridge she put her hand on the rail and did not say the name she was thinking, because you do not name a rival crossing a bridge at night in that city; the water remembers, and what you send comes back around your own throat. She had not believed it at nineteen and did not believe it now and had never once tested it.

At the mouth of the lane behind the cooper’s she passed a cart standing with its springs flat and a man sitting on the tail of it doing nothing at all.

He was not asleep and he was not waiting for a load. He was sitting on the tail of a laden cart in the dark, alone, in a lane one street from a pillow house, at the eleventh hour.

Riarhin looked at him for three seconds.

Then she went on, because she had seen eleven carts that month and because the man was not looking at the Court and because she had a heist in the morning and forty things in her head, and because the single simplest explanation for an armed man minding a loaded cart in Silkstrand is that somebody has paid him to mind a loaded cart in Silkstrand.

She was right about all of that.

People who knew what she was assumed she remembered everything. It was near enough true to be worth letting stand, and wrong in the one particular that mattered: she remembered what she had looked at. Her attention was a lamp and not a lit room. She could give the number of the steps on Threadneedle and the wear on the third of them and the colour of a courier’s coat-lining eleven days after seeing it — and she could walk past a man on a cart in the dark and keep nothing of him at all but cart, man, paid, because that was the whole of what she had spent on him.

Afterwards she would be able to recover the lane, and the smell of it, and the sound of the cooper’s shutters moving.

Not his face. In sixty-nine years she had never once been able to remember a thing she had not been attending to.

She went back over the water and slept four hours, which was more than she had expected to.

Act III

Chapter Twenty: Still

The job began well, which is the part nobody believes afterwards.

Nothing is owed on Still. No work is done, no wage accrues, no court sits, and in four fifths of the Amaranthine world a bailiff who tries a door on that day will find the whole street has read the same statute he has. In Silkstrand it means the pillow houses are full, and a house that is full is a house in which every person who works in it is downstairs.

The laundry slope was dry. The carter was drunk on schedule and in the right yard, and the boy who took his place had never countersigned a docket in his life and did not know he was supposed to. The seal held. Lena went up the slope first and Riarhin went second and the linen rooms smelled of lye and hot iron and somebody’s bad tobacco, and there was nobody in them, because there was never anybody in them at the fourth hour and for once the house behaved.

Two of Lena’s children were on the roofs. One was in the kitchen court with a basket, being a girl with a basket.

They were through the linen rooms in ninety seconds.

On the landing a woman came up the back way with an armful of towels and looked directly at Riarhin, and Riarhin stood aside for her with her arms full of the same towels, and the woman said something short and irritated about the hour and went past.

Lena did not look round. That was the single most professional thing Riarhin had seen anybody do in Silkstrand.

The counting room door was locked and the lock was nothing.

Lena had it in under a minute with two pieces of wire and her tongue between her teeth, and the whole time she was doing it she was breathing through her nose in a slow count, and Riarhin recognised the count because it was the one she used herself on a slope, and neither of them ever mentioned it.

Inside: a table, a lamp turned down, four chairs, the smell of ink and old paper and the particular dust that comes off ledgers.

And under the stair, the press. Three locks.

“Two of them are real,” Lena said.

“And the third?”

“The third is a noise.” She had her ear at it. “It’ll bark when it turns. That’s the point of it — she doesn’t care if you get in, she cares that somebody hears you get in.”

“Can you do it quiet?”

“I can do it slow.”

“How slow?”

“Eleven minutes.”

Riarhin looked at the door, and at the window, and at the thing she had built four versions of.

“Do it in eleven minutes.”

Chapter Twenty-One: What Was Left of It

It was not one bundle. That was the first surprise and it should not have been.

There were nine files in the press and four of them had nothing to do with anything, and Riarhin went through all nine, because she had learned a long time ago that a woman who takes only what she came for takes only what somebody expected her to want.

The fifth file had Kalrantha in it, and it was in pieces, and the pieces were wrong.

She read it on the floor with the lamp on the boards and Lena holding the shade so the light stayed off the window.

Consignment records with no contents. Not blanks — entries, complete, ruled and signed, in which the column where the cargo should be said only as before, and the entry before it said as before, and the one before that had been cut out of the book with a knife so cleanly that the stub was two fingers wide.

Payments moving through accounts that had no reason to exist: a chandler in Marcott paying a saddler in Oshiira for something neither of them had ever traded in.

A forwarding record that ended mid-column, at the foot of a page, and the next page began a different consignment in a different hand.

Three descriptions of the same object, in three hands, over what she made to be eighty years. The first said green glass in a brass frame. The second said a brass reliquary. The third said the article, eleven times, and never once said anything else, and whoever had written it had been careful and had been careful on purpose.

“It’s been gone at,” Lena said, over her shoulder.

“Yes.”

“Fire?”

“No.” Riarhin turned a leaf. “Fire takes the edges. Damp takes the bottom. Mice take the corners and the glue.” She laid three sheets side by side. “This has been gone at by hand, and by people who knew what they were looking for, and — look — not by the same hand.”

Lena looked.

“Different ink.”

“Different ink, different knife, different idea of what mattered.” Riarhin put her finger on the two-finger stub. “Whoever did that took the cargo out and left the dates. Whoever did this” — the other book, where the dates were gone and the cargo stood — “thought the dates were the dangerous part. They were not working together. They cannot have been. They disagreed about what to hide.”

She sat back on her heels.

“And they were eighty years apart.”

It should have ended there. That was what it was arranged to do.

But the thing about destroying a record is that the record is not where the journey is. The journey is in what the journey cost, and the cost gets written down by people who have never heard of the cargo and do not care.

Riarhin had spent two years learning the wrong lesson from Pell’s catalogue, which was that somebody would eventually write down what the thing was. Nobody ever had. What they had written down, over and over, in four languages and eight hands, was what it took to move it.

Fodder. You cannot hide fodder. Fodder tells you how many animals and how many days.

Water carried rather than drawn, which tells you the country.

Guard wages, which tell you the risk, and insurance, which tells you what somebody with money believed about the risk, and both of which are always, always paid by somebody who wants a receipt.

And days. Days are the honest number. Nobody erases days; there is no reason to.

The wages were Dhaharan, which meant tally-sheets, and Dhaharan caravan houses keep the Old Count and write their sheets with nine of the ten days named and the ninth left as a space. She had asked a clerk about that once, in another country, a long time ago, and had been told it was a scribal convention, in the voice of a man who had been asked before.

On four of the sheets somebody had written in the space.

Not a word. A mark — made four times, in four hands, across what she made to be sixty years, by four people who had each decided that this particular day required something put in it.

No two of the four had made the same mark.

Riarhin looked at that longer than she looked at anything else in the file. Then she set it aside, because it was not what she had come for, and because some part of her that she had not consulted had decided quite firmly that she was not going to think about it tonight.

She worked on the boards for thirty-one minutes with Lena holding the lamp and saying nothing at all, and at the end of it she had a shape.

Not a vault. Not a city. Not a tomb.

Fodder for eleven days beyond the last watered stage. Water carried, not drawn. Insurance that four separate houses had refused and a fifth had written at a rate that meant they expected to pay. Guards hired in Oshiira and paid off in Oshiira, which meant nobody had gone on with it and come back, or nobody had come back.

Eleven days east and south of the last place anybody sensible goes.

Ashaan. Past Galanina and every gate with a clerk behind it, off any road a foreigner would choose, out into the Red Wastes.

Riarhin sat on the floor of another woman’s counting room with the whole of it in front of her and understood exactly what she had.

She had a word she could not define, a desert the size of a country, no map, no survivor, no scholar who had got further, and the knowledge that the obscuring had been done on purpose, repeatedly, by people who had not agreed with each other about anything except that the next person should know less.

It was the worst piece of information she had ever paid for.

She felt better than she had felt in years.

She did not notice that either, which was becoming a habit.

“Well?” Lena said.

“I’ve got it.”

“Is it good?”

“It’s terrible.”

“Right,” said Lena, and began putting the other four files back exactly as they had been, because that was the arrangement, and because a clean theft leaves nobody holding anything.

Chapter Twenty-Two: Wrong

It did not go wrong because anybody was clever.

A woman called Ilsabet, who had worked in that house for six years and had a bad tooth, got up in the night to find oil of cloves, and the cloves were in the still-room, and the still-room is off the counting room passage.

That was all. There was no plan anywhere in it.

She came down the passage with a candle at the wrong hour of the wrong night and she saw the counting room door standing open, which it never did, and she did the single most reasonable thing available to her, which was to scream.

Riarhin got out of the counting room in four seconds and up the passage in six.

The trouble was not the scream. The trouble was that the scream was in the wrong part of the house — behind them, between them and the linen rooms, which meant the slope, which was the way out, now had a woman standing in it making a noise.

Riarhin adapted. It took her the time it takes to cross a passage. Kitchen court instead of slope; the girl with the basket was still in the kitchen court; she would be seen going out and that was acceptable because being seen going out at the fifth hour with a basket is a thing that happens.

That would have worked.

It failed because of the second thing, which also had no plan in it: the kitchen court gate had been chained on the inside that evening, by a man who had been told a fortnight ago to chain it if the carter was ever drunk again.

Riarhin put her hand on the chain and understood in half a second that she was now inside a building with two exits and both of them full.

“Roof,” said Lena.

“Your roof’s four storeys and I’ve got papers.”

“Then put the papers down.”

“No.”

And Lena, who owed her nothing and was owed nothing, and who had made that precise arrangement in a room above a cookshop so that this moment would be simple, said, “Right,” and turned round and went back into the house with her.

Na’ar came over the kitchen court wall at the sixth hour and the evening finally arrived at something he understood.

There were four of the house’s men in the court by then, which is four more than a pillow house usually has awake, and they were not soldiers, and he was.

It took eleven seconds. He did not kill anybody, which Riarhin noticed, and which she understood was not mercy but arithmetic — you cannot carry a dead man’s house through a city, and Na’ar had been doing this for thirty years, and he knew what a body cost afterwards. He broke an arm, a knee, a jaw and something in a fourth man’s chest, and he did it with the joy entirely visible, and then he put his back against the gate and pulled the chain out of the wall with the staple still on it.

“Out,” he said.

And that would have worked too.

Chapter Twenty-Three: Her City

There were eleven men in the lane.

Not house men. Not the Watch. Men from three different companies, standing easy, spread out with the gaps a professional leaves, and at the head of them a woman Riarhin had seen once, forty days ago, holding a door.

Celise.

“Put it down, dear,” Celise said.

They went back into the house, because going back into the house was the only direction with a floor.

What happened over the next half hour Riarhin would recover afterwards in complete order and would not enjoy.

The roofs went first. Lena sent a child up and the child came back down in under two minutes and said there were men on the ridge of the counting house opposite with lanterns, and that they were not moving, because they did not have to.

The Bell Street way went second: a door that had been unlocked every night for nineteen years was locked, and it was locked because a woman who had used it every night for nineteen years had been asked, that afternoon, whether she would mind.

The wharf lane went third, and it did not go because of guards. It went because the two porters who were going to be paid to be asleep were awake and apologetic and would not look at Lena, and one of them said, “She sent for my brother,” and that was the whole of it and there was nothing on earth to be done about it.

“She’s not chasing us,” Riarhin said.

Lena was breathing hard through her nose in the slow count.

“She’s closing doors,” Riarhin said. “She’s been closing them for an hour. She didn’t need to know what we wanted or where we’d be. She just needed to know somebody was in her house, and then close everything, one at a time, in the order a person would try them.”

“That’s four hundred people.”

“That’s four hundred pieces,” Riarhin said. “And she’s spending all of them tonight, and she can, because she’ll get them back.”

They ended in a long room on the first floor with three exits and men on all of them, and the fire in the grate burning quite normally, because nobody had thought to put it out.

Riarhin went through it one more time.

She had no tunnel. Lena had no forgotten stair; Lena had told her three days ago that there was no forgotten stair, and had been right, and had been right in the specific way of somebody who had checked. Na’ar could take a door — he could take any one door in that house — and the moment he did, the other two filled, and they were four people including a girl of nineteen and a woman holding a sheaf of papers.

She reached for the thing she had been reaching for since the lane.

The man on the cart. The eleventh hour, the lane behind the cooper’s, the flat springs.

She could recover the smell of the lane. She could recover the sound of the shutters moving, and the number of steps from the corner to the cart, and that the near wheel had a repaired felloe.

She could not recover his face. She had never looked at it. She had spent three seconds on him and all three had gone on the cart.

Cart, man, paid.

And there it stopped, the way it always stopped, because whatever she was, she was not a woman who remembered things. She was a woman who remembered what she had spent herself on, and she had spent nothing on him, and so there was nothing there — nothing at all, a clean and perfectly ordinary blank — and she stood in a burning-bright room in another woman’s house and understood that she had walked past the answer and had not been looking at it.

Serafine came in at the seventh hour with the veil on.

She did not bring men in with her. There were men, and they were in the doorway, and she came through them and stood in the middle of her own floor with her hands folded, and she took her time, and the time was the point.

“Well,” she said.

Riarhin said nothing.

“You went by the counting house and not into it,” Serafine said. “Somebody good told you to do that. I should like to know who, eventually, but not tonight.” The veil turned. “You have my fifth file in your hand. You may keep holding it. It will not become yours.”

“It isn’t yours either.”

“No,” Serafine agreed, pleasantly. “It is in my house. In this city that is a distinction that costs four hundred crowns and nine years to litigate, and you are welcome to begin.”

She looked at Lena then, for slightly longer than she had looked at anybody.

She did not say anything to her at all. That was worse than anything she could have said, and Riarhin watched Lena take it standing.

“Now,” Serafine said. “I am going to be reasonable, because I am always reasonable, and because it is the cheapest thing in the room. You will give me the file. You will tell me who sent you by the door. And then we will discuss what the three of you are going to do for me, at length, over several years.”

“And if we don’t?”

“Then we will discuss it a different way,” Serafine said, “and I would so much rather not, because I have eleven people downstairs whose evening this is not, and because hostages are vulgar, and because they work.”

It was, Riarhin thought, the most honest threat she had ever been given.

And it was at that point that Aryka Everblood, who had been standing at the back of the room for four minutes with her thumbs in her belt and had not been looked at by anybody, said:

“I have money. I have men.”

Chapter Twenty-Four: With the Men

Serafine turned her head.

It was the first movement Riarhin had seen her make that she had not chosen in advance.

“I beg your pardon?”

“I have money,” Aryka said again, in court Vhasian, unhurried, every vowel finished, a woman declining a second cup of tea. “And I have men.”

Riarhin looked at her.

Aryka was standing at the back of the room with her thumbs in her belt and her coat dusty at the hem, and she had been standing there for four minutes while a woman in a veil explained how the next several years of their lives would go, and she had not said a word, and she was not armed.

“Where is your money?” Serafine said.

Aryka paused. It was a fractional pause and Riarhin would take it out and look at it many times afterwards, because it was the only piece of theatre the woman allowed herself in the whole of that year.

“With the men.”

And something in Riarhin’s mind, which had been holding forty days of small correct observations in a drawer with the wrong label on it, tipped over.

The mercenaries who belonged to nobody.

The carts riding low.

A man touching two fingers to his brow in a street where he had been told not to.

What needs be done.

Dust on her boots at dusk. Every day. For forty days.

An armed man sitting alone on the tail of a laden cart at the eleventh hour in a lane one street from this house, whose face she had not looked at, because she had been counting the felloes of a wheel.

“And where,” said Serafine, slowly, “are your men?”

“Surrounding the building.”

Nobody moved.

Riarhin found that she had turned all the way round to look at Aryka Everblood and that she had no idea what her own face was doing.

Serafine recovered in about two seconds, which Riarhin respected.

“That,” she said, “is a great many men to move through my city without my hearing of it.”

“It isn’t, though.” Aryka took her thumbs out of her belt. “That’s the part I found delightful. There are four hundred armed men for hire within a mile of this room and nobody here counts them, because counting them would be like counting gulls. I have been buying gulls since Cinaria.”

“For what purpose?”

“Acasia,” Aryka said. “I’m building something there and it wants money, and I came south behind a woman who is very good at making a city show you where its money is kept, and I have spent forty days writing down everything she made move.”

She said it without any particular emphasis. She was not confessing. She was answering a question she had been asked, in order, because she had been asked it.

Riarhin heard herself say, “You said you were done.”

Aryka looked over at her.

“I was done,” she said. “I am done. With Viterra, with Vhasia, with Veralyn, with my cousins and my nephew and thirty years of a chair I was never going to be allowed to sit in. I told you that at a wall in the hills and I told you again at a ford and again at an inn, and you decided I was sulking.” A small shrug. “Which was fair. I’d have thought it too.”

“We have hostages,” Serafine said.

It was said perfectly evenly. She had not raised her voice once all night.

“There are eleven people downstairs whose evening this is not, as I mentioned, and there are four more upstairs, and we have ways of being — persuasive is the polite word. I dislike it. I will use it.”

Aryka nodded slowly, as though considering a reasonable offer.

“Yes, well,” she said. “That’s why I told them to set fire to this pillow house.”

The veil tilted.

Riarhin, who had spent forty days learning to read a piece of black cloth, watched Madam Serafine of the Velvet Court — who had answered every question in her house for nineteen years before it was finished being asked — find nothing whatever to say.

It lasted perhaps three heartbeats.

Then, from somewhere below and to the east, in the part of the house where the linen was kept, a woman began to scream a different sort of scream.

Na’ar smiled.

Chapter Twenty-Five: Doors

The fire did not take Serafine’s power. It took her floors.

That was the whole of it, and Riarhin understood it while she was going down the back stair with Lena’s fist in the back of her coat: there is no arrangement of four hundred obligations that holds a corridor when the corridor is full of people trying to leave it.

The men on the roofs came off the roofs, because men on a roof with a lit building under them stop being an arrangement and become men on a roof.

The eleven in the lane broke into threes, because three separate house doors opened at once and patrons came out of all of them, and half of Silkstrand’s Censors and factors and shipping men were suddenly in a public street in states of dress they would be explaining for a decade, and no company sergeant alive holds a cordon through that.

And Celise — Riarhin saw this from the turn of the stair and would remember it exactly — Celise stopped doing everything she had been doing, and went up, against the flow, towards the fourth floor, where the women who could not get out on their own were.

Serafine went with her.

She did not give an order to hold the doors. She could have. There was still an hour of the evening in which holding the doors would have got her the file.

She went up her own burning stair after her people, and left every door in the building to whoever wanted it.

Riarhin, going the other way with another woman’s papers inside her coat, found that she could not make that fact do what she wanted it to do, and stopped trying, and went out through the kitchen court.

The chain was still hanging out of the wall with the staple on it.

Na’ar came out last and did not hurry, and had a man over his shoulder in a way that suggested the man had been in the wrong place rather than on the wrong side, and he put him down against the opposite wall and went back for a second one.

Lena had four of her children in the court and was counting them by touching each one on the head, which Riarhin had not seen her do before.

“Nine,” Lena said. “All nine. Two went over the Bell Street wall.”

“Go.”

“I’m owed wages.”

“Lena.”

“I’m owed wages,” Lena said, “and you’re going to pay them tomorrow, in the fish market, at the second hour, and then we’re quits and nobody carries anything.” She was already moving. “Don’t be late. I don’t wait.”

“I know,” said Riarhin, to nobody, because the girl had gone.

Outside, in the street, the operation expanded.

Riarhin came round the corner of the cooper’s yard into a lane full of wagons and men who were not fighting anybody, and understood in one look what the next two hours were going to be.

The patrons of the Velvet Court came out of a burning pillow house into the arms of what they took to be rescue, and discovered that the soldiers in the street had a different understanding of the evening. Purses went. Rings went. A Censor’s brother in a borrowed coat was relieved of a chain worth four years of a carter’s wages and was so grateful to be alive that he thanked the man who took it.

Nobody was killed in the lane. Riarhin checked, later, because it mattered to her in a way she did not examine.

But the definition of the objective did what definitions do when there are wagons and insufficient supervision. The house next door was a bonded warehouse. The one beyond that was a goldsmith’s factor. By the third hour there were men in both, and in a third house that had nothing to do with anything except that its door had been left open, and what had begun as one woman exploiting one heist had become, by any reasonable description, a sack of four streets of downtown Silkstrand.

Riarhin stood in the middle of it with a stolen file inside her coat and watched a man carry a chair out of a building and put it on a wagon.

Somewhere under all of it there was an older thing turning over, and nobody in that street said a word about it, and Riarhin only had it because she had spent an afternoon with a woman selling fried dough.

Nineteen years ago a guildhouse in the Dye Yards had burned, because somebody had given the order to fire the roof, and eight children had got out of it.

Tonight a pillow house was burning, and the thieves were going out through the doors.

Riarhin did not say it to anybody. There was nobody to say it to who did not already know it better than she did.

Chapter Twenty-Six: The Wheel

By morning Silkstrand was smoking and the Watch had discovered the fire.

Riarhin paid Lena in the fish market at the second hour, in coin, counted out on a barrel, in front of a woman gutting mackerel who did not look up once.

“That’s the work,” Lena said.

“That’s the work.”

“Then we’re quits.”

“We’re quits.”

Lena put the money away in four places, which Riarhin noted and approved of, and then stood there for a moment with her hands in her pockets, nineteen years old and perfectly steady, in a city that was going to spend the next month finding out who had done what.

“You said I’d want a reason to go,” she said.

“I did.”

“I’ve got one.” She nodded across the water, where the smoke was. “She’ll work out it was local by tonight. She’ll work out it was me by the ninth day. And she isn’t going to do anything about it for about two years, because she’s got a house to rebuild and four hundred people to remind, and then she’s going to do something about it very carefully.”

“That’s an accurate reading.”

“I’ve had it a while.” Lena took her hands out of her pockets. “Brass Gate, maybe. There’s a road through Milhaven and a man there who owes my father’s people a favour that nobody’s collected.”

“Luck’s a loan.”

“So it’s about time somebody collected it,” Lena said, and grinned, and that was the last Riarhin saw of her for a long while.

She went and looked at the Velvet Court in daylight because she owed it that much.

The east wing had gone. The rest stood, blackened at the windows, with the roof open over the linen rooms and a great deal of water in the street, and people going in and out carrying things that were not worth carrying.

Serafine was in the road in a plain dress with no veil on.

Riarhin saw her face for the first time and it was an ordinary face, about fifty, tired, with a badly healed line at the jaw, and she was directing four men and a cart with the brisk unbothered competence of a woman who had been burned out before and had not enjoyed it either time.

She saw Riarhin.

She did not send anybody. She looked at her across forty feet of wet street for about four seconds — long enough to be certain she had been seen looking — and then went back to the cart.

That was all. There was no speech. There was not going to be a speech; there was going to be two years of nothing, and then something.

Riarhin found she preferred it to the alternative, and walked away with the whole of the rest of her life containing that woman in it somewhere.

She read the file properly in a room above a cookshop with the shutters open.

It said what it had said on the counting room floor. It did not get better. Eleven days beyond the last watered stage, out past everywhere anybody administers, into the Red Wastes; and a word; and eighty years of people deciding, one after another and out of step with each other, that whoever came next should know less.

Na’ar came in with bread.

“Good?”

“It’s a desert,” Riarhin said.

“How big?”

“Nobody knows. That’s the sort of desert it is.”

Na’ar tore the bread in half and gave her the larger piece, which he always did, and thought about it.

“Then we will need water,” he said, and sat down.

Chapter Twenty-Seven: The Red Wastes

The wind decided the route, which Riarhin resented and could not argue with.

She had assumed a ship to Ashaan, because Ashaan is on this sea and the Red Wastes are in Ashaan, and that is the shape the thing has on a chart. Four conversations on the wharves took the assumption away from her. The lane that was open in Haying was the Akilan lane, the long southwest run to Mbaro, and the lane she wanted was the one that would not be open again until the season turned, and no master on that water would take a fee to sail into a season.

She stood on a quay and had the geography explained to her by a woman with a chart and no patience, and understood that she had spent two years thinking about a desert and had never once thought about the air above the sea in front of it.

So: Mbaro. And then up-country, and then east, and into the Wastes from the outside.

Which was, it turned out, the better road anyway, and for a reason that had nothing to do with wind.

Ashaan is governed, on paper, by the Veiled Sisters. In practice it is administered by the Esoiti, who control every ledger and every gate in the country, and who are very good at both. To come into Ashaan by an Ashaani port is to be written down at the water’s edge by a clerk who will be believed, and thereafter to exist, in a place where existing is a service you pay for.

Riarhin had no intention of existing in Ashaan.

Below the Esoiti and not under them is the Black Hand, which hunts without a uniform. What she knew about the Hand she had from four people and it did not flatter anybody: that they are human, that they were bakers and clerks and mothers and soldiers before they were the Hand, that some of them chose the knife and most of them were offered a choice between service and vanishing, and that they do not sleep well. She had expected, from the name, something worse and simpler. What she had been given was worse and not simple at all.

And above all of it, the Sisters.

She had the shape of them from people who would not say the whole of it in a room with a window. Not officials. Not sorcerers, in the sense she understood the word. They do not negotiate and they do not threaten; they state what will be, and then it is. You cannot bribe a natural disaster. You can only try not to be standing where it is looking.

Riarhin had spent forty days learning to read a woman in a veil, and was going to a country where the veil is the law and the colours mean things — red for mourning, white for marriage, black for the Sisters, grey for a certain kind of agent, torn for a fugitive, and no veil at all for a fool.

She noticed the rhyme. She declined to make anything of it.

Her plan, arrived at over two days and one bad night, was to go around.

Not into Galanina. Not through any gate with an Esoiti clerk behind it. Mbaro first, and up onto the Oshiiran plateau where the millet gives out, and then east along the caravan water to the last place that has a name, and then into the Wastes from a direction nobody administers.

It was an excellent plan. It had the flaw that all such plans have, which is that a route designed to avoid attention is a shape, and a shape can be read, and the people best placed to read it are precisely the people it was drawn against.

It had a second flaw that was newer and worse, and she found it on the quay at Mbaro before she had found a guide.

An expedition is not a decision. It is a manifest. Animals, water-skins, grain, salt, canvas, hire, wages, a surety against the animals, and a weigh-house stamp on all of it — and Mbaro is a city of weigh-houses and ledgers with a Granary Tower that nothing else is permitted to overtop, and the Vermilion Corps weighs loyalty there as carefully as it weighs grain.

To go into the desert unnoticed, she would first have to be written down in the most careful accounts in Akilan.

Ledgers travel. She of all people knew what could be reconstructed out of fodder and water and days.

The Esoiti would notice.

And the Black Hand would notice the Esoiti noticing, which was a distinction Riarhin did not yet understand and which was going to cost her a great deal — because from where she stood there were two sets of people she had specifically intended to avoid, and both of them were about to become interested in her, and she could not tell them apart.

That was a problem for a later country.

What she needed now was maps, water, animals, a guide who had been out past the last named place and come back, and a quantity of knowledge she did not have.

And, most irritatingly, other people.

Because Oshiira was not the hills and Ashaan was not Silkstrand. In the hills she had been fluent; she could read a stone thrown at a plum tree. In Silkstrand she had been fluent in a way she had not enjoyed but had certainly possessed, because her mother had paid for the lessons without ever calling them lessons.

Out there she would notice everything and know what none of it meant. A price that was wrong, a greeting done in the second person, the dust on a stranger being white where it should be red — she would have all of it, exactly, in order, and be unable to say what any of it was for.

She was leaving the places where instinct made her fluent and going somewhere she was going to have to ask.

She turned that over for a while and found that it did not frighten her as much as it ought to have done.

There were two things she did not put in the reckoning, because she did not know they were in it.

The first was that Ashaan hangs its dead on the walls of its capital in brass, and that the light they give is green.

She had four sheets of paper and a description eighty years old and copied three times: green glass in a brass frame, out of the Himadri, a wish with a clause folded into it. She had never once been able to decide whether that was a thing or a rumour about a thing.

Now she was going to a country where green light in brass is furniture.

It was either the answer or it was a coincidence of the ordinary kind, and there was no instrument anywhere in the world that would tell her which from this side of the water, and she knew it, and she bought the passage anyway.

The second she did not notice at all.

In Ashaan you do not say the names of the dead, because the Sisters bind spirits to names, and a spoken dead name is a door. When a person in that country has done a serious wrong, what they offer in apology is a name — a dead one, a forgotten one, a name the Sisters do not have yet — and it is the most expensive thing they own, because almost nobody has more than a few.

Riarhin had every name she had ever been given.

Farrick. Parrin. Tacen. The two in the loft, whose names she had from a weeping man against a rock. Ylena. Gyllan. Kellen, who had the grey. Orvain. Kargath. A woman with a silver tooth on a wet night fifty years ago who had never told her one, and whose face she could produce entire.

She was carrying, into the only market in the world that trades in them, a fortune.

She did not know that. It was a fact she had, filed with ten thousand others, in the place where she kept things she had not spent herself on yet.

They took passage on the nineteenth of Solaris, on a millet hull running empty to Mbaro, with two horses she did not expect to see again and a Ykrul who had never been on salt water and was looking forward to it with an enthusiasm Riarhin found ominous.

Aryka came down to the water.

She was not coming north and Riarhin finally believed her, which had taken, in the end, a burning building and a sentence about men. She had the men, and she now had a great deal more money than she had arrived with, and four weeks past the smoke was Acasia — a broken march of petty warlords, free companies thick with other people’s agents, a Margravine’s iron law in one quarter and a Lame King’s paper legitimacy in another, and over the whole of it a Curse that eats broken promises.

Riarhin thought about that last part for slightly longer than was comfortable.

“You’ve made a lot of promises this month,” she said.

Aryka looked out at the harbour.

“I have.”

“In Acasia.”

“In Acasia.” She put her hands behind her back. “I know what the country does. I have known since I was nineteen and everybody who rides with me knows it too, which is the only reason any of them believe a word I say. It is a difficult place to lie in. That is what makes it worth having.”

Then she held out her hand, which was not a Vhasian gesture and not an Acasi one, and Riarhin took it.

“If you come back that way,” Aryka said, “there will be somewhere to come back to.”

“That’s a promise.”

“Yes,” said Aryka Everblood. “It is.”

Riarhin had a word and a desert.

For a long time she had gone wherever the road happened to run: work, town, another man who wanted killing, another fire, another morning. She had been extremely good at that and had mistaken it, for a while, for freedom.

Now she knew which road she wanted, and she wanted it, and there was a thing at the end of it that would be there whether or not she arrived.

She called that urgency. Na’ar stood at the rail beside her and did not ask her anything, and neither of them looked at it more closely than that.

Behind them, on a quay that was still smelling of last night, Aryka Everblood turned round and surveyed what was by any reasonable definition a sack.

Her voice carried a remarkable distance over water.

“Wagons! Carts, you twits. Gold is heavy. And you’ll have to drag them, the horses have fled.”

Somebody shouted a question from the far end of the quay.

“Where’s the Watch?”

“Fled on the horses.”

Aryka considered this.

“Good.”

Riarhin went on watching the city get smaller.

“Put down that marble bust, Keygen—”
