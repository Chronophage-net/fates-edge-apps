Part One The words at the gate

Chapter 1 — Other Appointments

Elena had wanted the room for its window. In winter the light reached the table until well past noon; in summer she could open the lower shutters and hear the fountain without being obliged to see everyone who washed something in it. The landlord had asked more because of the window. She had paid more. Now a woman with an infected heel sat in the best of that light, explaining that the room was too warm.

“It is,” Elena agreed. “Your stocking, please.”

“I could have taken it off at home.”

“I need to see how the dressing has worn. Slowly, if it hurts.”

The woman bent forward and winced. Elena knelt before she could put her foot on the floor. Three days ago she had opened a narrow place beside the heel and drawn out a sliver of wood blackened almost to the color of the wound. Yesterday the redness had begun to retreat. Today Belia had walked across Millhaven to show her that it was better.

Elena set the foot in its basin. The water had cooled enough to touch without first remembering not to.

“Who brought you?”

“I brought myself.”

“Ah.”

Belia looked down at her. She was a large woman, accustomed to lifting things without asking for assistance, and in a chair too low for her she appeared to have been folded carelessly. Her boots stood beside the door. The left had an extra piece of leather nailed across its heel.

“I wasn’t going to send for a litter.”

“You have a son with a handcart.”

“He has onions in it.”

Elena washed the wound. Belia gripped her own knee and made no further argument until the dressing was on, at which point she asked whether she might go home.

“If somebody takes you.”

“You could send the girl.”

At the other table Renn looked up from her copying. She was twenty-six, taller than Elena, and had been treating feet before Elena could afford this window. A lock of hair had escaped her scarf and hung beside a pale burn on her neck.

“I have the cooper next,” Renn said. “Let us send word to your son. You shouldn’t have to walk on that.”

Belia considered them both, then asked for paper to send to her son. Elena moved the basin aside. A little water had reached the floor where the boards dipped, and she put a rag over it before someone could slip. The dip annoyed her every time. Thane had offered to repair it if she could give him two days without patients. She had asked whether he could do it at night, and he had asked whether she could treat his next cut while asleep. They had been exceedingly polite to one another for an hour afterward.

Renn passed Belia a clean sheet and a blunt pencil. The woman wrote slowly, squinting into the light she had complained of. Outside, the fountain chain rattled. A child was making the bucket strike the lip on purpose; another was telling him to stop, with a mounting excitement that suggested she hoped he would not.

Elena washed her hands. The water made the skin between her fingers sting. She had promised herself a new salve, properly scented, the next time the spice carrier came. The plain fat worked perfectly well. She was tired of smelling it.

Sariel came in carrying a small box wrapped in red cloth.

“If that’s another lock,” Elena said, “leave it on the shelf.”

“It’s buttons.”

“For me?”

“For a woman who thinks her sister stole them. Her sister thinks she has lost her mind. I’ve found the box in the first woman’s own flour bin, so both of them are going to be disappointed.”

Sariel stood beside the door until Elena had dried her hands. Her old gray-green coat hung open, showing the new dark facing someone had stitched over its blue lining at the throat. She had once come home from a journey wearing it as though the purchase itself were an accomplishment. Now she used one of its pockets for wire and complained when the wire found its way through.

“Tema’s here,” she said. “He wants all five.”

“Can he wait until supper? I don’t want to hurry anyone out.”

“I’ve asked him to wait. He has brought a letter from Sarnai.”

Elena looked at her properly. The red cloth around the box had caught on a splinter. Sariel freed it with her thumbnail, taking more care than the cloth required.

“Is somebody ill?”

“Several people, he says. Somewhere west.”

Elena looked toward the woman waiting behind her. “I can’t leave this morning.”

“I know. I’ll tell him supper.”

Belia held up the paper. “Before anybody goes west, somebody can take this two streets.”

Sariel accepted it, read the direction, and looked at the patient.

“I’ll take it now,” Sariel said.

“Thank you. Tell him I have money for the onions.”

She left with both errands. Elena watched the door settle behind her, then turned back to Belia, who had managed to put half the stocking on over her dressing.

“That stays off.”

“I don’t care to sit with my foot showing.”

Elena took a cloth from the warmed shelf and laid it over the woman’s leg. It was one of the cloths she had bought for exactly this use, thick enough to feel like a covering, with a border that survived washing. For a moment Belia ceased trying to leave.

They met in the wool-store loft because it still had the largest table. The table now belonged, in disputed proportion, to themselves and a woman who cut sailcloth there on market mornings. Her shears lay on a square of felt. Kestra moved the felt and shears together before putting down her book.

“Nobody use those to cut a wick,” she said.

Benjamin, who had been reaching toward the lamp, withdrew his hand.

“I was going to move them.”

Kestra made room beside her book. “Here. She said the damp goes into the hinge.”

Thane came last, smelling of shaved horn. He had a drawing rolled into his left sleeve and a pale mark on his cheek where he had rubbed himself with a dusty wrist. Elena recognized the look of a man asked to stop work when he had only just understood how it should go.

“Before you tell us anything,” he said to Tema, “when?”

Tema had taken off his traveling coat. There was more gray in his hair than Elena remembered, although that happened whenever she saw him after an interval. He stood when Thane came in, then seemed to remember he was among people who had seen him unload spoiled grain and sat down again.

“Within four days would be best.”

“No.”

“Five?”

Thane drew the roll from his sleeve and smoothed it on his knee. “Dorra has a wheel assembly coming here. I told her I’d fit the new teeth. If the carrier arrives on time I need six days. If he doesn’t, I need longer.”

Tema nodded. “I can pay for another craftsman.”

“So can Dorra.”

Sariel sat backward on a chair, chin on the rail, watching Tema alter the beginning of whatever speech he had brought. Elena was pleased to see it happen. Then she was ashamed of the pleasure; the man looked tired, and the letter beside his hand was stained where rain had found it.

“Tell us,” she said.

Casta Ferrum had a new ceremony at its Bronze Gate. Tema began with that, and with the caravans that had passed safely since its introduction. Two companies formerly unwilling to travel without a dozen guards had reduced their escort. A baker from the eastern settlements had brought his daughters into town for a wedding and returned with them after dark. These things had happened. Tema put them on the table before the rest.

“The Prefect calls it a peace undertaking. Carriers swear not to aid raiders, not to conceal a theft, not to leave a duty unfinished. There are more words. Different words for different work.”

Kestra opened her book. “Swear to whom?”

“To the Prefect’s peace. With a hand on an old seal they dug out from under the gate.”

“An oath object.”

“That’s what the letter calls it.” Tema took the folded sheet from beside his cup and passed it to her. “Iron Seal of Varus. Mostly bronze, I’m told. The name is old.”

Elena waited while Kestra read. Benjamin had begun turning the lamp by its base, seeking a place where the light did not shine in Tema’s eyes. Sariel caught the lamp before it reached the shears.

“What happens when they break the undertaking?” Elena asked.

“They become ill.”

“What sort of ill?”

“A cough. Fevers. A man forgets a road he has driven for fifteen years. A porter puts down a basket and cannot remember who is paying him.”

“That can happen without an oath.”

Tema reached inside his coat, which hung over the chair behind him, and brought out a second, smaller paper. He did not unfold it immediately.

“One of my drivers stayed at Casta Ferrum. His wife sent this. He knows her when she comes into the room. Sometimes. He calls the children by the names of horses he used to own. Then he knows what he has done, and asks her to take them out.”

Elena stopped looking at the paper. “Is he here?”

“No. I couldn’t get him here. The officer at the gate says his undertaking requires him to remain available for carriage.”

Benjamin pushed the lamp away from him and left it alone.

Tema unfolded the second note. There were only six lines on it. He read the name of a local practitioner, Dometta, and an address beside the baths. Elena repeated the name so she would remember it.

“Sarnai says the object belongs below the Gate,” he went on. “The people who looked after the graves were turned away when the digging began. She’s tried asking. Marcellus will let them in if she accepts his terms first.”

“And she sent for us?” Sariel asked.

“She sent to me. She remembers you.”

Thane scratched his cheek and discovered the horn dust. “We haven’t seen her in years. I’d like to know what she expects of us.”

“No,” Tema said. “I came because I remembered you too.”

He let that sit for a moment, perhaps expecting it to do more work than it did. Then he said he wanted the Seal removed and delivered through him to Sarnai. He had money put aside. The road mattered to him, and a promise of payment dependent on the road becoming profitable would be worth more than kind words from a grateful traveler. He named three hundred marks and a share of the route’s earnings for six months.

Sariel turned her chair around.

“You’ve come to hire thieves.”

“I know you have done that work.”

“You know we have names on an old list in Silkstrand. There are people who still prefer that list to anything we’ve done since.”

“I didn’t come to threaten you.”

“Then don’t tell me how discreet you’ll be.”

Tema’s mouth closed. Elena had not heard him offer discretion, but she could see that he had been about to.

Outside, someone called for the loft key. Benjamin went down and returned with the sail cutter’s boy, who picked up the shears and inspected them suspiciously. He took the felt as well. They waited for his footsteps to leave the stairs.

Kestra laid Sarnai’s letter flat. “We don’t know what taking it out will do.”

“The people who tended it—” Tema began.

“Aren’t here. I would like to hear them. I would also like to know whether the Prefect’s physician has treated these cases and what the terms actually say.”

Elena nodded. “And speak to Dometta before we arrive promising to cure her patients.”

“All right. An investigation.” He reached for his cup, discovered it empty, and put it back. “What will that cost me?”

They did not answer together. Thane wanted time to finish the wheel. Elena had to arrange her patients and pay Renn for hours she had not expected to work. Benjamin said they would need a return allowance whether or not the theft went ahead, and Sariel asked how openly they could admit whom they represented. The sixty marks they finally agreed upon bought a visit, inquiries and a report. It did not buy the relic.

Tema counted thirty onto the table. Sariel divided the advance while Kestra wrote the limits on a new sheet. Elena could feel the next week tightening around her appointments. She wanted to go. She also wanted the coopers and sore heels and inconvenient people at her window to remain there when she returned, with their irritation intact.

“Six days,” Thane said, when the sheet reached him. “I can finish in six if the assembly comes tomorrow.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“I’ll tell you tomorrow.”

Tema accepted that with a visible effort. He signed below the fee and folded his copy into the coat pocket from which the driver’s letter had come. Elena took the smaller note with Dometta’s address. For now it was the only thing she wanted to carry.

Renn listened to the arrangements without sitting down. They were closing the room, and the last kettle had made the air thick. Elena took the clean dressings out of the drying cupboard while Renn checked the latch on the medicine box.

“I’ll take your morning patients,” Renn said. “The afternoons are mine already.”

“There will be extra washing.”

“Then pay Sella for extra washing.”

“I meant I should leave more cloth.”

Renn turned. “You are allowed to go, Elena. You are also allowed to employ me to look after people. You don’t have to put enough linen in this room to make me unnecessary.”

Elena set down the stack. She had cut and hemmed half of it herself. In the low light the folds looked blue.

“Belia won’t keep off that foot.”

“I met Belia before you did. She didn’t keep off things then either.”

They worked out the additional hours and wrote them down. Renn wanted the Sunday morning free. Her brother’s youngest was being named, and she intended to arrive early enough to object to whatever name had been chosen. Elena said she would ask Maela to cover. She did not offer to postpone the journey.

On the sixth morning Belia’s son arrived with the handcart, onions removed. Elena watched him help his mother through the door. Belia gave him directions he clearly knew, then glanced back at Elena.

“Bring a decent oil from the west. They sell rubbish here.”

“For your foot?”

“For my hair. My foot isn’t the whole of me.”

Elena promised to look. When they had gone, she put Dometta’s address in the inner compartment of her medicine bag, beside a letter from Tavia she had already read enough times to soften its folds. Tavia had written about a neighbor’s ridiculous wedding and a new pear tree. Elena had begun her reply with news of a commission. She took it out again and added the part about Belia and her hair.

By the time she locked the window, Thane was downstairs arguing with a carrier over the proper way to lift a wheel he had finished the previous night. Benjamin waited beside two traveling bundles and a third which Elena suspected contained blankets for people they had not met. Sariel leaned against the wall, eating the last of a warm roll.

“Are we ready?” she asked.

“Renn needs the other bag,” Benjamin said. “The one by the door. I’ll bring it up before we go.”

Elena gave Renn the key. It had worn a smooth place in her palm. Renn put it in her own pocket without ceremony and went inside to open the room.

Chapter 2 — The Passenger

The carrier overtook them above the last vineyards, where the road narrowed between walls and nobody could move aside without first deciding who would back up. He had bells on his leading mule. Sariel heard them for a long time before the cart appeared, and had time to become annoyed with its driver for hurrying and with herself for enjoying the sound.

“Halven,” Tema called, lifting a hand.

The man pulled up. His mule stopped with its nose almost against Benjamin’s shoulder and began to investigate the blanket roll.

“I’ve got someone for you,” Halven said.

Sariel looked at the cart. Three women sat among wrapped bundles, one holding a cage with a cloth over it. The youngest had her feet on the axle housing. She watched Tema greet the driver, then looked straight at Sariel.

“No, you haven’t,” she said. “I’m going to the same place.”

Halven turned in his seat. “I meant they could tell you about it.”

“I’ll ask them.”

He shrugged, not offended, and brought the mule forward until they reached a break in the wall. Sariel stepped onto the bank. The young woman climbed down, drew her bag after her and waited while Halven checked a strap. She was small, brown, dressed for weather rather than display. Four modest rings caught the light as she settled the bag on her shoulder. Her nose had been broken and reset. Sariel could see where it had healed, and wondered how young she had been.

“Lena,” she said. “You’re Sariel.”

“Yes.”

“I heard you were in Millhaven. By the time I got there you’d left.”

“Did you want to find us?”

“Not particularly. I wanted this cart. But the man who arranged it thought I ought to say hello.”

Sariel found herself smiling without pleasure. “Then hello.”

The girl flushed a little. “That came out badly. I know your names. From before. I thought you might prefer to know that.”

Behind Sariel, Benjamin had stopped offering the mule the end of a carrot. The animal nudged his hand until he opened it.

“The Coin?” Sariel asked.

Lena nodded.

Halven called that he would wait at the next watering place. She put her bag back in the cart and said she would walk for a while. There was no question of joining them; she simply took the road beside Sariel, and for the next hundred paces they both looked at the wall.

It had been easier, lately, not to explain the name. In Millhaven the five were people who could be hired to recover a mule, examine a troublesome lease, accompany a family through a bad stretch of country. Some jobs involved blades. More involved waiting for someone who was certain he had said a different hour. Their association with the ruined guildhouse was known, but it no longer entered every room before them.

Lena had come from the city where it still mattered.

“How long since you left Silkstrand?” Sariel asked.

“A few weeks.”

“Is it—”

She stopped. There were too many things she might mean, and the girl did not deserve the work of choosing for her.

“The dye yards,” she tried again. “Are they still building along the small canal?”

“Yes. They moved the unloading steps. You can walk through now without climbing over the rope shed.”

“I used to cut through there.”

“So did I.” Lena glanced at her. “You were taller than I was.”

“How old?”

“Eight, maybe nine. I wasn’t keeping a good count.”

Sariel felt the road incline beneath her feet. She had been counting everything then: people through a door, coins under a blanket, the hours before the next piece of bad news. A child going elsewhere had not entered the count at all.

“Were you in the house when it happened?”

“No. I got out another way.”

Lena’s voice had gone flat. Sariel let the question stop there.

Far ahead, the mule bells resumed. A gust moved through the vines and showed the pale backs of the leaves left on the pruned branches. The hills were beginning to green, although the shaded ruts still held ice in the mornings. Sariel buttoned her coat at the throat. She had been cold enough on the last night to lie with her knees against Benjamin’s, then impatient when he had woken and tried to put his entire blanket over her.

“Are you working at Casta Ferrum?” she asked.

“I have an introduction.”

“To whom?”

“Someone who pays.”

Lena looked uncomfortable as soon as she said it. Sariel almost reassured her that she knew how to keep a secret, which would have been a poor way to demonstrate it.

“I won’t ask again.”

“Thank you.”

They walked more easily after that. Lena asked whether the western road was still bad below the split oak. Sariel said the oak was down, but that the bad road had survived. Lena laughed properly, with her head tipped back, and then told her about the man in Millhaven who had bought the fallen timber before discovering he was expected to move it. Sariel knew him. By the time they caught up to the cart she had supplied the end of the story, which involved a disputed pigsty and did not improve the man’s prospects.

At the watering place Lena climbed onto the shaft to take down her bag. Benjamin offered to hold it while she adjusted the strap, and she handed it to him without hesitation. Sariel was relieved. She had been preparing herself for a girl who found an insult in every kindness. That would have been exhausting, and it would have reminded her too much of herself.

“Halven says your father’s people know someone in Millhaven,” Benjamin said.

“He had a favor to return. The carrier, not my father. Passage this far and a name when I get here.”

“Peyton?” Tema asked.

Lena turned toward him.

“I’ve done business with him,” Tema said. “He’s useful. If you agree on what useful means before you begin.”

“I’ve been told.”

“Then I’ll leave it.” He offered her the water jug. “There’s a cup in the handle.”

She drank, rinsed the cup with a little water, and fitted it back. Nobody remarked on her taking it.

The road house had a common fire large enough for the two carriers and a party of sheep buyers who had never heard a story they could bear to let somebody else finish. Sariel would have preferred a small room, but Tema had paid for shelter on the road rather than the exclusion of everyone who irritated her. She sat against the warm wall and unlaced her boots.

Lena bought a bowl from the woman at the hearth, a separate purchase with a little pile of copper laid beside it. The stew smelled of chickpeas and mutton fat. A green sauce in a cracked pot contained enough garlic to reach Sariel from three benches away.

“There’s room,” Elena told her.

Lena sat at the end, leaving her bag between her feet. The sheep buyers were arguing about an animal which had supposedly climbed a ladder. Thane listened with growing interest to the ladder, which did not seem to be what the storyteller intended. Kestra took a piece of bread and used it to mop a drip from the edge of her bowl before it reached her sleeve.

“You knew Kes,” Lena said to her.

Kestra’s hand stopped.

“Yes.”

“He used to come to the little steps by the charcoal landing. Sometimes. He’d bring the heels of the loaves.”

Sariel could see him in the doorway of the old kitchen, carrying a plate he had been told to take outside, looking as though it had been his idea. The memory arrived whole enough to be painful. She had not thought of that doorway for months.

“He brought food everywhere,” Benjamin said softly.

“Did he have a daughter?” Lena asked.

They looked at one another. Sariel wished they had not.

“Yes,” Kestra said. “She was grown by then.”

“I thought so. Someone told me she was dead.”

“We didn’t know that,” Sariel said. “We knew she was being held.”

Lena pushed a chickpea through the sauce on her bread. “I didn’t see him again.”

“No.”

The sheep buyers laughed. One slapped the table hard enough to make the cups jump. Elena put her hand around her own, then set it down farther from the edge.

Sariel wanted to say that Kes had helped them, that his help had had limits, that they had not known enough to be grateful when he could have heard it. She could feel a whole account assembling in her mouth. Lena had asked one question. Sariel ate instead.

After a while Lena said, “He used to tell me I was getting tall. I wasn’t.”

Benjamin smiled. “He told Thane he was getting quiet.”

Thane looked over from the sheep buyers. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“He said I ought to be quieter. That’s different.”

Lena smiled into her bowl. The smile stayed there while Elena asked whether she wanted more of the green sauce, and she said yes, please, a great deal.

Later they helped clear their end of the table. Sariel carried the bowls, Lena the cups. At the washing bench Lena asked whether they would expect her to tell them more about the fire.

“No,” Sariel said.

“I don’t remember much that would help.”

“You needn’t help.”

Lena nodded. She stood a moment with her hands in the warm rinsing water, though there were no cups left to wash. The inn woman came over and shooed them away so she could finish in her own fashion.

Benjamin was waiting on the stair with Sariel’s boots. He had scraped the worst of the mud from their soles, but had not tried to oil them after the last time, when she had spent a week sliding against the leather inside.

“I thought you might want them dry.”

“I do. Thank you.”

He handed them over. She took one in each hand and leaned her forehead against his chest. He smelled of smoke and the rough soap they had found beside the water trough. For a little while neither moved.

“Did you remember her?” he asked.

“No.”

“Neither did I.”

“She was very young.”

“So were we.”

Sariel lifted her head. In the stair light she could see the narrow scar under his ear, almost lost now among the ordinary changes in his face. She had once examined that cut every time he turned, angry that he had let someone get close enough to make it. Years later she could still find it before she found anything else.

“There’s a bed,” she said. “I paid the difference.”

He brightened. “Both of us?”

“If you don’t bring the spare blankets into it.”

He laughed, then pressed his mouth to her hair. “I brought two for the cart. That’s all.”

“Good.”

She had expected to be teased about the room. He did not tease her. Upstairs the mattress had a hollow in the middle and the shutter would not quite close. She put a boot against it, and they slept with their own belongings on the floor and other people’s voices coming faintly through the boards.

At a roadside weighing shed two days later, a clerk tried to charge Halven for the cage under the cloth. Its occupant, a gray hen, had remained silent until the mule stopped. Now it objected to the entire journey.

“Livestock,” the clerk said.

“Household fowl,” Halven replied.

“It’s alive.”

The woman who owned it began a vigorous explanation in which the hen’s intended place beside her daughter’s kitchen had considerably more importance than the clerk’s schedule. Sariel was behind the cart with Tema when she saw Lena step away.

There were two notices on the shed. The new one hung over half the old. Lena lifted its corner, read underneath, then went to the clerk and asked to see the household allowance. He pointed to the new notice without looking up.

“The one below,” she said. “Is it withdrawn?”

“It isn’t current.”

“Then will you write that on her paper?”

The clerk looked at her. She held the woman’s travel paper where he could reach it, but did not give it to him. People in the queue began looking at the notices. The clerk said there was no need to make a matter of a hen. He waved the cart through.

Sariel caught up beside the last wheel.

“You’d been to that shed before?”

“No.”

“How did you know there was another notice?”

“The nail was too long for one sheet.” Lena put the woman’s paper back beneath the cage. “And he’d charged the other cart for ducks. They were arguing when we came up.”

“I missed that.”

“You were talking to your employer.”

There was no accusation in it. Sariel heard one anyway, then had to let it go. She had spent the morning finding out which of Tema’s recent shipments had been held at the Gate. Lena had been watching the road. Both things were work.

The hen’s owner gave Lena an egg wrapped in a rag. She accepted it, thanked her, and tucked it into a hard-sided pocket of her bag. Sariel watched without comment.

By late afternoon the pale upper walls of Casta Ferrum showed between the foothills. The main road bent west toward them, crowded with traffic converging from the lower Acasian tracks. Smoke lay against the stone. Sariel could smell something sweet burning, perhaps orchard prunings, before she could distinguish the people at the eastern arch.

Halven stopped short of the queue. Lena climbed down with her bag and paid the last of her passage. A man waiting beside the road raised two fingers in greeting. She answered him, then turned to the five.

“I’ll see you in town.”

“Peyton?” Tema asked.

She nodded.

Sariel had a sudden impulse to tell her where they would lodge. Tema had already arranged the rooms; there was no secret to it. Still, she waited until Lena asked.

“The Split Almond,” Sariel said.

“I know where that is.”

“You haven’t been here.”

“I asked.”

Lena shifted the strap on her shoulder and went to meet the man. He took a folded introduction from her, read it, and returned it. They entered the crowd beside the waiting carts. Sariel followed them with her eyes until the hen began shrieking behind her and Halven asked whether she would kindly move so he could reach the gate before dark.

Chapter 3 — The Words at the Gate

Above the east arch, one of the bronze plates had weathered green while those beside it remained dark. Kestra tried to read the inscription beneath it and lost the words each time a cart moved. The letters had been made for people standing farther away. At the distance of the queue they were cuts in metal, each holding its own little strip of shadow.

“Brass Gate,” the woman behind her said to a boy. “You can remember that. Your uncle lives through it.”

“Bronze,” said the boy, who had apparently been preparing.

The woman tugged him back from a wheel. “Then you tell him where you’ve been.”

Kestra moved her bag to her other shoulder. They had reached the outer court, where three lines of traffic were being reduced to one by guards with white rods. The main passage ran west through the gatehouse toward the town. Its roof was deep enough to hide the daylight at the far end whenever a covered wagon went through. On the north side a narrow wicket admitted people on foot. A little farther north, beyond a dividing wall, deliveries turned into a service court through a separate cart door.

She watched a bread cart take that door. Its driver called a greeting to the guard and received one back. An open stair inside the court rose to windows with green shutters. Below the lowest stair, in a recessed doorway, someone was carrying out a bucket of pale grit.

Tema took their papers to the desk. Kestra stayed where he had left her, which was difficult when she could hear only every third word. A man with a cart of glazed bowls was explaining to Elena that he had lost nothing on this road for the first time in seven journeys.

“I used to put the best pieces under the straw and leave cracked ones where a thief would see them,” he said. “Then you arrive, and the cracked ones are what the buyer thinks you make.”

“Do the guards go with you now?”

“Some do. But they know the carrier has undertaken to report trouble. Before this, a man could see you robbed and say he’d taken a different road.”

He lifted the corner of a cloth. The bowls were the color of honey, each with a little dark leaf at the bottom. Elena bent to look. Her face, which had been set in the patient expression she wore during queues, changed.

“They’re beautiful.”

“My daughter does the leaf.”

“Does she sell them here?”

“In the west market tomorrow. If we get through.”

Kestra was glad to hear Elena ask where. She had been listening to Tema’s letter for too many nights, turning the same handful of reported symptoms in her head until Casta Ferrum had begun to seem a place populated entirely by victims and men who made them.

Tema came back with a small wooden tablet on a cord.

“Fourteen days,” he said. “Visitors under my undertaking to the customs office. Ordinary toll paid. No ceremony unless you take local licensed work that requires it.”

Kestra read the paper attached to the tablet. “Your undertaking?”

“A bond in money. I haven’t touched their relic. I told the clerk I wouldn’t hire people to investigate something and swear to it before they came back.”

“That was sensible.”

He gave her a tired smile. “I’d hoped someone might say so.”

The permission named the five separately. He passed her the sheet, and she folded it along its existing creases into the front of her working book. The tablet he gave to Sariel to show at their lodging. No one had been asked to repeat words they did not understand. Kestra felt the relief, then looked at the other queue and became less comfortable with it.

A porter stood beside a waist-high table under the north wall. A guard held the handles of his barrow while a clerk read aloud. On the table sat a wooden chest banded in iron. Its lid stood upright, and inside, on a shallow pad, lay a broad bronze face with an iron shank. The design was worn at its edges: an eagle above a broken wheel. The shank shone where it had been handled. Someone had tied a fresh white cord around the pad to keep it in position.

The porter was listening with his head tilted. He had a scarf tucked under his collar and a patch on one knee. His hand hovered above the object while the clerk repeated a phrase.

“Not knowingly to misdirect any load entrusted to me under the Prefect’s peace,” the clerk said.

The man repeated it. He spoke clearly, with the slight impatience of someone who had already said the words on another day.

“And to remain available for the assigned carriage until relieved by the office.”

The porter looked up. “I was relieved. At the lower yard. Darel said there was nothing else.”

“We’re renewing the undertaking, Damas. The prior question is being considered.”

“I’ve got a note.”

“Give it to the petition clerk afterward.”

The guard beside the barrow shifted his feet. Kestra could see that he knew the man. He was looking at the wheels instead of the table.

Damas put his palm on the bronze. The clerk prompted him again. This time he said the whole phrase, though he had to stop once to cough into his sleeve. When he lifted his hand there was no light, no sound, nothing that Kestra could distinguish from a tired man taking his hand off a cold object.

“Good,” the clerk said. “Your mark here.”

Damas reached for the pen. He looked at it for several seconds, then at the sheet.

The line behind him began to murmur. A woman called that he was blocking the table. Damas turned toward the sound, one hand raised apologetically. He took a step toward the wrong barrow.

“Yours is here,” the guard told him.

Damas stopped. His face had become wet. He was searching the court with the blank urgency of someone who has misplaced a child.

Elena was already moving when his knee folded.

She caught him under one arm; Benjamin caught the other. They lowered him onto the paving beside the table. A bundle fell from the barrow and opened, spreading cheap white caps into a muddy patch. The guard knelt to pick them up, then put them down again when Elena asked him for room.

Kestra came as far as the edge of the little circle and stopped. There were hands enough. She could see Damas’s chest moving under his shirt. He tried to sit up, coughing, and Elena held a cloth for him without pressing him down.

“I need you to stay here a moment,” she said. “You went faint. Do you know where you are?”

He looked past her toward the arch. “My load.”

“We have it.” Benjamin put his foot against the barrow wheel. “It’s here.”

“I didn’t leave it.”

“I know.”

“You tell them.”

Benjamin glanced at the clerk, who was holding the pen with which Damas had failed to sign. “I will.”

Kestra took out her working book, then put it away again. The porter watched the movement as if she had drawn a knife.

“I won’t write your name,” she told him.

He did not appear to hear. Elena asked whether he had eaten, whether there was pain in his chest, whether he had someone in town. He answered the first two. At the third he closed his eyes.

The clerk cleared his throat. “If he can be moved, this is the oath table.”

Elena looked up. “Then you will have to wait.”

The clerk reddened. He began to explain that there were people waiting, saw the guard’s expression, and stopped. He closed the lid of the chest before he moved it farther along the table. The iron hinges sounded disproportionately loud.

They took Damas through the wicket on a borrowed carrying chair. He objected until the guard told him that his barrow would be brought to the same place. Then he allowed Benjamin and another porter to lift him.

Kestra went ahead with the guard’s directions in her head: through the wicket, west along the gatehouse, turn left at the bathhouse drain, then right into the short lane. She could feel herself fitting the turns together, converting a city into a route she might be able to hold. It was a useful habit. She had to remind herself that she was not invoking anything merely because she was frightened.

Dometta’s door stood open. Two women sat on a bench inside, one with her sleeve pinned up, the other holding a baby asleep against a folded shawl. A copper pan hung above the hearth. Something bitter boiled in it, and from the next room came the unmistakable sound of someone being sick.

Dometta emerged wiping her hands. She was broad through the shoulders, with close-cut gray hair and a dark wool dress pulled up clear of the floor. She saw Damas and went directly to him.

“Again?”

He tried to answer and coughed instead.

“Bring him through. Keep the chair level.”

Elena introduced herself while they moved him. Dometta listened, nodded at the right places, and told her where to find a clean basin. Within a minute Elena had taken off her traveling coat and was working as though she had been admitted to the room rather than called to save it.

Kestra waited in the passage. Benjamin set down the empty chair beside her and flexed his hands. His palms were marked by the handles.

“They made him do it again,” he said.

“Yes.”

“He was already sick.”

“We don’t know whether—”

“I know what we don’t know.”

She stopped. He looked ashamed of the sharpness and began to apologize. Kestra touched his forearm.

“Don’t. I was going to make you listen to it while he’s in there.”

They stood together until a woman hurried into the passage with a basket under one arm and soap whitened into the cracks of her knuckles. She asked for Damas, and Dometta called her through. Benjamin moved the chair out of her way.

Celia’s voice reached them from the room, first angry, then lower. She had told him not to renew it. He had said the barrow license would be withdrawn. She knew that. She had said they would find something else. A long silence followed, broken by Damas asking why she had brought washing.

“I was at work,” she said. “It’s all right. I came.”

Kestra sat on the empty chair. Through the open street door she could see a boy rolling a hoop toward the baths. It fell, and he ran past it before turning back, laughing at his own mistake. The ordinary noise of the city kept entering the room.

At the Split Almond, the landlady took Tema’s wooden tablet, checked the accompanying names against a slate and showed them two rooms on the upper gallery. Kestra chose the bed nearest the window because she would want to read. Then she saw Elena’s face and offered it to her.

“I won’t sleep better for being able to see the yard,” Elena said. “Keep it.”

“Damas?”

“Asleep. Dometta has been treating him for twelve days.”

“Did she know what caused it?”

“She knew the ceremony preceded it. She also knew he was coughing before that. I like her.”

Kestra set her book on the table. It felt very clean. “Is he going to die?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Elena sat and began unlacing her boots. She had carried the medicine bag all afternoon and there was a deep crease across her shoulder where the strap had pressed. Kestra knelt to loosen the knot Elena could not reach without bending against it. Elena let her.

Below, someone was splitting almonds on a stone with a small wooden mallet. The nuts were bitter until roasted, the landlady had said, and had immediately offered them a dish already roasted, as though afraid she had made her whole house sound poisonous. Kestra had put two in her pocket. She took them out and set them beside Elena’s hand.

“They’re good.”

Elena ate one. “They are.”

For a few minutes they did nothing useful. The mallet tapped in the yard. Kestra watched the last daylight leave a patch of whitewashed wall and thought of the way Damas had looked at her book. She had wanted to make a record while he was still trying to know whether he would be punished. She would ask him before she wrote tomorrow.

Sariel came to the doorway with their remaining bags.

“Tema’s at the harness seller’s. He’s rented a room over it. He wants to see us after we’ve eaten, but I said he could come here.”

Elena leaned back. “Thank you.”

“He also says the Prefect knows we’re asking questions. The visitors’ clerk sent our names up.”

Kestra looked toward the window. From there she could see the upper corner of the Gate and one dark slit where the bronze-faced barrier rose into its housing. A workman stood on the walkway above it, moving slowly from one point to another with a lamp.

“So much for arriving quietly,” she said.

Sariel sat on the end of the spare bed. “We weren’t going to manage that with Benjamin carrying a man through the wicket.”

Kestra was about to answer when Elena put the second almond into her own hand.

“Eat,” she said. “Before Tema comes and we begin again.”

Chapter 4 — A Small Retrieval

The tribunal paid eight marks to recover a box worth perhaps half of one. Sariel asked why it was still in the hands of the person from whom they wanted it recovered. The clerk answered by laying three papers in front of her, none of which answered the question.

“Permission to enter the bonded store,” he said, touching the first. “Description of the property. Order for return to the Censor’s office.”

“And the man at the store will give it to me?”

“He has agreed to permit retrieval.”

Sariel looked at the wording again. “Has he agreed to unlock his door?”

The clerk sighed. For the first time since she had entered, she felt they might be about to speak to each other.

“He says the key was lost when his carter was detained. He says a carpenter will charge him for damage. He says the entire contents are under a claim and nobody may remove anything until the claim is heard. We have obtained an exception for the box. You were recommended as someone who could open the door without a carpenter.”

“By whom?”

“Tema.”

She would speak to Tema later. For now she picked up the description. A shallow blue case with brass corners, taken with a handcart from a room above a cobbler’s shop. No money listed. Papers, tools, personal effects. Deliver unopened to the Censor’s office; obtain acknowledgment from her assistant.

“Who owns it?”

“The person named in the order.”

The line said deposited material subject to protected examination. Sariel tapped it with a finger.

“I need to know whose door I am opening.”

“It’s Master Caro’s store. The order says so.”

“That’s the building. I asked about the box.”

The clerk looked beyond her to the waiting bench. A woman there was holding a broken cup wrapped in a cloth, and a man beside her was taking care not to look at it. Sariel had learned enough in municipal rooms to recognize how long the two of them might have been waiting. She lowered her voice.

“If someone comes home and finds me carrying her things out, I need more than three papers to tell her.”

“I’ll have Nerio meet you.” The clerk collected the sheets and tied them together. “The Censor’s assistant. He can settle it.”

“Before I open anything.”

“Yes.”

She took the papers. The eight marks remained in the drawer until the job was done.

Nerio met her at the store with a woman who looked as though she had been brought to identify a body. She wore a plain brown cloak despite the mild morning. One of its toggles had been replaced with a wooden peg, and she kept turning the peg beneath her thumb.

“This is Vettia,” Nerio said. “The case is hers. She has agreed that we may hold it.”

“Only the case,” Vettia said. “The linen in the cart isn’t mine. It belongs to my sister.”

“I’ll leave the linen,” Sariel told her.

Nerio was younger than the Censor’s title had led her to expect: a narrow-faced man in his thirties with an ink mark beside his ear. He had a pair of spectacles wrapped in cloth in his breast pocket but did not put them on. He looked at the door, then at the windows above it, then back at Vettia.

“Would you rather wait in the baker’s?”

“No.”

“It’s warm there.”

“I want to see what she takes.”

Sariel set her bag on the step. “Then stay. If I find anything else that looks like yours, you can tell me.”

The door had two locks. One was newer than its plate and fitted badly enough to snag whenever the timber swelled. The other had been made by someone who understood how a tool would enter the keyway and had put a small obstacle exactly where Sariel would have preferred to rest it. She settled on the step and opened Hest’s case across her knees.

Vettia watched the tools emerge.

“Will it take long?”

“A little while.”

“I was told you were good.”

“I am. The person who made this was good too.”

Vettia looked away. “I’m sorry.”

“You needn’t be.”

Sariel worked by feel. A cart passed close behind her and set the hanging shop sign rattling. She waited for it to stop before she tested the little spring again. Hest had disliked anyone claiming a lock was offended or stubborn; the parts were doing what they had been made to do, and if Sariel could not persuade them, she ought to find out why instead of assigning them motives. She had found the advice less useful with people.

Nerio shifted to block the view of two idlers across the street. Sariel noticed and appreciated it without looking up. After a few minutes Vettia stopped turning the peg.

The first lock opened. The second required her to lift the door a fraction, and she asked Nerio to do it with a wedge rather than put his fingers underneath. Inside, the store smelled of damp sacks and leather that had not dried before being packed. The little handcart stood at the rear beneath a cover weighted with stones.

Vettia went straight to it.

“My book,” she said.

The case was open. Its simple catch had been forced. A bundle of copying quills lay in one corner, with a cloth pouch and three volumes whose bindings had begun to split. Someone had turned the pages back hard enough to break the stitching of the smallest.

Sariel stood aside while Vettia took the book out. Between two leaves a pressed spray of yellow flowers had become brown dust. Vettia put her hand over it, then changed her mind and let the dust fall onto the cover.

“They weren’t in the report,” she said. “They didn’t need to do this.”

Nerio did not ask who they were. He took a clean cloth from his pocket and laid it on the cart so she could set the damaged book down.

Sariel checked the case against the tribunal description. The brass corners matched. So did the burn on the lid and the faded ribbon tied to one handle. Under the books was a folded packet addressed to the Censor. Its sealing wax had been pressed flat by something heavy, but remained unbroken.

“Do you want me to carry it as it is?” Sariel asked.

Vettia nodded. “Please don’t put that man’s inventory inside it.”

Sariel looked at Nerio.

“The inventory identifies her lodging,” he said quietly. “It can go into the protected packet at our office.”

“The tribunal wants acknowledgment of what I recovered.”

“They can have the case description and my signature. They’ll ask for the rest. Tell them to ask me.”

Sariel took the paper out of the case and folded it separately. She could do this job correctly and still deliver the wrong thing to the wrong desk. It was a familiar enough danger, but the flowers had made her dislike it in a way the clerk’s evasions had not.

Vettia lifted the broken book once more. “Can I keep this one?”

Nerio examined the cover, then passed it back. “Yes.”

“Will she be angry?”

“No.”

“She said all the material.”

“I’ll tell her which book. She won’t be angry with you.”

Vettia wrapped it in her cloak. Sariel closed the case and tied her own cord around it because the catch would no longer hold. When she relocked the store, she made Nerio test the door before she packed the tools. Vettia had gone to stand in the sunlight beside the bakery. She was rubbing the dried flower dust from the heel of her hand, very slowly.

The most direct route to Cassia’s office passed through the service court of the Gate. Nerio had permission to use it and held the cart door while Sariel carried the case in. Bread trays stood on a bench under an awning. Two men argued beside a pile of chipped stone; one was trying to show the other a crack by pouring water into it.

Nerio called the first man Octan. The engineer raised a hand without abandoning the crack. He was thick through the body, with a square bald patch at the crown of his head and a leather measuring cord coiled at his belt.

“Tell her I need the old section drawing,” he said. “The lower one.”

“I asked yesterday.”

“I know you asked. I need it.”

Nerio nodded and led Sariel toward the inside stair. The tread nearest the wall was worn into a hollow. Above it, a landing opened into the scriptorium: long tables, ruled sheets weighted at the corners, a smell of gum and drying wool. The green shutters she had seen from outside were half closed against the glare.

At the west end a heavy inner door stood behind a low brass rail. A guard sat beside it with his helmet on the floor between his feet. Sariel recognized the chest from the oath table when another official carried it through that door. The iron bands knocked softly against his buckle.

She kept walking.

Nerio took her across the end of the room and down a town-side stair into a passage that opened west of the gatehouse. The Archive stood opposite, a smaller building with patched plaster and a stone lintel carved before the newer street had reached it. He opened its door with a key on his belt.

“Wait here while I put this away,” he said.

Sariel placed the blue case on his table and took out the separate inventory. Nerio folded it into a plain cover, wrote a reference on that cover without Vettia’s name, and locked both beneath his desk. He gave Sariel an acknowledgment describing the case and the order number. His signature was firm. She had expected him to make it smaller.

“Does she know someone went through it?” Sariel asked.

“She’ll know today.”

“Vettia worked on the digging?”

Nerio’s expression changed.

“The lime on her old tools,” Sariel said. “And the section drawing you’ve been asked for. I’m not asking you to tell me where she’s staying.”

“I appreciate that.” He put away the key. “Please don’t ask her to tell you either.”

Sariel could feel him deciding how much else to say. She took pity on him.

“I came to recover a box. If she wants more work done, she can ask.”

He let out a breath. “Good.”

On the way back to the tribunal, Sariel stopped in the street opposite the Gate and looked up. The scriptorium’s north windows stood above the service court wall. Beyond the wall, at the rear, lay a dry yard with old cart ruts in its paving and a street gate left ajar. The distance from one window to the yard looked short enough to lower a person. A person was not a bronze relic in an iron-bound chest. She looked again before continuing.

Marcellus received her after the tribunal clerk had paid the eight marks. She had been intending to leave, but a soldier at the door said the Prefect wished to thank her for helping the porter. Sariel told him Elena had done the helping. The soldier said the Prefect knew that, and waited.

She followed him through a corridor warm with afternoon sun. In a small anteroom a boy was trying to fit a polished helmet onto a peg that had been placed too high. Marcellus came out, took it from him and moved it to a lower hook.

“Tell the armorer I want the strap replaced, not shortened again,” he said. “Show him where it rubs.”

The boy nodded and hurried away with the helmet under his arm. The Prefect watched until he had cleared the door.

He was wiry, a little shorter than Sariel, with a scar that pulled at the corner of his mouth when he spoke. His coat was good dark wool without much ornament. She had expected a display of bronze and found instead a desk with a chipped cup, a map weighted by an ordinary stone, and two small paintings turned toward the person who sat there.

“Sit, please. You’ve done the Censor a useful service.”

“She hired me.”

“So she did. Will you drink?”

Sariel accepted water. Marcellus poured it himself and sat on the other side of the desk rather than behind it. She did not like being aware that this was effective.

“Tema says you’re looking into complaints about the oath,” he said.

“We’re speaking to people.”

“I want you to see the roads as well as the treatment rooms. There’s a village east of the ridge where they had stopped repairing their outer doors. They’d been burned twice. Last month they fitted three new ones.”

“I’m glad.”

“So am I.” He looked at her, and for a moment she saw the fatigue beneath his formal attention. “You know something about losing a house.”

Her hand tightened around the cup.

He noticed. “I didn’t mean to use it against you. Your names were on the visitors’ paper. I read the account from Millhaven when it came through.”

“Several accounts went through.”

“The one in which you stayed.”

Sariel put down the cup. “There were people there we couldn’t leave.”

“There are people here I can’t leave.”

He did not smile over the correspondence. That made it harder to dismiss. She looked at one of the paintings. A man with a longer face than Marcellus stood beside a horse, his hand flat on its neck. The painter had made the animal too narrow through the chest.

“My brother,” Marcellus said. “He was killed on the north road. Before this posting.”

“I’m sorry.”

“He was bringing two families in. He’d told them the escort would be enough.” Marcellus moved the painting a little, until the sun ceased reflecting from its varnish. “I don’t mean that as a reason you should agree with me. You asked no questions, and I’ve answered one anyway.”

Sariel looked back at him. His composure had slipped just far enough that she believed the apology.

“Damas thought his work was finished,” she said. “He had been sent home.”

“By someone who wasn’t authorized to release him. That’s being examined.”

“Meanwhile he was asked to swear again.”

“I’ve instructed them to review renewals in cases of illness.”

“Before or after this morning?”

He paused. “After.”

She appreciated the answer and resented having to appreciate it.

A knock came at the door. Captain Flaccus entered carrying a narrow dispatch case. He was a compact man with a carefully brushed mustache, and he stopped at once when he saw a visitor.

Marcellus held out his hand for the case. “The western patrol?”

“Back. No losses. Two missing sheep recovered at the abandoned kiln.”

“Alive?”

“One. The other was cooking.”

Flaccus said it without pleasure. Marcellus opened the case, read the first lines and asked him to wait outside. Sariel rose before another courtesy could keep her in the room.

“If your company wants work here,” the Prefect said, “I have use for people who can speak to both sides without making a performance of it.”

“Which sides?”

He looked down at the dispatch. “You are right. There are more than two.”

She left with the eight marks in her pocket and the uncomfortably clear memory of the man moving his brother’s portrait out of the glare. Outside the tribunal, the woman with the broken cup was still waiting. The man beside her had gone. Sariel stopped and asked whether she had eaten.

“Not yet,” the woman said. “They might call me.”

Sariel crossed to the baker’s, bought two small rolls and came back. She gave one to the woman and sat down for the few minutes it took to eat the other. Then the clerk called a name, and the woman stood so quickly that crumbs fell down the front of her dress.

Chapter 5 — The Same Cough

Celia had sewn her children’s names into the inside of Damas’s cuff. Elena saw the blue stitches when she helped him turn his sleeve back. Luci above, Pavo below, the second name crowded by the hem.

“He can look,” Celia said. “When he’s frightened.”

Damas kept his eyes on the basin. “I know them this morning.”

“I know you do.”

He had slept badly, but his fever was lower. He could tell Elena what he had eaten and how many times he had woken, and could remember the name of the guard who had brought his barrow to Dometta’s door. When she asked whether the words at the Gate came back to him, he repeated half the undertaking without hesitation. His mouth formed the cadence before he seemed to know he was speaking.

Celia stood up and went into the passage.

Elena waited until he had finished. “You don’t need to say it again for me.”

“It’s so I can work.”

“Yes.”

“I never left anybody’s load.”

She set the cup where he could reach it. “Drink a little. Then tell me about being sent home.”

He described a loading yard, a man waving him away, the confusion after a wagon arrived without its second horse. At home his son had been sick. Damas had stayed the next morning because Celia had work, and by afternoon he had begun to cough. He had not known that a new duty had been entered against his name while he was absent. The office said the relieving foreman had no power to release an oath worker. The foreman had said everyone knew he sent porters home. Damas had returned with the note. The note had gone from one desk to another.

“He’s not a bad man,” Damas said. “Darel. He tried to tell them.”

“Did they listen?”

“They let me keep the barrow.”

Elena could not tell whether he considered that an answer. She checked the band of redness around his wrist, where he had begun rubbing the skin while asleep. Dometta had covered it with soft cloth. It was already better than the other wrist, which he refused to have covered because he wanted to look at the names.

Outside, Celia was speaking to someone about washing. Her work basket had been put under a chair. She had come intending to stay an hour and had been there since dawn.

“Will the children come?” Elena asked.

Damas shook his head, then nodded. His eyes filled. He rubbed them with the heel of his hand, annoyed.

“I want them. I don’t want them to see.”

“I can leave you the little room after noon. Celia can bring them there, if you both want it. You needn’t explain anything to them before they’ve sat down.”

He looked at the basin. “Pavo likes to bring stones in. He’ll put them everywhere.”

“That’s all right.”

“Luci gets cross. She wants the room tidy.”

Elena smiled, and let him keep talking.

Dometta refused to group her patients by the company’s theory. She had put two beds against the warmer wall, a chair near the door for those who could walk, and a low stool beside the hearth for a man who could breathe more easily sitting forward. When Elena suggested keeping the oath cases together, Dometta asked whether the oath cases were cold or warm.

Elena looked at the beds and understood the rebuke. “The feverish woman is cold. I’ll bring her another cover.”

“Good. We can sort the explanations when she’s stopped shivering.”

They worked through the morning. A boy with a burn had never seen the inside of the gatehouse; his cough had come from smoke when a pan caught fire. One carrier had sworn three times and remained well. Another had developed the wasting weeks after his ceremony, when a horse went lame and he failed to report at the hour written against his route. The physician attached to the garrison had called two of the cases winter fever and had been right about one.

Dometta kept the man’s first opinion and the correction together in her case notes. Elena liked that, although she did not like the physician’s cramped handwriting.

“Does he come here?”

“When he can. Not as often as I’d prefer.”

“Does the Prefect know what you’ve seen?”

“He has my letters. Cassia has copies. The physician has told him aloud.” Dometta wiped the table with a damp cloth. “He’s paid for blankets. Useful blankets. I haven’t sent them back.”

Elena took one from the shelf. A small municipal stamp had been sewn into its edge. She shook it out and carried it to the shivering woman.

By noon she had stopped expecting the cases to arrange themselves into a clean line. She could distinguish the ordinary wounds and some fevers. She could see that a man who feared punishment might forget a name without any curse helping him. She had also heard Damas recite an oath while being unable to recall the daughter he had just described, and she could not make that small by finding a sensible explanation for something else.

Kestra arrived with a packet of copied terms. She waited while Elena held a bowl for the burned boy, then asked whether they could speak outside.

“Did he agree?” Elena said.

“Who?”

“Damas. To being in your account.”

“I haven’t asked him yet. I copied the public forms.”

Elena dried the bowl. “Then let’s ask when he’s rested.”

In the yard Kestra spread the forms on a crate. The sun had reached the wall, loosening a scent of warm plaster and drying herbs. Somewhere behind the baths a woman was singing loudly enough to be heard over a pulley. Elena leaned against the wall and discovered how tired her knees were.

“The carrier’s terms allow for a horse dying,” Kestra said. “Not lameness. The office can grant a delay, but the man has to reach the office to ask for it.”

“Can his family ask?”

“On this form, no. On another one, yes. They’re not all the same.”

She showed Elena two clauses and a small addition in another hand. Elena followed the line with a finger. She could see what was unfair. She could not see why a bronze face should care whether one word had been added before another.

“Would changing this release them?”

“I don’t know. The clerk thinks a new undertaking replaces the old one. Damas was renewed yesterday, and you can see how much that helped.”

Elena looked toward the door.

“I shouldn’t have said it that way,” Kestra added. “I’m angry with the clerk. He kept telling me the forms were approved.”

“Did you tell him you were angry?”

“No. I made him copy the approval.”

For a moment Kestra looked so dissatisfied with herself that Elena wanted to put her arms around her. She did something less surprising and moved over on the bench by the wall. Kestra sat down, keeping the papers away from a damp place.

“Come and speak to Damas later,” Elena said. “He’ll be tired after the children. But perhaps tomorrow. You can ask him what he wants written.”

Kestra nodded. “I will.”

They stayed in the sunlight for the length of the song. Neither knew the words well enough to join in.

The children came carrying a flat loaf wrapped in a blue cloth. Luci held the loaf while Pavo carried nothing except a handful of stones he had been told to leave outside. He looked at Elena as if she might be susceptible to a different argument.

“Clean stones are fine in the yard,” she told him. “Not on the beds.”

He went solemnly to the basin. Celia shut her eyes for a moment, then laughed. Elena fetched another bowl before the washing water could be used for geology.

Damas sat in the little room with a blanket around his shoulders. When Luci entered he rose halfway and had to sit again. She went to him at once and put the loaf in his lap. Pavo followed, holding the wet stones with both hands.

Elena left the door open a crack and returned to the main room. She had promised privacy, not abandonment. Through the opening she could hear Luci describing a woman who had brought a goat into the laundry, and Damas asking why. The answer required several attempts because Pavo kept interrupting about the goat’s feet.

After a while Celia came out. She leaned against the doorframe and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

“He knows them.”

“He does.”

“For now.”

Elena wished she could say something other than yes. Celia lowered her hand and looked at the shelves.

“I want to take him home.”

“Dometta thinks he should stay another night.”

“I didn’t ask what she thought.”

Elena felt her own temper rise, quick and ungenerous. She had been working since morning; she had left people waiting at home; she was not the person who had made Damas sick. None of those things belonged in this conversation.

“What would be easier at home?” she asked.

“He could hear them. He wouldn’t have to wait for me to bring them.”

“And what would be harder?”

“The stairs. The fire. Going to work.” Celia wiped her face with the back of her wrist. “I know. I know the answer. I still want him home.”

“I understand.”

Celia looked at her skeptically.

“I understand wanting it,” Elena said. “If he sleeps well tonight, we can talk tomorrow about what would have to be arranged. I won’t promise before Dometta sees him.”

“All right.”

Celia returned to the little room. Pavo had begun putting the stones in a row across the table. Damas was giving them the names of people in the street, and the children were disputing his choices. Elena listened until somebody had to be moved because he was too round to be the butcher. Then she went back to wash the bowls.

In the afternoon a municipal clerk brought a list of workers due to renew their undertakings at the next public ceremony. Dometta read it, took a pen and crossed out three names.

“I can’t accept alterations,” he said.

“You can carry them.”

“The office requires a reason for each.”

She wrote reasons. The clerk watched her, then said the office might send for the people to confirm they were incapable of attending.

Elena set down the jar she was holding.

“You can see Damas,” she said. “He’s in the next room with his children. I’ll ask him if he wishes to speak to you. Don’t make him walk to prove he shouldn’t.”

The clerk’s face changed. He was young enough that she could see the embarrassment without effort. He looked at the crossed-out names.

“My sister’s on the next list,” he said quietly. “She’s a kitchen worker. I asked whether someone else could stand for her, because she has a child coming. They said the duty belongs to the person.”

Dometta stopped writing.

“When?”

“Another month, perhaps. She doesn’t want me asking.”

“Send her to me if she wants to come. I won’t tell the office you spoke.”

He swallowed and nodded. Dometta finished the reasons, blotted the page and gave it back. He folded it with unusual care, as though a badly placed crease might make the words easier to reject.

Elena watched him go. Until that moment she had been putting clerks on one side of the room in her mind and patients on the other. She had liked the simplicity. It had lasted less than a day.

Dometta set down the pen. “You’ll stay for food?”

“I should report to the others.”

“After food. If I send you back hungry they’ll think I’m keeping you chained to the beds.”

Elena smiled, but Dometta was already calling to a neighbor about an extra portion. There was no invitation to answer wittily. Elena accepted the bowl when it came.

They ate beside the open yard door. The dish was barley cooked in sour milk, with leeks browned almost black at the edges and a little oil poured over the top. Elena had expected to dislike the sourness. Instead she scraped the bowl clean. Dometta showed her the jar of oil and said which stall to buy it from, then asked about Millhaven’s winters.

“Wet,” Elena said. “The room I’ve rented has a good window, but the floor holds the cold.”

“Thick mat?”

“Hard to wash.”

“True.”

They discussed a raised wooden platform, then the problem of someone catching a heel on its edge. Elena found herself describing Renn and the cooper and Belia’s request for hair oil. Dometta listened while she ate. Once she laughed with a mouthful and had to reach for her cup.

It was a relief to talk to someone who did not need Elena to be either hopeful or brave. By the time she left, the street lamps had been lit and she had the name of a woman who made scented oil in small stoppered bottles. She meant to buy two.

At the Split Almond Benjamin was waiting with a plate covered by another plate. He had remembered what happened when food sat beside the hearth too long and had put it on the cool end of the table.

“I ate,” Elena said. “With Dometta.”

“Oh. Good.”

He started to carry it away. She stopped him.

“I could still eat some of that.”

He lifted the upper plate. There were white beans, two pieces of sausage and a heel of bread. Elena took the bread and sat down beside him. He told her about a horse that had refused to enter the town until its owner’s coat was put over a bright patch on the paving. She told him about Pavo washing stones. For a while neither asked whether tomorrow would be better.

Chapter 6 — The Silver Eye

The damaged page had been repaired by a person who cared more about keeping the edges together than about preserving the words. A strip of thin skin covered the final line, fixed with gum which had browned as it dried. Kestra held it sideways to the window. Beneath the repair she could distinguish the foot of a letter and something that might have been a number.

“Don’t wet it,” Cassia said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were looking at the water.”

“I was wondering how much of the damage it caused.”

Cassia took the page and turned it over. “Good. Start there.”

Kestra disliked being pleased by those three words. She had been admitted to the Archive as a working investigator, not a child whose attention ought to be rewarded. Still, the room held the smell of dry paper and warmed glue, and someone had put a cushion under the book so its spine would not have to open farther than it could bear. She had spent a great deal of her life explaining why a surface should be clean before a page was laid on it. Here the clean surface was waiting.

Cassia sat opposite, thin wrists emerging from sleeves too large for her. Her hair was white at the temples and still dark farther back, pinned without ornament. The silver lens covered her left eye in a shallow cup supported by a fine framework against the brow. Its surface did not reflect the room as a mirror would. When she moved, the window appeared in it a moment later than Kestra expected.

Nerio set a pot beside them and took away the cold one. He put Cassia’s cup on her right, within reach. She thanked him without looking up.

“This is the first store inventory after the excavation?” Kestra asked.

“The first surviving one. There was a list made in the yard.”

“And the date under the patch is the date of arrival?”

“Possibly.”

Kestra looked at the upper lines. “It lists a winter delivery. But the corner mark is from a summer issue.”

“The sheets were left over.”

“Or the delivery was copied later.”

Cassia paused. Then she passed the page back.

“Explain.”

Kestra did. The same hand wrote both the date and the description, but the description used an office name which did not appear on the earlier invoices. It might be an updated heading on an old sheet. It might mean the whole document had been copied after the new office was formed. Either way, the date beneath the repair could not establish what Cassia wanted it to establish by itself.

“I’d need another entry,” Kestra said. “Something paid or delivered that could be placed independently.”

Cassia smiled. It changed her face more than Kestra had expected.

“Yes. There are three. One agrees with you.”

“You knew?”

“I suspected. I wanted to see whether you’d follow the date or the material.”

Kestra sat back. “You could have asked me.”

The smile faded. “I could. I’m sorry.”

It was so prompt that Kestra had no prepared response. She reached for the page again.

“I’d still like to see the three entries.”

Nerio was already bringing them.

The invitation had come that morning while Kestra was arranging to speak with Damas. A note in Cassia’s own hand asked for her help comparing copies of the excavation account. It mentioned her work at the Ford and a correction she had made, years later, to a disputed shrine inventory on the Three Oaks road. Kestra had almost forgotten the inventory. Someone had recorded twelve lamp bowls as twelve lamps, and by the time she arrived the keeper was being asked to account for metal fittings which had never existed.

She had not expected the work to reach Casta Ferrum.

Now Cassia laid the three entries beside the damaged sheet and asked which marks Kestra trusted. They disagreed over one for nearly half an hour. Nerio brought a fourth document, muttered that both of them were neglecting the reverse, and settled it with a note about a missing mule.

Cassia laughed. The laugh turned into a cough she tried to suppress by pressing a cloth to her mouth. Nerio moved the cup closer.

“Enough for a moment,” he said.

She waved him away. Kestra closed the book.

“I need to stand up.”

Cassia gave her a look which made it clear she recognized the excuse. She accepted it anyway.

The Archive’s shelves were built at different times. Some sat on wooden feet, others had been fitted into the wall when the plaster was new. A little line of pale grit marked the bottom of a patched crack beside the west window. Kestra followed it with her eyes.

“The Gate settling?” she asked.

“This wall has been doing that for longer than the recent work. Octan checks the telltale every month.” Cassia pointed to a strip of fired clay bridging the crack. “If it breaks, we move the shelf. He has made that very plain.”

“You trust him?”

“With walls, entirely.”

Kestra liked the precision of the answer. She stepped back so Nerio could reach a drawer and saw the shallow blue case Sariel had described beneath his desk. Its cord was neatly tied, the damaged latch turned away from the room.

“I won’t open that today,” Cassia said.

Kestra looked at her.

“Your friend did useful work. The person whose papers those are needs to know what will happen next. We haven’t settled it.”

“All right.”

Cassia watched her for a moment, perhaps expecting further questions. Kestra had plenty, but she could wait. She returned to the table and asked to see the early observations of the Seal’s surface instead.

The impressions were made in several materials: wax, fine clay, soft lead. They had been packed separately to keep them from marking one another. The bronze face’s worn eagle appeared more sharply in some than in others. Beside the broken wheel, small signs disappeared into the wear. Kestra had seen comparable shapes used to bind a witness to an oath text, but she did not know these well enough to name them.

Cassia asked what she recognized.

“Parts. Not a complete form.”

“Good.”

Kestra looked up.

“I mean it,” Cassia said. “A familiar corner is a dangerous comfort. People will build an entire explanation from it.”

“Have you?”

“Yes.”

She did not elaborate. Kestra examined the next impression. A faint line crossed the wheel, straighter than the old cutting around it. She asked whether it was a crack.

“Later scratch. I think from the recovery tools.”

“Does it interrupt the working?”

“I have no evidence that it does.”

Cassia adjusted the framework at her brow. She tilted one wax impression toward the light, then away from it. Her unencumbered eye narrowed. Nerio reached for a shaded lamp on the side table but waited until she nodded before lighting it.

“What do you see?” Kestra asked.

“A pressure around the repeated sign. I can follow it farther in this impression than in the next.”

“Because this one is older?”

“Perhaps. Or because it was held longer. Or because I’m comparing two different waxes under a light I cannot keep still.”

The lamp flame stirred as Nerio moved. Cassia let the impression rest on its cloth.

Kestra leaned forward. “Could you tell whether a person had sworn by it?”

“Sometimes I can find a disturbance consistent with what I’ve seen after an oath. I couldn’t tell you what words they agreed to. I couldn’t tell you whether they lied. If I claimed more, I could make a very comfortable living terrifying people.”

Kestra glanced at Nerio. He was replacing the lead impression in its box. His expression had not changed.

“Will you look at Damas?” she asked.

“If he agrees. I’ve looked at others.”

“Did it help?”

“Not enough.”

The answer disappointed her more than it should have. She had come hoping the Censor’s eye would supply a distinction the treatment room lacked: something visible which could be set beside the differing forms and made to explain why one person was ill and another well. Cassia’s hands were shaking now as she adjusted the lens. The instrument itself seemed to have become another thing they could not quite interpret.

“Does it hurt?” Kestra asked.

“Yes.”

“Then take it off.”

Cassia looked startled. Nerio put down the box.

“I didn’t mean to order you,” Kestra said. “Only that we can go on with the paper.”

The older woman sat still for a moment. Then she lifted the framework from her face and laid it on the folded cloth Nerio had pushed toward her. Beneath it, her left eye was clouded and slightly closed. A red crescent marked the skin above it.

She pressed her fingertips there, very lightly.

“I forget that I can stop.”

Kestra reached for the damaged page and turned it so the light fell from Cassia’s good side.

They visited Damas late in the afternoon. He sat in the chair by the yard door, where Celia had put him to catch a little warmth. The children’s stones remained on a shelf. Elena asked him whether he wanted to speak to Kestra and the Censor, and waited for an answer.

“I don’t want another undertaking,” he said.

“There isn’t one,” Kestra told him. “I want to write down what happened. I can read it back. You can tell me if I’ve got it wrong.”

He looked at Celia. She did not answer for him.

“All right.”

Cassia stood beside the chair rather than immediately putting on the lens. She told Damas what it could and could not show. He listened, breathing through his mouth, and asked whether he would have to touch something.

“No.”

“Then you can look.”

She fitted the silver cup. Nerio stood at her shoulder. Kestra noticed him doing what Elena did beside a patient who might fall: near enough to reach, far enough not to make the person feel held.

Cassia asked Damas to turn his wrist, then to let it rest. The blue names inside his cuff showed against the dark wool. Her mouth tightened. She looked once toward the doorway and asked Elena to move the hanging basin because it caught the sun.

When she was done she removed the lens before speaking.

“There’s a disturbance. Similar to others after the ceremony.”

“Can you take it out?” Damas asked.

“No. I’m sorry.”

“Then what was the looking for?”

Cassia did not answer at once. Kestra felt the question strike her too.

“To understand enough to stop it being done again,” the Censor said. “And, I hope, enough to help you. I haven’t found that part yet.”

Damas looked away. “They said renewing would help.”

“I know.”

“I did what they told me.”

Cassia lowered herself onto the bench. She looked suddenly much older without the eye. “I know you did.”

Celia took Damas’s hand. The anger on her face was directed at Cassia, but she said nothing. Kestra opened her book only after Damas nodded again, and wrote the account as he gave it. Twice he stopped to find a name. Once Celia supplied it. The other time he would not let her, and everyone waited until he remembered.

When Kestra read the account back, he corrected the hour of the wagon’s arrival. It had been before the bread seller came, not after. That mattered to him. She crossed out the wrong hour and wrote the right one above it.

At the Archive, Cassia stopped in the doorway and reached for the wall. Nerio caught her elbow before Kestra could put down the books. For a moment she appeared to be listening to something in the empty passage.

“It’s only the step,” she said.

Nerio helped her over it. She knew the step; her foot had already cleared it. Kestra brought the chair forward without asking.

The spell passed slowly. Cassia drank water, refused food, then accepted half a piece of bread when Nerio buttered it. He knelt to remove a stone from the sole of her shoe. She looked down at his bent head.

“Thank you, my dear.”

He lifted his face. Something moved in it, too quickly for Kestra to name. He had been patient all afternoon; now he looked angry enough to leave the room.

Cassia touched his shoulder. “I’m sorry.”

He stood and took the shoe to the light. “It’s lodged deep. I’ll get a point.”

When he had gone, Cassia asked whether Kestra would return in two days. There were older drawings, kept separately from the modern reports. She wanted someone to compare the section beneath the western support with the current measurements.

“Thane could help with those,” Kestra said.

“Yes. Bring him if he wants to come.”

“And the people who used to tend the lower place. Sarnai’s people. Have you spoken to them?”

“Not recently enough.”

“We’re seeing her tomorrow.”

“Then ask what they remember. Let them finish before you show them what we have.”

Kestra nodded. She could already imagine the page where she would place the accounts beside one another, and she was trying not to love the neatness of it before she had heard them.

At the door Nerio returned her cloak. He had put the spectacles on at last. They made him look tired rather than scholarly.

“How long have you worked with her?” Kestra asked.

“Six years.”

“She knows you.”

“I know.” He handed over the cloak. “Some evenings she can’t get the name. That’s all.”

It plainly was not all, but Kestra did not ask him to make more of it for her. She thanked him for finding the reverse of the inventory. He smiled, briefly and with genuine pleasure.

Outside, a shopkeeper was carrying his wares indoors one tray at a time. Kestra stood aside while he negotiated the narrow door. She had been away from the others almost all day. She wanted to tell them about the date and the summer paper, about the impression that held a line more clearly than the others, about Cassia asking her to come back.

Then she remembered Damas asking what the looking was for.

At the Split Almond she took out his corrected account before she began to speak. Elena moved a lamp closer, and Benjamin made room on the table without putting away his supper. Kestra read them the change he had insisted on, the bread seller and the hour. Sariel listened with both hands resting around her cup. When Kestra finished, she asked what he had wanted to happen next.

“Go home,” Kestra said. “He wants to go home.”

No one answered for a while. Thane reached across to turn the page toward himself, and Kestra let him.

Chapter 7 — The Far Side of Market Day

Sariel recognized Yelü by the way the horse moved under her.

The rider’s coat was unfamiliar, dark green with a patched shoulder, and the hair which Sariel remembered coming loose about an impatient young face was braided tight against her head. But the mare had turned before the hand came back on the rein. Horse and woman moved through the market carts with the mild irritation of people obliged to make room for everyone else.

Yelü stopped beside a man who was selling onion strings from a hurdle. She bought two and hung them from her saddle. Then she looked at Sariel.

“You have been standing there quite a while.”

“I was making certain.”

“You could have called my name.”

“I wasn’t expecting you to be here.”

“My mother told me which road you would take. I wanted to meet you first.”

Benjamin came out of the leather seller’s booth with a pair of gloves tucked through his belt. Yelü examined him without disguise.

“You have kept most of your hair.”

“Through great personal effort.”

“And the others?”

“In town. Kestra is coming.”

“I meant their hair.”

Benjamin laughed, and Yelü smiled properly for the first time. The smile made her younger, then was gone as she glanced at the gate behind them.

Sariel had expected an escort out to Sarnai’s camp. Instead they accompanied Yelü through the last of the market. The company had permission to come and go during the hours of their inquiry; the doorkeeper at the north wicket had looked at the same paper three times, turning it to catch the light, before he let them out. Beyond the walls the trade ran along the old ditch and into a field where hoof prints held yesterday’s rain. Women sold wool from sheets spread over the grass. A potter had set his best blue bowls on a cart whose axle was beginning to complain. Between them, two boys tried to persuade a goat that an onion was food.

Yelü bought soap. She rejected a length of felt, accepted another and then put it back when the seller raised the price on seeing Benjamin. She spoke to him for some time in her own language. Sariel understood that he was too expensive before she understood any of the actual words.

“He has three pieces behind the cart,” Yelü told her as they moved on. “All cut from the same cloth. He says this one is thicker.”

“Did he charge you that last time?”

“No. And I paid his son to bring firewood yesterday. He knows I haven’t got more to spend.”

The son, sitting on the cart, pulled his cap over his ears and studied his hands.

Kestra found them by the wool sellers. She carried no scroll case today, only the little working book tucked into her coat, and she looked faintly disappointed that the meeting had begun without her. Yelü took her hand before she could apologize.

“My mother asked whether you would come,” Yelü said. “She remembers the paper you made at the Ford.”

“I remember having to write it again.”

“We still have it. She wanted you to know.”

Kestra’s expression softened. “I hope she is well.”

“Her hands trouble her. Otherwise, yes. Come and see.”

They left the market by a track beside the drainage ditch. The walls of Casta Ferrum rose behind them, a succession of repairs rather than one clean line: pale new stone under dark old battlements, a tower with its roof taken down, a strip of red laundry stirring over a postern. From this distance the bronze-faced outer gate looked gold. Sariel could see why a traveler might feel relieved when it opened.

Yelü dismounted where the track divided. A young man took her mare, then stood waiting while she unfastened the onions.

“The large string to my aunt. The other to us.”

He examined the strings. “They are the same.”

“My aunt asked for the larger ones. Those on your left, I think.”

He went, grinning. Yelü watched him put both over his own shoulders before she turned back.

“Mine,” she said, answering the question Sariel had not quite asked. “Those six shelters and the horses beyond. There are others away.”

Sariel had been looking for Sarnai’s standard. She shifted her attention to the nearer line of tents, where a woman knelt repairing a strap while a child held the end too high. A man’s boots stood beside the entrance of one shelter, the toes stuffed with wool. Smoke from the cooking hearth moved low over the grass before rising.

“How many people?” she asked.

“Twenty-three here. There are children with some of the others. I wish you could stay to meet them.”

“I’d like that.”

Yelü looked pleased. She pointed them toward the larger tent at the far end.

Sarnai was sitting on a low stool while a boy combed loose hair from a goatskin. The comb looked too fine for the work. She took it from him, inspected its teeth and said something that made him fetch another.

“People bring gifts,” she said to Sariel. “Then expect you to know what they are for.”

“It was meant for your hair?”

“My sister’s. She gave it to me.”

There was more gray in Sarnai’s hair than Sariel remembered, and a thickening at one knuckle that changed how she held the comb. Otherwise the principal change was that Sariel no longer mistook stillness for inactivity. Sarnai had looked over their clothes, their boots and Benjamin’s gloves before anyone was seated.

She asked after Elena, then Thane. She remembered the hand. Benjamin began to explain that he had found ways to work, and Sarnai interrupted gently.

“I asked how he was.”

“Busy,” Sariel said. “Annoyed that the Gate has been repaired badly. He may have found the one subject on which your Prefect and he could agree.”

“I remember how much it hurt him,” Sarnai said. “I’m glad he has work he wants to do.”

Yelü took the onion strings from the boy who had brought them and laid both beside the tent. He started to explain. She shook her head and sent him off before he could divide them again.

They drank something hot and sour from shallow cups. Sariel was grateful for it; the wind had gone through the market without appearing to move much, leaving her cold under her collar. A woman brought strips of mutton wrapped in thin bread, with chopped onions in a separate bowl. Benjamin took too much onion. He looked at his bread, accepted the difficulty and ate it.

Sarnai watched with the pleasure of a hostess whose food was being attended to.

“Tema said you would ask what he was buying before you sold it.”

“We asked him what he knew.”

“And?”

“He knew whom to ask next.”

“That is a valuable kind of knowing.”

Sariel set down her cup. “He wants us to take the Seal out of the Gate and bring it to him for you. We haven’t agreed to the taking.”

Sarnai nodded. There was no performance of surprise.

“Will you hear the people who want it returned?”

“That’s why we came.”

“It is not why everyone comes. Some wish to tell me where I ought to keep it afterward.”

A man and a woman had been sitting beyond the hearth, near enough to hear but not joining the food. The man rose slowly. His left boot had a thick sole, and the adjustment showed in the settled angle of his hip. The woman carried a flat parcel wrapped in dark felt. She was perhaps Sariel’s age, broad through the shoulders, with the short nails of someone who worked in cold water.

“Altan,” Sarnai said. “And Sare, his brother’s daughter. Their people kept the lower place.”

“Still keep it,” the woman said.

Sarnai inclined her head. “Yes.”

Sare set her parcel on a board between the cups. She unfolded the felt carefully, keeping grit away from the food. Inside lay a curved piece of fired clay, no larger than Sariel’s palm, with a hole pierced through one end. One side was smoke-black. On the other someone had scratched marks that looked at first like a badly drawn row of houses.

“From the old lamp dish,” Sare said. “Broken by the men who opened the chamber. We were allowed to collect what they threw out. Nothing that could be sold.”

Kestra leaned forward. She kept her hands in her lap.

“May I?”

Sare pushed the board toward her. Kestra touched the edge of the clay rather than lifting it.

“The marks are names?”

“Places in a family,” Altan said. “You would need the rest.”

“Does the city have it?”

“The city has a heap.”

Sare watched Kestra turn the fragment toward the light. “It stood beside the cover stone. There was a hollow beneath. The bronze thing lay in it, wrapped. We changed the cloth when it rotted. My grandmother did it before me.”

“What did she say it was?” Sariel asked.

“A thing left to keep an agreement.”

“Whose agreement?”

“Men who wanted to build over people they had killed.” Sare took the fragment back when Kestra offered it. “They said the road would not touch the graves again. The dead could keep what they had sworn by.”

Altan spoke after her, less sharply. He described the old passage, a wall repaired without moving the stone, the cups of water left at a shelf. His grandmother had remembered another keeper, and that keeper had learned from someone already old. There were disagreements about the words. In one account a commander had laid down the Seal willingly; in another his surviving soldiers had done it after he died. None of the keepers’ versions gave the city leave to put it back in a man’s hand.

Sariel could imagine the Prefect listening to this. She could imagine how easily he would ask for a date, then a witness, then the name of the man who had first witnessed it, until there was nothing on the table but an old woman’s grandson and a broken dish.

“The Censor has the excavation record,” Kestra said.

Sare’s expression changed. “Then perhaps she can find the rest of our lamp.”

“I can ask.”

“Ask her where they put the earth from under the cover. They swept it into sacks. Those may still be in the lower store.”

Kestra took out her little book.

Sariel let the questions continue. Returning the Seal would require more than putting a chest through a door. The hollow had to be cleared without cutting farther into the grave. The earth had to go back. Sare wanted the old cover stone supported and lowered, not dropped; Altan wanted to see the join himself, because the sketch a laborer had brought out showed a new drain where no drain should be. The two of them disagreed about whether fresh cloth would serve. Sare thought it would. Altan thought the stolen cloth ought to be found if it still existed.

“Does it matter which?” Sariel asked.

Altan looked at her. “We do not know.”

He did not seem ashamed of the answer. Kestra left room beneath the question in her book.

“And the people who are ill?” Benjamin asked.

Sare pressed one finger on the broken dish, stopping it from rocking.

“The first men became ill after they moved it. Before your Prefect began making everyone touch it.”

“Would returning it help them?”

“It helped before,” Altan said. “The accounts agree on that much.”

“They agree about an illness two hundred years ago,” Sarnai added. “They cannot tell you whether Damas will be well tomorrow.”

Sariel had not told her about Damas. She glanced at Yelü, who was scraping onion from the board into a cloth.

“His cousin sells us charcoal,” Yelü said.

Of course she did. Sariel had arrived with the porter’s name held ready, as though the city had waited for her to notice him.

They walked after the meal, leaving Altan and Sare to argue quietly over the felt. Sarnai took them to the crest above the camp, where the wind pressed the grass flat and the Gate could be seen without the market in front of it.

“The Prefect has offered us admission,” she said.

“To the chamber?”

“With soldiers present. We may put down flowers, say the words he approves and depart before the evening closing.”

“In return?”

“Each household using the road must answer for any rider sheltered in it. No food for a person named by the Prefect. No arms carried within the marked approaches. Reports of movement beyond them.”

“That’s more than passage.”

“He says passage cannot be safe without it.”

Below, a cart waited while two soldiers examined something in its back. The driver stood with a hand on his horse. At this distance all three men appeared patient.

“He kept the road open last winter,” Yelü said. “The west settlements had flour. People remember.”

“And he remembers them remembering,” Sarnai said.

Sariel asked whether the Seal had been shown at one of these meetings. Sarnai held out her own hand, palm up.

“Here. He said I would touch it when the words were agreed.”

“What did you do?”

“Kept discussing the words.”

Benjamin looked at the open hand. “He hadn’t sworn first?”

“He had sworn to fulfill the duties of his office.”

“Who decides what those are?”

Sarnai’s mouth moved, almost a smile. “That was one of the words we discussed.”

Sariel could hear a goat below them, objecting to whatever the boy was doing with it. The sound came up clearly, then was lost in the wind. She tucked her hands under her arms.

“We can investigate the building. Speak to Cassia. Find out whether the thing can be moved without hurting someone.”

“Someone else,” Yelü said.

Sariel accepted that. “Someone else.”

“And if it can?” Sarnai asked.

“Then we will have to decide whether we take Tema’s job.”

“He has priced delivery to me.”

“He has. He hasn’t priced what we learn first.”

Sarnai studied her. Sariel had used the sentence because she thought it sounded capable. In the silence afterward she heard the faint suggestion that knowledge would entitle her to revise everybody else’s wishes.

“I mean we won’t bring soldiers here because we failed to ask a question,” she said.

“That would be useful.”

Yelü crouched to pull a stone out of her boot. She remained there for a moment, looking toward the road.

“You opened the Ford,” she said. “People still say so.”

“A good many people opened it.”

“Yes. But you left. The people who stayed have been explaining the agreement to new men ever since. Some years they read it. Last year one wanted to see Arsai.”

Sariel looked at her.

“He thought the name meant a witness he could summon.”

The wind lifted a strand of hair from Yelü’s braid. She tucked it back with a dirty finger, rose and tested the boot.

“My cousin fetched his mother. She brought the boy. That made him read it.”

“Is Oyun well?” Benjamin asked.

“Well enough to dislike being fetched.”

He nodded slowly. Sariel was grateful he did not try to add anything kind.

On the way down Sarnai took Sariel aside. It required no ceremony; she simply walked more slowly, and Yelü went ahead with the others.

“If you bring it out,” Sarnai said, “bring it where we can speak before the Gate knows where it has gone.”

“To you.”

“Yes.”

Sariel looked toward the tents. “Tema said you wanted it buried.”

“I do.”

Sarnai’s pace did not change. She stepped over a rut, tested the ground with her heel and continued.

“The Prefect has spoken many promises to me. It would be useful to know that one of them would hold.”

Sariel could have objected. Instead she imagined Marcellus’s narrow hand laid over the bronze, and the surprising comfort of not needing to trust his expression afterward.

“We haven’t taken the job,” she said.

“I heard you.”

The market was packing when they returned. Yelü left them at the onion seller, who had reduced his prices and seemed personally aggrieved by it. The felt merchant called after her. She kept walking until he named the price she had offered earlier, then went back as though the conversation had only paused.

Kestra waited, interested. Benjamin bought the smaller of the remaining onion strings.

“We have onions at the lodging,” Sariel said.

“These will keep.”

“Where will you put them?”

He looked down at the gloves through his belt, the sword, the old purse and the space between them. “I’ll carry them.”

He did, all the way through the wicket and up the street. The doorkeeper made them wait while he checked their visitor paper again. Benjamin held the onions out of his way. A woman behind them laughed softly at something her companion said, and Benjamin looked at Sariel with the helpless pleasure he still took in being the cause of a harmless joke.

She took his free hand after the wicket, briefly, before the street became too narrow to walk abreast.

At the Split Almond Thane was waiting beside a stool turned upside down. Three metal pieces lay on the underside of its seat. He had grease on his left cheek and a very pleased expression.

“Don’t sit,” he said.

Benjamin looked at the stool. “I had worked that out.”

“Not there either. That’s for the hinge.”

Sariel hung her coat on the peg and went to see what he had found.

Chapter 8 — Work Worth Buying

The engineer wanted Thane to look at the Gate before the carts began moving. Thane wanted Elena to see the lower stair. Elena wanted the woman presently gripping her sleeve to release it without losing her balance.

“One thing at a time,” she said.

The woman, who had three grandchildren and a well-developed objection to being helped, mistook this for agreement and tried to stand. Her knee folded. Elena caught her under the arm and sat her back on the bench.

“Beginning with your foot on the floor.”

“It is on the floor.”

“Both of them.”

Dometta appeared in the doorway with a cup in each hand. She gave one to the woman and drank from the other.

“Let her finish, Avia. She has to go and see a gate. It has been complaining for longer than you have.”

“It should pay you, then.”

“The city will consider the question next month.”

Avia looked relieved. Elena adjusted the folded cloth under her heel. The knee had stiffened after a fall; there was no fever, no sudden swelling, but the stairs to Avia’s rooms were steep and her son was asking when she would be able to carry water again. Dometta had told him to carry it himself. He had replied that he was at work during the day, and Dometta had suggested night.

Elena liked her increasingly.

They made Avia take three steps with a borrowed stick. She disliked the stick more than she disliked being unable to walk, until she discovered that it could also be used to indicate where the chair ought to be put. Dometta moved it without argument. Elena wrote a few words for the son, including the times when his mother should try the steps, and left the paper under the cup where she could see it.

Outside, Thane had sat down on the low wall and begun repairing a strap on his tool roll. He did not ask why she was late. That pleased her until she saw that he had expected the delay and brought the strap especially.

“You could have gone ahead.”

“Octan won’t open the lower stair without two visitors and a city man. He says the fourth person is for carrying the others out.”

“I’d like to meet this optimist.”

“He has worked there twenty-six years.”

That explained a good deal.

The public lane was empty except for two sweepers pushing muddy straw toward the east arch. Up in the high slots the bronze-clad barrier hung above their heads, its lower teeth blunt with wear. Elena had passed under it three times already and had not looked closely at how much wood and metal was being persuaded to remain in the air.

Octan made her look now.

“That one is sitting lower,” he said, pointing with a short rod. “See the daylight over the west shoe? Shouldn’t be any.”

He was thick through the chest and narrow at the hips, his gray beard trimmed close so it would not catch in things. One sleeve of his work coat had been replaced in a darker cloth. The newer cuff was already blackened.

“I see it,” Elena said.

“Good. People usually say they do and look at the wrong side.”

He led them through the north wicket into the service court. Here the city had begun its day: a woman counted empty oil jars, two men carried folded screens toward the inner stair, and a clerk in a knitted cap argued with a delivery boy about the size of his bundle. The court door opened east onto the carrier lane. Elena stood a moment beside it, fixing the turns in her head. Through that door and left, past the dry yard, then two turns to Dometta’s rooms. A litter would fit the narrowest stretch if the handles were kept level.

Thane noticed where she was looking.

“Not yet,” he said softly.

“I know.”

She knew they had accepted an inquiry. She also knew that Sariel was finding the building interesting.

Kestra joined them at the stair with her little book. Octan gave her a lamp, waited while she checked its oil, then unlocked the door to the lower works.

The air below smelled of wet lime and the warm, animal breath of a place with no animals in it. Elena held the handrail. In places it had been polished by use; in others her fingers found flakes of orange rust. They went down a turn, crossed a short landing under the public road and descended again to a vestibule with three doors. One was a tool store. One led to a drain inspection channel, narrow enough that Octan was emphatic about nobody trying it. The third had been barred from the outside.

“Burial place,” he said. “That’s as far as we go.”

Fresh iron staples bit into old stone on either side. Under them someone had scraped away a strip of dark plaster. Elena could see the pale beginnings of marks beneath the abrasion, but could not make them into letters.

Kestra set down the lamp and walked back to the last turn, counting under her breath. Thane knelt by the base of the west wall. Octan crouched beside him, slower than Thane but with none of the fuss Elena was accustomed to hearing from men whose knees hurt.

They spoke quietly. Elena caught measurements, then the scrape of a tool. A little dust came away from a joint.

“That’s been packed,” Thane said.

“It was packed before my time.”

“The front has. Behind it, look.”

Octan took the tool, reached into the opened joint and frowned. He tried again from a different angle.

Elena watched his expression change without any great outward display. He sat back on his heels and looked up toward the ceiling, as though the moving carts could be seen through it.

“They said they had only cleared the chamber entrance.”

“Who did?”

“Excavation men. Orders from the Prefect’s office. I sent down two of mine to watch the drain. They were sent up again because it wasn’t drain work.”

“Have you had more drag since?”

“Yes.”

Thane wiped the tool and offered it back. Octan did not take it immediately.

“How much?” he asked.

“I can’t tell from here. Enough to let that guide move. More if they took stone from under the pier.”

Elena glanced toward the barred door. “Does the road need closing?”

Octan answered first. “Heavy loads, until we shore it. People will complain.”

“They can complain while walking,” Thane said.

Octan took the tool. “I’ll write that in the order.”

He went up ahead of them, calling for a man called Lert. Kestra remained by the barred door for another moment, making a mark in her book. When Elena asked whether she had seen anything, she shook her head.

“Just the distance. Seventy-four ordinary paces, taking the turns. I wanted it before I forgot which landing I had counted twice.”

Elena was glad of the admission. There was enough below the Gate that seemed willing to be misunderstood.

Above the lane, the machinery occupied a long room open on one side to the wall walk. Grease-dark wheels stood on heavy bearings. A control line passed over a drum and down through a slot. Every small motion had a larger piece waiting to answer it.

Thane listened while Octan described the trouble. He asked to see the shoe, then the guide, then where the guide was fastened. Elena stayed beside the wall, out of the way of men who knew where they were going. From here the eastern road looked narrow and heavily used. A cart stood beyond the gate with a mound of turnips under a net; the driver had begun eating one while he waited.

Octan’s apprentice brought a wooden model from a shelf. It had been made for training, with a weight tied to a string and a sliding scrap of oak for the barrier. Thane bent over it. When he drew a small metal collar from his pocket, Elena recognized one of the pieces he had forbidden Benjamin to sit on the night before.

“Pellia made this?” Octan asked.

“It’s hers. I haven’t bought it.”

“She’ll sell you the whole forge if you look at it like that.”

Thane was already fitting the collar over the model’s guide.

Elena saw the pleasure arrive in him. It changed the set of his shoulders, took away the caution with which he ordinarily explained an idea to people who might mistake it for a promise. Here he could stop in the middle and try something. Octan watched. The apprentice leaned across his shoulder until Octan pushed him aside without looking.

The model jammed. Thane moved the collar, tried again and grinned when the strip of wood descended crookedly but stopped above the board.

“Not a full descent,” he said. “Only the last travel, if the west side slips. Brace the guide here. The collar keeps it from going out before the blocks take it.”

“And if the brace goes?”

“The pin gives first.”

Octan took the model away from him and tested it with his own hand. “Provided we know what the pin will take.”

“We can find out.”

“Not with the Gate.”

Thane looked offended. Octan’s smile was brief and unapologetic.

They argued over the thickness of a pin while Elena went down to the court. The morning carts were being unloaded into smaller loads. Men protested, carried turnips by the basket and returned to protest again. She helped an old driver lift his water jar from the cart rather than watching him twist under it. He thanked her and asked whether she knew when the road would open properly.

“No.”

“At least you say so.”

There was already a line of people who had found a use for being made to wait. A boy sold hot bean cakes. Two women inspected each other’s cloth. Beyond them, a soldier asked a porter to repeat his name, and the porter answered loudly enough that the women stopped speaking.

Elena bought two cakes and went back upstairs before she could forget whom the second was for.

Pellia’s forge stood behind the baths, where the air smelled alternately of hot iron and people trying to become clean. The smith had one close-cropped white eyebrow and one dark one. She wore her sleeves pinned above her elbows and listened to Octan’s explanation while turning a small piece of red metal with her tongs.

“You want me to make something that breaks,” she said.

“At a known load,” Thane replied.

“I’ll need the load and the direction. There’s no use making a weak pin and hoping.”

He showed her the model. She set the iron down and washed her hands, because she would not blacken the little wooden grooves. Then she tested his arrangement, frowned and asked why the collar was there rather than below the shoe.

He answered at length. Elena ate her cake by the door. She understood perhaps half, but it was pleasant to hear him speaking to someone who wanted all of the explanation. Pellia interrupted him twice. The second time he stopped, considered and moved the collar lower.

“Oh,” he said. “Yes.”

Elena had seen him say that with Kestra, or with Dorra when they met in Millhaven. He did not always say it easily. Today he seemed delighted that the thing could be made better before he owned it.

Pellia named a price. Octan made a small sound through his teeth.

“That includes testing,” she said.

“It ought to include the turnips waiting outside.”

Thane drew out his purse.

Elena looked at him. “The city is hiring you.”

“The city can buy its bracing. I want the collar pattern and the fitting.”

“For after?”

“For several things.”

She knew that voice too. A commission had given him a reason to be here; it had not given him permission to enjoy what he found. He counted out his own money on the clean end of the bench. Pellia pushed two coins back and made him take the smaller pin as well.

“You’ll ruin the first one learning,” she said. “I’d rather you didn’t tell people I made a bad pin.”

On the way out Elena bought a blue glass clasp from a tray in the bathkeeper’s window. It was shaped like a fish, badly enough that it might also have been a leaf. The bathkeeper offered a better one for twice the price.

“I like this one.”

Thane held it to the light for her. “Is it meant to be a fish?”

“I think so. I like the color.”

“So do I.” He turned it once more, letting the light come through the blue glass, then held her bag while she pinned it to the plain part where it served no useful purpose.

Dometta lived over her rooms, in a warm upper chamber with a view of three roofs and a fig tree that had refused to die. The table was much too large for the space. Everyone had to move sideways past it, and Dometta defended it with the air of someone who had fought this battle before.

“My mother had eight children. I am not throwing out the table because seven learned to live elsewhere.”

“You were the one who stayed?”

“I was the one who acquired the house. Staying happened afterward.”

She put a pot of beans on the table, then a dish of crisp onions and a jug of thin wine. A wedge of hard cheese stood beneath a damp cloth. Elena had been invited for supper and intended to eat enough to make that invitation worthwhile.

They spoke about Avia’s stairs, about a plaster that Dometta had stopped using because it came away with more skin than either of them liked, about the cost of clean wool. Dometta had worked in three towns before returning here. She knew the south road by its bad inns and the west road by a man she had once thought of marrying.

“What happened?” Elena asked.

“He sold horses.”

“That doesn’t sound terminal.”

“He sold mine while I was visiting my sister.”

Elena laughed with her mouth full and had to put down her spoon. Dometta went on eating, pleased.

“Good price,” she added. “He mentioned that several times.”

Outside, the last light caught the fig leaves. A child in the next house was practicing a song and getting the same passage wrong. After the third attempt a man’s voice joined in, confidently and also wrong.

Elena thought of Tavia in Silkstrand, of the way her letters sometimes ran out of room at the bottom and continued up the side. There was one folded in the back of her case. She had answered the questions and left the rest for a quieter evening. Now she wanted to write about the fish clasp, which was a ridiculous thing to send across that much country and exactly the sort of thing Tavia would answer.

“Have you someone waiting?” Dometta asked.

“People. Work.”

“I meant someone who will be annoyed you bought yourself that and not them.”

Elena touched the clasp. “Yes.”

“Good. Send an accurate description. Make it worse.”

The child next door started again. This time Dometta sang the troublesome notes softly over her beans, getting them right. Elena listened until she finished.

“Why don’t you tell them?”

“I did yesterday. They’ve decided they prefer it.”

They ate the last of the onions without improving anyone.

Nerio came just as Elena was helping wash the dishes. His knock was hurried enough that Dometta put down the cloth before she called him in.

“Is she worse?”

“She says she isn’t.”

“That was not an answer.”

“No. I thought you might recognize it.”

Elena dried her hands. Nerio looked older in the doorway than he had at the Archive, his face drawn by the effort of behaving as though he had only come on an ordinary errand. He held Cassia’s discarded silver lens in a small lined box. The metal rim had left a red curve across the folded cloth.

“She forgot to put it away,” he said. “Then told me I had taken it.”

Dometta closed the medicine cupboard and took down her bag.

Cassia was sitting at her desk when they arrived, one lamp lit and another burning too low. Without the lens the left side of her face looked unexpectedly bare. Elena had not noticed how much of its severity belonged to the device.

“I asked him to fetch a record,” Cassia said.

“You asked for someone who left the Archive last year,” Nerio replied.

“A record can be fetched by another person.”

“So I thought.”

Dometta bent over Cassia’s wrist. Elena moved the lamp before the Censor could object. Ink had dried on her fingers. Beside the blotting cloth lay a biscuit bitten once and put down.

Cassia watched Elena look at it.

“I was interrupted.”

“By the work?”

“Among other things.”

Elena brought the biscuit within reach and poured a little water. She did not ask Cassia to admit she had forgotten it. The woman picked it up, swallowed with difficulty and set it down again.

Nerio stood beside the shelves. Cassia turned toward him.

“Bring the blue folder, if you please. The one about the lower works.”

He fetched it. She took it without saying his name.

Elena waited while Dometta finished. When they stepped into the passage, she asked quietly how long Cassia had been avoiding names.

Dometta looked back at the open door. “Long enough to find other ways of sounding impolite.”

“Does Nerio know?”

“Yes.”

Inside, Cassia told him the folder was the wrong shade of blue. He answered that he had brought the one she wanted, then opened it at the marked page and placed it beneath her good eye.

She touched his sleeve before he could move away.

“Thank you.”

He remained there while she read.

Chapter 9 — What Was Dug Up

Cassia had put a cushion on the visitor’s chair.

Kestra saw it when she entered and spent several foolish moments wondering whether it was for her. It was a plain thing, green wool faded almost to gray, with a repair along one edge. Nerio moved a stack of folders from the seat beside it and told her the light would be better there.

“I thought we would use the long table,” Kestra said.

“The Censor has brought what you asked for.”

Cassia was at her own desk, turning a page with the flat of her hand. Her left eye was uncovered. The silver lens lay on a folded cloth, beside the little bottle used to clean its inner rim.

“The long table is being used by a man who wishes to prove that his father’s field was wider,” she said. “He has brought his grandfather’s map. We may be some time.”

Kestra sat. The cushion was warm from the sun. She opened her book at the questions she had written after seeing Sare’s broken dish, and Cassia drew it toward her.

“May I read this?”

“Yes. The lower line is mine. The one above is what she said.”

“I see.”

Cassia read both twice. It was the second reading that pleased Kestra. Most people heard a distinction once and then proceeded as though it made no difference.

“You have kept the uncertainty,” Cassia said. “Good. I have three copies here in which someone has improved it away.”

She set a heavy folder between them and worked loose its cloth ties. The top sheet was a drawing of the Gate’s lower foundations. Kestra recognized the stair and the barred door, then found herself looking for the place where Thane had opened the joint. The drawing showed intact stone there. A later hand had added a small circle in red.

“That is where the face was removed,” Cassia said. “The circle was drawn after the first inspection.”

“After the work?”

“After the first authorized inspection of work already done.”

Kestra looked up. Cassia met her eyes without the lens between them.

“Keep those apart too.”

Nerio brought a second lamp, although the room was still bright, and placed it where it would not shine in Cassia’s uncovered eye. She thanked him. He adjusted the shutter and withdrew to a smaller desk near the door.

The early pages were ordinary enough: labor hired, stone removed, a disagreement over a drain, sketches made by someone who drew hands better than arches. In the margin beside one measurement a worker had drawn his own thumb to show how much space remained. Kestra smiled at it before she saw the next page.

The cover stone lay tilted on blocks. Under it was a shallow recess, the outline of a wrapped object, a lamp on a shelf. The artist had tried to distinguish cloth from earth with little parallel strokes. Someone had written bronze die with iron stem beneath the shape and, later, crossed out die.

“The Seal,” Kestra said.

“Before they called it by its name.”

“Why Iron?”

“The shank is iron. Or the name survives from an earlier object. I cannot tell you. The bronze face has been recut.”

Kestra leaned closer. The device was there, an eagle above a broken wheel. One claw had been worn almost smooth. The drawing gave the scale against the excavator’s hand; it was smaller than she had imagined, large enough to sit heavily in the palm.

She remembered Damas trying to close his fingers around a cup.

Cassia followed her glance and turned the page.

“The first two men were ill within a week. Both had been sleeping near the recovered objects. One improved when he was sent home. The other did not.”

“Had they touched it?”

“Yes.”

“Had they sworn anything?”

“The second had. An undertaking that nothing recovered would be concealed or removed from the Prefect’s custody. We have the words. The first was a boy carrying baskets. He had been told to keep his hands off and had disobeyed.”

“Then touch alone—”

“Did not do the same thing to both. That is what we can say.”

Kestra stopped with the sentence half formed. She liked that Cassia would not let her finish it too easily. She also disliked being corrected before she had decided whether she meant it.

They compared the accounts. The basket carrier had dreamed that someone was asking him his name from behind a wall. He slept badly for three nights after leaving, then slept well. The sworn worker coughed, grew confused over familiar tasks and insisted he had returned everything he took. He had a small piece of bronze in his room, a buckle found among the rubble and kept because no one had valued it. When he surrendered the buckle, he did not recover.

Kestra copied the sequence. She did not copy the man’s name into the page Sariel would carry outside the Archive.

“Did they try taking the Seal away from him?”

“He had already been sent home.”

“Farther away, then?”

“His family took him west. We received one letter afterward. He was still ill.”

Cassia paused. She put a finger between the pages and pressed the heel of her other hand to her brow.

Kestra waited, then asked whether she should stop.

“No. Give me the water.”

It was too far from Cassia’s good side. Kestra moved it closer and continued reading while the older woman drank.

A later list recorded the sworn workers. Some remained well. Beside their names were duties narrow enough to perform: open a store at the stated hour, present an object for inspection, carry a sealed message to a named office. Another page held the broader promises. One man had undertaken to keep his sons from carrying weapons beyond the city. One of the sons had already gone east. The father had said so before touching the Seal. The clerk had written it beneath his name.

“They knew,” Kestra said.

“Yes.”

“He told them.”

“Yes.”

The answer was flat now because there was nothing else in it. Kestra felt her earlier pleasure cooling into an anger without a useful direction. She wanted to ask how any person trained to keep records could have written that down and allowed the ceremony to continue. Cassia was already turning to another page.

“Before you ask me what I did,” she said, “I ought to show you.”

The document was a recommendation in Cassia’s own hand.

Kestra knew the writing by then: small clear letters, an impatient hook at the end of a word when the thought was already moving on. The recommendation proposed using the recovered instrument in cases of deliberate commercial deception, under the Censor’s supervision, with terms supplied in advance. It required a record of consent.

“I thought we could stop a certain kind of lie,” Cassia said. “A man sells the same load twice, or declares a weight he knows is false. You must understand how long it can take to make that matter. The injured person may be ruined before the hearing ends.”

Kestra did understand. She had copied enough claims whose dates had outlived the people who made them. She wished Cassia had given her a worse reason.

“The first case was a warehouse owner. He had concealed a side room and charged carriers for grain he said had spoiled. The room was found. He accepted the terms in front of two witnesses. He paid the men he had cheated.”

“Did he become ill?”

“No.”

“So they called it a success.”

“I called it a success.”

Cassia let Kestra look at the recommendation for as long as she wanted. The signature at the bottom was firm. There was no space beside it in which a later explanation could be mistaken for part of the original.

“What did you promise?” Kestra asked.

Cassia drew the next sheet out herself.

“To report all material findings relating to the recovery and use of the instrument to the Prefect’s office. To withhold no testimony bearing on the city’s safe custody of it.”

The words were set down in a clerk’s hand. Beneath them Cassia had signed again. The witness column named two people Kestra did not know.

“And Vettia?”

Cassia’s hand stopped on the edge of the paper.

Kestra lowered her voice. “Sariel told us enough to understand that someone could be hurt. She didn’t tell the lodging house.”

“I know.” Cassia looked toward Nerio, who was still working by the door. “I keep finding that I want to hear the assurance again.”

Kestra had expected a defense. The admission disarmed her more effectively than a defense would have done.

Cassia described the report without naming the place where Vettia now slept. The copyist had recorded orders to continue below the western pier after two workers objected. When the first sickness appeared, she had copied the original instructions before they were replaced by a cleaner version. The new version made the laborers responsible for exceeding the work. Vettia’s copy proved otherwise, but publishing it would also show who had made it. There were only three people who could have seen that paper, and two were still in the Prefect’s household.

“I delayed,” Cassia said. “I asked for a second account. I requested the original, knowing which original they would send. When the time came to present the findings, I submitted the measurements and withheld her statement.”

“How long before you were ill?”

“The cough that evening. I had already forgotten a name that morning, but I refused to connect it. There was smoke in the street. A bad night. I am old enough to find convenient explanations for many things.”

Kestra looked at the silver lens on its cloth.

“Could you see the binding?”

“Disturbance. A repeat in the residue where there ought to have been variation. When I looked at my hand, I thought I saw the shape of the Seal without the Seal being there.”

“Thought?”

“I was exhausted. The lens hurt. I tried twice more and saw less, not more.”

She touched the skin beside her left eye. The mark there was red and shiny, recently salved.

“I tried removing one of the smaller signs from an impression. The surface changed. The illness did not. Afterward I lost half a day and could not remember what I had asked my assistant to bring. He tells me I was very angry with him.”

At the other desk Nerio’s pen stopped. Cassia did not turn around.

“I won’t do that again,” she said.

Kestra had been reaching for a scrap of paper on which to draw the repeated sign. She folded her hands together instead.

It was not that she had expected to solve the thing at once. She had expected, though, that her arrival would give Cassia a fresh way to proceed. The thought had been pleasant. She felt ashamed of its persistence even now, with the woman sitting opposite her unable to name the man who had brought her water.

“I can compare the earlier accounts,” Kestra said. “The ones from before the excavation.”

“That would help.”

“Without altering anything.”

Cassia nodded. “Start there.”

They found the older account in a bundle whose first pages concerned roofing expenses.

Nerio knew where it was. He had already brought it to Cassia once; she remembered the roofing and had sent it back. He did not mention that until she asked why she had not seen it before. Then he answered plainly, and she closed her eyes for a moment.

“I am sorry.”

“I know. Here.”

He put the bundle on the cushion beside Kestra and helped her separate the folded leaves. The copy had been made three generations earlier from something older, with gaps left where the copyist could not read. A few words had been supplied in another ink. Kestra ignored those on the first pass.

There was an illness after the reopening of the lower place. A keeper had asked for the return of the thing laid beneath the cover. The commander’s men had refused, then relented after several of their own became sick. Earth was brought back in baskets. The bronze was wrapped and put beneath the stone. The account named the number of laborers who lowered it, then spent a line on the cost of oil.

Kestra read it aloud. Cassia followed with one finger.

“It doesn’t say they were well,” Kestra said when she reached the end.

“No. Read the next year’s expenses.”

She did. One of the men named as sick was paid to repair the same roof. Another received wages for transporting the new lintel. Two names did not recur.

“Enough to ask,” Kestra said.

“Enough to ask carefully.”

The account could not tell them how quickly the men recovered. It did not explain why the illness had begun, or whether the renewed burial did everything credited to it. But it described the hollow and the shelf for the lamp, and the earth brought back, and the return of an object the soldiers had first wished to keep.

Sare had asked for the sacks from beneath the cover. Kestra found them in the modern excavation inventory, grouped with material awaiting disposal. Their storage mark pointed to the room beside the lower vestibule.

“They’re still there?”

Nerio reached for the most recent sheet. “They were last counted six days ago.”

“Can you prevent them being emptied?”

“I can issue a preservation notice,” Cassia said. “Whether that prevents anything depends on who is holding the shovel.”

This time the bitterness in her voice was not directed at Kestra. She pulled a clean sheet toward herself and began the order. Her hand shook enough that the first letter was wrong. She crossed it out and started again below.

Kestra waited until she had finished before asking for a copy of the older account.

“For the keepers,” she said. “They should see it.”

Cassia nodded. “Take the drawing too. The names on the modern report remain here.”

“I understand.”

“I know you do.”

Kestra looked up at that, and Cassia smiled tiredly. The smile made her want, absurdly, to do a better job than she had already intended.

Sare met her beyond the east arch at the place where the potter had left his cart. The bowls were packed away. He had spread a mat beneath the axle and was lying there with one knee up, investigating the noise it made.

Kestra read the old account while Sare followed the drawing. Altan was with her, carrying a small sack of fresh cloth and a wooden measuring rod. He listened without interrupting. When Kestra reached the return of the earth, his hand closed on the rod.

“That is how she said it,” he murmured.

“Your grandmother?”

“My aunt. She remembered being made to fetch more baskets.”

“From this reopening?”

“No. The keeping afterward. They renewed the edge of the grave when the drain failed. We weren’t allowed to move it farther back.”

Kestra was relieved that he corrected her. She had nearly supplied him with the confirmation she wanted.

Sare pointed to the new drain on the modern drawing. “This crosses the corner. It must be stopped before the cover goes down.”

“Thane can look at it.”

“He can help us see what has to be done. We will look at it.”

“Yes.”

The potter emerged with a handful of old grease and asked whether any of them had a rag. Altan gave him a strip from the sack. Sare watched, then took the sack and tied it shut.

“Not the rest,” she told him. “That is for below.”

He held up his empty hands in acknowledgment. Kestra tucked the copies into their wrapper and offered them to Sare, who took them under her arm without ceremony.

“What will the Censor charge for these?” she asked.

“Nothing. She wants you to have them.”

Sare rubbed the fold of the wrapper between finger and thumb. She looked toward the Gate, where a clerk was calling for the next cart.

“Tell her I have them,” she said. “And ask whether she has found our dish.”

Kestra said she would.

Chapter 10 — A Professional Offer

Lena was waiting at the Split Almond with a chipped cup and no tea in it.

The woman who kept the lodging had given her the cup to justify her presence in the courtyard. Sariel recognized the arrangement. A person could sit for quite a while with a cup, provided she did not begin asking about the rooms. Lena had been watching the carpenter across the court fit a new latch. He was working from the wrong side of the door and had not yet realized it.

Sariel set down her bag.

“You could tell him.”

“He hasn’t asked.”

“He’ll have to take it off again.”

“I know.”

The carpenter turned the latch, shut the door and discovered that he had locked himself out. He stared at it. Lena looked away, allowing him to discover what had happened without an audience.

Sariel drew up the other chair.

“Have you been waiting long?”

“Long enough to see the beginning of that. I need to speak to you before you go back to the Gate.”

“About work?”

“Yes.”

Sariel took the empty cup inside, paid for tea for two and returned with a pot. Lena accepted her cup after asking what it cost. Sariel told her. The girl put half on the table without making an argument of it.

They waited while the carpenter borrowed a thinner tool from the kitchen.

“I can get into the service court tomorrow evening,” Lena said. “The men carrying the screens use the east cart door. There are enough bundles to make the clerk impatient, but he counts them on the way in, not the people coming out.”

“You have someone among them?”

“I have a way in.”

Sariel poured. She liked being told enough to judge the offer and no more. She also disliked that Lena had judged exactly how much she needed.

“And from the court?”

“To the upper stair. Through the scriptorium before the evening clerk locks it. There is a short time when the day men have left and the night men haven’t come up.”

“The reliquary is locked all day.”

“Yes.”

“You’ve looked at it.”

Lena met her eyes. “I couldn’t do it quietly with the time I would have. You might.”

It was said without flattery. Sariel let herself enjoy it nonetheless.

“Who told you I could?”

“The people who saw you bring the tribunal box back. Also your hands. You keep the tension tool separate from the others because its tip is soft.”

Sariel glanced at the old case lying half open in her bag. Hest would have approved of the observation and then asked why the case had been left where a stranger could see it.

“The reliquary has other protections,” Sariel said.

“I expected that. Is your friend able to read them?”

“Some.”

“And the chest is heavier than you want to carry through a shift change.”

“Perhaps you should tell me whose chest you think it is.”

“The one with the Seal. Rusk has paid for the attempt. He hasn’t paid me yet.”

The admission changed the courtyard. Sariel became aware of the kitchen door, the carpenter only a few paces away, the pot between them which required her to keep sitting as though they had found ordinary things to discuss.

“Come upstairs,” she said.

Lena looked at the stair, then at Sariel’s bag.

“Bring the tea.”

Sariel showed her the room before closing the door. Two beds, a table, a window onto the court. Benjamin’s gloves lay on the nearer blanket. There was no one behind the screen.

Lena chose the chair with the wall behind it. Sariel sat on the edge of the other bed.

“How much has Rusk promised you?”

“That depends on what you want to buy.”

“I don’t want to pay twice for the same object.”

“Nor do I want to steal it twice.”

Sariel waited. Lena considered her cup, then put it down.

“Peyton introduced us. Rusk wanted someone who could get past the clerks without starting a fight. I agreed to open the way and reach the chest if I could. He was meant to take it from there.”

“And if you couldn’t reach it?”

“He still owes for the work done. I made him say it in front of Peyton.”

“Who does he intend to sell it to?”

“I didn’t agree to find that out.”

Sariel had the sudden, unwelcome memory of herself at nineteen, taking pride in knowing which questions belonged to a job. She had been wrong about several of them. She did not want to put that thought into Lena’s lap and watch her refuse it.

Instead she asked, “Would he release his claim for money?”

“Yes.”

“You sound certain.”

“He has been waiting for someone to offer.”

The answer irritated Sariel less than it should have. Rusk had always been easier to understand when he wanted something he could carry away.

“I will have to speak to Tema. And the others.”

“Then speak to them.”

Lena reached for her cup. Her sleeve slipped back, showing a thin old scar across the wrist. Sariel remembered the children who had run errands through the Coin’s kitchen, how easily she had failed to count them as people with anywhere else to be.

“You could have come to us sooner,” she said. “If you’d known where we were.”

Lena’s cup stopped short of her mouth.

“I know where you are.”

“I meant after—”

“I was a child. I went where there was food.”

Sariel felt the heat reach her face. She had meant to make a place available in retrospect, which was a generous thing to do at no expense and years too late.

“I know. I’m sorry. That came out badly.”

Lena put down the cup. She looked younger when she was angry, although Sariel knew better than to say that too.

“I don’t want you to hire me because of the Coin. I don’t want Rusk to be allowed to pay me less because Peyton knows my father’s name. If you need the way I found, make an offer. If you don’t, I can go.”

“I need it.”

The plainness of the answer let some of the strain leave Lena’s face.

“Then tell me how you’d take the chest.”

Sariel drew the room on the back of a discarded wrapper. She did not draw the lock’s wards. She indicated the service court, upper stair, scriptorium and reliquary, then the north window and the old dry cart yard below it. Lena leaned forward when she reached the alternative descent.

“You’ve measured that?”

“Thane has. A little over twelve feet. He can make a cradle for the chest.”

“The yard gate is chained at night.”

“From the street side. An ordinary lock. We can open it before we go in.”

“Who holds it?”

“Elena, if we agree to this. She can use the yard as a place to receive anyone hurt.”

Lena looked at the drawn window for a long time.

“I can make the shift change work,” she said. “I can’t promise the court will stay empty afterward.”

“I wouldn’t ask you to.”

“People do.”

Sariel nodded. She had done so herself, once or twice, using less obvious words.

They worked through the route until they had reached a point where neither could say more without consulting someone else. Lena wanted forty silver marks for the extraction, paid when her part was finished. If Tema subsequently lost the object, if Sarnai rejected it, if the five decided they had made a terrible mistake, her work would still have been done.

“No retainer,” she added before Sariel could ask. “Put the money aside if you like. I’ll collect it afterward.”

“And Rusk?”

“Separate. I won’t take your money and pretend he has released me.”

Sariel folded the wrapper along the drawn wall of the reliquary. She would keep the route in her inner coat pocket until Thane had checked it, then burn it.

“Will you meet us with him?”

“Yes. But don’t make me stand outside while you decide what I’ve agreed.”

“I won’t.”

Lena got up. At the door she looked back at Hest’s case.

“She let you keep it?”

Sariel did not ask who she meant.

“She told me to take it.”

Lena nodded once. She left the empty cup on the table and went down to the courtyard, where the carpenter had finally opened his door.

Tema listened to the report standing at his rented desk. It was too high for him. The harness seller had loaned it with the room, and Tema had solved the difficulty with a folded cloth under his elbow rather than asking for another.

Sariel gave him the useful facts first. The Seal had been removed from a burial chamber. The people who kept the place could describe how to return it. Cassia’s papers supported them, though they did not promise a cure. The Gate’s lower work had been damaged by the excavation. Taking the Seal away from the Prefect would stop him using it while it was absent; they could not promise what would happen to those already ill.

Tema listened without trying to finish her sentences. That, more than his better coat, showed how far his business had grown.

When she had finished he paid the remaining thirty marks for the investigation. He counted them onto the desk, pushed them across and waited while Kestra put them in the company purse.

“Now the other job,” he said.

Benjamin stood by the window. Thane had brought the outline of the chest cradle and was rubbing a black mark from the edge of the paper. Elena sat with her bag at her feet. Sariel could feel all four of them waiting for her to describe the arrangement with Lena exactly as it had been made.

She did.

Elena asked whether Lena understood that the alternative exit involved a lowering rope and a heavy chest. Thane answered that she had asked for the measurements. Benjamin wanted to know how many of Rusk’s men would be nearby. They did not know yet.

“Then that has to be settled before we enter,” he said.

Tema nodded. “I can pay for his withdrawal.”

“And if he won’t withdraw?”

“We do not attempt the theft tomorrow.”

Sariel glanced at him. She had expected more pressure. He looked tired enough to resent that expectation if she voiced it.

“I need the road open,” he said. “I don’t need you dead inside a gatehouse with my name in your pocket.”

Thane set down his drawing.

“I can make the cradle. I want Octan’s repair crew kept out of the route. They don’t know about this, and they shouldn’t have to choose a side because they happen to be carrying a beam.”

They adjusted the time. Lena’s changeover left a narrow interval after the heavy work ended. It would be enough if the screens were late, as the screens often were. Sariel would confirm that with her. Elena would arrange a hired room behind Dometta’s practice and pay for the space whether they used it or not. Benjamin would speak to the man at the upper stair, someone he had already met during the inspections. Kestra would look at the ward surface in daylight while Thane measured the reliquary threshold under his legitimate repair order.

No one said it would be easy. Sariel found she was less eager to say it herself with all their work laid out in front of her.

Tema wrote the retrieval terms. Three hundred marks, one hundred twenty now, the remainder when they delivered the Seal to him for Sarnai. A small share of the reopened route’s earnings followed, for six months only. Lena’s fee and the purchase of Rusk’s withdrawal were authorized expenses in addition to the five’s pay.

Kestra read it aloud. When she came to the delivery, Elena put out her hand.

“What if taking it worsens the illness?”

Tema lowered his pen.

“Then you bring me word, not a false completion. We decide what to do with what you have learned.”

“That needs to be in there.”

He wrote it. The phrase was clumsy, but nobody tried to make it more elegant after Elena nodded.

They each signed or marked the agreement. Sariel waited until the others had finished before she added her name. It was a small satisfaction to see five decisions on the paper, and she did not distrust it simply because it was small.

Rusk met them in the room above a cookshop outside the east wall. The window overlooked a lane full of steaming washing. He had chosen a table too close to the stove and was sweating through the collar of his shirt.

Two of his men waited downstairs. A third sat by the window with one boot off, scraping something from the sole. None was a man Sariel remembered from the Ford. Rusk remembered her, however. He rose halfway, recognized Benjamin behind her and sat again.

“I heard you had become respectable.”

“We’re being paid,” Sariel said.

“That will do.”

Lena arrived with Tema. She took the chair next to Sariel, leaving the merchant opposite Rusk. The arrangement put her in sight of both men when the terms were read.

Rusk had a small bronze badge pinned to the inside of his coat. Sariel saw it when he reached for his cup: an old gate clasp, worn thin at the holes, its eagle reduced to a hooked shape.

“Where did you get that?” she asked.

“Man who kept the lower stores before Marcellus. Said it belonged with the work.”

“Did he sell it to you?”

“He needed money.”

Rusk touched the clasp, then tucked the coat closed. “He told me what they had found. I paid for his telling as well.”

It explained some of the speed with which he had arrived. It did not make the room more comfortable.

Tema named the proposed withdrawal. Rusk demanded more. The argument lasted long enough for the stove to cool and the man by the window to put his boot on. Sariel let Tema do it. He knew which sums he could pay without pretending they were harmless. Rusk knew how much of his interest was time already spent and how much was the chance to keep them talking until they had no choice.

At sixty marks Tema stopped.

“Twenty now. Forty after the extraction. You withdraw your men from the service court and the approach. You release Lena from the retrieval arrangement. You make no separate attempt on the object.”

Rusk looked at Lena. “You’re content with that?”

“If you are.”

“You’ve made another price.”

“Yes. For work after yours ends.”

He rubbed the back of his neck. Sariel could not tell whether he resented losing the thief or learning she had found a use for herself without him.

“The forty is due when it’s out,” he said.

“Provided you have done what you’re paid to do,” Tema replied.

They wrote the condition plainly. Rusk read it himself, moving one thick finger beneath the line. Lena asked for the release to include any claim through Peyton. Rusk said Peyton was his affair. She waited until he added the words anyway.

Tema gave him the twenty marks. Rusk tested two between his fingers and tipped the rest into his purse.

When they went downstairs the men at the cookshop door stood aside. One had a long narrow face and a scar tucked into the left corner of his mouth. He watched Lena pass.

“Corven,” Rusk called from above. “Leave it. They’re hired elsewhere.”

Corven moved his foot from the stair. Lena did not look at him.

Outside, Tema stopped to fold the agreement into his inner pocket. Sariel waited until he had buttoned the coat over it.

“You believe he’ll keep away?” she asked.

“I believe he has accepted a price for it. So have the people who heard him.”

Benjamin looked back at the cookshop. “We should still use the window if the court changes.”

“We will,” Sariel said.

Lena walked beside her through the market’s empty frames. The light was going from the street. Somewhere a woman was calling children home, one name after another, growing less patient as she repeated them.

“Tomorrow,” Lena said. “Before the screens arrive. I’ll show you which bundle.”

Sariel nodded.

“I’ll be there early,” Sariel said.

Lena looked relieved to have the time settled. They parted at the next corner.

Chapter 11 — Before the Shift Changes

Dometta made Elena move the beds before she agreed to anything else.

The back room had held sacks of clean wool, a broken chair and a table waiting for someone to find it useful. With the sacks stacked under the window there was space for two narrow bedsteads and a passage between them. Elena tried the width with her bag on her shoulder. Dometta made her try it carrying a basin as well.

“If you have to turn sideways, you’ll drop it on someone.”

They moved the second bed again. The floorboards showed where the table had stood for years, a pale rectangle under the dust. Elena swept it while Dometta went to speak to the woman who owned the room next door.

The agreement took time. The woman wanted to know whether there would be shouting, whether soldiers would come, who would carry water if the cistern ran low and whether she could still hang washing in the little yard. Elena had answers for two of these. Dometta would not let her improve the others.

“There may be trouble,” she said. “I won’t tell you there won’t. I’ll pay for the room through the week, and your niece can sleep upstairs with me if you would rather have her away from it tonight.”

The woman looked at the prepared beds.

“Is this to do with the people who forget?”

“Yes,” Elena said.

“My brother says they’re putting it on. To get out of their work.”

“Does he know Damas?”

“Yes.”

“Ask him to visit. He doesn’t have to believe me.”

The woman stood a little longer, rubbing a rough place on the doorpost with her thumb. Then she said she would take her niece to her sister’s and that the sheets were in the top cupboard. She wanted them washed afterward, regardless of whether they looked dirty.

Elena paid her from the expense purse and wrote down what the room cost. She would put the account in Tema’s hands herself. The coin felt thin and ordinary beside the room they were asking for.

When the woman had gone, Dometta said, “I could still say no.”

“I know.”

“Don’t answer too quickly. I mean I could send you elsewhere now.”

Elena put down the broom. “Yes. You could.”

“I’ve worked here a long time. I like this house. I like being able to complain about the people in it and expect to see them again tomorrow.”

Elena looked toward the window, where the fig tree threw moving shadows over the stacked wool.

“If you help us, I can’t promise you keep all that,” she said. “I wish I could.”

Dometta nodded slowly. She took the broom and finished the corner Elena had missed.

“Then don’t. Help me with the sheets.”

The morning passed without becoming the kind of morning Elena would have chosen for a theft.

There were people to see. One of Avia’s neighbors had come to complain about the stick tapping through the floor. Dometta invited her upstairs to carry water; she declined but stopped complaining. A boy had put his thumb under a wheel to find out whether the wheel would stop. It had not. Elena examined the swelling while he explained that his brother had said it was too small to hurt. The brother waited outside the door and was made to come in for the explanation of what to watch for.

Damas woke after noon. Celia had been trying to mend one of his shirts without taking it entirely out of her lap. The stitches crowded together where her attention had gone to the bed. She put it aside when he moved.

“There’s soup,” she told him.

He watched her pour it. “Did I go to work?”

“No.”

“They’ll ask.”

“I sent word.”

He tried to sit too quickly. Elena put a hand behind his shoulders, then let him find his own position against the rolled blanket. He looked at her hand as though he knew it belonged to someone he ought to recognize.

“I’m Elena,” she said. “I’ve been here before.”

“I know that.”

“All right.”

He was angry with her, and the anger seemed to steady him for a moment. He took the cup Celia offered and drank. After two mouthfuls he lowered it.

“Too much salt.”

“You said it had none yesterday.”

“Yesterday it hadn’t.”

Celia laughed suddenly. It was not a large laugh, but Elena saw her shoulders move with it. Damas looked pleased before he remembered he had meant to complain.

Elena left them alone with the soup. She went into the back room and sat on one of the bare beds until the impulse to cry had passed. She was tired, that was part of it. She had been making lists in her head all morning, trying to make each possibility come with a basin, a bandage or a person who could help. None of those things could make Damas know the child who had stood at his bedside.

She took Tavia’s letter from her case and read the last page again. There was a sentence about a patch of wall painted yellow, because the room had been dim and no one with money had intended to live in it. Tavia had bought the paint herself. She described the color twice, unsuccessfully, then told Elena to imagine sunlight on cheap butter.

Elena smiled. She wrote about the fish clasp on the empty half-sheet she had been saving. She told Tavia she had a room with a fig tree outside and that she was working with a woman she liked. There was more she wanted to say than could safely travel in Tema’s post. She folded the letter without pretending she had said all of it.

Benjamin found her cleaning the handles of the spare litter.

He had a loaf wrapped in cloth and a packet of soft cheese. He set them on the windowsill away from the washed linen, then took the other end of the litter without asking whether it was heavy.

“How long will you need it?” he asked.

“I hope not at all.”

“I meant for cleaning.”

She looked up. He smiled gently, and she was grateful that he had given her an ordinary question to answer.

“A little longer. There’s dirt under the binding.”

He unwound it. His new gloves were tucked into his belt, already scuffed at the thumb. Elena noticed a short cut on one finger, the kind of injury he would not remember receiving unless asked.

“Don’t bleed on it.”

He wrapped the finger in a clean scrap, then continued. They worked in silence for several minutes. It was a companionable silence, with enough small noises to keep it from becoming a vigil: the scrape of the brush, Dometta moving a stool in the next room, someone walking down the lane carrying loose metal in a basket.

“I spoke to Joss,” Benjamin said at last.

“The stair attendant?”

“He’s on from the evening change until midnight. He thinks we’re finishing the inspection. I told him Thane might need access after the other workers leave.”

“Will he let you up?”

“For a while. He’ll want the order.”

“Do you have it?”

“Octan’s work permission. It gets us as far as the scriptorium. It won’t explain the chest.”

Elena stopped scrubbing.

“What will you do if he sees it?”

“I’ll get him away from the stair. Keep him downstairs until they’ve passed.”

“How?”

Benjamin looked at the loosened binding in his hands. “I don’t know exactly. I can’t say I won’t frighten him.”

The answer made her uncomfortable. She had asked because she wanted him to have a harmless way of doing something that might not permit one.

“Don’t promise him something you can’t keep,” she said.

“I won’t.” He paused, then added, “I’ll try not to. I can hear myself saying he’ll be all right before I know it.”

She set down the brush. “So can I.”

He looked at her then, properly. For a moment neither had anything practical to add.

“I’m frightened for you,” he said. “All of you. It used to feel easier when I could tell myself I would take the worst of it.”

“You couldn’t always.”

“I know.”

She touched his forearm. He let the touch stay.

“Stay with Thane until you’re clear,” she said. “If he tells you something needs holding, ask him which hand. Let that be enough until the next thing happens.”

Benjamin nodded. “And you?”

“I’ll be in the dry yard when you need me. Dometta knows what to do here. I have to believe she can do it without me.”

“She can.”

“Yes.” Elena picked up the brush again. “I would rather she proved it on a quieter evening.”

They ate the bread sitting on the ends of the two beds, with the cleaned litter between them. Benjamin had brought too much. Elena wrapped the remainder for Dometta instead of making a joke about it.

The dry yard had been used for carts before the new service court was enclosed. Its old wheel ruts remained in the hard earth, filled with finer dust. Along the north wall a shallow roof sheltered broken screens and lengths of timber. The gate into the lane was wide enough for a cart but hung unevenly; one side had to be lifted as it opened.

Elena tested it with Sariel. The ordinary street-side padlock came free under Sariel’s tools, but they left the chain in place so it looked closed. A folded wedge beneath the sagging leaf kept it from grinding when they lifted it.

“Who else uses this?” Elena asked.

“Pellia stores charcoal under the roof. She knows we’ll be moving timber this evening. She hasn’t agreed to anything else.”

“Will she be here?”

“No. Thane checked.”

Sariel led her to the spot beneath the reliquary’s north window. The wall rose sheer for a little more than twelve feet. The sill was broad enough to sit on, narrow enough that Elena disliked imagining anyone trying it while carrying a chest.

“We lower the chest by itself,” Sariel said, seeing her look. “Then people. That’s only if the court route closes.”

“And if someone is hurt?”

“Benjamin has a sling. Thane will rig two lines so one can hold while the other moves.”

Elena looked at the ground. Old stone scraps lay among the dust. She began clearing them from beneath the window. Sariel knelt and helped.

They made a level place with two folded wool pads, then set the covered lantern behind a broken screen where it could be opened without shining into the lane. Thane had borrowed a small handcart for the chest. They left it under the lean-to, its wheel blocked with a stone, close enough to bring forward without crossing the landing place. A second pad went nearer the gate, out of the fall line. Elena put her small bag beside it. The larger case remained with Dometta. She would not carry every medicine into a yard simply because she was afraid of needing one.

Sariel watched her check the dressings.

“I’m glad you’re here,” she said.

Elena kept her hands in the bag a moment longer.

“So am I. I wish that made the rest simpler.”

Sariel sat back on her heels. There was dust on the blue lining of her old coat, showing where the cuff had been turned. She brushed it away, unsuccessfully.

“I spoke badly to Lena yesterday,” she said. “As though we had a place for her before she asked for one.”

“Does she want a place?”

“I don’t think she wants me deciding that.”

Elena tied the bag shut. “Then ask about the work.”

“I did. She was very clear.”

Sariel smiled, but the smile was uneasy. Elena did not try to make her feel better. Instead she pointed to the second wool pad.

“If you come down first, stand there. Leave this side clear for whoever follows.”

Sariel moved over and looked up at the window from the new position.

“All right.”

The first screens arrived late in the afternoon, lashed flat on a low cart. Elena saw them pass the end of the lane while she was carrying a covered jug from Dometta’s house. A young woman walked beside the second horse with her hood up. Lena did not look toward the yard.

Elena set the jug beside the lantern. She had time to return to the practice once, make certain the water was warm and tell Dometta she was leaving. That was the plan. She had rehearsed it because the two turns were short enough to tempt her into believing she could be in both places at once.

At the first turn she met a man supporting another man under the arms. Blood had soaked the injured man’s sleeve from wrist to elbow. He was trying to keep it from dripping on his trousers.

“Dometta?” the helper asked.

“Yes. Come this way.”

“A screen fitting broke,” he said. “Caught Osto when it fell. They told us to finish the unloading first.”

Osto tried to speak. His face had gone the color of old candle wax.

“Don’t,” Elena said. “Walk with me. We’re almost there.”

She took his uninjured side and felt how much weight he was keeping from the other man by effort alone. The cut had to be pressed now. She used the cloth from the jug, folded thick over his wrist, and made him hold it while they moved.

At the practice Dometta saw them through the doorway and cleared the front bench with one sweep of her arm. The bowl on it shattered. She did not look down.

Elena helped lower Osto onto the bench, kept pressure while Dometta opened his sleeve and began explaining what had happened. In the pause while the other woman examined the wound, she heard the Gate’s evening bell.

Dometta looked at her.

“I have him,” she said.

Elena knew she did. She still took a breath before she let go.

“Press here. The bleeding slows when his wrist is supported. He was awake all the way from the lane.”

“I have him, Elena.”

She stepped back. Her hand left a red mark on the door as she went out. She wiped it with the clean edge of the cloth, then stopped wiping and ran for the yard.

Chapter 12 — The Chest

The screen beside Sariel’s face smelled of damp wool and old incense. She had helped carry it from the cart, which made her arrival ordinary, and now she had to keep carrying it until someone told her where it went.

The clerk in the service court wanted it against the wall. The man who managed the ceremonies wanted it under the stairs. Neither seemed willing to touch it. Sariel shifted her grip before the binding cut farther into her palm.

“Choose,” Lena said from the other end. “There’s another coming through the door.”

The clerk looked behind him. Two men had indeed begun to bring in the next screen. He stepped aside and pointed under the stairs. Sariel and Lena set down their load, wedged it upright and left by the side furthest from his table.

Benjamin stood beside Joss at the foot of the inner stair. He had Octan’s permission open between them. Joss was reading slowly, with the serious annoyance of a man asked to distinguish two kinds of authorized work at the end of a long day.

Thane waited behind him carrying the rolled cradle in a piece of sacking. Kestra had a lamp and her book. No one had attempted to make her look like a porter. She would have failed, and been offended by the attempt.

Sariel touched Benjamin’s elbow as she passed. He looked up once, then returned to the paper.

“There are two bolts we couldn’t reach while the desks were in place,” he said. “If we don’t do them now, your night men will have us in their way later.”

Joss looked at Thane. “Are you bringing the desks out?”

“Only moving them. We’ll put them back.”

“That one by the west wall sticks.”

“I saw.”

Joss gave back the permission. “Before the second bell. If you need longer, you come and tell me.”

Benjamin folded the paper into his coat. “Thank you.”

Sariel took the upper stair without hurrying. Behind her, one of the screen carriers asked where the injured man had gone. Someone answered that he had been sent to Dometta. The conversation continued beneath her feet, ordinary and angry, until the turn of the stair hid it.

The scriptorium held the day’s warmth. Ink dried in little bowls between the desks. A man had left half a pear beside a stack of folded notices, the cut surface already browning. At the eastern window, scraps of sealing wax lay in a shallow tray.

Lena went directly to the last desk and took the empty tray beneath it. She set it by the door. It was a small rearrangement, but now anyone coming up would have to move it or step over it, and the light metal feet would make a sound on the stone.

Sariel examined the reliquary door at the west end. She had seen its outer lock during the tribunal work. In daylight, with someone watching, she had been able to learn only the shape and the depth of the keyway. Now she could put her fingers against the plate and feel where it had been filed after casting.

Hest’s soft tension tool lay warm from her hand. She set it, took a narrow pick from the case and began.

The lock was well made. That was helpful until it was not. Two wards gave cleanly; the third was not where she expected it to be, and when she eased the pressure the entire mechanism settled back with a faint click.

Lena looked toward the outer door. She did not ask how long.

Sariel tried a different angle. Her palm was tender where the screen binding had bitten. The discomfort made it harder to judge how much pressure she was using, and she had to take her hand away, flex it and begin again.

Kestra put the lamp on the floor behind her shoulder. The light came across the plate rather than directly into her eyes. Sariel nodded without turning.

There. The third ward was a paired piece, one leaf sprung behind the other. She had opened a smaller version in Millhaven for a man who had lost his wife’s key and hoped to replace it before she returned. His wife had arrived while Sariel was still there and told her where the key was. He had paid anyway.

The memory steadied her. She could do this. It was a lock, with metal inside it, and someone had made it to open.

She changed picks. The first leaf lifted. She held it, found the second and felt the pressure shift under her thumb. When the bolt drew back she breathed out carefully through her nose.

“Open,” she said.

She replaced both picks, fastened Hest’s case and tucked it securely into her bag.

Thane came forward with the sacking. Lena took the door edge and eased it inward. Its lower hinge complained. She stopped at once, lifted a little with both hands and continued. The complaint faded to a rub.

No voice called from below.

The reliquary was narrow, with a high north window and an old stone shelf along the west wall. The chest stood on a low wooden plinth near the middle. It was smaller than the collection of difficulties surrounding it: dark wood, two iron bands, a hasp closed with a pin. The base had iron feet. Dust had gathered around them in neat dark crescents.

Across the floor between the plinth and the door ran a strip of brass, no wider than two fingers, set into the stone. A procession of tiny cuts covered its surface. Kestra raised the lamp. The cuts seemed to deepen as the light moved, then became ordinary scratches again.

“Here,” she said. “Don’t put the frame down across it.”

Sariel had heard the explanation that morning, after Kestra’s authorized inspection. The strip was meant to answer contact with the chest’s iron. It was an old protection, narrow in its purpose and still dangerous within it. Kestra could follow enough of the work to recognize what it had been asked to do. She could not promise to erase a sign without waking the rest.

Thane unfolded the cradle on the near side. Its wooden rails would carry the chest clear of the floor, their padded clamps closing against the wood between the iron bands. Two handles at either end gave room for hands. He fitted them together while Sariel stepped over the strip and approached the plinth.

Lena was already beside the chest. She had got there first because she had been watching the room while Sariel watched the floor. She touched the hasp with one finger, then pulled her hand away.

“Warm,” she said.

Sariel felt the same warmth through the closed lid when she steadied it. There was no fire nearby. She did not try to explain it to herself. She had work that required her hands to remain steady.

The chest was fixed to the plinth by an ordinary iron peg through the rear band. Sariel found its head under a fold of old cloth, drew it slowly and laid it where she would remember it. It came out with a dry scrape. The wood beneath had been polished by repeated lifting and replacing.

“Ready,” she said.

Thane handed the cradle over the brass strip one rail at a time. Sariel and Lena fitted it around the chest while he checked the clamps from the other side. Benjamin came into the doorway, saw that he could not add his size to the room without impeding someone and waited.

“On your count,” Lena said to Sariel.

Sariel looked at each end, made sure both of Lena’s hands were where she expected, then counted them into the lift.

The weight came up unevenly. Something inside the chest shifted toward Sariel. She tightened her grip and felt Lena adjust at once. They held still until Thane could slide a pad between the rail and the second band.

“Again,” he said. “A little higher.”

This time the chest rose level. They moved it one short step toward the door, then another. Sariel watched the nearest iron foot pass over the brass. She had been afraid of a sound, of a force she would recognize too late. Nothing happened. Lena’s breathing stayed quiet and measured.

When all four feet had cleared the strip, Thane pointed to a clean patch of stone by the north wall.

“Down there.”

They lowered it together. Sariel’s arms shook after she let go. She wiped her palms on her trousers, looked at Lena and found the girl smiling with relief.

For one moment it was a good theft. A difficult lock opened, an object moved without waking what guarded it, five people using the things they had learned. Sariel allowed herself that moment. Then a man’s voice rose from the court below.

“I’m telling you, they’ve got permission. Put the sword away.”

Benjamin turned from the doorway.

It was Joss.

Lena reached the scriptorium window first. It overlooked the service court at an angle, enough to show the cart door and the lower stair. She looked down and pulled back before Sariel joined her.

“Rusk.”

Sariel went cold with anger before she saw him.

He stood beside the clerk’s table, his coat open. Three armed men had followed him into the court. The screen carriers were trying to move their cart out of the way. One horse had been backed against a stack of empty jars. Joss was at the foot of the stairs with both hands raised, speaking to Corven, whose sword was out.

Rusk looked up. He could not see Sariel clearly, but he knew where to aim his voice.

“The price has changed.”

She wanted to throw something at him. The impulse was so immediate that her hand closed on the window latch.

“You were paid to leave.”

“And I’ll leave. Another hundred.”

“Your agreement is with Tema.”

“He’s a busy man. You have the object.”

Behind Rusk, a carrier tried to lead the horse toward the door. One of the mercenaries stopped him with an arm across his chest. The man protested. Rusk did not turn.

Sariel moved away from the window. Benjamin had already started for the stair.

“Get Joss out of there,” she said. “Don’t try to clear the whole court.”

He nodded and went.

Thane was at the north window in the reliquary, opening the shutter. Lena followed him and took the coiled rope from beneath the sacking. Her face had lost its momentary pleasure.

“You expected him to watch,” Sariel said.

“In the lane. I knew he’d want to see it come out.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“He was paid to keep away from the court. I thought he’d want the rest of the money.”

Sariel heard the younger woman’s anger trying to stay useful. She looked past her to the open window. It was possible to lower the chest, go after it and leave Lena to explain her agreement to Rusk. The alternative came to her quickly, fully shaped, with all the convenient reasons already attached.

Lena must have seen something in her face. She stopped unwinding the rope.

Sariel forced herself to speak plainly.

“We’re taking the window. I want you with us. Thane needs someone at the upper line while the chest goes down. I can go first and receive it with Elena. Can you do that part?”

Lena looked from her to the chest.

“Yes.”

“If the stair goes, you leave the rope and come. We don’t keep the chest at your expense.”

“Tell him too.”

Sariel turned to Thane. He had heard. He nodded once and kept feeding the rope through the broad sling around the window pier.

The stone was sound there; he had checked it during the measurement. He put a folded piece of sacking under the line where it would bear against the edge. The second line lay ready for the sling. Kestra moved the lamp from the floor to the west shelf, away from their feet.

Sariel leaned out. The dry yard was darker than the court, the sky above it a flat strip of evening. For a moment she saw nobody below. Then a lantern opened behind the broken screen and Elena stepped into view, lifting one hand.

Relief hurt more than Sariel expected. She held up two fingers, then pointed to the chest. Elena moved the wool pads into place.

Behind her, Benjamin shouted.

Sariel reached the outer doorway in time to see Joss come up the stairs backward.

He was holding the rail with one hand. Benjamin had the other, pulling him away from Corven, who had followed with his sword extended too far in front of him. The mercenary’s face was bright with the excitement of a man who had mistaken a retreat for an invitation.

“Stay back,” Benjamin said.

Joss stumbled over the last step. Sariel caught his shoulder and pulled him into the scriptorium. His cap fell off. He tried to go back for it.

“Leave it,” she said. “Come here.”

Corven lunged. Benjamin moved against him rather than away, striking the sword arm aside with the flat of Orren’s blade. The two men hit the railing together. Old iron tore from its staple with a sound like a hammer pulled from wet wood. Corven’s boot slipped off the step. He dropped sideways onto the broken end.

His scream filled the stairwell.

Benjamin caught his coat with his left hand before the weight dragged him farther down. He drove his own sword into its scabbard and used his freed hand to support Corven’s hip. Corven had let go of his sword. It clattered against the steps and stopped out of sight.

Sariel saw blood on the iron and understood where it had gone. The broken point had entered the outside of his left thigh. He was hanging against it, trying to straighten the leg and driving himself harder onto the metal.

“Don’t move,” Benjamin told him. “Listen to me. Don’t move.”

Rusk was coming up below. Sariel stepped to the head of the stair with her knife drawn.

“Your man is on the rail.”

“Then get him off.”

“Come another step and you’ll push him through it.”

Rusk stopped. The pause gave her enough time to see the fear under Corven’s fury. He could not get his breath between the cries. His hands kept searching for something to hold that did not hurt him more.

Sariel looked back at Joss.

“Get downstairs by the west work stair. It comes out beside the housing. You know it?”

He nodded too quickly.

“Take the people away from the foot of this one. There are men with swords, and the rail has broken. Don’t stand here with us.”

He stared at her knife, then at Benjamin holding Corven. Whatever he had thought they were doing before, he knew something else now. She let him see her face.

“Go.”

He went through the scriptorium toward the work door. She heard him fumbling with its latch, then his footsteps on the other stair.

Benjamin had his left arm under Corven’s shoulders. His right hand braced the man’s hip, keeping the weight off the rail. Sweat shone on his forehead.

“I need help lifting,” he said.

Sariel put away her knife. Rusk remained below, one hand against the wall. She could not attend to him and the wound together. She chose the wound because Benjamin could not release it.

They lifted Corven just far enough to bring his leg free. Blood ran down his boot. Sariel pressed the folded cloth from her pocket against the hole and held it while Benjamin got him over the last step. Corven struck her shoulder with his free hand, blindly, then clutched at her sleeve.

“I’m holding it,” she said. “Stop hitting me. I’m holding it.”

He stopped. His breath came in a rough whistle.

Thane appeared at the doorway with a longer pad. He slid it beneath Sariel’s hands and tied it firmly above the knee, keeping pressure on the wound rather than closing the whole leg in a tourniquet. He used his left hand to pull the knot and the heel of his right to hold the fold.

“Can he walk?” Benjamin asked.

“No,” Sariel said.

“Then move.”

He lifted Corven under the arms and hauled him into the scriptorium. Sariel supported the injured leg. They were clumsy, too large for the space between the first two desks. One desk scraped the floor. The abandoned pear rolled off its plate.

At the top of the stair Rusk called her name.

Sariel turned. He had not followed the blood past the doorway.

“Leave him. I’ll see to him.”

Below Rusk, the court was full of people trying to decide whether to run. His men still held the cart door. Sariel saw no one coming with cloth, no one clearing a way for a litter.

“Move your men out,” she said.

“The chest first.”

She shut the scriptorium door in his face. There was a bar on the inside, ordinary oak. She dropped it into place and shoved the desk against it.

The sound Rusk made when he hit the door was almost helpful. It ended the last uncertainty about which way they were going.

Benjamin brought Corven into the reliquary and lowered him beside the north wall. The mercenary had begun to shiver. He looked at the chest, then at Lena, and tried to say something. Lena knelt long enough to move his hand away from the dressing.

“Keep it there,” she told him, putting it flat on his stomach. “You’re pulling it loose.”

Thane checked the upper sling. The line ran around the sound stone pier, through the bearing loop and down toward the yard. A second rope lay beside it for people. The chest was still in its cradle, iron feet clear of the brass strip across the floor.

Kestra held the lamp while Sariel tested the first line. It took her weight without movement in the sling. She looked down at Elena, waiting beside the wool pads with her face turned upward. There was blood on the healer’s cuff already, not theirs.

“We have a man hurt,” Sariel called softly.

Elena put her bag on the nearer pad and pointed to the clear ground beside her.

“Bring him where I can reach him.”

The door behind them shook again. Benjamin went to it, listening, then returned to Corven.

“I can hold the line after I get him up,” he said. “But someone has to keep his leg still.”

“I will,” Lena answered.

Sariel looked at her. The girl had taken off all four rings and tucked them into a pocket. Her hands were bare on the rope.

“Tell me before you move the chest,” she said. “Don’t make me guess.”

“I’ll tell you.”

Sariel put one leg over the sill. The evening air reached under her coat, cool against the sweat at her back. Below, Elena moved the lantern farther from the landing place, then raised both hands to show she was ready.

Part Three A promise that holds

Chapter 13 — The Lowering Rope

The chest would fit through the window if they turned it. The man would not, unless someone moved his injured leg.

Kestra had measured the opening herself. She remembered crouching in the dry yard with the cord tight between her hands, being pleased that she could give Thane a useful answer instead of asking him for one. Twelve feet to the sill. A comfortable width for the chest in its cradle. A man could sit astride the stone and turn himself outward.

Corven could not sit. Benjamin had brought him through the scriptorium with one arm beneath his shoulders and the other under his knees; the broken iron had torn the outside of his left thigh, and blood ran off the end of his boot although there was nothing wrong with his foot. He was trying to pull himself away from Benjamin. Every effort put his leg against the floor.

“Let go of his belt,” Kestra told him.

“He’s taking my purse.”

Benjamin looked down. “I’m keeping your trousers on.”

The answer did nothing for Corven. He held on harder. His face had gone the color of the wall around the window, where years of hands had rubbed away the limewash.

From the court came Rusk’s voice, surprisingly clear. He was telling someone to put down a hook. A different man shouted that he would put it down when they got out of his way. The two voices struck the underside of the scriptorium floor. There were more people in the court than had been there when they came in.

Sariel had one foot over the sill. She looked at Kestra, then at the blood.

“Tell Elena.”

Kestra leaned out. The yard below was a rectangle of dusty earth and old stone scraps, with a darker stripe beside the wall. Elena stood in that stripe. Her head lifted at once.

“A man with us. Left thigh, bleeding. The iron came out.”

“Can he press on it?”

Kestra turned. Corven was still holding Benjamin’s belt.

“He’s frightened.”

“Then someone else. Fold cloth hard, press with a flat hand. Don’t poke anything into it. I’ll be here.”

Sariel slid down the outside of the wall on the rope, bracing her feet against the masonry. The rope moved through Thane’s left hand and around the timber they had set across the window. He controlled its speed with his hip against the wrapped end. His right hand kept the loose line clear; it did not bear her weight.

Kestra took the cloth Lena passed her. It had been folded for the chest. She folded it again, knelt at Corven’s left side and put it over the wound.

“This will hurt.”

“It hurts now.”

“I know.” She pressed.

He made a noise that brought Benjamin’s gaze down to her hand. She kept pressing. The cloth turned dark through the middle, then stopped spreading quite so fast.

Corven’s fingers left Benjamin’s belt. One caught Kestra by the sleeve.

“You can’t leave me.”

She could see the scar above his eyebrow and a broken stitch in the neck of his shirt. He was older than she had thought. She had first seen him with a weapon in his hand, and given him the age of the boys who used to wait outside the Coin looking for somebody smaller. Now his hair was thinning above both temples.

“We are working out how to take you,” she said. “You’ll have to listen.”

Lena went to the door and stood with her ear near the gap. She had shut it without shooting its bolt. The wood was old, and the bolt would have announced itself to the passage.

“They’re coming up,” she said.

Thane lifted the chest by the cradle’s side handles. Benjamin left Corven in Kestra’s care and took the other end. Together they moved it to the sill. The iron-bound box looked absurdly ordinary under the linen: an object someone might send across town with a porter and a warning about scratches.

The bronze face within it struck Kestra’s thoughts with sudden, exact clarity. She had seen it only for a moment. Eagle, broken wheel, the worn place beside the shank where a thumb would settle. For an instant she could feel that place under her own thumb.

She looked at her hand. Both hands were on the blood-soaked cloth.

“Kestra.”

Lena had said her name twice. Kestra blinked.

“Can you stop the thing on the floor if they shove the box across it?”

The alarm band ran beneath the chest’s old position, a thin inlay almost lost among the tiles. She had found where it turned toward the threshold. They had lifted clear of it, as planned. Nobody had broken it.

“No. I know where it goes. I don’t know what else it calls.”

Lena glanced at the door. “Leave it, then.”

Kestra had been preparing an explanation. It remained in her mouth, unused, while Lena went to help Thane turn the cradle.

They sent the chest out on its end. The padding caught the rough stone; Thane stopped them before the cradle twisted, put his forearm beneath it and eased it past. Down below, Sariel held the guide line away from the wall. The box descended with a faint grinding sound from the rope. Kestra watched through the opening between Benjamin’s elbows until Sariel took its weight.

The footsteps outside stopped.

“Open this.”

Lena put her finger against her lips. The man outside was breathing hard. He had run up the stair and expected the door to be open. Kestra found herself resenting him for having enough breath to waste.

“Corven?”

The injured man turned his head.

Benjamin covered his mouth. Corven jerked beneath the hand, eyes wide, and Kestra nearly lifted her pressure from his leg to pull Benjamin away.

Benjamin bent close. “You shout and he comes in with the others. We’re putting you down the rope. Nod if you understand.”

Corven shook his head, then stopped. His eyes moved to the window. He nodded once. Benjamin took his hand away.

Lena slid the bolt. The sound was very small.

The man outside struck the door with his shoulder.

“Now he knows,” Thane said.

“He was going to try the latch.” Lena dragged the little copying bench against the door, putting one end beneath the latch plate. “Tie your man.”

They had a broad sling for the chest. Thane retrieved it from the line Sariel sent back, shook out its turns and made a seat beneath Corven’s buttocks. Another strip passed around his chest. Benjamin helped lift him while Kestra kept the cloth against his thigh. It was an awkward, indecent business. Corven wept with rage at being moved, swore at Benjamin for touching him and then begged him not to let go.

Elena called up, “Send him feet first. Keep the hurt leg straight.”

“Can you hold this yourself?” Kestra asked.

Corven stared at her.

She took his right wrist and put his palm on the folded cloth. “Here. Press. That’s your work until she takes you.”

He pressed so hard his fingers trembled.

The door shook again. One foot of the copying bench hopped and settled back against the grout between the tiles. Lena stood beside it with Sariel’s spare wedge in her hand, watching the gap at the bottom instead of the man hitting it.

Benjamin lifted Corven to the sill. Thane fed the line. For a dreadful moment the injured leg bent against the stone and Corven flung both hands toward the window frame. Blood sprang out beneath the cloth.

“Hold the dressing!” Kestra shouted.

Her voice sounded like someone else’s. Corven looked up at her, startled into obedience. He put his hand back.

Then he was outside, being turned slowly in the sling while Sariel kept him clear of the wall. Elena caught his boots and guided them down. They laid him on the flat handcart they had brought for the chest. Sariel pulled off her coat and rolled it beneath his knee only when Elena told her where.

The man at the door had stopped striking it. Kestra wished he had not. There was a scrape, a murmur, then a metallic tap low in the frame.

“They have a bar,” Lena said. “Down.”

Kestra straddled the sill. She had climbed ropes before, in practice, in daylight, when no one was asking her to remember whether she had put her feet in the right place while a door broke behind her. Her skirts gathered beneath her hips. She could not see her left foot.

Thane adjusted the line beside her. “Sit back into it. You’re making the wall carry you.”

“I can’t see where my foot is.”

“Below you. Let the rope take the weight first. I’ve got it.”

She heard the effort in his voice and sat back. The line took her. Her foot found a projection in the stone. She went down in three ungraceful movements, scraped her wrist on the last one and arrived on the gravel with her mouth full of lime dust.

Sariel touched her shoulder and moved her clear.

The chest lay beside the cart. They had not put it underneath Corven. Elena was kneeling with both sleeves rolled above her elbows. The blood on the cloth looked black in the hooded lamp Sariel held. Corven kept asking who had his sword.

“We haven’t brought your sword,” Elena said. “Stay still. I need to see whether this is holding.”

Thane came down next. He used the sling beneath his thighs, left hand on the moving line and his restricted fingers clear of the stone. Kestra steadied his landing. He thanked her breathlessly and stayed against her arm until his legs steadied.

Lena followed without the sling. She came down so quickly that the rope slapped the wall. Above, the door gave with a splitting crack.

Benjamin appeared in the opening. A hand caught the back of his shirt.

He turned inside the window. Kestra saw only his shoulders, the raised elbow, a brief flash from a blade that remained sheathed at his hip. The hand disappeared. Something heavy hit the floor of the reliquary.

“Ben,” Sariel said.

He threw the free end of the rope out, swung over the sill and dropped the last distance. His knees bent under him. He stayed down a moment, one hand on the gravel.

A man leaned from the window above. Lena scooped gravel from the yard and flung it upward. It rattled against the sill. The man ducked, and by the time he looked again they were moving.

Benjamin and Thane carried the chest between them. Sariel took the cart’s shafts, Kestra its rear rail. Elena walked beside Corven with her palm still pressed over his hand. Lena went ahead to the yard gate.

The gate opened inward. Kestra remembered that useless fact very clearly. Lena had to step back to pull it, and the cart was already coming. Sariel stopped with the shafts lifted. The injured man groaned. For a moment every one of them had to wait while Lena made enough room for the door.

Then they were in the lane. The air smelled of banked cooking fires and wet hides. Someone at the far end was emptying a bucket. A woman stood in a doorway, looked at the blood on Elena’s sleeves and stepped back into her house.

“Left,” Sariel said.

The chest knocked Benjamin’s knee. He corrected his grip without stopping. Kestra could hear his breath and Thane’s, two different rhythms behind her. She pushed the cart over a rut and felt Corven’s weight come against her through the wood.

At the next turn a watch lantern moved across the street.

Lena kept walking. She crossed ahead of them, lifted her chin to the watchman and said something Kestra could not hear. He pointed toward the Gate. Lena turned her head that way as if she were trying to understand. Sariel pulled the cart through the crossing behind his back.

They were almost clear when Corven said, very loudly, “My captain will pay.”

Elena bent beside his face. “Corven, be quiet. We’re nearly there.”

The watchman began to turn. Lena stepped on his foot.

His shout followed them into the second lane. Hers was louder. She wanted to know why he was standing where he had been standing, why a person could not use a street in this city without being seized, and whether he intended to hold her there until morning over a shoe. Kestra had never heard so much indignation fitted into so little breath.

Dometta’s back door opened before they reached it. She had a lamp in one hand and a carving knife in the other.

“Which of you?” she asked.

“None,” Elena said. “Rusk’s man.”

Dometta looked at Corven, then the cloth under Elena’s hand. She put the knife down. The worker with the bandaged wrist was asleep on the nearer bed; she pulled the curtain between them before clearing the table.

The hired back room smelled of clean wool and the fennel Dometta had hung above the door. They set the chest on a folded blanket against the interior wall, away from the two prepared beds and clear of the passage to the yard. Kestra looked at the beds. They would have to find somewhere else for Corven to lie.

Kestra wanted to put something over it. She had already seen it wrapped in linen; another covering would change nothing. She fetched a second blanket anyway.

Thane watched her tuck it around the corners. He sat on the floor with his back to the opposite wall and his right hand resting on his knee. A thin cut crossed his left thumb where the rope had drawn through it.

“Let Elena see that when she can,” Kestra said.

“It will keep.”

She fetched clean water and a strip of linen herself. He let her wash the thumb, then took over the wrapping when she made the fold too thick. She sat beside him until he finished.

In the other room Corven was trying to pay Dometta to keep his name out of her book. She asked him to stop moving while she washed the wound. He began again, offering more, and this time her answer was loud enough to carry through the kitchen.

Kestra let her head touch the wall.

For a moment there was a row of men standing across the room. They were too close together. Their shoulders passed into one another. One held a wooden cup at the height of his mouth, waiting for somebody to say something before he drank.

She opened her eyes. She had not known she had shut them.

“Thane.”

He was watching the blanket over the chest.

“Yes.”

“Did you see—”

“I heard boots. More than Ben’s.”

Neither of them moved for a few breaths. Then Kestra got up, took his good arm when he offered it and pulled him to his feet. They went into the kitchen. The door remained open. Benjamin, who had agreed to take the first watch, moved his chair out of the back room and sat where he could see the chest through the doorway.

Elena listened when Kestra told her what had happened. She looked toward the two beds, then went to speak to Dometta. Corven would stay on the treatment table until they could clear a place in the front room. The wounded worker had someone coming to collect him; they would use that bed afterward. Kestra watched Elena carry the clean blankets out of the room they had prepared so carefully, keeping clear of the chest.

Lena came in by the yard a little later. The side of one shoe was black with the watchman’s polish. She accepted water, washed her hands and took a place on the bottom stair. Nobody asked her how she had got away.

Sariel set her restored coat over the banister. There was blood on the lining and a tear at the hem. She looked at Benjamin’s shirt, which had lost most of its collar, and began to laugh. He stared at her until she pointed. Then he felt the strip hanging down his back and laughed too, silently at first, with his lips pressed together.

Kestra did not find it funny. She laughed until her scraped wrist hurt.

Elena came out at last with her sleeves wet to the shoulder.

“He’ll need watching,” she said. “He’s lost enough blood to be difficult in a different way for a while. Who is awake?”

Benjamin raised a hand. Thane and Lena did too. Sariel touched Elena’s sleeve and made room on the bench.

She looked around the kitchen. “Wake me in two hours. Sooner if he gets worse.”

She took a clean cup from the shelf, filled it and drank before she sat beside Sariel.

Chapter 14 — No Improvement

Damas had made his own way to the chair by the stove. Elena noticed the chair before she noticed him, because it belonged on the other side of the room. He had carried it over with the old professional care that kept a load from striking a doorpost, and set it exactly where Dometta had asked him not to sit.

“The heat won’t help your breathing there,” Elena said.

“I was cold.”

“I can bring the blanket.”

“I can bring my own blanket.”

He looked toward the bed. Celia had folded the blanket over its foot. He knew what it was and where to find it. Elena waited while he stood, crossed the room and picked it up. He brought it back, sat down and looked at the cloth in his lap as though he had been given an errand whose purpose had escaped him.

She knelt to pull it over his feet.

“You ought to move the chair,” he said.

“Yes. I ought.”

They moved it together. He coughed afterward, bending forward, the blanket gathered in his fists. Nothing came up. Elena counted his breaths against the small pulse in his wrist and waited until he could sit back.

She had expected something better this morning. She had refused to promise Celia anything, refused to let Sariel call the removal a cure, and still she had opened Dometta’s treatment-room door with an expectation in her body. A man getting up. Cassia sending an impatient note. One of the sick children asking for food.

Damas was exactly as he had been yesterday, except that yesterday she had let him sleep.

Outside, a crier was reading the notice of the theft. He had a good voice for distances and poor breath for punctuation. He ran together the description of the missing chest and the punishment for concealing it, so that it sounded as if the box itself were to be confined pending examination.

Damas listened with his head turned.

“They’ll close the gate.”

“They’ve left a passage for ordinary traffic,” Elena said. “There are inspections.”

“Inspections.” He rubbed the blanket between his fingers. “Will she come through?”

“Celia is at the washhouse. She’ll come when the first sheets are hung.”

He nodded. After a moment he said, “That’s right. That’s her name.”

Elena kept her hand on his wrist until he no longer seemed to need someone holding it.

Corven woke angry at the pillow.

It was a rolled cloth beneath his left leg, low enough to support the weight without bending the wound open. He had pulled it away in his sleep. Dometta put it back, and he struck at her wrist.

She caught his hand before it reached her.

“Keep that hand on the blanket. I’ll tell you before I touch the wound again.”

“I want my captain.”

“You can send for him after I’ve changed the bandage. Lie back, please.”

She released him. Elena held the basin while Dometta changed the outer dressing. The pad beneath was stained but no longer wet through. He would keep the leg if fever did not take hold and he obeyed instructions for longer than the interval between hearing them and being left alone.

Benjamin sat by the door. Corven kept looking at him. Whatever else had happened in the reliquary, he had decided Benjamin was the person responsible for it.

“You broke Mell’s jaw.”

Benjamin shifted in his chair. “He had hold of me.”

“Mell had a tooth loose already.”

Benjamin looked away. “I didn’t see his face afterward.”

Corven was quiet while Dometta spread the fresh bandage. He tried to lift his head to see what she was doing and could not keep it up. Elena adjusted the second pillow, the one beneath his shoulders.

“How many men were with Rusk?” Benjamin asked.

“Ask him.”

“I will. How many?”

“You going to turn me in?”

Dometta tied off the linen and looked at Benjamin. “Let him rest now. He can talk after he’s eaten.”

Elena took her outside before the conversation resumed. In the kitchen, Thane was cutting a new pair of supports for the handcart. The old pair lay against the wall. One had split during the journey over the ruts, and Elena had not noticed.

“Did you sleep?” Dometta asked him.

“Some.”

“Did it happen while that was in your hand?”

He put the knife down.

Dometta brought Elena to the yard door. Her sleeves were already damp from washing the first set of dressings. The apple tree over the wall had put out little clenched buds. A bird kept trying the same twig and finding it too thin.

“I can keep him until he can travel,” she said. “I can’t have the captain and his friends coming in here to discuss whose wound it is. I have women bringing children. I have a woman whose husband follows her when she comes. My door has to mean something ordinary.”

“I’ll speak to Sariel.”

“Speak to your man too. He sat there all night with a sword. Damas won’t ask for the pot with him in the doorway.”

Elena felt irritation rise on Benjamin’s behalf. He had been awake because there were six of them, a stolen relic and a mercenary unable to lift his own head. It took an effort to look back through the door before answering.

From Damas’s chair the hilt of Orren’s sword would have been the first thing visible each time Benjamin leaned forward.

“I’ll move the watch to the back room threshold. The blade can stay covered.”

“Thank you.”

Dometta pressed her thumb against the place where Corven had caught her. Elena saw the beginning of a bruise.

“You’re entitled to be annoyed,” she said.

“I’m frightened someone will come for him while there are children in here.”

Elena touched her arm. “We’ll keep the back door watched. And I’ll speak to Sariel about moving him as soon as he can travel.”

Dometta nodded. She stood beside her a moment longer before going to start the oats.

They ate in turns. The grain had been cooked with a heel of hard cheese; Dometta shaved more over each bowl and then defended the cheese from Benjamin with the knife. He had not meant to take it. Elena believed him because he looked so injured by the accusation.

When she took over the watch, he put the sword on the floor beneath his chair and covered it with his coat.

“You could have told me,” he said.

“I’ve only just noticed.”

“I meant you could have told me Damas was frightened.”

She took his empty bowl. “So could he. He didn’t want to.”

Benjamin nodded. He carried his chair through the door himself, picking it up so that it did not scrape.

Nerio came near midday with a basket over one arm. The basket held two old books and a clean shirt. Anyone watching would have seen a conscientious assistant bringing things to an invalid. Elena put a hand over the books before he could begin taking them out.

“Who is the shirt for?”

“Me, if you let me wash.”

“There’s water in the kitchen.”

He looked toward the back room. The blanket-covered chest was visible through the open door. Benjamin sat at the threshold with a bowl on his knees, asleep upright. Thane had stopped cutting wood and was listening.

Nerio lowered his voice. “She asks whether there’s been any change.”

“None that I would trust. Has she improved?”

He set the basket down very carefully. “She can’t keep broth down. She called me by my predecessor’s name this morning. He died five years ago.”

Elena reached for her bag.

“She says don’t come until you’ve read this.” He took a folded paper from the inside cover of one book. “The examinations. She thought the dates might help.”

The dates were in Cassia’s firm narrow hand, each followed by a name and a brief account of an undertaking. A driver had promised to use no unlicensed unloading place. His axle broke on the approach and he shifted his cargo onto a verge. A gate porter had undertaken to perform whatever lawful duty his overseer required; he failed to appear after a night watching over a feverish child. A woman who sold candles had omitted an inherited share in her mother’s business when asked to name every interest from which she earned.

Damas’s entry was near the bottom. Elena had heard the terms from Celia. They remained harder to bear written out, with space beside them for further observations.

“Does Marcellus have this?”

“He has the examination returns. These are her comparisons.”

“And she wants me to do what with them?”

“Tell her if there’s another explanation.”

Elena thought of the patients who had never touched the Seal. One had a winter cough and improved when they moved her bed away from the smoky hearth. Another had a fever that came down, leaving him eager to leave the practice and complain about its charges. She could still distinguish them. That did not help Damas.

She folded the paper along Cassia’s creases and put it back between the book’s boards. “Leave these for Kestra. Wash while I get ready.”

Nerio looked at the basket as if surprised that it had contained what he had brought. His eyes were red. Elena sent him to eat before washing, and he accepted without the obligatory refusal that would have made her like him less.

At Cassia’s house the shutters were open. The room was too cold. Cassia had insisted on air, then forgotten why she was shivering. Elena closed the lower panels and put another cover over her knees.

“I expect you to tell me that removing an object does not annul a completed act,” Cassia said.

“I expect you to drink.”

Cassia took two mouthfuls and pushed the cup away. The skin beneath her left eye had been rubbed raw by the silver lens. Its empty setting lay on a cloth beside her bed. Without it, her face seemed younger in a way Elena did not like: less able to keep what hurt her at a useful distance.

“I was awake when they took it,” Cassia said. “I knew the hour. Nothing changed.”

“You knew the hour because we told you when they were going.”

The Censor glanced at her. “Yes. You’re right.”

Elena checked her pulse, then the small swellings beneath her jaw. Cassia submitted to it with an impatience that occasionally broke into shame. She disliked being caught wanting the impossible. Elena could do nothing useful with that except keep her hands gentle.

“Your men were near the chest afterward,” Cassia said. “Did they dream?”

“Some. Kestra saw people when she shut her eyes. Thane heard walking. Both stopped after they moved farther away.”

“No fever?”

“None.”

Cassia looked toward the open upper shutter. A strip of blue sky sat between it and the roof opposite.

“I should have separated those accounts sooner.”

Elena held the cup to her again. This time she drank more.

When Nerio returned, Cassia asked him to move the writing board. She used no name. Elena saw the assistant notice and pretend not to. He brought the board, settled it across her knees and waited while she looked at the pen.

“There were three men at my office,” he said. “They’ve taken Octan for examination. Not confinement, they said. They left two guards in his house.”

Cassia’s fingers closed around the pen.

“Who signed?”

“Flaccus.”

“Get the warrant number.”

“I have it.”

He drew a narrow slip from his sleeve and put it beside her hand. Cassia copied the number, stopped halfway through it and looked at him.

He said the remaining figures aloud. She wrote them.

Elena left them working. On the stair she had to stop and put her palm against the wall. She had not touched the Seal. She had not sworn anything. The shaking in her hands was entirely her own.

By late afternoon Dometta’s front room had filled with people who had no appointment.

Some wanted medicine. Some wanted to know whether a cough counted as the new sickness, and whether a grandmother who had forgotten a grandson’s birthday should be brought in. A carter wanted a paper saying his brother was too ill to attend the postponed ceremony. He did not bring his brother. He brought the brother’s good coat, folded over his arm as proof that he had expected to fetch him home.

Elena saw those she could help and refused to invent certainty for the others. Dometta was less patient with the coat. She sent the man to get the brother, then called him back and asked where the brother was lodged, because he might be too ill to walk.

A woman came carrying a bread pan. Her husband worked in the Gate’s maintenance shed. Soldiers had come for him while she was putting the loaves into the oven. She had taken the pan out when they began searching the house, and had brought it along without knowing she still held it.

“They say the thieves had help,” she told Elena. “They say people opened doors.”

Elena kept her hands folded in her lap. There were impressions of three fingertips in the uncooked dough.

“Which shed?”

“The north. He keeps the grease and the spare pins. He doesn’t even have a key to the upper rooms.”

Sariel appeared in the kitchen doorway. She had come back from Tema’s office with a little packet of money and a face Elena could not read. The woman turned toward her.

“Someone’s being paid for it,” she said. “There always is. They could at least say he wasn’t there.”

Sariel stood still.

Elena asked for the husband’s name and wrote it on the back of an old medicine wrapper. Sariel took the wrapper from her when the woman left. She put it in the flat pocket inside her coat, separate from Tema’s packet.

“Can you get it to Cassia?” Elena asked.

“Yes.”

“Don’t promise anything else.”

“I haven’t.”

The answer was sharp, then Sariel’s shoulders dropped. She looked through the back-room door at the chest. Benjamin had shifted his chair again, far enough away that he could see both the box and the street beyond the kitchen window.

“I thought it would give them a reason to bargain,” Sariel said.

“It has.”

Elena regretted the way it sounded. She did not regret saying it. Sariel nodded as if there were something more precise to answer than either of them had yet found.

That evening Celia came with the children. Damas watched Luci take her scarf off. He smiled before the girl reached his chair, and Elena felt a painful little lift of hope. Then he called her by Celia’s name.

Luci stopped.

Her mother took the scarf from her and hung it on the peg beside the door. Pavo crossed the room and climbed onto the arm of his father’s chair, too big for the place and determined to fit.

“That’s Luci,” he said. “I’m Pavo. You’re in my way.”

Damas moved his elbow.

The boy settled himself and began telling him about a cat that had got into the washhouse. He told it badly, starting with the end, going back to identify the cat, then insisting that the important part was the woman who had tried to catch it. Damas listened. Luci sat on the blanket over his feet. After a while she interrupted to correct her brother, and he ignored her so extravagantly that their father laughed.

Elena left the door open when she went to fetch their supper.

Chapter 15 — A Promise That Holds

The Prefect’s offer arrived in a clean sleeve.

Sariel was in Tema’s counting room, trying to get a needle through the torn lining of her coat. She had borrowed the needle from the harness seller’s wife, who had looked at the damaged silk, then at Sariel, and chosen one considerably larger than Sariel would have liked. The stitches went in straight enough. The knot kept escaping her.

Tema stood at the window counting carts. He was not writing the number down. When one turned away from the Gate he put his tongue against a tooth and made a little noise in his throat.

“You could help,” Sariel said.

“With which part?”

“The knot.”

“I would have paid someone.”

“I’d like to put the coat on before I die.”

He came over and held the lining flat. She got the thread through and pulled it tight. The mended place puckered immediately.

Tema examined it. “You’ll have a pleat.”

“I like pleats.”

He was still holding the coat when the messenger entered. The young man wore the city’s gray working livery, newly brushed, with a braid at the cuff to distinguish him from the clerks who had to sit at their desks. He looked at the needle and the merchant’s hands before presenting his packet.

Tema signed for it on the little board the messenger carried. The young man took his board back, bowed to neither of them and left. Sariel heard him hesitate on the stair, perhaps wondering whether he had forgotten something important.

Inside the sleeve was a letter addressed to Tema and, through him, to any persons able to arrange the return of the city’s ceremonial property. Marcellus offered safe passage out of Casta Ferrum for those persons and their goods, protection against prosecution for the removal itself, and two hundred marks on delivery. He would receive a representative at the west end of the public passage at noon.

“Protection against whose prosecution?” Sariel asked.

“His.” Tema read it again. “And those acting under his present authority.”

“Present.”

“You would want him to specify it if he couldn’t bind anyone else.”

She disliked that Tema was right. She took the letter, read the rest and found no undertaking about the sick, the detained workers or the ceremony. The paper smelled faintly of the oil used on its sleeve. Someone had been careful with it.

“Does he know where it is?”

Tema looked at her. “If he did, would he send two hundred marks?”

“He hasn’t sent them.”

“Then we should find out how much of this he means.”

She made him let go of her coat before standing. The last stitch pulled loose. She swore, and Tema, with an unexpectedly tired smile, tucked the loose thread through the lining where it would not show.

Flaccus waited in the public passage beside a cartload of glazed bowls. A soldier was tapping their rims with the handle of his knife, checking the straw beneath them. Each tap made the potter flinch.

Sariel stopped beyond reach of the knife and held up the letter.

“The Prefect’s occupied,” Flaccus said.

“So am I.”

He looked at the sleeves of her coat. She had washed the blood out of the cuffs. The damp had left dark patches.

“Then I’ll be brief. The safe conduct is ready. The money can be brought. He’ll receive the chest here, in front of witnesses, and sign your departure before any search of your baggage.”

“Who chooses the witnesses?”

“Choose one yourself.”

She had expected a refusal. She let it pass without showing surprise.

Beyond Flaccus the Gate’s big leaves stood against the inner walls. One had a dull bronze strip along its leading edge. The other strip had been polished recently. Carts passed between them in fits, each halt setting off a new wave of complaints from the eastern queue. Up on the walkway a man with a spanner knelt beside the control housing. He had Octan’s bent-backed apprentice with him, but Octan himself was absent.

“The men you took yesterday,” Sariel said. “Does the offer include them?”

“Which men?”

“The ones whose names you have.”

Flaccus’s jaw moved. “They haven’t been charged.”

“Then let them go.”

“I don’t know whether you’ve noticed, but a guarded building was entered. A man was hurt. Civic property was carried through an open window. Some of us have to ask how.”

He turned to stop the soldier tapping the bowls. The potter let out a breath so deep Sariel heard it over the queue.

“Ask me,” she said.

Flaccus looked back. “I am.”

They stood close enough to the moving cart that she could see her reflection in a blue glaze: eyes stretched wide, face curved toward the rim. She had made herself easy to approach. She had not thought how much it would resemble standing still to be counted.

“I can carry an answer to people who might help,” she said. “I won’t carry this one unless the workers are included.”

“Bring the object back and the Prefect will consider their position.”

“Their position is in a locked room.”

Flaccus lowered his voice. “Three carriers were killed on this road before winter. One had his daughter riding with him. The cart arrived here without either of them. Since the new arrangements, no convoy under the Gate’s protection has been attacked between here and the east marker. You may find the arrangements unpleasant. I find the cart unpleasant.”

Sariel looked past him. A little girl sat on a bundle in the next wagon, eating an apple with both hands. Her mother tucked a blanket behind her back as the cart jolted forward.

“Was the daughter found?”

“No.”

Flaccus did not add anything. She could not tell whether he had planned the silence. It worked on her anyway.

She put the letter back in its sleeve. “If he puts the workers into the safe conduct, send it to Tema. If he promises to suspend the ceremony, put that in too.”

“He won’t.”

“Then we’ll both have saved a walk.”

She went out through the north wicket, past the woman who had brought bread dough to Dometta. The woman was waiting with a clean shirt for her husband. She recognized Sariel and raised the shirt a little, asking without words whether it was worth staying.

Sariel stopped.

“Cassia has his name. I’ll go back to her office.”

“They said he didn’t need a shirt because he wasn’t being held.”

Sariel took the shirt. The woman had folded it around a piece of bread. Its hard edge pressed her fingers through the cloth.

When she returned to Flaccus he looked irritated before she spoke. She held the shirt out.

“His wife has been waiting out there since morning. Please get this to him.”

The captain took it. Sariel waited while he gave it to a soldier and named the man. Only then did she leave.

Peyton was waiting on the counting-room stair when she got back. He had brought fried onions in a paper cone and was eating them with a wooden splinter. The smell filled the stairwell. Tema’s harness seller, below, kept opening his shop door and looking up in a way that suggested a complaint he could not decide how to phrase.

“I thought you were staying out of this,” Sariel said.

“I wanted to speak to you before I go. I’ve been waiting half the morning.”

He offered her the cone. She took one onion, too hot, and burned the front of her tongue. Peyton continued eating with evident satisfaction.

“Rusk has a proposition.”

“Rusk had one.”

“He says his men were forced to enter because someone changed the hour.”

Sariel looked at him.

Peyton picked out a particularly dark piece. “I didn’t say it was a good account.”

“I suppose we’d pay him again, and this time he would discover a new obligation to keep the bargain.”

“No. He has a buyer. A man who wants the Seal gone from this region and has the money to put it on a westbound cart. It would be sealed in a stone-lined case. No road ceremony. No Prefect looking for someone to embarrass.”

“No sick people?”

“I didn’t say that either.”

She sat two steps above him, leaving room for people to pass. A workman came up carrying a saddle between his arms. They both moved their knees, and he went through Tema’s door without thanking either of them.

“Why are you delivering it?” Sariel asked.

“Because Rusk owes me money. If he sells something, he may pay.”

“He doesn’t have it to sell.”

“He has a buyer. Sometimes that is enough.”

Peyton tipped the last onions into his mouth. Sariel watched his rings move. None of them matched Lena’s. She had caught herself looking for a resemblance and felt foolish.

“Lena is owed forty for the work,” she said. “That won’t pass through Rusk.”

“It won’t pass through me.”

“And you aren’t collecting anything from her?”

Peyton looked up sharply. “Ask her. She’ll explain it slowly.”

Sariel smiled despite herself. He folded the empty paper into a little square and tucked it into his coat. Before he stood he named a price large enough to pay for the journey, the stolen chest and most of the separate lives the five had been trying to maintain.

“He wants an answer tonight,” Peyton said.

“He won’t have it tonight.”

Peyton nodded. “I’ll tell him.”

He went down past the harness seller, who at last asked whether the food stall was still open. Peyton gave him directions with surprising care.

Sarnai was watching a horse being shod when Sariel reached the camp. The animal had a pale mane and the aggrieved expression of a person receiving inferior service. Yelü held its head while the smith checked the fit of the shoe. Each time he stepped away, the horse tried to put its foot down on the place where the hot iron had been.

“He remembers the wrong part,” Yelü said.

“He remembers being allowed to stand,” Sarnai answered.

She looked older in the late light than she had at their first meeting. Her hair had gone white along one temple; she had bound the other side so neatly that the difference looked deliberate. She greeted Sariel without leaving the horse, then asked whether the object was intact.

“The chest hasn’t been opened again.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No. It’s the answer I can give.”

Sarnai accepted it with a small movement of her chin. She waited while the smith drove the first nails. Sariel found that she did not mind. The horse’s breath warmed the cold air. Somewhere behind the tents a child was practicing a whistle and failing to get the second note.

When the foot was down, Sarnai walked with her beyond the picket line. Yelü stayed to speak to the smith. The ground sloped toward the city, giving them a view of the Gate’s upper works above its near roofs.

“He has offered you money,” Sarnai said.

“Safe conduct.”

“That too.”

Sariel told her the terms. Sarnai listened without interrupting, then held out her hand for the letter. Sariel took it from her inner pocket and passed it over. The khatun read slowly. Her eyes were good; the formal turns of Ecktorian troubled her more than the light.

“He can let you out,” she said. “He could also let my keepers in.”

“Yes.”

“He could stop taking our people aside from the market road. He could tell his collectors not to move the eastern marker whenever a tenant fences a field. He could sign those things with the same hand.”

She returned the paper. Sariel put it away.

“A signature wouldn’t stop him changing his mind.”

Sarnai looked directly at her then. Sariel had known what the answer would be before she spoke, and had asked for it anyway.

“You have something that might.”

The whistle behind them found its second note. It held, thin and triumphant, until someone shouted for the child to stop frightening the animals.

Sariel put her hands in her coat pockets. The mended lining caught a fingernail.

“You want him to swear.”

“I want him to do what he asks others to do.”

“He hasn’t agreed.”

“Neither had the men he prevented from carrying their winter feed through his gate.”

There was no clever answer to that. Sariel looked at the road below. Two carts stood close together where the watch had stopped them; from this distance they resembled a single vehicle too long to turn. A man walked around them with a lamp, although the sky was still light.

She imagined Marcellus at the table. His hand on the worn bronze, the terms named in front of people who had heard his earlier refusals. She imagined the relief of leaving a city with a promise that could not quietly become something else after her back was turned.

She had spent years asking for conditions small enough to be believed. A bed paid for until a specified morning. Work that ended at the receiving yard. A meeting where everyone could leave. The picture of his hand gave her a pleasure that did not depend on any of those careful limits.

“Would you return it afterward?” she asked.

“Altan and Sare would return it.”

“They want the oath first?”

Sarnai’s mouth tightened. “They want the grave restored. I want my people able to reach it next winter.”

Yelü came down the slope with the horse’s old shoe in her hand. She had heard the last sentence. She looked from her mother to Sariel and did not pretend otherwise.

“If he swears, who decides when he’s failed?” she asked.

“The terms decide,” Sarnai said.

“He wrote Damas’s terms too.”

Sarnai turned. For a moment the two women stood like people ready to quarrel over something older than the conversation. Then the khatun took the old shoe from her daughter’s hand and examined the split at its heel.

“You will hear the terms before anything is done,” she said.

Sariel should have said that she had agreed to nothing. She knew how to say it, pleasantly, with enough room left for both women to preserve their dignity. Instead she asked how much passage Sarnai intended to demand.

They talked until the lamps came out along the picket line.

On the way back Sariel bought a baked pear from a woman closing her stall. It had been cooked in its skin with a little sharp wine. The juice ran over her wrist when she bit it. She ate standing beside the woman’s empty trays, watching the Gate until the woman asked her to move so she could fold the stall.

She brought the offers to the others that night. She put Marcellus’s letter on the table, named Rusk’s buyer and price, and told them what Sarnai wanted before anyone asked what she thought of it.

Benjamin read the letter twice. Thane went on shaping the replacement handcart support. Elena had Damas’s little girl asleep against her side, and kept her voice low when she asked where the keepers stood.

Kestra watched Sariel rather than the paper.

“Would you do it?” she asked.

Sariel looked at the ring left by her cup. She could still taste the pear.

“I might,” she said. “That’s why I’m telling you now.”

Chapter 16 — The Archive’s Custody

Cassia had corrected Kestra’s copy of the excavation plan in red.

The corrections were small. One stair had acquired a landing; the line Kestra had taken for the edge of a foundation was the earlier excavator’s attempt to show an obstruction he had not removed. Beside it Cassia had written, Observed from above. Depth conjectural. She had left the original line visible.

Kestra stood at the table reading the note twice. It was the first piece of her own work she had found there, among the Archive’s stitched books and flat packets. Nerio had made room for it beneath a polished stone so it would not curl. She tried not to be pleased by the stone.

“She wants the dimensions checked,” he said. “Only those you actually saw.”

“I can do that.”

“She also wants you to eat. There is bread in the cupboard. I can’t bring another tray up those stairs today.”

He went into the passage with an armful of papers. Kestra watched him stop at the foot of the stair and move the top sheet so it would not fall. Then he climbed, one slow step at a time.

The house had not been designed as an Archive. Its front room faced a narrow street where two neighbors could argue without raising their voices. A window at the back looked into a well court. Cassia had fitted shelves wherever the walls would take them, leaving an alcove for a chair, a lamp and the bronze stand that supported a book too large to hold.

The chair had a cushion worn flat in the middle. A strip of silk had been sewn over a tear at the front edge, neatly on one side and badly on the other. Kestra wondered which side Cassia had done first.

She ate a heel of bread over a plate, away from the drawings. There was a little pot of plum preserve beside it. The preserve had been bought from a stall she passed every morning, a stall she had assumed sold only pickled fruit because she had never stopped to ask. She took another piece of bread and put more preserve on it.

Cassia came down before she finished. Nerio was a step below her, with one hand ready beside her elbow and the papers tucked under his other arm. She did not take the offered hand. At the last step her knee failed, and she caught the rail so hard that the wood cracked against its fastening.

Kestra stood. Nerio caught her elbow after all. Between them they brought her to the chair.

“I could have come upstairs,” Kestra said.

“I wanted to see my room.”

There was nothing to say to that. Kestra moved the large book stand so Cassia could see the window, then wished she had asked first.

“Leave it there,” Cassia said. “Thank you.”

Nerio set the papers on the table and went to fetch the cup Elena had left with measured scratches on its side. Cassia watched him go. Her lips moved once. No sound came out.

Kestra sat on the plain chair beside the drawings.

“I looked at the returns,” she said. “The people who were ill near the Seal before it was moved aren’t all among the people who accepted an oath.”

“No.”

“I thought the copying error was in your list.”

“So did I. I kept sending for the clerks.”

They worked through the names, comparing Cassia’s examinations with the porters’ roster. It was slow work. Some people used household names in one place and their own names in another. Two had the same first name and fathers whose names had been rendered differently by the clerk. Kestra would have resolved them into one person if Cassia had not known that one worked nights and the other had a son who delivered her firewood.

Outside, a woman shook a rug. Dust moved through the sunlit slit between houses. Cassia waited for a cart to pass before saying a name she did not want heard in the street.

Kestra found six people whose brief sickness had ceased before the first ceremony. She marked them in her working book, then held the page toward Cassia.

“Those are only the ones we can place.”

“Write that beneath it.”

She wrote it. Cassia watched her, and for a while Kestra felt the easy concentration she had sometimes reached alone at home, except that now another person could follow where she was going. She did not have to prepare a little speech to justify caring about a difference between two accounts. Cassia already cared.

“Would the removal have made the short effects worse?” Kestra asked.

“Possibly. Or you had no one leaving the room before they began. I haven’t enough accounts of a chest in motion.”

“You don’t think what we saw was an order?”

“Did you receive one?”

Kestra thought of the standing men and the cup. “I felt I had missed one.”

Cassia set her hand flat on the papers. The nails had been cut very short.

“Then record that. Don’t improve it into something more definite because we could use an answer.”

Kestra nodded. She wanted to tell her how difficult that was, how much of her working life had been spent making uncertain things clear enough for someone else to act. But Cassia looked exhausted, and the words would have turned into a request for praise.

She wrote the account while the memory remained exact.

The proposed transfer occupied four pages.

Cassia had drafted it before the theft. The pages were clean except for two amendments in the margin. A formal surrender would place the Iron Seal in the Archive’s custody as an antiquity recovered from an improperly opened burial ground. The Prefect would lose access. Two officers with separate keys would supervise any examination. The object would not be used in a public ceremony without authorization from outside Casta Ferrum.

Kestra read the last provision again.

“Authorization would still be possible.”

“Anything we forbid here can be reversed by someone with greater authority.”

“You’ve written a way to permit it.”

“I’ve written a way to prevent Marcellus permitting it by himself.”

Cassia was leaning back in the chair with her eyes shut. She opened them when Kestra said nothing.

“I know what you think of that. I’ve been thinking it for a long time. If you require me to promise that no future officer will be cruel, I cannot do it. If you ask whether I can keep this object out of his hand next week, I can give you an arrangement with people attached to it.”

“Who would have the second key?”

“For the present, the Tribunal’s senior recorder. After the provincial examiner comes, one of their appointees. I would ask you to stay through the transfer. You know the route by which it was moved. You understand what you have and have not seen.”

Kestra looked at the four pages. There was a place for her name among the witnesses. It had been left blank.

“I have a home in Millhaven.”

“I haven’t asked you to leave it.”

“A practice. Work.”

“This would be paid work. A month, perhaps less. I would secure the payment before you undertook it.”

Cassia spoke without flattery, which made it harder to resist. Kestra could imagine the month. The room, the stand, the books brought out without somebody asking why she needed them. She could imagine coming home with a question resolved well enough to teach it. There had been years in which all she could offer the others was a warning that she could not make more precise.

“What happens to the people already bound?” she asked.

“We try restitution under controlled conditions.”

“You take it down and bring it back afterward.”

“If we can. If it has to remain, we establish a guarded chamber and preserve access for the keepers.”

Kestra laid the pages on the table. “Their access would depend on your officers.”

“Their access depends on soldiers now. I won’t pretend I can give you an unoccupied city.”

Nerio came in with the cup. Cassia reached for it, missed the handle and watched him reposition it without comment. He stayed until she had drunk to the first scratch, then took the cup away and looked at Kestra over her head.

Kestra could see what he wanted her to do. Agree, or refuse, but stop making this take so long.

She asked him to stay.

He put the cup on the side table.

“If the Seal came here,” she said, “would you be among the people required to account for it?”

“I account for everything that comes here.”

“Would that mean touching it?”

He looked at Cassia. Cassia’s face changed, the slightest tightening beside her mouth.

“Not under my direction,” she said.

“The province might insist?”

“It might. It might also insist that an object brought here in defiance of a military prefect be properly secured. I am trying to leave us enough standing to refuse the first without losing the second.”

Nerio drew a chair from beneath the side table and sat. It made Cassia look at him as if he had entered the argument without being invited.

“I won’t touch it,” he said.

“No one is asking you to.”

“I thought it best to say it before there were four copies.”

Kestra felt an impulse to smile and did not. Nerio was frightened. He had folded his hands between his knees, each gripping the other so hard the knuckles shone.

Cassia looked at him for a long time.

“Very well,” she said. “That will be written.”

He relaxed a little. Kestra read the transfer again, searching for the place where that refusal could be made to stand up against the rest of it. Each provision had a purpose she understood. Each depended on someone using their discretion as Cassia meant it to be used.

She remembered Cassia’s account of approving the first ceremonies. Not a fool’s account. A careful woman’s, with actual fraud before her and a device that appeared capable of making a witness reliable. The Censor had not begun by wanting a man unable to name his child.

Kestra moved the four pages back across the table.

“I can’t recommend this.”

Cassia closed her eyes again. “You prefer a burial the next Prefect can open.”

“I prefer not to give the next Prefect an object already admitted as a usable civic instrument. The keepers can ask us to record what was taken. Their right to enter. What you found when you investigated. They can have witnesses from outside your office.”

“And when he sends soldiers?”

Kestra had no answer that would make Cassia stop being right about soldiers. She looked at Nerio, still sitting with his hands between his knees.

“Then people will have to oppose him. I don’t think we can spare them that by keeping the Seal.”

Cassia turned her face toward the window. The rug was hanging over the opposite sill now, motionless except where its fringe stirred.

“I wanted it properly studied,” she said. “I still want that. You needn’t look at me as if it was only an excuse.”

“I don’t.”

Kestra waited until Cassia looked back.

“I wanted to stay,” she said. “When you asked. I was already deciding which books I’d read first.”

Cassia’s mouth softened. She glanced toward the bronze stand, then back at the younger woman.

“The large one is badly indexed. You would have hated it.”

“I’d have complained.”

“Yes.” Cassia’s eyes filled unexpectedly. She pressed the heel of her hand beneath the good one. “I would have enjoyed that.”

Kestra sat with her. Nerio got up to close the lower shutter. The light became gentler. They did not discuss the transfer again.

Before leaving, Kestra asked about Octan.

Cassia took the copied warrant from beneath a wooden weight. She had started a reply. Her writing was smaller toward the bottom, as though the available strength had had to be fitted into the remaining lines.

“They’ve no declaration against him,” she said. “Only an assertion that he possessed the means of access. By that reasoning they could confine every porter.”

“They’ve taken the man who keeps the spare pins too.”

“I know. Sariel brought his name.”

The two entries stood together in the reply. Kestra touched the edge of the paper rather than either name.

“Will this free them?”

“It should obtain an answer. Flaccus dislikes writing down something he can’t defend. If I insist that they be charged or released to their work, he may choose the work.”

“May I take it?”

Cassia nodded. Nerio sanded the last lines, shook the excess into its little box and folded the sheet. Cassia signed the outer address before he put it into a plain wrapper. Kestra watched where the signature sat. She would not have to open the paper to show whose it was.

At the door Cassia called her back.

“A stay of the ceremony is possible.”

Kestra waited.

“I can’t write it as though I had only just discovered what was being done. He has my earlier approvals. He would lay them beside it and I would look like an old woman who had forgotten her own hand.”

Nerio said, “You haven’t forgotten.”

“No.” She looked at him with sudden anger, then let it go. “I would have to describe what I did. All of it that concerns the Seal.”

“Not the witness,” Kestra said.

“No. There are ways to reserve identifying evidence for an examiner. They are imperfect. I will need help keeping the distinctions clear.”

Kestra held up the wrapped reply. “I’ll deliver this first.”

“Come back when you can.”

She went out with the order inside her coat. In the street she stopped at the preserve stall and bought a small jar of plum. The woman asked whether she wanted the sweet batch or the sharp, and let her taste both from different ends of a clean stick.

Kestra chose the sharp one. She had eaten the sweet one in Cassia’s room, but she thought Elena would like this better. The woman tied cloth over the jar and put it into her hands warm from a place in the sun.

At the Gate, the clerk who received Cassia’s reply asked her to wait while he checked the seal. She stood beside the public bench holding the preserve, with traffic edging past and a child staring at the chalk dust on her cuffs. When the clerk returned he gave her an acknowledgment folded in half. She put it in her book, on the page beside the six names.

Octan was brought out before she left. He had no belt and carried his boots, their laces removed. He sat on the bench to put them on. Kestra waited until he had got his feet into both, then offered him the string from around the jar.

He looked at it, then at her.

“One would be something,” he said.

She cut the string in half with her little knife. It was not enough for either boot, but he made it serve.

Chapter 17 — What We Agreed to Do

Elena had told Corven he could sit up for the first time that morning. He took it as permission to demand his boots.

“You can sit,” she said. “You can put your feet on that stool. You can’t stand yet.”

“I have to go where I can be paid.”

She adjusted the stool so his left heel would not hang over its edge. “Then send for whoever pays you.”

He looked toward the kitchen door. Benjamin was beyond it, slicing yesterday’s loaf. Each cut made the table rock. Dometta had put a folded leather scrap beneath one foot, but someone had moved it while sweeping and nobody could remember where.

“Won’t come here,” Corven muttered.

Elena left the matter there. She put his boots beneath the stool, where he could see them, and wrote the time of the dressing on the slate by his bed. If she hid the boots he would spend the whole morning trying to get them back. If he could see them, he might save enough strength to be angry about something else.

In the kitchen, Sariel had found the missing leather scrap. She crouched to slide it beneath the table while Benjamin held the bread knife clear. Elena watched the ease with which they made room for one another. There had been a time when Sariel would have made him put the knife down, and he would have apologized for holding it. Now he stood still and asked whether the piece was thick enough.

“Press your end.”

He did. The table held.

Thane came in carrying the last support for the handcart. He stopped when he saw their repair, tested the table with his left palm and left it alone. Elena moved his bowl closer.

Kestra put Cassia’s draft provisions on the clear end of the table. Beside them she set a jar of plum preserve.

“I brought that yesterday. I forgot to tell anyone.”

Elena opened it and tasted the spoon. The sharpness made the sides of her mouth ache. She passed it to Benjamin, who spread far too much on a piece of bread and then pretended he had meant to do so.

They ate before they began. Sariel fetched Dometta’s bowl so the practitioner could have something when she came back from a house call. Thane cut his bread into pieces he could hold without tearing. Kestra kept turning one folded corner of the paper flat, then catching herself and taking her hand away.

The chest stood in the locked back room. Benjamin had brought his chair to the kitchen threshold. Through the little inner window they could see the blanket over it. The distance was enough to keep them from hearing the boots, most of the time.

“I want to say something before we discuss what Cassia can write,” Benjamin said.

Sariel looked up at once.

“I’d like him made to swear. Marcellus. I know it isn’t what we hired ourselves to do. I’m saying I want it.”

Thane set his bread down.

“To what?”

“Letting them reach the grave. Leaving the market road alone. Not sending men to take someone from his house because his key happened to fit a door.”

“For how long?”

“I don’t know.” Benjamin looked at Sariel. “Long enough that we can go.”

Elena had been ready to answer the first part. The last stopped her. He watched people leave all the time now. Thane to Dorra’s works. Kestra to a shrine or a room where she could read. Sariel on jobs that did not need a second pair of feet at every door. He no longer tried to fill the road behind them with provisions. Elena had mistaken that for having ceased to worry.

“We could require him to keep the current terms,” Sariel said. “Not invent a whole settlement. The burial access he already recognizes when it suits him.”

Kestra turned the paper so the blank side faced upward. “I can’t tell you whether the Seal would recognize his loss of authority. If he swears to let people through and the province removes him, he may no longer be able to.”

“Then say while he’s Prefect.”

“A deputy could refuse his instruction.”

“Then he has to give the instruction. Properly, before witnesses.”

Elena listened to Sariel finding the next version, her voice becoming calmer as the problem became more workable. She did that with locks. One failed approach led to a better question, and the better question usually got them through.

“Damas promised to do what his overseer required,” she said. “He thought he had permission to go home. The man who dismissed him wasn’t authorized to do it. Afterward he couldn’t carry the loads anyway.”

“Marcellus isn’t Damas.” Sariel’s answer came quickly. “We wouldn’t be putting it on a porter. We would be putting it on the person who is making these decisions.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t speak as though we don’t see the difference.”

Elena looked down at her bowl. There was a clot of preserve on the rim. She had wanted to make the argument easier by giving it a face everyone already cared about. Sariel had heard her doing it.

“You’re right,” she said. “I don’t know what it would do to him. I know I couldn’t fix it if we got it wrong. I want that left in the room while you decide.”

Sariel rubbed both hands over her face. When she lowered them, she looked tired rather than angry.

“I’m not trying to get you to treat him afterward.”

“I would, if he needed it. That’s what makes it difficult.”

Thane leaned back. “There’s a man in Dorra’s yard who can’t use the west stair. He lost his balance once, fell half the height and came back to work before his leg was ready. They changed his place. Put him where he could sit. If we did this and Marcellus couldn’t do the job anymore, what could we change?”

“Remove the oath,” Benjamin said, then stopped.

Kestra shook her head.

Thane waited. He did not point at her or explain the correction. He looked at the handcart support lying beside his chair, where he had cut away too much wood and fitted another piece to make the join sound.

Sariel rose and went to the kitchen window. Out in the yard, a washerwoman was pulling linen off a line. She had two pegs in her mouth. She dropped one, swore around the other and went on folding.

“He’ll sign something,” Sariel said. “With no Seal. Because he’s embarrassed and Cassia has made it expensive to refuse. Then we leave. We know what a signature is worth when nobody he fears is still looking.”

Benjamin stood beside her, leaving enough space that she could move away. She did not.

Elena wanted to promise that she would return to Casta Ferrum, that she would keep writing to Dometta, that she would come every winter if she had to. The promises gathered easily. Her practice in Millhaven could be handed to other people again. Someone would cover the patients. The rooms would still be there when she came back.

She looked at the unused bowl she had put out for Dometta. She could not buy the decision by arranging another woman’s work in her head.

“We need people here who can keep asking,” she said. “Dometta already does. Octan. The keepers. I can write to Dometta and keep the reply with our papers. I can tell her what help I can actually give. I can’t promise to become this city’s healer.”

“No one’s asking you to,” Benjamin said gently.

“I was about to offer.”

He looked at her, then smiled with no amusement in it. He knew that temptation too.

Kestra drew the four pages back toward herself. “Cassia offered the Archive. It would have guards and two keys and people trained to examine the thing. I wanted to help her. There was a place for me.”

Thane turned to her. “For how long?”

“A month. More if I asked, probably.”

“You could do the month.”

“I could. But the object would still be available, and the people guarding it would have to make arrangements like the ones that brought us here. I’ve told her I won’t recommend custody in the Archive. That doesn’t decide it for the rest of you.”

Sariel returned to the table. She picked up the bread knife and wiped a smear of preserve from its flat with a piece of bread. Her hands had grown steadier while she stood at the window.

“Sarnai hasn’t offered us the Seal to use,” she said. “She asked us to make a bargain with it before it’s put back. Altan and Sare haven’t agreed. I kept talking as though I could bring them around.”

“Could you?” Thane asked.

“Perhaps. If I put it in the right words. They want to get into the chamber badly enough.”

She ate the piece of bread. No one answered her for a while. Elena heard Corven call from the other room. She went to him, helped him move his leg off the stool and brought him the pot. He was ashamed of needing it and tried to give the instructions himself. She let him until he told her to leave him standing.

“You aren’t ready. Sit back. I’ll turn away.”

When she returned to the kitchen, the others had moved the papers to the center of the table. Benjamin had put his hand over Marcellus’s letter, obscuring the offered payment.

“We take it down,” he said. “Let the keepers finish what was interrupted. Without another oath.”

Sariel nodded. She did not look relieved. Elena was glad of that, then ashamed of being glad.

“Tema will need to authorize the change,” Kestra said.

“I’ll bring him,” Sariel answered. “And then we ask Sarnai.”

“Ask,” Thane repeated, quietly enough that it was almost for himself.

Sariel met his eyes. “Yes.”

Tema came after noon with his copy of the contract in a stiff leather folder. He had eaten elsewhere. Elena was grateful; she did not want to make another meal in order to ask someone for something.

He read their proposed change while she took Dometta’s place at the front door. Two patients came, then a girl collecting a jar for her grandmother. When Elena returned, Tema had taken off his coat. Sariel sat opposite him. The other three had drawn up chairs close enough to hear without leaning.

“The fee was for retrieval and delivery to me for Sarnai,” he said. “I don’t want to quarrel about what delivery means after you’ve decided it means something else.”

“Neither do we,” Sariel said. “That’s why this is here.”

She touched the blank space below their proposed amendment.

Tema looked at the chest through the inner window. “You retrieved it. I have not paid the balance because it hasn’t been delivered. I don’t intend to pretend you did no work.”

“Then don’t,” Thane said.

The merchant turned toward him. Thane met his gaze and waited.

“The three hundred remains,” Tema said. “For the removal, the attempt at restitution and transfer into the keepers’ custody under an agreement we can all accept. Your investigation is already paid. I won’t have that folded back into this as if we had never commissioned it separately.”

Kestra wrote the distinction into the amendment. Elena watched Tema’s mouth as he read along. He was less irritated by the amount than by having to admit that his expected useful object would go into the ground.

“The route share doesn’t remain,” he said.

Sariel leaned forward. “The route may reopen because of this.”

“It may. But I offered the share when I expected to negotiate its conditions with something in my hands. If we’re surrendering that advantage before the passage terms are settled, I have a different risk. I can pay the fee. I won’t pledge six months’ earnings as if the rest were unchanged.”

Elena saw Benjamin glance at Sariel. They had talked about that share on the road. It would not have made anyone wealthy, but it might have paid for winter work they could choose rather than take.

Sariel sat back. “The expenses you’ve already authorized stay separate.”

“Rusk’s remainder isn’t an expense now.”

“No. His advance is spent. Lena is owed forty for getting the chest out, even if we bury it badly. If she takes another job, we’ll price it separately.”

“I’ll cover reasonable additional work up to thirty.”

“She’ll hear that as the amount available,” Sariel said. “Not as something she owes us because you made it available.”

Tema nodded. “Write it.”

They went through the amendment without discussing all the reasons again. The custody would pass to Altan and Sare, with Sarnai’s acknowledgment, after the return to the burial chamber. The five were hired to undertake the attempt, not guarantee that a working no living person fully understood would succeed. No restored charter was promised. No future work was included.

Tema signed first. He passed the pen to Kestra, who signed beneath him, then to the others. Thane made his practiced left-hand mark with the paper turned at an angle. Sariel sanded the page and folded it into the same leather folder as the original.

“I need a copy,” she said.

“You’ll have one before evening.”

He put the folder inside his coat. On his way out he stopped beside Elena.

“I do want the road open,” he said. “I haven’t forgotten why we came.”

“I know.”

He looked as though he had expected more of a defense to be necessary. She let him go without requiring it.

Cassia refused the carrying chair the first time Elena mentioned it.

“I can walk.”

“Across this room. Can you walk down the service stair and come back after standing in the cold?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then we take a chair.”

Cassia looked at Nerio. He had plainly agreed with Elena before she came, and plainly wished she had not made it so easy to see. Cassia turned away from both of them.

Elena waited. She had brought no medicines out of her bag. This part of the visit had to be completed before she could begin the familiar work that would make them both feel less exposed.

“If I remain here, he will say the order was written when I was too ill to know what I signed,” Cassia said.

“He may say that if you go.”

“Then people can see me answer.”

“Yes.”

Cassia looked back, surprised by the agreement.

“The stair could make you much worse,” Elena continued. “You may not be able to finish speaking. I can’t promise to keep you conscious because the work needs a witness. We can take water, blankets, someone to carry you. You need to know that those may not be enough.”

“I understand.”

Elena disliked how quickly she said it. She checked herself before asking her to prove it.

“Then I’ll come,” she said. “With the chair.”

Cassia let out a breath. “Borrow it. Don’t spend my money on a thing I’ll hate afterward.”

Nerio smiled at that, openly relieved. Elena took the cup from the side table and sat down to watch Cassia drink. Outside, a woman called for someone to fetch a child from the well court. A door shut. The ordinary sounds went on while the old woman worked her way down to the next scratch on the cup.

Chapter 18 — Lena’s Price

Sarnai did not sit down when Sariel told her what they had decided.

She stood beside the chest in which her winter furs had been packed, one hand on a folded sleeve. The tent smelled of warmed felt and a sharp salve somebody had rubbed into the leather of a saddle. Outside, riders were preparing for the market road. The horses made little impatient sounds whenever the people attending them stopped to talk.

“You agreed to bring it to me,” she said.

“Tema hired us to do that. He’s agreed to change the work. We still need your agreement and the keepers’.”

“You need mine last.”

Sariel felt her carefully prepared answer become useless. She had wanted to arrive with enough decided that Sarnai would not have to spend the morning supervising five people who were still unsure of their own intentions. Now it sounded as if they had settled everything and come to collect her assent.

“We won’t make Marcellus swear,” she said. “That part is decided. I don’t want to disguise it as a question. The return to the chamber is yours to refuse.”

Sarnai looked at her for a while. Then she took the sleeve out of the chest, shook it and hung the coat from a peg. It was a practical act performed with more force than the coat required.

“I am tired of being asked to leave a useful weapon behind because the person holding it has begun to feel ashamed,” she said. “You spoke willingly enough the other evening.”

“I did.”

“Have you brought an alternative he will respect?”

“Cassia will publish her part in the ceremonies. She can stay the next one long enough for us to enter. Afterward there will be witnesses to what was done below.”

“And if nothing happens?”

“Then people will still be ill. We’ll have to get them out and keep asking why.”

Sariel heard how poor it sounded. She had once believed an honest answer ought to possess a special persuasiveness. Sarnai did not look persuaded.

The khatun called for Yelü and the keepers. While they came, she folded the emptied wrapping cloth and put it beneath the chest. Sariel stood near the entrance, close enough to feel the cold at her back. She could have stepped outside and waited, but Sarnai had not dismissed her.

Altan came in carrying a small bundle of tools. Sare followed, her hair still damp from washing. Neither had dressed for a ceremony. Yelü stood just inside the flap, where she could see the people preparing to leave.

Sarnai told them the proposal in fewer words than Sariel had used. When she finished, Sare asked who would open the burial door.

“The city’s key holder, under Cassia’s order,” Sariel said. “We aren’t breaking it this time.”

“And the stone within?”

“Your tools. Thane can carry what you need.”

Altan put his bundle on the floor and untied one end. The chisels were different ages, their handles polished in different places. He lifted the broadest, examined the edge and set it beside the others.

“I would rather have been there before they fitted the new masonry,” he said. “There are things I cannot promise until I see what they covered.”

“Will you try?”

He looked at Sare. She nodded without looking at Sarnai.

“We will try.”

Sarnai’s face remained still. Sariel knew better than to thank her as though the keepers had answered for all three.

Yelü asked how many attendants Cassia could name. Sariel gave her the number they had discussed, with room for the men carrying the chair and the people who knew the mechanism.

“My people come under my word,” Yelü said. “They won’t take instructions from Benjamin unless I put them with him. Some will stay outside the arch. If soldiers close it, I don’t want everyone we have caught on the same side.”

“We need help clearing the public lane.”

“I’ll speak to him about that. He can show me where he means.”

Sariel nodded. Yelü’s eye remained on her, requiring her to understand the distinction rather than repeat it aloud.

At last Sarnai sat. She motioned Sariel to the opposite stool.

“The written settlement must acknowledge what was taken,” she said. “Not only its return. Our access does not begin as a gift from this Prefect.”

Sariel brought out Tema’s copied amendment, unfolded it and placed it where Sarnai could read. The merchant had left a space for the khatun’s acknowledgment. Sarnai read the full sheet, called for her own pen and added a line above that space.

Sariel waited while the ink dried. The tent had warmed with so many people in it. Outside, the riders began to move.

“I still think you are giving him too much,” Sarnai said as she returned the paper.

“I know.”

“You needn’t agree with me. You do need to remember I said it.”

Sariel folded the amendment carefully and put it back inside her coat. She could remember that without difficulty.

Lena chose the place where she was paid.

It was the yard behind Peyton’s hired store, in view of two women mending sacks. The women had no interest in the transaction except that it occupied the only dry section of the bench. One moved her basket with a sigh. Lena thanked her and spread a square of clean cloth over the wood.

Sariel counted out forty marks. Tema had put them into a separate purse, tied with a different cord from the money remaining for the company’s expenses. She emptied it completely and laid the purse beside the coins.

Lena counted once. She separated the money into four small portions and put them away without hurrying. Sariel watched the practiced movements and tried not to look as if she were learning where the money went.

“That’s the work,” Lena said.

“Yes.”

Sariel sat on the far end of the bench. One of the sack menders pulled a length of twine between them and went back to her sewing. Lena waited.

“There’s another job,” Sariel said. “You can hear it or leave. The first one is finished either way.”

“I heard you’re taking the box back.”

“Below the Gate. Into the chamber it came from.”

“Not to the room we took it out of.”

“No.”

Lena sat beside her, leaving the cloth between them.

Sariel described the entry, the order and the public lane. She told Lena where she herself would go with the chest. She did not call the lower stair an escape route. It was a way out they would have to keep open, beneath a gate whose support had been damaged before they arrived.

“Thane can hold the mechanism through a limited movement,” she said. “He can’t keep someone on the upper walk from locking the control line. We need someone there who can release it if they do.”

Lena leaned forward, elbows on her knees. “Will I be named?”

“As an attendant with the maintenance party.”

“Then I have a reason to be on the upper walk.”

“Until somebody decides the order doesn’t apply.”

“Who stays above with me?”

“Thane at the housing. Octan knows the controls. Benjamin below, with Yelü’s people helping the traffic.”

“You aren’t up there.”

“No.”

The sack mender beside them drew her needle through a thick seam. It came free suddenly. She blew out a breath and shifted the cloth on her knee.

Lena looked at Sariel’s coat pocket, where the old pick case made a small square beneath the wool.

“What if you decide you need me downstairs?”

“I won’t.”

“You didn’t decide you needed a wounded man in the window either.”

Sariel turned toward her. Lena had said it without accusation. She wanted an answer to the actual problem.

“If something changes, I’ll ask,” Sariel said. “You can refuse. Keeping the upper way clear is the job I’m offering.”

“And after the control line?”

“The north wicket. It opens separately. You leave when you’ve done what you can, or when the position can’t be held.”

“Does everyone know that?”

“I’ll tell them.”

“Tell them with me there.”

Sariel nodded.

“Thirty marks,” she said. “For the agreed work. Whatever happens to the Seal afterward.”

“No share of a future road?”

“We gave ours up.”

Lena looked at her for a moment, then down at the cloth. She folded it and put it away.

“I want to see the housing with Thane before I say yes.”

“He’s there this afternoon.”

“Then I’ll go.”

Sariel stood. She had almost made the mistake of thanking her for accepting. She put the empty purse into her own bag instead.

Lena caught her sleeve lightly.

“I know you didn’t have to bring Corven.”

“We did bring him.”

“I know. I’m saying I saw it.”

Sariel stopped looking for the next thing to say. Lena let go of the sleeve. The sack mender took possession of the bench as soon as they moved.

The afternoon inspection was conducted with the Gate open. That was Octan’s condition. He wanted no one to use the sound of his work as an excuse to close the passage and search the waiting carts again.

Sariel stood below the housing while Thane showed Lena the line. Octan had been released without a charge and with an instruction to remain available. He wore his own belt again, but he had knotted a new bootlace so tightly that the leather pinched. Twice Sariel saw him shift his weight off that foot.

The maintenance permit admitted only a small group. Benjamin and Yelü waited at the edge of the court, where a soldier could see them. Below the upper walk, a boy was sweeping grit out of a recess. He worked around the visitors without asking anyone to move until Thane noticed and made room.

Sariel could hear the technical discussion without following all of it. Thane traced the movement with his left hand. Lena watched his hand, then the actual line. She asked where the weight would go if the catch failed. He showed her the timber that would take the first shock and the part that was meant to break afterward.

“You’ll lose the fitting,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You bought it.”

“I did.”

She looked at him. He looked back, plainly not wanting to discuss whether he had come to regret the purchase. Sariel understood that reluctance. He had enjoyed buying it. She wanted him to have enjoyed it, even now.

Lena followed Octan along the upper walk to the narrow way that brought them down toward the north wicket. She returned on foot rather than performing a climb for anyone’s benefit. At the housing she put her palm on the rail, felt the vibration of the moving gate and withdrew it.

“I can do that,” she said.

They went down together. Sariel told Benjamin and Yelü the limit Lena had asked for: upper controls, then the independent wicket; no assumption that she would be sent below. Benjamin nodded. Yelü asked Lena whether she could call down if the wicket began to close. Lena said yes, so long as no one expected her to make herself heard from inside the stone stair.

They agreed on an ordinary shout from the upper walk while there was still a view of the lane. If people could not hear it, Yelü’s nearest attendant would carry the warning. Sariel was relieved by how little needed to be invented.

Kestra came up from the lower service stair with Nerio. She had checked it again in daylight, writing the turns in her working book. She would have to walk and prepare it again tomorrow for the rite. The lower chamber remained barred. Nothing she marked would tell anyone what lay beyond that door.

“Seventy-four paces outward,” she told Sariel. “From the vestibule to this court. It can guide people along that stair while I’m below. It won’t keep it from being blocked.”

Sariel looked at the stair entrance. Men had carried goods through it for years, judging by the chips in the lower edge. A person with a cart could make it impassable in less time than it had taken Kestra to measure it.

“We’ll keep someone here,” Yelü said.

“Under your command?”

“Yes.”

That was enough. Sariel folded her rough sketch and put it away. There was no second descent waiting beyond its edge, no rooftop she had kept secret because she liked being the one who knew. She would be below with the others between her and the street.

Back at the practice, Benjamin found her trying to repair the coat again.

“It will hold for tomorrow,” he said.

“I know. It’s annoying me.”

He sat on the bed beside her, leaving space for the needle case. The room was the smaller one above Dometta’s kitchen, hired for a night so that two of them could sleep without listening to Corven. The ceiling sloped steeply. Benjamin had struck his head on it when they first came up and now remembered to remain bent until he reached the bed.

Sariel pulled the lining flat. The blue had faded at the seams. She could remember buying it so clearly that the worn cloth still surprised her.

“You’re not happy about the decision,” she said.

“I think it’s the decision I can live with. That isn’t quite the same.”

She nodded. He took the case when she offered it and held it open while she chose a finer needle. Below them, Dometta was asking someone to stop moving the stool. A door opened, letting in the smell of supper, then shut again.

“If it goes badly,” Sariel began.

Benjamin looked at her.

She stopped. She had meant to give him a permission or an instruction, something that would let her believe the uncertainty had been attended to. He was already going to be above, choosing which person to help through a closing passage. She could not improve that by giving him another promise to remember.

“I’d like you to stay here tonight,” she said.

His expression changed. “I was going to.”

“Good.”

She let the warmth of his shoulder remain against hers while she sewed. The new stitch was smaller and considerably worse. Benjamin watched without offering advice.

Later, when they went downstairs, the borrowed carrying chair occupied most of the kitchen. Nerio had brought it from a household near the Archive, with a condition that it be back before the week’s end. Elena was testing a folded blanket against its hard seat. Thane had removed a projecting peg so it would pass the narrowest turn without striking the wall.

Lena stood by the yard door. She had accepted the new job in front of Tema and had asked Kestra to put its limits beneath the fee. The paper was drying on the table. Sariel read it, made her mark beside Lena’s and left it with Kestra for the morning copy.

“Cassia is still writing,” Nerio said. “She wants the admission read aloud before she signs it at dawn.”

“I’ll come,” Kestra answered.

Elena set the last clean bandages in her bag. Dometta laid a bowl beside the chair for the person who would forget to eat. Nobody claimed it at first. Then Thane sat on the bottom step and pulled it toward him.

Sariel took the old pick case from her coat and checked its clasp. There was a little rust on one of the unused hooks. She rubbed it away with the corner of her sleeve, returned the hook to its place and shut the case.

Tomorrow they would ask for the keys.

Chapter 19 — The Stay

Cassia crossed out the word unforeseen.

It had taken her three attempts to draw a line through it. The first was too short; the second wandered into the line above. Kestra held the sheet steady while she made the third.

“Leave the correction visible,” Cassia said.

“It will be.”

“The copies too.”

Nerio had stopped writing. He looked at the blackened word, then at the space he had already left for it on his own page.

“I can omit it from mine.”

“I don’t want it omitted. I want it crossed out.”

He dipped his pen again.

The Archive’s morning shutters were open, though the sun had not yet reached the windows. From the street came the scraping of a baker’s board and a woman’s argument with someone who was unloading in front of her door. The air smelled of hot bran and the sharp paste Nerio had made for mounting the notices. On Cassia’s desk, beside the excavation book, lay a cup of broth gone cold enough to show a skin.

Kestra turned the cup a little, putting its handle within reach. Cassia did not take it.

They had been working since before the lamps were extinguished. At first Kestra had disliked the admission’s careful language. It sounded so much like the other reports in the room that she feared a reader could reach the end without understanding what had happened. Then she had reached the sentence in which Cassia named the people she had recommended for the first oath ceremony. The pen rested above the paper a long time before the Censor put down including persons whose livelihood required continued admission to the Gate.

“Is that accurate?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Then say so if they ask you.”

Kestra read it again. “It doesn’t say you knew they couldn’t refuse.”

Cassia’s uncovered eye moved toward her. Her clouded left eye was covered by a plain dark patch. Her silver lens lay in its padded box, too painful to wear that morning.

“I knew they would find refusal difficult.”

“So did they.”

Nerio’s pen stopped a second time.

Cassia leaned back. For a moment Kestra thought she had lost the sentence. The Censor’s fingers moved on the arm of her chair, rehearsing a shape. Then she reached for the pen.

“Give me the page.”

She wrote slowly, without asking Kestra to steady her hand.

I knew refusal would cost some their work.

“That is as far as I knew,” she said.

Kestra nodded. She had wanted something fiercer and found she could not improve it.

Elena came while Nerio was drying the last copy. She set her bag beneath the desk and took Cassia’s wrist without moving the papers.

“Have you been able to eat?”

Cassia glanced at the cup. “A little. I keep losing hold of it.”

Nerio moved the papers away from the stain on her cuff.

Elena drew a stool beside her. “Let me help before we go. I don’t want you trying those stairs with nothing in you.”

She fetched a clean spoon from the little room behind the office. Cassia accepted it with an irritation that reassured Kestra more than her earlier composure. She managed two spoonfuls herself, rested, and let Elena hold the cup for the next.

Nerio stood by the window with the sheets raised into the light. He had sand on the front of his robe and a line of black ink along his thumb. On his first morning in the Archive he had probably been as careful as Kestra was now being. She wondered how long it took to stop dressing for the room one hoped to deserve.

Cassia followed her glance.

“He makes excellent copies,” she said. “He’s wasted on this.”

“I can hear you,” Nerio said.

“I know.”

“You have never minded wasting me on copies before.”

Cassia began to answer. Her mouth remained slightly open. Elena waited with the spoon above the cup.

“Give me the next sheet,” Cassia said at last.

Nerio brought it. He did not meet Kestra’s eyes.

The names of the authorized attendants occupied its lower half. The five were there, and Lena under the name she had agreed to give. Altan and Sare, custodians of the disturbed burial. Nerio as recording clerk. Octan and the workers needed to secure the passage. Yelü and a small named party for the surface clearance; they were to carry no drawn weapons inside the court.

Sarnai had declined to enter the chamber. She would remain outside, where she could make decisions for the people who had come with her and where no fall of masonry could conveniently end the argument.

The order stayed the administration of further oaths pending examination of the disputed object and admitted the keepers to restore the place from which it had been removed. It did not transfer the Gate to anyone. It did not dissolve the garrison. Cassia had made Kestra read those limits aloud twice, because the people at the arch would have to understand them in less accommodating circumstances.

“Who receives the first?” Elena asked.

“Flaccus,” Nerio said. “At the Gate. The Tribunal receives the admission and the second signed order. A third goes to the provincial office by courier. We keep the fourth.”

“And if the courier is stopped?”

“He has two copies,” Cassia said. “One is going with a licensed carrier. The other isn’t.”

Kestra looked up.

“The same words,” Cassia added. “A different horse.”

Elena set the spoon in the cup. “You can walk as far as the court if we stop when I ask. Below, you will sit on the chair they are carrying. You aren’t to improve the appearance of the proceeding by standing.”

Cassia was watching the document.

“I need your answer,” Elena said.

“Yes.”

“And if you cannot answer me down there, Nerio helps me bring you out. We do not wait for the ceremony to finish.”

“I would prefer—”

“So would I. This is how far I can go with you.”

Kestra expected another exchange, perhaps something about the necessity of witnessing the restoration. Cassia put down her pen and drew the broth toward her with both hands.

“Then we stop when you ask.”

Elena stayed until she had eaten a little more.

The difficulty came in the annex.

A copying clerk Kestra knew by sight found the passage about the withheld excavation report, read it twice, and put his finger on the space where a witness’s name would ordinarily have appeared.

“You cannot lodge a statement with a person missing.”

“The person isn’t missing,” Nerio said. “The name is reserved.”

“Reserved to whom?”

“The examining officer appointed to the inquiry.”

“Which officer?”

“No officer has been appointed yet.”

The clerk looked satisfied, as though Nerio had offered him the loose thread he needed. Behind him a girl held a stack of blank forms against her chest. She was trying to listen without seeming to stop working.

Kestra took the admission from Nerio and laid it beside the excavation book. The original report’s identifying leaf was folded into a separate cover. She kept her hand on it.

“The Censor identifies her own act,” she said. “She held back a report. She gives the date and the register reference. The missing name does not prevent you recording that.”

“It prevents verification.”

“Today?”

He glanced toward the passage where Cassia waited with Elena.

“She’s admitting something that may remove her from office,” Kestra said. “You can record it before deciding whether she’s done enough to convince you.”

Nerio reached for a scrap and began writing a short reservation for the register: a supplementary identifying statement retained under protection, to be produced to the examining officer in person. He offered it to the clerk.

“That gives you a place to say you weren’t shown it.”

The man read the sentence. His thumb worried the edge of his sleeve. Kestra could see him imagining a later room in which someone would ask why he had accepted a defective admission. She had made him into a small enemy because he stood between her and the door; he was a man trying not to be the only signature left on a page when everyone else had gone.

“Put your objection in full,” she said. “We aren’t asking you to approve it.”

He took the scrap.

The girl with the forms shifted them into one arm and brought him a fresh sheet. When he had finished, she shook the sand over the wet lines herself.

They left a copy on the public board in the annex. Nerio pressed the pasted corners with his ink-stained thumb. Kestra stood beside him while a carrier began reading over her shoulder.

“Is that the old chamber?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Under the road?”

She stepped aside so he could see the whole notice. A second man leaned in, and the carrier began reading it aloud, stumbling over Cassia’s titles but making a good clear job of the sentence about work.

Kestra carried the reserved leaf back to Cassia. The Censor put it into the inner pocket of her coat, beneath the ordinary personal seal she wore at her throat. She tapped the pocket once, checking it had settled flat.

Then they went out.

The chest was waiting on a small handcart in Dometta’s doorway. Benjamin stood at its handles. Sariel had wrapped its iron corners in folded cloth, leaving the carrying straps free. It looked less like a dangerous relic than something bought for a room in which one expected to grow old.

Kestra did not go close until she had checked the brown wool at her throat. Her own disc was warm from her skin. The gesture did nothing to the chest. She knew that; she still needed the familiar weight of the bronze between finger and thumb.

Dometta came out with a covered bowl and put it in Elena’s hands.

“For afterward,” she said. “You keep finding reasons not to eat in my house.”

“I’ve eaten in your house.”

“Standing up. Usually while accusing someone else of the same thing.”

Elena gave the bowl to a hired attendant with the rest of the supplies. Then she leaned toward Dometta and said something Kestra could not hear. Dometta’s face changed. She took Elena’s hand, squeezed it hard, and let her go.

Thane was already at the Gate with Octan. Lena had gone ahead to look at the upper works before the court filled with people. She had left no promise to be at a particular window if something else needed doing. Sariel had accepted that and looked unhappy about it, which Kestra regarded as progress in both of them.

Altan and Sare approached from the eastern lane carrying their own tools. The older man had a narrow bundle of cloth under his arm. Sare carried a shallow basket covered with hide. Neither had dressed for a spectacle. Sare’s boots were muddy at the heel, and Altan had missed a piece of white thread on his coat. Yelü waited at the corner with her named attendants, letting the two keepers come the last few yards alone.

Cassia straightened when she saw them.

“I have recorded the taking,” she said to Altan. “And my part in the use afterward.”

Altan looked at the papers under Nerio’s arm.

“You will say it below too.”

“Yes.”

Sare lifted the cloth from one corner of the chest, not touching its lid.

“The iron should be covered until we are there.”

Sariel replaced the cloth. Nobody asked Sare whether that was necessary. Kestra liked the relief of having one instruction she could follow without qualifying it.

They moved through the morning street slowly enough for Cassia. Benjamin’s handcart rattled on the joints between paving stones. A boy walked beside it for half a street, trying to see through the wrappings, until his mother called him back. Shops were lifting their shutters. Someone had washed an upper room and sent a line of gray water down a wall; Sariel shifted the cart left before Benjamin rolled into it.

Near the Gate a woman carrying a basket of onions recognized Elena and tried to stop her. She had a question about a dressing. Elena answered while walking, then stopped anyway to look at the woman’s wrist. Cassia leaned against a post and shut her eye.

Kestra felt the morning getting away from them. Every interrupted step brought another imagined order from the Prefect’s rooms. She opened her mouth to hurry Elena, saw the damp red line beneath the dressing, and closed it.

When they went on, Cassia was looking at her.

“I’m sorry we have to go so slowly,” the Censor said.

Kestra felt ashamed of having looked impatient. “It isn’t you I want to hurry.”

“I know.” Cassia waited until Elena returned, then took her arm again.

They passed through the still-open public arch with the morning traffic and turned north into the carrier lane. Looking back, Kestra saw the Gate’s east face above the last row of stalls. Sunlight struck the worn bronze plates high above the public road. Below, a line of carts stretched out toward the Acasian approach, while townspeople passed through the narrow wicket with baskets and work rolls. The ordinary toll bell rang. A guard stepped back to let a cart of brushwood clear the turn.

To their left, beyond the main passage, the service-court door stood open.

Kestra could see Flaccus through it, reading a paper brought by a runner. Octan waited beside him with his tools untouched on the ground. On the upper walkway, one brief movement of brown cloth vanished behind the counterweight housing.

Sariel set her hand on the cart’s side and brought it to a halt.

Nerio took the order from the hard cover beneath his arm. Kestra watched him separate the Gate’s copy from their own and hand it to Cassia without disturbing the reserved document in her coat.

The Censor accepted Elena’s elbow for the walk across the court.

“Captain,” she said.

Flaccus looked up. Behind him the public toll bell rang again, impatiently, because nobody had brought out the change.

Chapter 20 — The Closing Passage

Sariel had entered the court more successfully when she was stealing from it.

That evening there had been a man to distract, a hinge to keep quiet, a stretch of paving to cross before the next worker came down the stair. This morning there were twelve perfectly legitimate reasons for not moving an inch. Flaccus wanted the order read by the duty clerk. The duty clerk wanted the admission from the Tribunal. Nerio had brought it, so the duty clerk wanted to know whether the Tribunal had accepted it. Cassia showed him the receiving mark. He bent nearer to examine the mark while Elena watched the Censor’s knees.

The handcart handles grew warm beneath Benjamin’s palms. He had put it down, but his hands remained around them.

Sariel loosened one of the corner cloths before it could slip under a wheel. Across the court, the guard who had let them through kept glancing at her face. She had kept her hood up during the theft. In the daylight she could feel how little protection that had been.

“Your copy is for the Gate,” Cassia told the clerk. “The admission is for inspection. Return it to my assistant.”

The clerk reluctantly separated the papers.

Flaccus had read enough to dislike the order. His jaw set harder when he reached the attendants’ names. He looked at Yelü, whose sword remained tied into its sheath with a strip of plain cloth.

“How many with you?”

“The number written there.”

“And outside?”

“My people can stand on a road.”

“They will stand clear of this road.”

Yelü glanced through the open cart door, where her attendants had already moved a waiting carrier aside to leave a lane. “They are.”

“This is a stay pending examination of the taking and its effects,” Cassia replied. “The original custodians have been admitted to restore the place. You may post witnesses. You may not administer another oath during the stay.”

“That ceremony cannot proceed without—”

“Then refraining will not inconvenience you.”

Elena moved her hand a little higher under Cassia’s elbow. The Censor let the argument rest.

The captain turned on him. “We are receiving a party.”

“Keep the passage working,” the captain said.

Octan took that for the permission it was worth. He beckoned Thane toward the upper stair.

“A waymark, outward. The Censor’s order names the working.”

Someone had left a rake in the route of the handcart. Benjamin moved it and was immediately accused of taking it.

The sun reached the court. One patch on the wall grew bright enough to hurt her eyes. She stood in its warmth and remembered a different morning with Benjamin when they had set out to meet a buyer who never arrived. They had spent the rest of that day doing nothing worth being paid for. There had been a very poor musician and a pear divided with the wrong knife. She had resented the lost wages until well into the afternoon.

She wanted another useless day with him.

“What?”

“I’d like another day to ourselves. When this is done.”

He smiled. “So would I.”

He fitted it around the traveling rod while Octan directed two workers placing diagonal braces against sound stone. The blocks meant to receive the barrier were beside the passage, ready to be pulled into position once the carts were clear.

It was not an impressive-looking defense. Sariel had liked it more when Thane described it on a table, where the gate weighed nothing and no one stood beneath it.

Lena appeared at the upper rail, crouched to speak to Octan, and went behind the housing again. A guard noticed and started up after her.

“She’s named,” Sariel said.

“Names don’t tell me what she’s doing.”

“Ask the engineer.”

The guard did. Octan said, loudly, that she was doing something he had asked her to do, and would the guard either carry the breakfast pot up or keep off the stair. The guard carried the pot. Sariel decided she liked Octan more in circumstances where she was not stealing from his workplace.

Kestra emerged from the lower stair and turned her sandglass over on a broad stone beside the entrance. She had walked to the vestibule and back. Now she knelt to chalk the upper mark, a small deliberate shape placed where traffic would not erase it. She went down again, placing the other marks at the turns.

The escort guard looked bored. Sariel was grateful to him for it.

Nerio brought the city key to the burial door. The duty clerk made him sign for it before releasing the ring. Sare examined the key without taking it, then told Nerio to keep it until they were down. Altan had sat on the edge of a low trough to save his legs. He watched the main road through the court’s inner opening, where a driver was backing a light cart to make room for one wider than the arch appeared willing to accept.

Cassia’s chair stood ready beside the lower stair. It was an ordinary narrow carrying chair with two poles passed through leather slings, borrowed from a household near the Archive. Their elderly mother went to the baths in it and had asked that it be returned before the end of the week. Sariel had promised to try, and then removed try when the woman looked at her.

She disliked remembering that now.

Elena helped Cassia into it. The Censor’s head dropped back for a moment before she found the upright position again. Nerio crouched beside her and tucked the hem of her coat away from the pole.

Kestra returned. A fine pale line crossed one cheek where she had touched it with the chalk in her hand. She checked the sandglass, waited for the last grains, and put it away.

“Ready?” Sariel asked.

“The route is prepared. I’ll call it from below if we need it.”

No urgency in her voice, no little performance of how much the waiting had cost her. Sariel nodded and turned to Benjamin.

They lifted the chest from the cart together.

The covering pulled tight against an iron corner.

Flaccus saw it.

“Put that down.”

Benjamin stopped. Sariel kept her end at her waist. She could feel the object inside shift against its padding, a weight too concentrated for the size of the box.

Cassia raised her head.

“The object is being restored under the order you’ve received.”

“It is stolen property under a military recovery warrant.”

“Taken from the place we are returning it to.”

“Put it down.”

His hand had moved to his sword. He had not drawn it. Two men behind him set the butts of their spears on the paving and looked at each other, seeking the point at which the morning had become something they could be blamed for.

Sariel lowered her end carefully. Benjamin followed. She left both carrying straps upright.

“The stay lists the object,” she said.

“It does not cancel the Prefect’s warrant.”

“Then you have a question for the Prefect. While he answers, there is a sick woman waiting to go downstairs.”

“You will all wait.”

Flaccus pointed to the court door. A soldier began swinging it inward. Outside, someone protested that his cart was still halfway across the opening. Yelü turned and stepped between the door and the shaft.

“Move the cart first,” she said.

The soldier pushed harder. Yelü’s palm went flat against the wood.

From the upper works Octan shouted down that he had men in the passage. Flaccus shouted back to lower the main barrier to half-height and stop the carts. He pointed toward the wicket for the people. From where he stood, he could not see the horse backing in the arch.

For a moment nobody moved.

Then the captain repeated the order to his own gate sergeant, using the man’s name.

A warning bell rang above the arch, different from the toll bell: three uneven blows. The driver beneath the barrier looked up instead of moving. His horse felt the reins tighten and backed against the cart behind it. Sariel heard wood knock wood. A woman screamed at someone to stop pushing.

Benjamin had already left the chest.

“Forward!” he called to the first driver. “Give him the reins. Forward, there’s room.”

There was very little room. Benjamin took the bridle close to the bit and turned the horse toward the wider side of the opening. A guard blocked him with a spear held across the road.

“You want this cart here when it comes down?” Benjamin said.

The guard shifted aside.

Sariel seized one strap of the chest. Altan took the other before she could call him. His grip was dry and surprisingly strong.

“Down,” she said.

A soldier stepped toward them.

Nerio put the open order between his face and the chest. “Your name, please.”

The man blinked.

“If you are preventing the Censor’s attendants entering, I have to record who did it.”

He was too young to know whether that was true, and too sensible to give Nerio his name until he had found out. Sariel and Altan passed behind him.

Sare went ahead to the stair with the tools. Elena directed the chair bearers to lift. Cassia gripped the chair arms and tried to speak to Flaccus, but the words broke under a cough. He ordered her to remain where she was.

Elena looked at the barrier beginning to move above the crowded public road.

“She cannot remain in this,” she said, and took the front of the chair toward the lower stair.

Sariel waited at the first tread while the bearers brought Cassia down behind Sare. Below them Kestra’s chalk shone dully in the ordinary lamplight. There was no magic in it yet.

A grinding shriek came from the west guide. The barrier stopped, moved a finger’s breadth, and stopped again.

Octan swore so loudly Sariel heard every word.

She looked up. Thane was braced beside the open housing, his good hand around a lever, his right forearm set against the rail. Lena had gone higher, along the walkway above the control line. A loop of rope had caught behind a turning wheel. She lay on her stomach and reached for it with a hook.

The captain was shouting for the court to be cleared. Yelü’s attendants had pulled the cart out of the side doorway and were holding that entrance open. In the public arch Benjamin had got the first horse through; the following cart had slewed, pinning a man’s basket against the stone. He was trying to save the basket instead of himself.

Sariel set the chest down on the stair and took two steps back toward the court.

Benjamin looked over his shoulder.

“Take it,” he said.

“I can—”

“We have the opening. Take it down.”

She could see the opening. Yelü had reached the wicket and was directing people north, away from the jammed carts. One of her riders passed a child through to someone outside. Benjamin put his foot through the trapped basket, breaking it free, and pushed its owner toward her.

He had work. He was doing it.

Sariel went back to the chest. Altan waited without asking why she had left him holding it.

“Ready?” she said.

“Lift on the straight leg. Mine isn’t.”

She shifted her grip and they took the weight together.

At the first turn she could still see the upper walkway. Lena had freed part of the caught loop and was reaching farther, her boot hooked beneath a rail. Thane looked across at her, said something lost in the grinding, and she shook her head with fierce impatience.

Then the turn shut them from sight.

Sariel carried the chest down, taking the corners slowly enough that Altan could keep his footing. The air cooled against her face. Above, the warning bell began again and cut off halfway through a stroke.

At the lower landing, Sare held the lamp while Nerio tried the borrowed key. It went into the lock but would not turn. Sariel lowered the chest, put two fingers beneath the hasp and lifted its weight clear of the bolt.

“Now.”

The key turned.

Sare took the bar off its brackets. Along the top of the old door, beneath a strip of flaking whitewash, Sariel could see letters scratched into the stone. A later mason had cut straight across the last few to make room for a lintel.

Altan touched the uncut part once before he went through.

Chapter 21 — Under the Names

The drawing had given the chamber a straight eastern wall. Standing inside the door, Kestra could see why. The surveyor had stopped where the lamps stopped.

Beyond the new stonework the wall continued at an angle, disappearing into a narrow recess. There were shelves cut into it, too shallow to hold a body, deep enough for a bowl or a folded cloth. Most were empty. One held a little cup with its handle turned toward the stone. Damp had collected beneath it in a dark oval, though the ceiling above was dry.

Sare went there first. She set her lamp on the floor and reached behind the cup. When her hand came out it held a pinch of grit. She rubbed it between finger and thumb and showed Altan.

“Cutting dust,” he said.

They looked across at the newer masonry.

Kestra knew the dimensions, the weight removed, the height of the Gate’s west support. None of those figures had prepared her for the raw place where a workman’s chisel had gone through the edge of a person’s name. The interrupted letters disappeared into a joint. Whoever had laid the block had needed a straight surface and made one.

Sariel and Altan put the chest down beside an open rectangle in the floor. Its cover had been broken into three unequal pieces and stacked against the near wall. A square of finer gravel showed where the chest itself had once stood after being lifted out. There were drag marks leading toward the door.

Sare knelt beside the opening. She did not lean over it at once. She took off her gloves and folded them inside one another, then put them in her basket.

“The sacks first,” she said. “Before we open the chest.”

Kestra went back to the vestibule with Nerio. The little storeroom door stood beside the burial entrance, its latch crossed by Cassia’s preservation notice. Someone had tested the pasted edge and decided not to tear it. Nerio read the storage mark aloud while Kestra compared it with the copy in her book.

He unlocked the door with the second key on the city’s ring.

The sacks had been stacked beneath spare paving stones. One had split along a seam, spilling dark earth onto the floor. Nerio crouched, swore quietly, and began moving the stones. Kestra helped him clear the nearest sack. It weighed much more than it appeared to. She tried to lift it alone, felt the scrape at her wrist stretch, and stopped.

“Both of us,” Nerio said.

They carried it across the vestibule and set it where Sare directed. Sariel came to help with the others. The split sack had to be wrapped in an empty canvas before it could be lifted. Nerio fetched a flat board and scraped up the loose earth; Sare swept the last of it onto the board with her own brush, keeping the pale chips of new mortar separate.

Kestra had expected Cassia’s notice to have protected the sacks from any damage. She looked at the paving stones, the torn seam, the little space left for people to work. The notice had kept the material here. Other hands would still have to put it back.

Altan opened one sack and pressed his palm into the dark earth. He brought out a small piece of cloth, almost black, and held it cupped until Sare laid a clean strip beneath it. Neither said what it had been.

Only when the sacks were inside did Sare let them carry Cassia’s chair through. The bearers set it on the sound paving beside the door, where Elena could reach her from either side. Nerio stood near its back, holding a small shaded lamp and the hard cover containing their copy of the order. Elena took the cover from him and laid it beneath the chair, clear of the damp patch on the floor.

“Lamp here,” she said.

He put it on the broad sill of an empty recess. Its light settled over Cassia’s face. She looked smaller down here, stripped of the desk and the high-backed chair and all the objects that usually agreed she was the person to hear.

Kestra wanted to ask whether she could see anything without the silver lens. It was a foolish question. Cassia was looking at the same damaged room as everyone else.

Above them a heavy impact traveled through the ceiling. Dust sifted from the newer joint. The bearers looked up together.

Elena caught one by the sleeve.

“Stay beside the chair. If I ask you to lift, don’t wait to be asked twice.”

“Will the roof—”

“I don’t know. I need you to hear me.”

He nodded, swallowing.

Sare took the chest’s key from Sariel. She had seen the lock opened already, but she checked the key against it before turning it. Altan spread his narrow cloth beside the grave, smoothing its edge with the heel of his hand.

When the lid rose, Kestra heard a sound behind her.

It might have been a shoe turning on grit. She looked toward the recess, where no one stood. The sound came again, with the smaller answering scrape of someone moving to make room.

Sare went to the new drain before lifting the wrapping from the Seal.

It entered the grave’s near corner through a channel hacked into the old bedding. Water had left a fine black line along one edge. Thane’s proposed plug lay in her basket, sized from the drawing; she tried it against the opening and withdrew it when one corner caught.

“Too high,” she said.

Altan knelt beside her. He marked the projecting edge with his thumbnail and worked at it with a small chisel, taking off less than Kestra thought necessary. Sare tried it again. This time it settled far enough for her to pack the joint with the clay she had brought, pushing it firmly into the uneven sides with a flat piece of wood.

Sariel held the lamp close while Altan watched the water. A drop gathered, hung beneath the stone, and fell. They waited for the next. It did not come.

“Where will it go now?” Sariel asked.

“Along the older channel,” Sare said. She pointed toward a groove beyond the grave’s edge. “We’ll clear that before we leave. The rain mustn’t sit here.”

Kestra moved the lamp to the groove. It had been choked with cutting dust. Nerio fetched the board again, and they scraped it clear while Sare finished sealing the new opening. When the first thread of water found its old course, Altan followed it with his finger until it passed the grave.

Sare wiped her hands on a cloth and lifted the wrapping from the Seal.

The bronze face lay uppermost, the old legion’s eagle spread above the broken wheel. In the light the worn device looked almost pleasant. Generations of handling had softened its harsh edges; the shank had darkened where hands fitted it. Kestra had known objects like that all her life, things made comfortable by repeated use. She disliked the familiarity more than she had disliked the dreams.

Altan said a few words in Ykrul. Sare answered. Neither raised a hand toward the rest of them or asked anyone to repeat the words.

“You can stand farther away,” Sare told Kestra.

“I can help.”

“You can hold the lamp where I put it.”

Kestra did.

The bottom of the opening was shallow, no deeper than her elbow to her fingertips. Old cloth lay against one end, stained into the shape of what it had covered. Beside it was a dark hollow in which the Seal’s shank had rested. Sare placed the bronze above that hollow without yet lowering it. She turned it once, trying the angle, then set it on Altan’s cloth.

The old man bent over the grave.

“I need the small brush.”

Sare passed it to him. He began clearing the loose cutting dust from the edge, taking care not to mix it with the darker earth beneath. His hand shook slightly. He rested his wrist on a flat stone and continued.

Kestra had imagined a rite. There was one, clearly; she could hear it in the quiet exchange of words between the keepers, in the order of what they touched. Yet the first work was the removal of what the builders had dropped. It took time. A crumb too large for the brush had to be lifted with the point of a narrow wooden tool. Sare picked a curl of mortar out of the old cloth and put it in a separate bowl.

At the door, Sariel listened to the stair.

No one was speaking above. That worried Kestra more than the noise had.

Cassia moved in the chair.

“I would record that the original recess—”

“Later,” Elena said.

“I can still dictate.”

“Yes. Later.”

Cassia shut her eye. Kestra felt the familiar impulse to take the sentence for her, to preserve the precise distinction before illness carried it away. She reached toward her working book, then found Sare watching the lamp dip.

She steadied it.

When the loose cutting dust was gone, Sare opened the preserved sacks. She returned the dark earth by shallow handfuls, easing it beneath the places where the old bedding had been scooped out. Nerio held the split seam closed while Altan took the last earth from the damaged sack. The work left their cuffs blackened and their fingers stiff. Kestra shifted the lamp from one hand to the other without being asked.

Altan lifted a thin sliver of dressed stone from beneath the edge of the broken cover. Under it, exposed for the first time, lay the continuation of the name the mason had cut. The letters were shallower than the knife marks Kestra knew on contemporary memorials. She could not read them. Sare touched two with one fingertip and made a sound in her throat that was almost a laugh, surprised and unhappy.

“What is it?” Kestra asked.

“My grandfather said the last part was a woman’s name. His brother said it was a place.”

“Can you tell?”

“No.”

Sare lowered the sliver into the bowl with the mortar.

From the narrow recess behind Kestra came the sound of someone drawing breath to answer a question.

No answer followed.

A man appeared in the doorway with blood on his sleeve. One of the Gate’s workers, Kestra thought, though she did not know him well enough to find his name. He had come down without a lamp and held one hand against the wall.

Elena rose immediately.

“Who sent you?”

“Octan. The little passage—it’s filling. They said come down here while they get the cart out.”

He saw the open grave and stopped.

“Don’t lean there,” Sare said.

He lifted his palm from the wall. A smear of blood remained over the faint letters.

“I didn’t know.”

Elena took him by the uninjured elbow and led him into the vestibule, where there was more light from the open stair. She returned for a roll of linen and gave Nerio a brief instruction about Cassia’s water. Then she went out again.

Kestra moved to the door, leaving Sare the lamp. The injured worker had a long scrape across the upper arm, bleeding freely but not in spurts. Elena seated him on the lowest sound step and folded a pad over it.

“What happened above?” Kestra asked.

“Line caught. Girl’s on it. Octan wants everyone clear of the public arch, but the captain’s got men at the wrong end.”

“Is the service court clear?”

“Clear enough. Better than the road.”

That was the route Kestra had prepared. It led from this vestibule up to the service court, not through the public arch. From the court people could reach the narrow wicket once Benjamin and Yelü had opened the approach. She could get those below to the place where other people were working. Beyond that, they would need the ordinary directions of a living person with a clear view.

She went to the foot of the stair and looked up. The lamps there had dimmed in the dust. At the first bend she could still see her chalk mark, placed on the underside of a stone lip where boots would not touch it.

The wounded man squinted past her.

“Which way is the road?”

“Behind you and above. We aren’t going that way.”

“I came down the other stair.”

“There is only this stair from the court.”

He pointed toward the burial door. “No, there. There were people.”

Elena’s hand tightened on the folded pad.

Kestra turned. Beyond the open chamber lay only the low shelves and the narrow recess. The man began to rise.

“Sit,” Elena said. She said it as she would to someone about to pull a wound open. He sat.

Kestra put her book and spare chalk in the bag on her shoulder, closed it, and took the Traveler’s disc from beneath her collar. The brown wool pulled against the back of her neck. Her wrist was scraped raw where the lowering rope had caught it during the theft; she wrapped the cord across her unhurt fingers instead.

“I can mark the way outward now,” she said. “From here to the court. I’ll need the stair kept quiet for a minute.”

Sariel came through the burial door. “Quiet how?”

“Don’t speak to me unless you want me to stop.”

“I can manage that.”

“And if someone enters from above, let them know where to step. The route won’t keep them out.”

Sariel nodded and took a place beside the door, within reach of Elena and the injured man. She had dust in her hair and a dark mark at the throat where the chest’s strap had rubbed.

Kestra faced up the stair.

She had prepared the turns in daylight. She held them now in their walked order: the low tread with the chipped nose, the bend beside the drain, the last six steps opening into the court. Seventy-four ordinary paces. The line had no business in the chamber behind her, no knowledge of where the keepers meant to set the Seal. She left that room out of the working, though she could feel how easy it would be to want the light to reach everyone.

The first words came dry. She swallowed and started again before she had bound the breath to the invocation.

After that there was only the measure she had learned, the words turning over the held disc, the slow passage of the working through her chest and throat. A shout reached down from above. Sariel answered it. Kestra let the syllables remain noise.

Halfway through, the stone beneath her left boot trembled.

She wanted to look behind her. Her mind furnished the chair overturned, Cassia on the floor, a wall coming down over the open grave. Nothing she could see supported any of it. She kept her gaze on the first chalk mark and finished the next phrase.

The full minute seemed much longer than it had beside a practice stair. Near its end the bronze grew hot enough that she had to resist loosening her fingers. Then the route caught.

A faint light ran up the edge of the first tread. It reached the turn, disappeared around it, and returned higher in a broken line where the stair changed direction. It was not bright enough to make the chamber pleasant. It was clear enough to follow with dust in the air and fear pulling one’s attention toward every sound.

The worker let out a breath.

“Oh,” he said. “That one.”

Kestra lowered the disc. For a moment her body expected the next phrase and could not understand that she had finished. Her knees shook. She put her free hand against the wall, waited until the stair stopped seeming farther away, and stepped out of the starting mark.

“Willing people from here,” she told Elena. Her voice was rough. “About ten minutes.”

Elena nodded. She had heard the limits before; this time she looked at Kestra’s face rather than the light.

“Sit against the wall until you know where your feet are.”

Kestra meant to refuse and found she was already sitting. Sariel brought her the water skin without speaking. She drank too quickly, coughed, and was furious with herself until Sariel took the skin back and waited without offering advice.

The wounded worker rose at Elena’s instruction. He followed the light to the first bend, keeping his good hand above the new dressing. His footsteps became ordinary as he climbed.

Inside the chamber, Altan had cleared the resting place.

Kestra stood in the doorway while her legs settled beneath her. The light on the stair remained at her back. Sare held the Seal above the old hollow. Its iron shank caught the lamplight once, a narrow hard gleam, before she lowered it into the dark.

Cassia opened her eye.

“I authorized the use,” she said.

Her voice was weak. Altan stopped working to hear her.

“I believed we could distinguish deliberate deception from incapacity. I was told—” She coughed and pressed her lips together. “I accepted what I was told. I continued after I had reason to doubt it.”

Nerio held the cup. She took a little water and looked toward the opening in the floor.

“I withheld a report. I do not withdraw that decision.”

Sare waited.

“I should have disclosed my own part sooner.”

Altan nodded once. He did not offer forgiveness or tell her the distinction was good. He lifted the edge of the old cloth, and Sare eased it around the bronze face without tightening it against the metal.

A sound rose from the recesses. Several people turning at once, the rustle of a sleeve against another person’s coat. Kestra could see each empty shelf in the lamplight. The little cup remained turned toward the wall.

Sare said the words she and Altan had practiced together before coming in. Kestra understood only the repeated name of the burial and the word for return. Her ignorance frightened her briefly. She wanted a language in which she could discover a mistake while there was still time to correct it.

Then she heard Sare falter, and Altan supply a word gently, with none of the alarm Kestra had expected. His niece repeated it. They continued.

Above them the Gate groaned.

This time the noise did not stop. It traveled through the new masonry in a long, low shudder, setting little grains of mortar jumping on the broken cover. Elena moved behind Cassia’s chair. Nerio stooped for their papers.

Sariel looked from the open grave to the doorway. The Seal lay within easy reach, the bronze face still uncovered by stone. She took one step toward it.

Kestra could not have stopped her quickly. Her own legs had only just become reliable again. She watched Sariel put a hand on the floor beside the grave, lean close enough to see the wrapped object, and hold there.

Nothing had changed in Cassia’s face.

Altan reached for the smallest piece of the cover. It was too heavy for him at that angle. Sariel took its other end.

“Where?” she asked.

Sare moved the lamp to show her the join.

Chapter 22 — The Familiar Name

Elena knew the sound of a person becoming unable to draw another breath. Cassia had not made it yet.

She bent close enough to feel the air move against her cheek. The Censor’s skin was hot beneath the collar, her lips dry. She had spent too much of what remained in speaking to the keepers. Elena had known she would. Knowing had not made the cost easier to watch.

“Look at me.”

Cassia’s eye opened.

“We’re going up.”

“No.”

“They can finish. You have witnessed the return.”

Cassia tried to turn toward the grave. The movement brought on a cough that folded her over her knees. Elena held her there while it passed, supporting her forehead and watching the fingers clench on the chair arm.

Nerio stood on the other side with the water.

“Not yet,” Elena told him.

He lowered it. His face was very pale beneath the ink at his temple. He had rubbed a dirty hand there earlier, in the office, and nobody had told him.

Across the chamber Sariel and Altan eased the first piece of stone onto its bedding. It touched down with a small click that Elena heard through the Gate’s long groaning. Sare brushed loose grit out of the join. The work was close to finished. Elena did not know what close meant to Cassia’s body.

The Censor lifted her head.

“Let me see it covered.”

Elena wanted to tell her that she had already had what she asked for. The promise in the office had been plain. If she became unable to answer, they would take her out. She was answering now, using that small remaining advantage with the exactness Elena had liked in her when it had been directed at someone else.

“One more piece,” Elena said. “Then we lift.”

Cassia nodded once.

Behind them, a chair bearer whispered to the other that the stairs were lit. He sounded relieved enough to be talking about a door opening into his own kitchen. Elena turned and saw Kestra standing against the vestibule wall, her head lowered, the bronze disc held between both palms. The light ran upward past her.

It had cost her. It was working.

Nerio wet a cloth and passed it to Elena. She wiped Cassia’s mouth and the hot crease below her jaw. The older woman shut her eye again, but her hand remained on Elena’s wrist.

The next piece of cover would not lie flat.

Sare raised it with a narrow lever while Sariel held the edge. Altan worked a splinter out from beneath. Elena watched their shoulders strain. She had given them a single piece as though stone could be hurried by an agreement made at a sickbed.

A sharp crack sounded above.

The chair bearer nearest the door ducked. Dust sprang from the wall in a fine pale cloud. One of the lamps guttered, recovered, and burned with a taller flame.

“Keep it level,” Sare said.

Sariel’s boot slipped a little on the dust. She caught herself without dropping the stone. Altan cleared the obstruction and withdrew his hand.

“Now.”

They lowered together.

For a few seconds Elena heard only her own breathing and the sound of Sare passing her brush across the join. The pressure in the room had not lifted. Cassia still leaned forward, struggling through each breath.

Nerio looked at Elena. She shook her head very slightly. She would not give him a result she did not have.

Another shout reached them from the stair.

“Down there! Are you coming?”

Sariel turned toward the voice. “Who is it?”

“One of the gate men. The bar’s caught. They’re getting people out by the wicket.”

“Is the court clear?”

“Clear for you. Come on.”

Elena put a hand beneath Cassia’s elbow.

“We lift now.”

The Censor tried to speak. The first syllable broke. Elena nodded to the bearers and they raised the chair, slowly enough for Cassia to keep her head against its back.

Nerio stooped for the order beneath the seat. Its hard cover had caught against one of the chair’s feet. He tugged; a corner of the paper folded under itself.

“Don’t—” Cassia began.

He stopped, looking up.

“Nerio, don’t pull it. Lift the foot.”

Elena’s hand remained on the Censor’s shoulder. She felt the tremor that went through it after the name.

Nerio stood without the papers.

Cassia opened her eye wider. Her gaze moved from him to Elena, then back to him. She began crying with an anger that made the first tears look like an effort to clear her sight.

He knelt again, raised the chair foot and drew the cover free. This time he brought it up carefully in both hands.

“I have it,” he said.

Cassia reached toward his sleeve. He came close enough for her to catch it. She held him for the space of two breaths, then let go so he could walk beside the chair.

Elena looked toward the grave.

Sare had pressed the final narrow fragment into place. Altan was sweeping the last of the old earth across the seam, his palm turned so that nothing was lost through his fingers. There was no light beneath the stones. Nothing rose from them.

Cassia was still too weak to stand.

“Slowly,” Elena told the bearers. “Mind her feet.”

They carried her out.

The first flight was easy enough. The chair fitted between the walls with a hand’s breadth to spare. Kestra followed behind Nerio, making sure the rear bearer saw the turn before he reached it. Sare came with the lamps. Sariel and Altan remained a few moments longer in the chamber, gathering tools and putting the broken edges of the grave back under the keepers’ cloth.

At the bend Cassia sagged sideways.

Elena caught her before her cheek reached the stone. “Stop.”

The front bearer halted with one foot on the next tread. The chair tilted. Elena moved under Cassia’s near shoulder, taking enough weight to bring her upright, while Nerio braced the back.

“Lower to the landing. Gently.”

There was barely room for all of them. The front bearer backed down until the chair’s feet touched. Elena knelt in the narrow space beside it and felt for the pulse beneath Cassia’s jaw.

It was there, rapid and thin. She opened the Censor’s coat, loosening the collar without disturbing the inner pocket. Cassia’s ordinary seal slid against the wool and struck the chair arm with a small hard knock.

“Can you hear me?” Elena asked.

No answer.

She felt the air at Cassia’s mouth, watched the small movement beneath her ribs, and put two fingers under her chin to keep the head from falling forward. Nerio crouched above her.

“She knew me.”

“Yes.”

“She knew—”

“I heard. Help me keep her upright.”

His hands settled where Elena placed them. She was not angry with him. She had wanted to say the name again herself, as if the pleasure of hearing it might help Cassia’s body catch up.

Behind them the narrow light continued along the stair. Kestra had counted something under her breath and stopped. She watched Elena, waiting to be given work.

“Water on the cloth,” Elena said. “A little.”

Kestra passed it forward. Elena cooled Cassia’s temples and the back of her neck. The Censor stirred, made a weak irritated sound, and tried to push the cloth away.

Elena nearly laughed. She swallowed it, afraid it would become something else.

“We’ll take the next flight more slowly.”

“No,” Cassia murmured.

“You don’t get to hurry it.”

Cassia’s fingers moved against Nerio’s sleeve. He bent his head to hear her. Whatever she said made his face crumple for a moment before he regained it.

“There’s time,” he told her. “You can tell me outside.”

Sariel arrived below with Altan. She saw the chair stopped and remained on the lower step, keeping everyone behind her from crowding into the bend. Elena gave her a quick nod. Sariel returned it and lifted one hand to Sare, asking for patience.

The Censor’s breathing became more regular. Elena waited through several breaths before asking the bearers to lift again.

At the top of the stair the light led them into daylight full of floating dust.

The main barrier had descended crookedly across the public arch.

Its west corner sat on two splintered oak blocks. The east remained higher, held in its guide. Bronze plates had buckled along one edge; bright new wood showed where a facing strip had torn away. A broken pin lay on the paving beneath the upper housing, too small an object to explain the noise Elena had heard below.

The public road could not be used. Through the narrow wicket to the north, people were still moving one at a time. Benjamin stood at the inside turn, helping a limping man through. Yelü received him outside and passed him to two of her own people. Neither looked down toward the stair until the chair reached the court.

Thane was sitting on the lowest step of the upper works. Blood showed through the bandage on his left thumb. He held the damaged collar in the crook of his right arm, two restricted fingers tucked against its side. Lena crouched beside him, trying to untie something at his wrist. He moved his hand away to show her he could do it, then evidently found he could not and offered it back.

Octan walked across the court shouting names. When a worker answered, he pointed the man toward a place where he was needed. His voice sounded hoarse and almost tearful. There were no dead bodies in the public passage that Elena could see. There were too many people sitting down, and one woman holding a child’s arm very still.

She wanted to go to all of them.

Dometta was already there.

The older practitioner came through the service-court door with two helpers and a basket. She looked at Cassia, then at Elena.

“Here first?”

“Yes. Fainted on the turn. Breathing is better now. She spoke his name.”

Dometta glanced at Nerio. He nodded, unable to stop nodding.

They carried the chair into a strip of shade beside the court door. Dometta knelt to examine Cassia while Elena began giving her the changes in order. She got as far as the second cough and realized she was telling too much too quickly.

Dometta put a hand on her forearm.

“I’ve got her. Go and sit down for a moment.”

“There are—”

“I can see them. Sit while I listen to her chest.”

Elena sat on the threshold, facing the court. The stone felt hot through her trousers. She had not realized how cold she had become underground.

A soldier approached with a drawn sword. Her body tightened before she saw that he was using it to cut a strip from a torn awning. He handed the cloth to a woman pressing her palm against a bleeding ear. Then he sheathed the blade and stood looking at Flaccus.

The captain had blood on his lower lip. Yelü was speaking to him through the wicket’s inner opening, each word distinct enough to carry.

“You shut it on the people already inside. You will let the rest through.”

“The Prefect ordered the object secured.”

“The object is below. Those are people in front of you.”

Flaccus looked toward the lower stair. Sare stood there with Altan, their basket and tools between them. Sariel was beside Kestra, who had both hands around her water skin. The line of light on the stair weakened and went out as Elena watched.

The captain called two soldiers over and sent them to assist at the wicket. He did not look at Yelü again.

Benjamin brought the last limping worker into the court. His shirt was torn at one shoulder; the skin beneath seemed whole. He saw Elena on the threshold and made for her, then stopped when Dometta called him to carry a bench.

Elena watched him lift it with one of the attendants. Her eyes filled so abruptly she had to turn her face away.

No one asked her what was wrong.

They did not leave the Gate at once.

Cassia needed rest before the short walk to the Archive. The injured needed washing and binding; several people who had been able to stand through the danger began shaking when someone asked them where they lived. The garrison counted its own men. Octan counted everyone, twice, and became furious when a worker he had marked absent walked in from the lane carrying a drink.

Elena treated Thane’s thumb while he described the pin’s failure in a level, tired voice. He had followed Octan’s signal, released what he could, and let the rest settle onto the blocks. Lena had pulled the seized loop clear in time for the line to run under control. One of the braces had split. It was still in place now, ugly and visibly overburdened.

“Don’t go under it,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

She looked at him.

“Not today,” he added, then rested his head against the wall. “Elena, I am very tired.”

“I know.”

She finished tying the bandage and let him remain against her shoulder for a moment. The ruined fitting lay beside his boot. She had seen him turn it in his hands with such pleasure when he bought it. She wanted to say they would find another and could hear, before speaking, how little that would answer.

“That was a good thing you made,” she said instead.

He shut his eyes. “It was.”

When she went back to Cassia, the Censor was awake. Nerio sat on the ground beside the chair with the papers on his knees. He was reading a sentence aloud, stopping at the punctuation she indicated with one lifted finger.

“You can leave that until tomorrow,” Elena said.

Cassia looked up. “He says the cover is cracked.”

“It is,” Nerio said. “She’s telling me how to repair it.”

Elena stood there for a while, listening to them disagree about glue.

Chapter 23 — Earned Pay

Six days after the burial, Sariel stood outside the Tribunal trying to remove a pale line of mortar from her cuff.

It had survived two washings. Water darkened it and made it disappear for a little while; then the sleeve dried and it came back. Benjamin had offered to cut the cuff away and turn it. She had said no because she liked the lining, and because she was tired of improving things by removing pieces of them.

He stood beside her now, wearing the coat he had brushed for the hearing. The brushing had exposed a thin patch at one elbow. He kept feeling it with his opposite hand.

“You look all right,” she said.

“I wasn’t asking.”

“I know. I wanted you to know anyway.”

He let his hand fall. After a moment he took hers.

The steps were crowded with people who had business at the Gate. Several had been called as witnesses; others had decided that if anyone was answering questions today, they had questions of their own. A wheelwright carried the damaged wheel from the morning of the closure, although the examiner had specifically said that objects need not be brought unless requested. He had rolled it uphill from his yard and was not going to roll it back without showing someone.

Yelü stood near him, talking to a woman whose brother had been detained after the theft. Sarnai had taken the shade beside the west wall. She wore no ceremonial ornaments. The absence made Sariel more conscious of the people who had come with her, each dressed to be recognized in their own right.

The provincial examiner had arrived the previous evening with an instruction suspending Marcellus’s local command pending the inquiry. The captain now acting at the Gate was a man Sariel had barely noticed before, chiefly remembered for always having his helmet under one arm. He looked frightened by how many people wished to speak to him. Octan had caught him at dawn and refused to let him go until he understood the difference between a clear pedestrian lane and a repaired cart passage.

Flaccus was waiting inside to give evidence. Sariel had seen him enter, without sword or cloak. She wondered whether he felt the missing weight, and whether his hand kept going to the place where he expected it to be.

Marcellus arrived by the side street.

The crowd noticed him in parts. First the people nearest, who moved without speaking; then those behind, who looked over their shoulders to see why a gap had opened. He wore a plain dark coat, but the men accompanying him wore the Prefect’s colors. His suspension had not made him walk alone.

He stopped when he saw Sarnai. She inclined her head. He returned the movement precisely, as if they were beginning a meeting he still expected to control.

Then he saw Sariel.

“Your account has been put before mine.”

“I was called this morning.”

“So was I.”

He looked at Benjamin, then at their joined hands. Sariel resisted the impulse to release them.

“The southern road was closed three times the season before I took command,” Marcellus said. “No one has brought the bodies from that year to this hearing.”

Behind him the wheelwright shifted his wheel, making room for a woman to pass. The iron rim scraped the stone.

“I remember the figures you showed us,” Sariel said.

“They were people.”

“Yes.”

The answer seemed to irritate him. He had prepared for her to dismiss them.

“Someone will still have to keep the road open.”

Sariel looked at the step below him, where a wet footprint was drying in the sun. She had once wanted to tell men like this that they misunderstood her. That she understood the useful part, the pressure, the thing they feared losing. She no longer knew what reassurance she hoped to buy by making that plain.

“Damas came to the hearing,” she said. “He’s sitting inside. You can ask him about the road.”

Marcellus’s jaw moved beneath the old scar. He passed her without answering.

Benjamin squeezed her hand once before letting go.

The examiner asked Sariel to begin with the original commission.

She had expected questions about the theft and had prepared the route in her mind: service court, stair, scriptorium, reliquary, window. Instead she found herself explaining Tema’s first visit to Millhaven and the limits of the investigation fee. The examiner wanted dates, who had been present, and what had been said before anyone agreed to carry the Seal away.

Sariel supplied what she remembered. Where she did not remember the exact words, she said so. It was slower than a good performance and left her with fewer chances to choose the shape of the room.

Tema sat two benches behind her. When she reached the later amendment, he rose to produce it from the inner pocket of his coat. The examiner’s clerk unfolded the paper on the table, read the signatures and passed it to his superior.

“You relinquished a share in the route’s earnings?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because we changed the job.”

“To destruction of the object?”

“To restitution. It wasn’t destroyed.”

The examiner waited.

“The keepers have it in the burial. Their access is being agreed separately. We are not authorized to remove it again.”

“By this contract.”

“By us either,” Sariel said, and heard how little legal usefulness the answer possessed.

The examiner wrote something down.

She began describing Rusk’s withdrawal agreement. For the first time Tema looked uncomfortable. Sariel went through the advance, the promised remainder, the armed entry at the change of shift. She gave Corven’s name and where he was being treated. The injured man had agreed to be questioned about the stair and his employer. He had not agreed that the rest of his life was the examiner’s business. Elena had made that distinction very clear before letting anyone into his room.

A clerk asked which charter empowered the company to operate beyond its home town.

Sariel had known the question would come eventually. She put both hands flat on her knees.

“We were hired by Tema in our own names.”

“The Silk Coin?”

“That is the company we came from.”

“Do you hold a surviving charter?”

“No.”

The clerk glanced at the examiner, who turned back to the agreement.

“Then enter them as named contractors for the present matter. No finding concerning a guild charter is sought or made.”

Sariel let out a breath very carefully. Across the room Benjamin was watching her. She could not tell him yet whether she felt relieved or diminished. She was glad, suddenly, that he would ask later rather than decide for her now.

The questions continued. They asked her to show where the chest had been lowered and how many people had passed through the window. They asked why she had not brought the object directly to the Prefect when he offered safe passage.

“Because he meant to use it again.”

“Did he say that?”

“He said the ceremony would resume when it was recovered.”

This time the examiner looked toward Marcellus, who sat upright in the front row. The former Prefect did not move.

Sariel was released from the witness chair before noon. Her back hurt from sitting so straight. At the outer desk Kestra collected their visitors’ paper, extended for another fourteen days under Tema’s continuing bond. She put it back in the front of her working book. The repair had already outgrown their first permission to remain.

The agreement about the burial took most of the afternoon and part of the next morning.

Sariel was not required for all of it. She remained for the passage concerning the keepers because Sare asked her to, and because leaving while Sare argued for a key seemed a poor ending to bringing the Seal back.

The city wanted a guard present at every visit. Altan wanted two visits in each season and admission after a death among the households that kept the place. Sarnai wanted a right of entry that did not disappear whenever the commander changed. The examiner would not grant an unlimited armed approach to the Gate.

Yelü took the disputed draft outside, walked the route with the acting captain, and returned with a practical alteration. The keepers would enter by the service court on named days, with provision for additional visits and a way to challenge a refusal. Their attendants could wait in the eastern lane without blocking traffic. No one would be required to touch the Seal to obtain that access.

Sare read the new wording herself.

“If the lane is blocked by your traffic?” she asked.

“Then the waiting place shifts to the dry yard,” the captain said.

“The yard has another gate.”

“I’ll have it opened.”

“Put that down too.”

He looked toward the examiner. The examiner told the clerk to put it down.

There was no great moment when the room became satisfied. Altan continued to dislike the times of admission; the captain objected to the duty of supplying a key-holder without advance notice after a death. They settled on a response time he believed he could staff. Sare made him read it back. Sarnai insisted on an acknowledgment that the Seal had been taken from a place under the keepers’ care without their assent.

When that sentence was entered, Altan put his hand over Sare’s for a moment. She left it there while she read the next paragraph.

Sariel went out to find Benjamin sitting on the steps with Damas.

The porter was thinner than he had been at the Gate. His coat hung loosely over his shoulders. He had a small paper packet open on one knee and was offering Benjamin something from it.

“Celia says I buy them when I’m worried,” he told Sariel. “I’ve apparently bought enough to worry a very large household.”

They were sugared seeds, stuck together in clumps. She took one and sat on the step below them.

“Luci dislikes the hard ones,” Damas said. “Pavo likes nothing else.”

He looked at the packet, then away. Benjamin kept chewing. Sariel did too.

After a while Damas asked whether there were still soldiers in the market lane. He had to go home by it, and he did not want another conversation about where he ought to have been working.

“I’ll walk that way,” Benjamin said.

“I’m not frightened.”

“All right.”

Damas folded the packet closed, slowly, getting the corners even. When he stood, he waited for Benjamin.

Tema paid the balance that evening in the room above the harness seller.

The window was open, and warm leather, hoof grease and the sharp smell of shaved horn came up from the shop below. The five sat around a table too small for the papers and the supper Tema had ordered. He moved the bread onto the window ledge so Kestra could spread the amended agreement flat.

He counted one hundred and eighty marks into the common purse. Kestra checked the sum, marked the completed payment beside the earlier advance, and gave him his signed copy. The sixty marks for investigation had been settled before the theft. No profit share remained to calculate. Sariel had been doing that arithmetic involuntarily for days, imagining a small regular payment arriving in Millhaven while they were occupied elsewhere. It stopped now, and she missed it more than she expected.

Tema put the separate packet for Lena beside her place. Thirty marks, earned by the work at the Gate. Lena opened it, counted without hurrying, and divided the coins between two pockets. The earlier forty had been paid before she accepted the second job; no part of her remained on account.

Thane had been watching the harness seller’s boy through the open stair door. The boy was working a new hole in a strap with a tool too large for it. Thane began to rise, thought better of it, and sat down again.

Tema followed his gaze.

“He’ll learn.”

“He could learn with a smaller awl.”

“Tell him after you’ve eaten.”

Thane accepted that. Elena pushed the warm dish within reach of his left hand, then took some for herself without checking whether everyone else had been served.

It was beans cooked with a little smoked meat and more onion than Sariel usually liked. The onion had become sweet enough that she found herself scraping the dish for the last soft pieces. Lena ate slowly, listening more than speaking. When Tema’s shopkeeper came upstairs to ask who had ordered wine, she accepted a cup and paid for her own second one.

Benjamin described the first time he had tried to mend a harness. He made no claim to having succeeded. Thane listened with increasing distress, interrupted once to ask which end of the strap Benjamin meant, and finally put his face in his good hand.

“It held until we reached home,” Benjamin said.

“Ben, the horse was holding it together.”

Benjamin laughed, and after a moment Thane did too. Sariel watched Lena smile into her cup. It changed her face enough to show how young she was, then changed it back when she noticed Sariel watching.

Sariel looked away and took another piece of bread.

Lena left before the dishes were cleared. Sariel went downstairs a little later and found her in the open shop, speaking to the boy about a needle case. He brought out two; she chose the plainer and paid without bargaining.

Outside, the evening had cooled the paving. Somewhere near the baths a man was singing badly to someone who occasionally joined in. Lena tucked the needle case into an inner pocket and settled her bag higher on her shoulder.

“Do you have a road?” Sariel asked.

“For the first part.”

Sariel nodded.

She had thought of several things to say over supper. That they were glad she had come. That there would be work in Millhaven if she wanted it. That they had known her as a child and would have looked for her if they had known where to begin. One by one the sentences had revealed what they asked from her.

“I remember the small court behind the Coin,” Lena said.

Sariel turned toward her.

“The kitchen court?”

“No. Where the broken chairs went. There was a place behind the water butt. You could sit there without anyone knowing.”

“Yes.”

“Kes knew. He brought me half a pear once and told me he needed the other half back if I didn’t eat it.”

Sariel remembered the pear trees outside the guild’s reach, the arguments when fruit came in bruised, Kes returning from an errand with things he did not explain. She had not known about the water butt.

“He did that,” she said.

Lena looked down the lane. “I used to think he couldn’t find me if I kept my feet in.”

They stood with that for a while.

“What happened to the court?” Lena asked.

“After the fire? I don’t know what they did with it.”

“I haven’t been back.”

“Neither have I.”

Lena adjusted the bag again. “I don’t want to tonight.”

“I know.”

Sariel meant to say more, but Lena looked at her, and this time she understood where the conversation ended.

“Thank you for the line,” she said instead.

Lena’s hand went briefly to the pocket containing the new needle case.

“You paid me.”

“Yes.”

The girl considered her for a moment, then gave her a small nod. She went toward the baths, where the two bad singers had begun another song.

Sariel returned upstairs. Benjamin had saved her a little of the beans in a separate bowl and was trying to keep Tema from setting a cup in it.

Chapter 24 — The First Cart

The woman with the geese arrived before the men who were supposed to keep the lane clear.

Elena found her sitting on the front of a two-wheeled cart, reins tucked beneath her thigh, eating an apple. The birds were covered by a loose woven sheet. From beneath it came an occasional soft complaint, answered each time by the woman without looking down.

“No, you’re quite all right.”

Elena put her treatment bag on the bench beside the service-court door. The bench had been moved into the sun so the washed wood could dry. She laid a clean cloth over one end and began setting out what Dometta had asked her to bring.

“You aren’t taking them through yet,” a young guard told the woman.

“I can see that.”

“The lane needs to be clear.”

“It is clear behind me.”

He went to find someone senior enough to make her move. She finished her apple and held the core out beneath the sheet. A beak took it neatly. Elena smiled before turning away.

They were opening the road to carts that morning. Pedestrians had passed through the wicket since the danger ended, ducking beneath a temporary board at one point and complaining about the width at another. The main barrier had been lifted into a new support and secured against descent while the west guide was rebuilt. For now, passage would be controlled by removable timber bars and men who would have to stand in the rain to move them. Octan had obtained their wages in writing before agreeing to the arrangement.

The bronze plates looked less grand at eye level. Several had been taken down and laid against the wall. One bore a long scratch through an old victory figure’s face. A worker had covered it with sacking so no one would cut a hand on the curled edge.

Elena knelt beside the water bucket and tested its temperature. Still warm. Dometta had sent a boy ahead with it wrapped in a blanket; she would have to thank him when he came back for the blanket. She had forgotten his name and disliked herself for making the comparison that immediately followed.

She took out the small jar of salve and found a note folded beneath it.

Not all of it. You still have to travel.

Dometta’s writing leaned so far to the right that it seemed impatient with the edge of the paper. Elena folded the note and put it in her coat. There was something sustaining about being cared for by a woman who expected to argue with her again.

Across the court a muffled voice called from beneath the open housing.

“Octan. The thin one.”

The engineer looked at the two strips in his hand, selected one and crouched.

“The other thin one,” Thane said.

“This is the one you marked.”

“I marked the face, not the edge. Turn it.”

Octan turned it, looked, and passed the other strip under the housing. He stayed crouched, watching the work instead of pretending he had always intended to do that.

Thane had been permitted to work only after Elena checked his thumb. He had been very clear that permission was a condition of her continued help, not a new authority over his body. She had been very clear that he was making an objection she had not raised. They had sat for a while afterward, each annoyed enough to want the other to speak first. In the end he had asked whether she could put the bandage knot farther from the tool’s grip, and she had done so.

Now his boots moved against the paving as he pushed himself farther beneath the housing. His tool roll lay open on the left side, where he could reach it. The ruined collar was gone. Its pieces had been left with Pellia, who wanted to see how the pin had torn before agreeing what she would change in the next one.

Benjamin stood near the public arch, walking the cleared lane with a broom. He was not particularly good at sweeping; he kept stopping to move larger pieces by hand. Sariel followed him with a basket for the debris and made no effort to improve his method. They were talking about something Elena could not hear. Sariel laughed once, openly, and leaned her shoulder into his before moving past to collect a wedge of broken timber.

Kestra sat on an upturned crate beside the wicket. She had a sheet across her knees, checking the names of those still owed for the days of detention. This was an ordinary paid task for the inquiry clerk. She had accepted it after making sure the money would come from the office and not from the people waiting to be paid. When one man tried to tell her his entire account of the closure, she listened for a while, then pointed him toward the clerk authorized to take it. He went with visible reluctance.

The morning held more people than Elena had expected. Some wanted to see the first cart through. Others needed to bring things home before another afternoon passed. A baker had sent a boy with a tray balanced on his head. The bathhouse porter came for a load of fuel. Two women carrying rolled bedding asked whether the household admission times had changed with the command, and neither of the guards knew.

The acting captain arrived carrying his helmet. Octan caught him before he could put it on.

“We aren’t ready.”

“I haven’t said you are.”

“I want you to tell them.”

The captain looked at the gathering beyond the arch and went to speak to it. Elena appreciated him for doing it himself.

The first injury was a splinter.

A boy had helped lift a packing board without waiting for the men at the other end. He came to Elena holding out his palm, more offended than frightened. His mother followed at a distance, carrying two bundles and refusing to be hurried.

“He said he knew how,” she told Elena.

The boy turned his head. “I did know how.”

Elena took his wrist gently. “Let me see how deep it went.”

He let her look, keeping his mouth shut against anything his mother might mistake for crying. The splinter was broad and shallow. She washed the skin, lifted its exposed end with the tip of her fine tweezers, and eased it free. He watched with an expression of interest that survived the sting.

“Can I have it?”

“Yes. Don’t put it in someone.”

His mother set down one bundle long enough to give Elena a small coin. Elena accepted it. The boy carried the splinter away on his open palm, suddenly very careful of where he stepped.

Dometta reached the court as they left.

“I thought you’d have a queue.”

“Only one so far.”

“Good.”

She set a folded cloth on the bench and sat beside it. For a little while neither spoke. They watched a worker climb down from the upper rail with his boots over one shoulder, having been sent barefoot along a freshly dressed surface. The young guard tried to stop him stepping into the public lane. He pointed at the debris beneath the guard’s own feet until the man looked down and moved.

“How is Cassia?” Elena asked.

“Awake. Cross about being tired. Asked for a book too heavy to hold up.”

“Did you give it to her?”

“Nerio found a stand.”

Elena pictured the two of them in the office, the Censor’s head inclined toward the text, the young man’s hand holding a place when she looked away. She would miss being able to walk there and find out whether the name had stayed. It had stayed for days now. Her worry had not learned to take the evidence calmly.

Elena leaned back against the warm stone. There was a soreness beneath one shoulder blade from the ascent, less sharp each morning. She stretched until it eased.

Dometta watched her without comment. When Elena was settled again, she opened the folded cloth and offered her a plum, dried flat and dusted with a little flour to keep it from sticking.

Elena took one. It was tart enough to wake the sides of her mouth. They ate slowly while the geese complained under their sheet.

The dispute over the first cart began as soon as the captain said they could begin considering passage.

The woman with the geese had been there first. A carter carrying fresh plaster said the contents of his wagon would spoil. The bathhouse porter said he had a permit dated three days earlier. A man whose load was wrapped too carefully for anyone to identify insisted that he held precedence under an agreement with the previous Prefect.

The captain asked to see it.

The man spent so long searching his coat that Sariel walked over to the goose cart and asked its owner whether she could back six feet without disturbing the birds.

“I can back,” the woman said. “I won’t leave.”

“No one is asking you to leave. We need the angle.”

The woman looked ahead. The temporary posts narrowed the turn inside the arch. A cart entering too close to the north wall would have to straighten beside the new support. She studied the space, clicked her tongue to the horse, and backed with such neatness that Benjamin stopped sweeping to watch.

Sariel thanked her. The woman nodded without forgiving the delay.

Under the housing, Thane called for everyone to stand clear of the foot of the guide. Octan repeated the instruction more loudly. The workers lifted the temporary support a little, checked the packing, and lowered it again. Nothing fell. Thane came out on his back, pushing with his heels, and sat up slowly.

Elena saw his hand go to the bandaged thumb. He took it away before she could decide whether to rise.

“Let me look,” Octan said.

Thane moved aside. The engineer knelt where he had been and inspected the join himself. When he stood, he rubbed the dust from his hands and looked up the length of the guide.

“One cart,” he told the captain. “Then we check it again.”

The captain gave the order to move the timber bars.

A cheer started somewhere outside and died when no one else joined it. Most of the waiting people were busy making sure their own reins, loads and children were ready.

Dometta stood to help a woman whose bandage had loosened. Elena went to the bucket and washed the last of the salve from her hands. She had been using the ointment sparingly, as the note requested, but it clung between her fingers. She worked soap into the creases, thinking of the jars in her own room in Millhaven and the woman covering her morning appointments. She would have several things to ask when she returned. She hoped the answers would not begin with an apology.

A warm smell reached her before the person carrying it.

“Elena.”

Damas stood beside the bench with a small loaf in a paper wrapper. Its crust had split along one side, showing a pale steaming seam. He held it out.

“Celia sent this.”

“My hands are wet. Put it there, please.”

He looked at the bench. “That’s where you had the boy’s hand.”

“On a cloth. It’s clean now.”

He did not put the bread down. She lifted her hands above the bucket and looked for the towel. Dometta had taken it to the woman with the loose dressing.

“I’ll have it in a moment,” Elena said.

Damas broke a piece from the loaf, keeping it inside the paper, and offered the exposed edge toward her. She hesitated. He looked suddenly uncertain, as if she might explain another rule he had forgotten.

She leaned forward and took a bite.

It was too hot. She straightened quickly, breathing through her mouth, and Damas began to laugh. She tried to tell him not to, then laughed herself, swallowing before the bread was ready to be swallowed. He held out the loaf again when she could breathe.

“Wait this time,” he said.

She waited. The crust smelled of the oven floor, warm flour and a little smoke. Celia had rubbed oil into the top while it was hot. Elena could taste it on her lips.

Across the lane Sariel looked up.

For a moment her face grew still, then softened. She lifted two fingers from the cart rail in a small acknowledgment.

Elena turned to see who stood beyond her. A woman in a plain brown coat was moving between the waiting carriers. Someone stepped across with a sack; when he passed, the woman had gone. There was only the crowd shifting to make room for the first cart.

“Who was that?” Damas asked.

“A friend,” Elena said, and was not certain Sariel or Lena would have chosen the word.

She did not correct it.

The goose cart started forward. Its owner walked beside the horse with the reins held loosely, murmuring to him as she had murmured to the birds. Benjamin stood inside the turn to guide the near wheel clear of the temporary post.

“Keep coming,” he said. “A little toward me.”

The cart moved. Under the woven sheet the birds objected all at once.

“Easy. Easy.”

The wheel reached the bend and stopped against a raised joint in the old paving. The horse leaned into the traces. Benjamin held up a hand before the driver asked it to pull harder.

Sariel was already crossing the lane.

Elena took the loaf from Damas in its paper wrapper, her hands dry enough now. She stood beside him while Sariel put her shoulder against the cart and Benjamin took the wheel low, where he could lift without catching his fingers against the post.

“Together,” he said.

The driver eased the horse forward. Sariel pushed. Benjamin lifted. The wheel came over the joint, turned once, and carried the cart through into the sun.
