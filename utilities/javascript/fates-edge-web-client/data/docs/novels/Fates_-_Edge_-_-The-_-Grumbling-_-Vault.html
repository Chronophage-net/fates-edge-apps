The Countess’s Irregulars — Book Two

Part I — A respectable errand

Chapter 1 — The deceased objects

The cabinet came down with a noise like a horse breaking its legs.

Daska got her shoulder beneath it. Gilt tore through her sleeve. Pello hung from the cornice with a folded paper between his teeth and two fingers caught beneath the brass molding. The auctioneer stopped shouting for the watch and began shouting about the cabinet.

Rosceline had nearly finished with its owner.

“You see what happens,” she told him, “when people are obliged to hurry.”

Master Arves stood red to the ears beside an open display case. In his hand was a letter he had never seen before Rosceline found it in his pocket. It purported to offer his late aunt’s pearls to a purchaser in another city. The pearls had disappeared that morning. Rosceline knew where they were. She had moved the little velvet tray into the drawer below the case while admiring the clasp.

They would be found. The difficulty of explaining the letter would last longer.

Behind her, Briska crawled beneath the cabinet with a short iron bar.

“When I say lift, take the weight straight up. The foot’s turned.”

“I am lifting,” Daska said. “Choose another word.”

The auction-room smelled of wet wool, beeswax and money being spent reluctantly. Rain struck the high windows. Every time the door opened, a colder smell entered from the street, carrying horse dung and the greasy smoke of the chestnut seller’s brazier. Rosceline had dressed for a prosperous widow’s errand: dark blue, plain rings, a narrow band of white at her throat. The expression required less preparation. She had known several widows who had earned it.

Arves looked past her. “Those are my men.”

“Your cabinet. My men. And women. I find the distinction matters when something lands on them.”

She laid a hand on his sleeve and turned him toward the auctioneer’s clerk. The young man held a list of the missing objects as if it might excuse him from finding them. A spot of ink had reached the skin above his collar.

“You were about to ask Master Arves where he acquired the key to this case.”

“It’s my aunt’s collection!”

“Yes, you have been very clear about that. Were the pearls specifically left to you?”

The clerk looked at his list.

Rosceline let him. Across the room, Ilaro Selci sat at a small desk beneath an unlit lamp, ostensibly comparing the cabinet’s valuation with the old catalog. He had put a finger beside the description of a repair to its cornice. A paper could remain hidden for forty years if everyone thought the compartment contained a piece of decorative wood. He had found the carpenter’s charge for making that compartment. Briska had found the spring.

The lifting jack had then slid on the polished floor.

A bright metal crack reached Rosceline through Arves’s objections. She did not turn. Neither did Arves; she had asked about his aunt’s second will.

“There was no second will.”

“Then you should welcome a search. Really, Master Arves, you need not raise your voice. Your aunt’s friends are listening very carefully already.”

Three women on the bench beside the door had stopped pretending to examine their purchases. One took her gloves off, slowly, to hear better.

“Now,” Briska said behind them.

Daska made a deep, involuntary sound. Wood scraped stone. Pello’s boots hit the floor, followed by his body. He rolled clear with the folded survey still in his teeth. The cabinet settled onto the blocks Briska had thrust beneath it. Its doors swung open. An empty shelf fell out.

The clerk winced.

“An old repair,” Rosceline assured him.

“It wasn’t old when your people did it.”

She considered him with greater interest. He had looked timid when they entered. Beneath the ink and damp hair there appeared to be somebody capable of keeping his place in an argument.

“Then we shall have to discuss an allowance.”

Daska had a strip of gilt veneer embedded in her sleeve. She pulled it free. Six weeks ago an arm wound had kept her out of a dance she had complained about for the next ten days. She flexed that arm now, testing the shoulder, and looked toward Rosceline with a patience that was going to end soon.

Rosceline produced the collection order.

The cabinet had been sold that morning to a buyer who lived in a house the auctioneer admired. The buyer existed. She had paid for the cabinet. She had also agreed, for a consideration, to send it to Briska’s workshop before taking delivery. All the parts of the transaction were respectable except the letter, the missing pearls and the reason they wanted the cabinet emptied.

“If Master Arves disputes the sale,” Rosceline said, “we can leave it here and send someone from the purchaser’s household to explain the inconvenience.”

The auctioneer had reached them. He took the order, recognized the seal and swallowed the first thing he intended to say.

“The damage must be assessed.”

“Naturally. By somebody who saw it standing before it fell. Your clerk has been admirably attentive.”

The clerk lifted his eyes. Rosceline gave him her best acknowledgment of professional worth. It had bought her more time than money ever had.

Pello stood beside the cabinet, left hand tucked beneath his other arm. The paper was gone from his mouth. There was a pale new band around the upper part of his forefinger. He watched the clerk writing down a figure and looked away before Rosceline could read his opinion of it.

Ilaro came from the little desk with the catalog shut.

“They’re charging for the shelf twice,” he said.

The auctioneer opened his mouth.

Ilaro pointed to the clerk’s first entry. “It was already listed as detached. That’s how you described it when you sold it.”

The clerk looked, scratched out a line and kept writing.

Rosceline paid. She felt better about the expense after watching the auctioneer’s face.

Getting the cabinet through the door required two porters, both regulars, and Daska taking the front corner while Briska held the damaged base together. Pello walked ahead to clear the stairs. Rosceline stayed beside Arves until the last leg had crossed the threshold.

“I’m sure this will be cleared up,” she said.

“You put that letter in my coat.”

It was almost a pleasure to be accused correctly.

“I shouldn’t repeat that in front of your aunt’s friends. They have enough to talk about.”

She took her umbrella from the clerk. Rain beaded on its polished handle. Behind him, an attendant opened the drawer below the pearl case and exclaimed.

Rosceline went out before the discovery improved anyone’s temper.

The cabinet was roped to a cart. Pello had already wrapped the stolen survey in oilcloth beneath his coat. Daska stood under the shop’s awning, squeezing rain from her torn sleeve.

“Pearls found?” she asked.

“Exactly where I said they would be.”

“He still has the letter.”

Rosceline looked back through the window. Arves was trying to speak to all three women at once. He had dismissed a servant for spilling wine while they waited for the sale to begin. The servant was now holding the pearl tray and enjoying a better view.

“Yes,” she said.

Daska took the umbrella out of her hand and held it over Briska, who had knelt in the rain to lash the cabinet’s split foot.

They had discussed the letter before entering. Daska had disliked it then as well. Rosceline preferred disagreements that did not require another explanation.

Pello gave the carter their destination. Ilaro climbed onto the seat beside him and sat with his eyes shut. Beneath the awning, Briska wiped her hands on the last clean part of her coat.

“The jack needs wider feet.”

“The cabinet needed fewer legs,” Daska said. “I’m having lunch before we move it again.”

Rosceline folded the collection order into her purse. The rain had ceased. Across the street, the chestnut seller lifted his pan and shook a small bright fall of embers into the gutter.

She bought five paper cones and one extra for the clerk, who would be there for hours explaining things that were not his fault.

Chapter 2 — A useful little stone

The Countess inspected the survey before asking about the cabinet.

Rosceline appreciated that. Her employer had the ability to distinguish between an object she wanted and the damage required to obtain it, provided the damage occurred in someone else’s house.

“Our purchaser is expecting delivery tomorrow,” Serafine said.

“She shall have it. With a stronger base than she bought.”

Briska made a small sound. Rosceline passed her the plate of almonds.

They sat in a room overlooking an enclosed garden, where rain hung on the leaves of a lemon tree. Celise had placed a low stool beside Ilaro’s chair, within reach of his notebook. She had moved a vase before Daska sat down. The Countess wore a fine dark veil and a coat whose lining showed briefly at the wrist, red as the inside of a cut fig.

“There is a stone beneath an abandoned Aeler installation,” she said. “It was used to separate linked protections when repairs were required. I want it brought here.”

“In working condition,” Briska said.

“With its charge intact.”

Celise put a square carrying case on the table. Inside, in a nest of gray felt, lay a pale stone capsule no longer than Rosceline’s hand. Its two halves fitted together along a stepped seam. There was no ornament except a shallow groove for a retaining band.

Briska reached toward it, stopped and looked at the Countess.

“Empty,” Serafine said. “No working in the case or the capsule. You may examine both.”

Briska lifted the upper half. The inner recess had six unequal faces.

“This was made for a particular object.”

“From an old fitting drawing. One survived separately from the survey.”

“An old drawing isn’t an old fitting.”

“That is one reason I am employing you.”

Rosceline watched Briska turn the capsule in the light. The artisan had been prepared to be patronized and had received a useful answer instead. It left her with nothing to correct immediately.

The Countess spread a second sheet beside the survey. It showed a narrow black stone held in a collar, its tip just clear of a mounting.

“The Keystone of Unmaking,” she said.

Ilaro looked at the drawing. “A rather broad advertisement.”

“The installation gave it a broad reach. Carried separately, it should interrupt connected wards across a room. Once, with the stored charge it holds. After that it requires apparatus nobody in Silkstrand is known to possess.”

“And nobody is going to try it before paying us?” Briska asked.

“Nobody in my house.”

“How does it discharge?”

“Contact with a working, outside its capsule. There is a release on the collar. I have had those instructions read by two people, independently.”

Pello had been looking at the case handle. He set two fingers beneath it and lifted the empty case just clear of the table.

“Wet?” he asked.

“The capsule tolerates water,” Celise said. “We tested the closure. Dry the outer case before putting it away.”

He lowered it and nodded.

The Countess put a narrow strip of blue paper beside the drawings. There was a seal at one end and a fragment of red wax clinging to the other, as if it had been torn off something in a hurry.

“Three nights ago someone went into one of my storehouses without opening a door. The woman sleeping above it came down to find the inner protections folded away from the stairs. She did not lose anything.”

“Somebody wanted her to notice,” Ilaro said.

“Somebody wanted me to notice.”

Rosceline touched the edge of the strip. Its fibers had been cut into tiny regular slits. The labor of preparing it was almost more disagreeable than the threat.

“A demonstration?”

“An inferior one. Bought from a man who is now offering access to the original instrument.”

“And charging for the time until he sells it to somebody else.”

The Countess did not answer that. In the garden a servant shook water from a canvas cover. The drops pattered against the sill like scattered seed.

“Vasko Rell,” Celise said. “Salvage broker. Surface leaseholder of the old counting-house and approach. He bought a claim to what lies beneath it. Several families dispute the sale.”

“With weapons?” Daska asked.

“Through correspondence, so far.”

“How long has he held it?” Ilaro asked.

“Eleven weeks.”

“And he hasn’t taken the stone.”

“No.”

Ilaro put the number beside the man’s name. He did not supply a conclusion for the room’s benefit.

Briska leaned over the survey. “Who can close this door from outside?”

“I have no current account of the gate.”

“We’ll get one.” She looked at Pello. He had already turned the sheet to examine the slope above the entrance.

They were offered fifteen hundred marks between them, with road expenses paid from a separate purse. The survey theft had been an additional commission and would be settled that afternoon. Rosceline named a larger advance. The Countess considered the damaged cabinet and gave her half the difference.

Their guide was waiting at the foothill inn.

“Durin Stoneshield,” Serafine said. “He lived at the works before they were abandoned. His references are uneven.”

“How uneven?” Daska asked.

Celise consulted a sheet. “The first calls him a man of exceptional historical knowledge. The second recommends paying his account before discussing the first.”

Briska took an almond. “Has anybody asked him where he worked?”

“He supplied three ranks,” Celise said. “They appeared to increase with the length of the interview.”

“Send the smallest one,” Rosceline said. “We have enough to carry.”

The Countess let the smile reach her mouth. It remained there while she repeated the terms: the genuine stone, charged, in the fitted capsule; payment on delivery; no obligation to proceed if the installation proved impassable, although expenses would have to be accounted for. Rell could not be promised a private evening in her house. Rosceline was disappointed that this had required saying.

When the others had taken the drawings downstairs, the Countess opened another book.

Rosceline stayed where she was.

Sarem’s lesson-book had been mended. A narrow piece of new cloth supported the failing blue spine without covering it. She ran a fingertip along the edge of the repair. No stitches entered the old writing.

“The woman downstairs?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“She does good work.”

The Countess turned to a different surviving mark. Rosceline touched it.

“There is no need,” Sarem said, “to look so pleased with yourself.”

A pause. Then his laugh, unexpectedly close, breaking on the breath he had tried to hold.

The sound stopped. Outside, the servant began folding the wet canvas. Rosceline kept her finger beside the mark. She remembered nothing of the argument that had made him laugh. She wanted it back with a violence that would have embarrassed her had there been anyone in the room she trusted with the sight.

“Three,” the Countess said after a while.

“I can count.”

“I know.”

Rosceline took out the agreement bearing the first completed recovery. She placed it on the table without opening it.

“The vault is the next.”

“If you accept it.”

She turned the book slightly so its spine rested flat. The laugh would be the same next time. Whatever she brought back, whoever went with her, it would end at the same place.

“I accept.”

Downstairs, Daska was speaking to the singer who had once waited there with only half her stage paint on. They were arguing pleasantly about shoes. Pello stood by the door with the empty case; Briska had taken the capsule out again to check something against the drawing.

Rosceline thanked Celise for the almonds and took her place in the company.

“We shall need boots,” she said. “I understand the mountains have made very little provision for our comfort.”

Chapter 3 — What the map leaves out

Ilaro had three versions of the mountain and trusted the ugliest.

The first was a perspective drawing of a handsome doorway beneath a ridge. Little trees had been arranged to improve the approach. The second was the survey they had taken from the cabinet. The third consisted of greasy amendments pasted over the survey, with their edges lifted where damp had entered the glue.

He put a knife under the lowest corner and stopped when the old paper resisted.

“Steam?” Briska asked.

“Perhaps. Not yet.”

The workshop shutters were closed against the white morning. Briska had taken her hammer downstairs. From the yard came a furious woman’s voice, a pause, and Pello saying something too quietly to distinguish. His relative had arrived with the cart they would hire. She appeared to believe its rate improved every time he called her cousin.

Ilaro shifted the small lamp so its light crossed the paper sideways.

The paste marks overlapped. Each amendment had been made after something failed: a gutter added, a stair struck out, a low room marked with a warning he could not read. The polished original showed no need for any of these things. Whoever commissioned it had purchased a mountain with no maintenance.

Below the low room, a stair descended to a row of doorways drawn in another hand. No measurements accompanied them.

“My parent brought back rubbings of doors like those,” Briska said. “The giants’ work stopped above them.”

“Another way out?”

“No levels. No section. You cannot make an exit out of a row of rectangles.”

Ilaro put a question mark beside the stair and returned to the surveyed rooms.

He liked the surveyor better in the later additions. The same careful hand had grown cramped, the ink cheaper. A little line showed where the service walk had been supported after part of its original mounting broke. Beside it was an expense for three men and a mule.

“It was used,” he said.

Briska came around the bench. She had the capsule open and a smear of gray dust on her cheek.

“They didn’t just build a ceremonial way into the ceiling. Somebody paid to keep this walk standing.”

“When?”

He pointed at the cramped date. “Before our guide was born, probably. Or before his grandfather was born. I don’t know what he means by old.”

“Neither does he, if Celise’s letters are fair.”

Ilaro traced the walk with the blunt end of his pencil. It ran from the outer hall, past a store recess, through the narrow inner throat and up to a shutter on the mountain side. A separate heavy line joined the four columns in the last chamber. He could not tell whether it represented a conduit or a structural tie.

Briska looked at the end of it.

“Regulator, perhaps.”

“Can we get out there?”

“If it still joins what this says it joins.”

He marked the outside mouth with a ring. Pello would have to see it. The mountain in the paper was obedient; it stayed still while he considered it. He had learned to distrust that advantage.

Downstairs the woman laughed, sharply. A cart shaft struck the wall.

Ilaro put his pencil down. He had been about to ask Briska something about the four columns. The question departed with the noise, leaving the unpleasant certainty that it had been necessary. He read his last two lines. Briska waited, turning the capsule’s upper half in her hands.

“The mounting,” he said at last. “Can we take the stone out without using it?”

“If the drawing’s accurate. There’s a retaining pin. I want a hook small enough to get behind it.”

“Make two.”

“I’m making three.”

She went back to her work. He wrote the question down even though she had answered it.

Pello’s cousin came upstairs wearing a green scarf and carrying a length of frayed harness. She put it on the bench as evidence.

“Your people return things like this.”

Pello remained by the door. “That was Enzo.”

“Your people.”

“He can pay you.”

“He has paid me. I want you to look at it before you borrow mine.”

Pello looked at the harness. He took it up and brought it nearer the window to see where the stitching had pulled. His cousin’s face softened against her wishes.

“I brought the mule that doesn’t bite,” she said.

He looked up.

“Often. Don’t feed her from your sleeve.”

She inspected their cases, tested a strap with her thumbnail and took the advance. When she called him Canaletti, it sounded like something she had found on the bottom of a shoe. At the stair she kissed the side of his head hard enough to move his cap.

“Write to your mother. A note written after you get back does not make her stop worrying before you leave.”

He waited until she was downstairs before putting the cap straight.

Ilaro gave him the survey. “This outside opening. I need to know what it looks like, not what you think it does.”

Pello studied it.

“Rungs, height, what the bars are fixed into,” Ilaro said. “Any water marks. And somewhere to fasten a rope.”

Pello folded the page only on its old creases and slid it into a flat leather packet. “I’ll draw it.”

On the next bench Briska had made two identical outer cases. The Countess’s original stood at the back with its handle raised. The new one had a slightly warmer color on its hinges. Briska rubbed a dirty cloth across them until the difference disappeared.

“That is comforting,” Rosceline said from the doorway. “I had begun to fear we were going to carry off the object everybody expected.”

She brought food and a narrow folded letter. Nobody took the letter before asking what it was.

“An introduction to Master Rell. We are conducting an assessment for a collector who dislikes paying for legends before finding out whether the legend fits through his door.”

“Which collector?” Ilaro asked.

“A real one. He has agreed to be interested for ten days.”

“After that?”

“He has a wedding to attend.”

Ilaro took the letter. The clerk’s hand was formal; the seal had been applied carelessly. An open triangle showed where the wax had not reached the bottom of the die.

“Who paid the clerk?”

“Celise. Through the house account.”

He made a note to ask how discreet the clerk’s office was. Daska opened the food parcel, and a smell of hot onions entered the room. For a while there was only the practical question of keeping grease off the drawings.

Briska demonstrated the false core after they had eaten. It was black pottery weighted with lead, heavy enough to feel right through a closed case. The true capsule would travel inside the duplicate outer case. When they reached the mounting, the capsule could come out, take the stone and go into Pello’s body sling. The dummy would occupy the same felt cradle.

“Anyone who opens it will know,” she said.

Rosceline examined the duplicate catch. “Then we must provide a reason for them to open it later.”

“A magical examination will know without opening it.”

“You make my work sound difficult.”

Briska held out her hand for the case. “It is.”

Pello put the empty capsule into his sling and tightened the straps beneath his coat. He bent, climbed onto the bench, stepped down. Nothing knocked against his ribs. Briska tried to pull it loose and could not. She looked satisfied for the first time that morning.

The jack lay beside them with wider feet fitted to its base. Its screw turned cleanly. Ilaro watched Briska put her hand over the mark where the side brace had begun to bow beneath the cabinet.

“I won’t promise a sideways load,” she said.

“We’ll avoid dropping a mountain on its edge,” Daska told her.

Ilaro opened his notebook to the page with the clerk’s name. He had written who paid and nothing beneath it. Rosceline was eating the last onion pastry. He asked her again, and this time wrote the answer before she began explaining why it would be quite all right.

Chapter 4 — An officer of considerable distinction

They reached the foothills on the fifth evening, with the mule in better spirits than any of them.

Briska had spent most of the last mile watching the buildings change. Cut stone rose through plaster. Door lintels widened; chimneys crouched against the wind. The road crossed a stream that ran too quickly for anybody to persuade it through a city pipe. Above the inn, black firs stood on a slope patched with the last dirty snow.

The sign showed a red mountain goat with a barrel between its horns. Someone had improved its expression each time the wood was repainted. It now appeared furious about the arrangement.

Durin Stoneshield occupied the bench beneath it.

He had a beard the color of old rope, a mail shirt that failed to meet below his belly, and a hammer propped against his knee. The hammer was beautiful. Its head had been forged with four shallow faces and a seam of bright metal through the eye. The handle was too smooth where no one had gripped it in years.

“You’re late,” he told Briska.

“We were told before dark.”

“It’s dark in the vault already.”

He appeared pleased with this and reached for a cup beside him.

The innkeeper came out carrying a bowl. “Eat first.”

“A man cannot eat on command.”

“You’ve been doing it without command since noon.”

She put the bowl on his lap and went to the cart. Pello handed her the carrier’s note. She read it, looked at the mule and called someone to take the harness off before discussing money. Briska liked her.

Durin regarded the jack protruding from Briska’s bag.

“Lowland work.”

“Mine.”

“That explains the feet.”

“They’re for bearing on damaged stone.”

“Good stone doesn’t damage.”

She looked up at the mountain, where half a cliff had fallen into the streambed.

Durin followed her glance and ate a spoonful from the bowl. “Weather’s changed.”

They left the cart and the original empty outer case in the inn’s locked shed. Pello checked the door before giving the keeper a coin for the room. They would go on with packs at dawn; the mule stayed. Briska had no wish to test the claim that this was the one that did not bite while carrying it over a scree slope.

Inside, the common room smelled of cabbage, wet leather and resin burning green in the hearth. Daska stood near the fire, slowly extending the knee she had kept bent on the cart. Ilaro had gone upstairs at once. Rosceline was choosing between two rooms by the position of their windows and pretending it was about sleep.

Briska put her tools on a table and asked Durin about the door.

He answered by describing a battle.

There had been twelve enemies in the first account. By the time the innkeeper brought bread, they held a ridge. His hammer had struck a helmet so hard the man inside had forgotten his allegiance. Briska examined the handle. A small dark crescent near the head might have been blood, pitch or a stain added at leisure.

“The mounting on the bronze door,” she said. “Top bearing or bottom?”

“I never bothered with doors while the battle was still in progress.”

“After it, then.”

“A commander has people.”

“Which side was the grease fitting on?”

Durin tore his bread into the bowl. “Behind the left return. Bad place. You had to go in with your elbow against your ear, and it dripped on your collar all day afterward.”

Briska waited.

He looked up at her. “Commanders inspect.”

“Did yours?”

“Very closely.”

His ears had gone a darker color beneath the gray hair. She took the survey from its packet and laid it before him. He pushed the bowl aside without spilling anything on the paper. The care was immediate and entirely unlike his stories.

“They took that stair away,” he said.

“The later note says so.”

“I remember it going. Hulven tried to make us carry a pump up after the lower steps cracked. Said it was only a small pump.”

His finger traced the replacement walk. He missed a bend, returned to it and frowned.

“We had it broader than this.”

“The drawing may be wrong.”

“Of course it is. Look at the shoulders they’ve allowed a man.”

Briska glanced at his mail. She decided against saying it.

Daska came to the table with a cup of beer. Durin tried to take it. She moved it past him and drank.

“Did you work here long?” she asked.

“The Iron Avengers do not measure service by the year.”

“How did they measure it?”

He considered her for a moment, then began an account involving a banner made from a slain enemy’s horse. Briska folded the survey while he explained that the horse had been a volunteer.

Rosceline arrived in time to be introduced as their commander. She accepted this with an ease Briska found troubling.

“I’ve been trying to tell them for months,” Rosceline said. “They insist on asking what I mean to do before allowing me to do it.”

Durin nodded with the solemn fellow-feeling of a man recognizing another victim of subordinates.

“I have agreed a fee,” he told her. “The first half has proved insufficient.”

“For what?”

“The undertaking.”

The innkeeper, passing behind him, set down three empty cups.

Rosceline looked at the cups and back at him. “We’ll settle the balance when we return. Your room and meals here are already included. I shall ask them to serve the meals in something larger than a cup.”

Durin took offense, recovered enough to eat, and asked whether she knew the Spirit Shield Warriors. She let him explain them while taking the only chair from which the firelight improved everyone’s face. Briska watched her make a little mark on the cloth beside her plate, testing whether the damp edge of her sleeve would stain.

After supper, Durin attempted to carry his hammer upstairs.

The first step was manageable. On the second, the head struck the post and pulled him sideways. Daska caught his elbow. Briska took the handle before it crushed either of their feet.

“I can carry it,” he said.

“You can carry that end,” Briska told him. “Keep it below my shoulder.”

They arrived at his room in a procession that had lost most of its dignity by the landing. Daska opened the door. Durin went inside to explain to the empty room that the stairs had been replaced by a criminal.

Briska set the hammer against the bed.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “you tell us before trying anything like that.”

“I have led companies through burning gates.”

“Then one ordinary staircase should have been a pleasure.”

She closed the door while he was choosing an answer.

In her own room, Briska unwrapped the jack. The improved feet had stayed firm on every test. The lateral brace still troubled her. She worked it back and forth under the little lamp until a patch of metal brightened where it rubbed.

Her failed valve casting lay in its tin beneath the files. She placed it beside the jack, rested a finger against its hollow neck and whispered the small request she had brought to every difficult piece of work since she could afford metal of her own.

No flame moved. No power ran through the room. Down the passage, Durin began to snore with the abrupt confidence of a saw meeting sound wood.

Briska took a file to the bright patch and worked until it no longer caught.

Part II — The mountain listens

Chapter 5 — The man above the door

By noon Rosceline had acquired a blister, a dislike of fir trees and a very clear idea of who had chosen the path.

“We took this road in armor,” Durin said.

He was sitting on a rock while Pello adjusted the strap of his hammer. He had insisted on bringing it. Pello had looped the handle through the old man’s pack so the head could rest against his hip instead of dragging him sideways. Every few steps it struck a buckle and announced him to the hillside.

Briska had passed him to Pello after he tried to demonstrate an old marching step beside the stream. She needed both hands for the tool pack, she said. Pello looked at the pack, which had a shoulder strap, and said nothing.

“You were younger,” Daska told Durin.

“Better supplied.”

Rosceline sat on the next rock and took her boot off. The lining had creased beneath her heel. A tiny piece of linen, incorrectly placed, had made the entire morning worse. She pulled it flat and put the boot on again.

Ahead, a ruined counting-house spread across a shelf in the mountain. Most of its upper floor was gone. New canvas covered the part that remained, and a narrow plume of smoke rose from a chimney built inside an older one. A cart stood beside the door, wheels chocked, with no load under its cover except sacks of grain.

The men watching them had crossbows.

“A sensible welcome,” Rosceline said. “We shall be asked whether we’ve come to steal something, and everyone will feel better after I say no.”

Vasko Rell met them beneath the canvas.

He had a broad face, neat boots and a coat made for travel without suggesting he had ever enjoyed it. His left wrist was wrapped in brown leather over something that occasionally caught the light. A small square seal hung at his belt. When he took their introduction, he held the paper well clear of the dust on the table.

“Madame Rosceline. Master Tellan wrote promptly.”

“He dislikes losing a purchase to somebody who answers his letters more quickly. I’ve come to see whether you have something he can use.”

“He already has my description.”

“He has several descriptions. Yours is the most expensive.”

Rell smiled. She preferred it to false offense. The man knew what he was selling and expected to be disliked for the price.

Behind him, a guard in a red cap sharpened a short knife on a strip of stone. Another was repairing a sling for his crossbow. The third, younger and narrow through the shoulders, stood where he could see the path and everyone’s hands.

Rell introduced the young one as Senn and told him to find water. He did not introduce the others.

“Your guide,” he said, looking at Durin.

“Known to you?”

“He made an assessment for me last month.”

Durin straightened. “I made no agreement.”

“You made an assessment of my beer and the amount you expected to be paid.”

“Both required correction.”

Pello took the hammer from Durin before he could use its handle to point at the broker. Rosceline moved into the space left by the weapon.

“We shall record dimensions, condition and the likely expense of removal. Nothing is to be disturbed beyond what an examination requires.”

“You brought a lifting jack.”

“I’m told objects are often heavier underneath.”

Briska gave her a look. Rell let it pass and unfolded a paper of his own: the lease, stamped at the bottom by three different offices. The last mark was so fresh the ink had stained the facing fold.

“The title includes fixtures and any service instrument remaining in the works. You understand that an inspection gives your employer no claim to possession.”

Rosceline read the three lines he indicated. The wording had been chosen by someone expecting disagreement about every noun.

“Naturally. I shall want a copy.”

“Senn will make one.”

Senn had brought the water. He put the cups down and went to find ink.

Ilaro stood at the edge of the shade, looking toward the stacked equipment behind the cart. Rosceline saw a new hauling chain, two iron restraints and a roll of padded blankets. There were no long skids for moving a heavy object, no lifting frame, scarcely any timber. Rell watched her notice the chain and said it was for the door.

“Has it fallen?” she asked.

“It has moved unexpectedly.”

“How far did you get?”

“Far enough to establish that specialist assistance would improve the return.”

He made the admission without embarrassment. She would have liked him more if he had been easier to mock.

The smith behind the house struck a wheel rim. Ilaro’s eyes closed. When he opened them, he looked at Rosceline and then at the chain. She waited for a question; he took his notebook out instead.

Rell gave them the rest of the afternoon. He would wait for their report at the outer door. His men would see that nobody else disturbed them.

“We value privacy,” Rosceline said.

“Most people do, once they’ve paid for it.”

The last climb began behind the counting-house and crossed a steep patch of loose stone. An old inclined gallery entered the ridge above them, its outer arch broken away. Pello stopped where a lesser track divided from the main path.

He pointed at the survey’s marked opening. Briska followed his finger up the slope.

“That’s a chimney,” Durin said. “We burned petitioners in it.”

“Did you?” Daska asked.

“Their petitions. A man should be allowed to finish.”

Pello looked at her. She took the hammer and put her free hand under Durin’s elbow. Pello went up the lesser path without explaining the transfer.

Rosceline waited at the fork while he climbed. A cold wind came down off the ridge, lifting the edge of her scarf. From below she could hear Rell’s smith again. The sound arrived late, after the arm had risen from the wheel.

Pello disappeared behind a slab of rock. A few minutes later a rope dropped over its lip. He came down, drew a short line on the survey, and showed Ilaro the charcoal on his fingers.

“Bars across the mouth. One bent. Space beside it. Rungs inside, then a shutter lower down. Fastenings are on the inside.”

“Air?” Briska asked.

“Cold. Blowing out. Smells of iron.”

“Fresh water?”

He shook his head.

“And the rope?” Ilaro asked.

“Around the whole slab. Not the cracked edge.”

Ilaro put his finger on the little drawing. “Leave it.”

Daska had stopped to listen to Durin describing a night march. The old man stood comfortably on the broader path with his hand tucked into her bent arm. His hammer lay across her shoulders. She might have been taking a favored uncle to a feast, except that she kept shifting the handle away from her ear when he attempted to gesture with it.

At the gallery mouth, Rosceline looked back.

Rell was still watching. He raised a hand with the ease of a man who had hired someone to do what they were about to attempt.

She returned the gesture and went into the cold.

Chapter 6 — The first knock

The bronze door had been polished by hands nobody remembered.

Ilaro could see their work at shoulder height, where the dark surface thinned to a cloudy gold. Higher up, runes occupied shallow bands beneath a crust of mineral bloom. The doorway filled the end of the inclined gallery. A modern iron chain ran through its outer lifting eye and back to a winch fixed in the wall. Its links shone with new grease.

There was no visible handle.

Durin stood beside him while Daska and Pello put down their packs. Briska had gone to the left return to find the fitting he described. She found it exactly where he said it would be.

“Told you,” he said.

“You did,” she answered. “Pello, the lamp a little lower.”

The old man leaned toward Ilaro. “She takes after the lowland side.”

“I suggest you tell her when she’s finished with the door.”

“Do you know the old script?”

“Some.”

Durin pointed to a band of letters. “It says we open to honor and close to fear.”

Ilaro brought the lamp nearer. He could read one of the words clearly. It was a warning about keeping the lower channel unobstructed.

“Does it?” he said.

He had been paid, once, to determine which of two seals had been laid over a disputed room first. The useful part of that examination had been a small working that made the edges of an active binding visible for less than a breath. He had used it on four doors since. It had never opened anything.

He told Briska what he intended to do.

She took her hand off the bronze. Rosceline moved back. Daska drew Durin out of reach when he began asking which school Ilaro belonged to.

Ilaro laid two fingers against the lower band and made the examination.

The seal showed itself as a pale thread within the bronze. He saw a second line beneath it, a third running away from the door, and something further inside that answered before he had finished looking.

Light entered the stone under his feet.

It moved through a groove too narrow to see without it, down the slope and beneath the door. His fingertips went numb. From inside the mountain came one slow knock, deep enough to move the air against his chest.

He took his hand away.

Nobody spoke until the sound had finished returning along the gallery.

“It took it,” he said.

Rosceline looked at his hand. “Took what?”

“The working. I wasn’t pushing through the seal. I was looking at its edge.” He flexed his fingers. Sensation returned unpleasantly, as though the tips had been held too close to a fire. “Don’t cast against the door.”

Briska knelt by the lit groove. She held a screwdriver above it without touching. The light reached toward the metal and sank back when she withdrew it.

“It carries inward,” she said.

“I saw it.”

“Then it can hear us,” Durin announced.

“That wasn’t hearing,” Ilaro said.

“You haven’t met it.”

He returned to the door and thumped it with his fist. Nothing happened. He thumped again, harder, and hurt his knuckles. Daska caught his wrist before the third attempt.

“Come here. Show me the way people used to go in.”

She did not ask whether he remembered. Ilaro watched the old man accept that courtesy as his due. He pointed toward the floor, then toward the upper runes, distracted by something they reminded him of.

“We brought a prince through this door. The chain broke above his head. Three of us took it on our shoulders.”

Pello looked at the lifting eye. The new chain was thick enough to anchor a small ship.

“Where did you stand?” Daska asked.

“Third stone from the mouth. Knew it would shift. I told them.”

“This mouth or the gallery?”

“The door. Nobody calls the gallery a mouth. Except Hulven, when he was drunk, and that was most afternoons.”

Briska set her screwdriver beside the third slab. Pello tapped its edge with the heel of his knife. A hollow sound came back, small and useful after the mountain’s knock.

They cleared the dirt from the joints. The slab had an iron ring folded into one side, seized by rust. Pello wrapped a rag around his hand and pulled. It did not move. Briska put a bar beneath it and worked gradually while Daska took the weight.

A dark opening appeared. It smelled of stale grease and cold wet stone.

“You see?” Durin said. “A prince would never have found that.”

The crawlspace ran underneath the door’s left side. Pello put the lamp down inside it. A narrow handle showed beyond a bend, connected to a rod disappearing into the wall.

“I can reach,” Daska said.

She took off her pack and gave Ilaro the hammer she was still carrying. Its weight surprised him enough to hurt his shoulder. Pello immediately took the lower end, and together they set it on the floor.

“Keep him here,” Daska said, nodding toward Durin.

The old man watched her lower herself into the hole. “I used to do that standing.”

“You were smaller,” she called back.

Ilaro sat on the closed end of the hammer handle. Durin looked at him with wounded interest.

“You’ll blunt it.”

“I don’t think I can.”

From below, Daska asked which way to pull. Briska lay on her stomach to see the linkage. They exchanged three short instructions. Ilaro could hear the effort in the monk’s breath before the handle moved.

A clunk traveled up through the stone. The bronze door shifted inward a thumb’s breadth. Dust fell from its top edge.

“Stop there,” Briska said.

She set a wedge, checked the lower bearing, then told Daska to continue. The door opened slowly against the pull of the modern chain. Pello had to feed slack through its outer eye. Someone had left enough length for it to open fully; someone could also haul that length back from the winch.

Ilaro made a note.

Daska came out with her coat black from shoulder to hip. A streak of grease crossed one cheek. Durin reached to wipe it away, thought better of the expression on her face and offered his handkerchief instead.

She inspected it.

“Keep that for a worse emergency.”

He seemed to consider this a compliment to the cloth.

Beyond the door, a hall extended into dark. Three suits of armor stood at intervals along its right wall. On the left, a narrow walk rested on iron brackets above a low store recess. At the far end a throat in the stone led to a larger space, where four upright shapes held a weak blue light between them.

No one had lit those shapes before Ilaro touched the door.

He waited while his companions shouldered the packs. Rosceline passed him, looked once at the open notebook and then at the new chain.

“I saw it,” she said.

He would have liked to know which of the several things she might mean. Daska was already stepping over the threshold with Durin. Pello followed, paying rope out of a coil.

Ilaro put the lamp in his right hand and went after them.

Chapter 7 — The wrong footsteps

The first suit of armor turned its head when Pello stepped over the channel.

It did not turn far. A little shift inside the high collar, accompanied by the gritty complaint of something rubbing where no oil remained. Pello stopped with one foot in the air. The head stopped too.

Rosceline watched the gap beneath its visor.

Nothing looked back through it. The darkness there was complete, although their lamp should have reached the opposite side. Rust furred the lower edge like dried blood. A chain hung from the creature’s right wrist to a ring in its breastplate, with enough slack to strike across the passage.

“Back,” Briska whispered.

Pello lowered his foot onto the stone he had left. The collar made its small sound again. The head returned to its former angle.

Durin drew breath to speak. Daska laid two fingers on his sleeve. He kept the breath, visibly offended.

The first guardian stood opposite the store recess. They could reach that cover if they crossed its strip of floor. Above them, the raised service walk had a missing section near the far throat. Getting up to it meant passing the same suit and finding the broken ladder on the other side.

Rosceline considered the distances.

“It followed his foot,” she said.

“The groove under it,” Briska answered.

“Perhaps. If it turns toward the other wall, we can cross behind it.”

Ilaro looked at her. “With what?”

She showed him a copper piece. “A sound.”

“An ordinary one?”

“An ordinary sound in a useful place.”

“The door took my examination.”

“I’m not touching the door.”

She knew how little that answered. The blank slit in the visor bothered her more than she wanted to admit. She had made guards turn toward quarrels, dropped purses, women calling their names from rooms where nobody waited. This thing had no name she could offer it.

“We can test it with a stone,” Pello said.

He rolled one across the floor. It clicked against the far wall. The guardian did not move.

Rosceline put her thumbnail against the copper and made a footstep sound on the strip beyond the suit.

The sound arrived properly: a heel, then the softer scrape of leather. The suit turned toward it.

Blue light filled the hollow beneath its breastplate.

She stopped the working at once. The coin became so cold it stuck to her thumb. Across the hall, the other two suits lifted their heads.

“There,” Ilaro said, without raising his voice.

The first guardian took a step. Its iron sole struck the glowing channel and left a bright print behind. It turned back toward Rosceline with none of the uncertainty a human guard would have brought to the change.

She drew her rapier.

The chain whipped across the passage.

Daska pulled her backward by the collar. The ring at the chain’s end hit the stone where her head had been and tore a fist-sized piece from it. Chips stung her mouth. She tasted grit and the little hot sweetness of blood.

“Don’t give it your blade,” Daska said.

The monk moved forward under the returning chain. Her feet made almost no sound. She got both hands against the iron elbow, turned with it, and discovered what the thing weighed.

It lifted her off the ground.

For a moment she hung from the arm with her boots clear of the floor. Then she let go, landed badly on one knee and rolled as the fist struck where her back had been. Stone cracked beneath it. The whole suit leaned into the blow, and Rosceline saw the gap behind its left knee.

“Briska.”

“I see it.”

The artisan thrust her bar into the joint while Daska was still drawing its attention. Metal shrieked. A retaining plate lifted. Briska sprang away as the armor tried to straighten, taking the bar with her instead of leaving it trapped.

Pello cast his rope across the creature’s upper body. The loop slipped off one shoulder, caught under the other and drew tight. He ran the free end around a wall bracket. The bracket bent with a squeal that made Ilaro flinch.

Rosceline slashed at the chain’s exposed fastening. Her point struck iron and skipped. A spark hit her glove.

“I said don’t give it the blade.”

“I was hoping it would be more receptive in pieces.”

The guardian pulled against Pello’s rope. He yielded a pace, then another, keeping it turning so its weight would fall toward the empty wall. His left hand slipped. Rope ripped skin from the two fingers the cabinet had caught.

Daska moved into the gap at the knee.

She did not strike it. She put her foot against the exposed lower brace, caught the bent plate with both hands and let the guardian attempt its next step.

The brace folded the wrong way.

A sound like a tree splitting filled the hall. The knee opened. Daska dropped out from beneath the falling thigh as the entire suit came down against the tightened rope. Pello screamed once through his teeth and released enough line to keep the bracket from tearing free.

The guardian struck the wall shoulder first.

Rust blew out of its collar. Rosceline turned her face into her sleeve. Dust entered her nose and throat, sharp and bitter as old coins. Beneath it came a hotter smell. One of the channels flared white where the breastplate’s hanging strip tore across it, then went dark.

The thing tried to rise. Its left leg remained folded beneath it. The right hand clawed a long bright mark into the stone.

“Now,” Briska said.

They crossed into the store recess. Daska took Durin by the back of his pack and pulled him after her. He had drawn a short knife and was apparently deciding which of several unsuitable places to put it. She folded his hand against his own chest until they were behind the stone lip.

Rosceline came last. The second guardian had taken three steps toward the broken first. It stopped at the darkened strip. Its head moved from side to side.

She put a foot on the alcove floor. Nothing answered it.

Briska leaned out, watched the second suit turn, and drew a small mark in the dust beside the entrance. The third stood farther down the hall, one arm lifted. A patch of light moved through its fingers and disappeared.

Rosceline sheathed the rapier. Her wrist hurt from striking iron. She disliked that very ordinary consequence more than the cuts inside her mouth.

Ilaro reached her last, bent nearly double to keep below the lip. He sat immediately, lamp between his boots, and pressed his palms against his closed eyes.

“That also took it,” she told him.

He lowered one hand. His face had gone gray beneath the dust.

“Yes.”

There was nothing to do with the admission except keep it.

Durin was examining the knife Daska had made him put away.

“Should have gone for the codpiece,” he said.

The monk turned toward him. Rosceline saw the answer gather and admired her for swallowing it.

“You’re going to sit there,” Daska said, pointing to a low stone shelf. “And I’m going to have a drink of water before you explain.”

He sat. Pello held his injured hand against his coat. Briska took out a clean cloth and looked at Daska, who nodded.

Rosceline touched her torn lip and tasted blood on the tip of her tongue. Beyond the recess, the fallen suit dragged its right hand once more across the floor. The sound stopped at the broken channel.

She watched until it tried again.

Chapter 8 — A goat would have been cheaper

Briska put the little walker on the floor and wound it six turns.

Its feet were thin folded brass. A spring drove a pair of opposed cams, so each leg lifted clear instead of dragging. The copper bell between them had come from a seller who claimed it could be heard through a locked door. That part had proved true. His neighbors had verified it without being asked.

She released the catch.

The walker moved beautifully for three feet. Then the loose shale under one leg tipped, the cam met a piece of grit, and the entire arrangement folded sideways into a noise much too small to justify what it had cost.

Durin leaned forward on his shelf.

“We used a goat.”

Ilaro lay with his coat beneath his head and his eyes closed. “Was the goat in the original estimate?”

“We ate it afterward.”

“Then perhaps we can recover something.”

Briska retrieved the walker. Its long spring had torn out of one clip. She straightened the clip with the pliers and resisted the urge to look at anyone.

Daska was cleaning Pello’s fingers. She had put a cup between his knees and told him to hold it with his good hand. He watched the water darken as she rinsed the cloth. The rope had opened the skin in two long shallow strips. Neither finger appeared broken from the cabinet, but both had become thoroughly angry about the day.

“Still bend?” she asked.

He demonstrated.

“Don’t do that again until I’ve covered them.”

Durin looked at the walker. “An Avenger would have sent a boy.”

“Instead of the goat?” Daska asked.

“For the goat. Why does everybody take the worst part of a sentence and keep it?”

Rosceline sat on an upturned crate, rubbing rust from her skirt with a corner of damp linen. The crates had been empty so long that their insides smelled of nothing at all. Around them were broken hoops, a copper pot without a bottom, and three pegs worn smooth by tools taken away generations ago.

Briska checked the walker’s feet. One still carried a fragment of dark metallic grit. She set the broken mechanism at the edge of the recess and pressed its bell with her thumb.

The second guardian turned its head.

She waited. It turned back.

She moved the walker a hand’s breadth to the left and pressed again. Nothing.

Across the entrance ran the remains of a narrow bronze inlay. It had been broken near the alcove’s lip by a falling crate or years of careless feet. The floor beyond it was connected to the guardians’ strips. The recess was not.

Briska used the end of her rule to push the bell into the main groove. The suit lifted a foot.

She snatched it back.

“It goes through the floor,” she said.

Ilaro opened one eye.

She showed him, once, with the smallest pressure she could apply. The movement repeated. He sat up enough to see the dark break where the first guardian had fallen.

“That’s why it stopped following us.”

“And why the stone Pello threw did nothing. He missed the strip.”

“Can you draw the others away?”

“If I can get something to ring where I put it.”

She inspected the wrecked walker. Its moving legs had become a liability in the time since she made them. The bell and long spring might yet be useful. She took the device apart with rather more force than it required.

Pello held out the little bag of bread once Daska had finished tying his hand. Briska took a piece. Salt caught the raw place where she had bitten her cheek during the fight. She chewed on the other side.

They ate quietly for a while. Durin made an elaborate business of finding a comfortable place for his knee. At last Daska rolled a spare cloth beneath it. He tested the height and accepted it with a grunt.

“What did you actually do here?” she asked.

“We held the inner works when the lower gates failed. Hulven took the front. I had the rear company.”

“How many?”

“Enough.”

The monk was checking the knot on Pello’s bandage. He let her, watching the entrance over her shoulder.

Durin’s voice grew companionable. “There were chests brought down all night. The men in front carried them. The men behind made sure they kept carrying. Nobody opened one. That was discipline.”

Daska stopped with the bandage end between her fingers.

“Did the kitchens get any of them?”

“What?”

“Nothing. Go on.”

Rosceline had turned her skirt inside out across her lap. She was picking a piece of iron scale from the seam with the point of a pin.

Durin warmed to his account. “Hulven wanted the big door opened. Always the big door with him. I told the boy you lift the little tongue first and hold it till the weight kisses down. Let the old beast empty its lungs before you open its mouth, I said. You could tell by the cold coming through.”

Pello’s fingers twitched as Daska retied the cloth. She loosened it a little.

“Eat, Durin. Please. There’s no point saving that piece of cheese until it goes hard.”

The old man looked at the cheese as though it had changed allegiance while he was talking. He took a bite. Rosceline worked the last fleck of rust out with her pin and held it up to see whether it had torn the thread.

Briska put the walker’s usable pieces into her bag.

“Ilaro needs it quiet,” Daska said to Durin when he had swallowed. “Come here. Help me hold this strap.”

She gave him Pello’s torn pack binding. He pulled the wrong end once, then understood. His thick fingers held it very steadily while she cut a clean length from the spare. He watched the knife with the intense attention of a man who wanted to be given one next.

Pello rose and looked out of the recess. The second guardian had turned far enough away to expose its back.

“I’m checking the outer door.”

Briska went with him to the lip. He set his feet on the dull stone between the inlays, placed a hand against the wall and passed beyond the fallen armor. The rope he had used to turn it remained caught under its shoulder; he left it. He had a second coil at his belt.

She watched until the gallery swallowed him.

The quiet did something to the company. Ilaro slept. Daska leaned back against the wall with the repaired strap over one knee, her eyes half closed. Durin forgot to complain while working a crumb of cheese out of his beard. Rosceline looked up from her clothes and watched the monk for a moment before passing her the water.

Briska fitted the remaining bell to a length of spring wire. It rang when she flicked it. The note traveled through her fingers and into the stone beneath her hand.

She stopped before the suit could answer.

Pello returned with grease on his sleeve. He waited until Daska looked up, then bent to speak near Ilaro’s ear without touching him.

“The chain’s tight now.”

Ilaro sat up too quickly and had to wait before asking where.

“Across the door’s travel. Somebody hauled it while we were inside. I could see daylight, but I couldn’t get my shoulder through.”

Briska looked at the black streak on his sleeve.

“You tried the lifting eye?”

He nodded.

Ilaro reached for his notebook. Rosceline put her skirt down.

Outside, the second guardian turned its head toward a human voice calling from the outer hall.

“Madame Rosceline,” Rell said. “You’ve taken rather longer than an assessment.”

Part III — The inspection

Chapter 9 — The terms at the other end

Ilaro had expected the man to wait outside.

It was the most comfortable part of the arrangement Rell had made, and Ilaro had mistaken comfort for preference. A broker who wanted the work done would come in if he feared losing the people doing it. The chain was there to keep them available. The restraints beside the cart were smaller versions of the same provision.

“Don’t take the case out,” he told Briska.

She left it beneath her tool bag.

Rosceline went to the lip of the recess. “We’ve had difficulty reaching the part your description called accessible.”

“I see you reached it.”

“Come a little farther and you can see what reached us.”

Rell did not. He stood beyond the fallen armor, Senn at his shoulder, the man in the red cap a pace behind. A third figure remained in the outer doorway. There was space under the bronze for him to have crawled in; the chain could be tightened again without that man coming after them.

Rell looked at the second guardian and waited for it to stop moving.

“You have a method.”

“We charge for it,” Rosceline said.

Ilaro found the page with the clerk’s name. He had put a mark beside the way Celise paid. The clerk had not needed to guess their employer; the answer had arrived with the fee. The broker could have checked it before they left Silkstrand.

“He knows,” Ilaro said softly.

“I had begun to suspect,” Rosceline answered.

“The Countess is an excellent purchaser,” Rell called. “Less excellent at observing other people’s titles.”

Durin rose from the shelf. Daska put the repaired strap into his hand and directed him toward Ilaro with a touch between his shoulders. She needed to get her pack on without the old man explaining it to her.

“Keep that end off the floor,” she told him.

Durin gave Ilaro the look of a man unfairly occupied with logistics. Ilaro accepted the other end and folded it into his bag.

Rosceline had gone very still. “Master Tellan will be disappointed to hear you think him a fiction.”

“His clerk sent me the request. Your name. The source of the payment. A useful clarification.”

“How much?”

“Less than the survey appears to have cost you.”

Ilaro watched her mouth. It wanted to smile and could not quite choose the right version.

Rell stepped across the lit channel.

Something at his wrist flashed beneath the leather. Briska caught Ilaro’s sleeve and pointed. He turned in time to see a thread of blue light run ahead of the broker’s boot.

The second guardian bowed its head.

“It recognizes an authorized claimant,” Rell said.

“What are you wearing?” Briska asked.

“Protection.”

“Against what?”

“The sort of accident that happens in old works.”

Ilaro got to his feet. He had to use the shelf with his left hand. By the time he was standing, the flare had reached the four shapes beyond the inner throat. A sound came back from there, very low, like air being drawn through a hole in wet clay.

“Your protection is feeding it,” he said.

Rell looked toward the inner chamber. “Then it has accepted the assurance.”

“That isn’t what I said.”

“I heard you.”

He had. That was the difficult part. Ilaro could tell a man he had misunderstood; he could not make him prefer the correction to the explanation that let him continue.

Senn had watched the light. His eyes moved from Rell’s wrist to the guardian’s lowered head. He kept his crossbow pointed at the floor, but his thumb shifted along the stock.

“You can unwrap it,” Briska said. “If it has a release, stop it working.”

Rell put his other hand over the leather. He did not remove it. “You’re asking me to disarm in front of people hired to take my property.”

“We’re asking you not to do that again,” Daska said.

The man in the red cap moved his crossbow to a more useful angle. Daska looked at him once and then continued fastening her pack. Ilaro saw her choose a position that would put the fallen armor between them if she stepped left.

Durin began to explain how feuds used to be settled. Ilaro put the folded strap into his hands.

“Hold this. I need to hear the broker.”

The old man held it. For the moment that was enough.

Rell’s terms were simple. They would complete the examination in his presence. If the instrument could be removed, they would remove it. He would pay them for the labor and let them return to Silkstrand with a disappointing account for their employer. No one need be hurt. His men at the door had orders to admit nobody else.

“And to let us out?” Ilaro asked.

“When I tell them.”

Briska shut her tool bag. Rosceline turned back into the recess and stood beside Ilaro, where Rell could see them speaking but not hear every word.

“The high opening?” she asked.

“Exists,” Pello said. “Shutter’s inside.”

“Could be a discharge route,” Briska added. “We don’t know what comes through it.”

Rosceline nodded. “Then we look. We’ve come a long way to be frightened by a man with better paperwork.”

Ilaro waited until she looked at him.

“He brought blankets for a valuable object. Chains for people. No lifting frame and no men who know the machinery. He has been waiting for someone like us.”

“Yes.”

“His offer may be genuine. It saves him paying us until we’ve done it.”

Her eyes sharpened. She had heard the part he meant her to hear.

“I shall listen very carefully.”

Briska made the bell ring against a strip of metal laid across the floor channel. The second guardian moved toward it. She pulled the strip away with a cord before it could reach her. The others crossed behind it, feet placed on the dead stone Pello indicated.

Rell watched the method and copied the route. His men came through one at a time. The third figure stayed at the outer door.

“Then use it.”

They entered the inner chamber together. The four shapes were pillars, taller than houses. Light moved inside them with the slow insistence of something looking for a way to continue.

Chapter 10 — Half the fee

Rell offered seven hundred and fifty marks.

Rosceline disliked him more for arriving at the exact half than she would have for offering nothing. He had learned their price along with the name of their employer. Somewhere in Silkstrand a clerk was congratulating himself on making a profitable copy.

“Paid here?” she asked.

“An order on my agent.”

“The same one who can afford to buy information about his competitors’ business?”

“He’ll recognize the seal.”

Rell took it from his belt. The square metal face lay in his palm, fine dark lines running inward toward a piece of polished quartz. She heard a tiny note beneath the scrape of its chain, too steady to be metal alone.

“It certifies what I send him,” he said. “The order will be honored.”

Beyond his shoulder, a black stone hung in a collar above the altar. It was smaller than the drawing had made it seem. Light entered its lower edge, disappeared, then returned through a rib beneath the mounting. That rib curved down into what Rosceline had first taken for the altar’s supports.

One of the supports had fingers.

She let her gaze follow the immense folded forearm to a shoulder almost buried in the far side of the stone. A head lay between it and the opposite arm, flat against the altar as if the creature had put its face down to sleep. Its mouth was a seam in the metal. The seam widened slightly, then shut.

“What is that?” she asked Durin.

“Primus.”

“Does it always do that?”

He looked at the mouth. “I never cared for its manners.”

Rell moved where he could see both the stone and the door. Senn stayed at the throat. The red-capped man took the opposite pillar, crossbow against his hip. Rosceline marked the positions without turning her head. The narrow lane beneath the raised walk ran along the left wall, from the doorway to a damaged ladder beyond the altar. If the Primus unfolded into the middle, that lane would be all the floor left.

“Half the fee,” Rell said. “Your expenses remain your employer’s concern. I won’t ask where the survey came from.”

“How generous.”

“You’ve kept your equipment, your health and the prospect of being paid. Your reputation can survive a failed commission.”

She glanced at Pello’s bandage.

“Most of our health.”

“My men didn’t do that.”

He was right, which did nothing to improve the offer.

She could finish the extraction and give Rell the genuine object. He would have what he wanted. She crossed to the left pillar. Briska was examining the mounting through a narrow gap. Daska stood at her shoulder with Durin, who wanted to know why Rell’s men were allowed to lean on old stone when he had been told not to touch anything.

“The guide. I meant the guide.”

Daska laid the hammer against the pillar and went to help Pello fix the first rope. Durin settled beside Rosceline with an audible release of breath. He smelled of damp mail, bread and the bitter mineral grease that seemed to belong to the mountain.

“I heard.”

“A real offer.”

“Possibly. Can you remove it?”

“If I can get behind the collar. I need the walk.”

His bandaged hand stayed close to his body; he took most of the strain on his feet and the rope around his hips. Daska stood below until he was past the missing section.

She looked back at Briska.

“Use the second packing.”

The artisan’s face closed.

“He’ll want to look.”

“I have.”

Ilaro joined them. He had left the lamp on a low ledge where it lit the ladder without shining into his eyes. He looked at Rosceline for several seconds before asking what she had agreed to.

“Removal,” she said. “Not ownership.”

She knew he disliked the answer. She had intended him to. A better answer would have required her to give up the small, vicious pleasure taking shape behind Rell’s self-satisfaction.

“I will remove the stone,” she said. “I won’t promise what happens if that seal touches the dummy.”

“Be clear back.”

Rosceline felt Durin’s attention swing toward them. She had no intention of conducting a public quarrel for his benefit.

Briska glanced at the shutter above. “We need that open first.”

“Then open it.”

Rell called to ask whether there was a difficulty. Rosceline turned with a smile she had spent the length of the exchange preparing.

“Several, my dear man. We are charging you a very reasonable sum for the privilege of watching us solve them.”

Beside her, Durin began to describe an ancestral treasure. She took his elbow before he could wander toward the altar and asked him what it looked like.

Chapter 11 — His ancestral treasure

It had belonged to a king, Durin said.

Rosceline did not ask which king. That would have cost them the time he needed to invent one. She asked whether it was larger than the case they had brought.

“It will have to be carried in something. I am trying to spare you the indignity of a sack.”

“Gold. With a stone in it.”

“A small inheritance, then.”

“A royal clasp.”

He had begun with a third keystone, discovered while Rosceline was persuading him to stay beside the pillar. The prize had acquired a chain, then a royal wearer, and had now become something that might fit through a customs inspection. She watched its dimensions diminish with interest.

Across the chamber, Briska followed Pello up the repaired ladder. A short iron grating hinged out from the raised walk to the back of the mounting. It stood just below the collar that held the Keystone above the altar. From where Rell stood, the broad black stone would conceal their hands but not their heads.

The Primus lay folded on the farther side. Each breath opened its mouth seam enough to show a deeper line of light. Nothing else moved. Rosceline had begun to count the interval, not because she knew what it meant but because she disliked waiting for the sound.

Pello crouched by the shutter. He tried the wheel, stopped, and called down that it would not turn.

He shook his head. “Something holding it from behind.”

“Not by opening that into our faces.”

Rell moved to hear better. “A difficulty with the stone?”

It was becoming warmer. Sweat gathered beneath her scarf. The breath entering the Primus smelled of heated dust, and what came back was dry enough to hurt the nose. Rell unfastened his coat. Senn wiped his upper lip against his shoulder without taking his hand from the crossbow.

Durin tugged Rosceline’s sleeve.

“My clasp would be in the stores.”

“I was assured my property would be respected.”

He tried to take up the hammer. Rosceline put a foot over the handle and continued speaking pleasantly to Rell.

“You have had someone in here before us.”

“I told you we examined it.”

“How did they leave?”

“Through the door.”

“All of them?”

He looked at her for a moment too long.

“They were paid.”

The answer was useful, although not for anything she could safely accuse him of yet.

Briska had returned to the mounting. She opened the empty capsule and set both halves on a cloth. Her hands were steady. Rosceline could see the effort of keeping them so in the way she held her elbows against the narrow grating.

Pello checked a shallow recess below the shutter, using the tip of his knife to lift debris from the bottom. He brought out something small and brown. A fitting, Rosceline thought, until he turned it and she saw the corroded tongue.

She pointed to Durin.

Pello looked at the object, then at the old man, and lowered it on a short piece of cord.

Durin reached before Rosceline could. He nearly stepped on her pinned foot. She freed the hammer handle and caught his coat at the same time, receiving the buckle in her other hand.

It was old. One corner had gone. Under the corrosion, a little stamped pattern might once have been leaves.

“This?” she asked.

Durin took it with both hands.

The expression on his face stopped her next remark. He was looking at the worn bend beside the tongue. His thumb moved over it twice, very slowly. For a moment she thought he knew the person who had worn it.

“Close enough,” he said.

“To a king’s clasp?”

“Things alter.”

He put it inside his coat and looked toward the shutter above. “There used to be a boy up there. He’d take the little tongue while we were below. Hulven couldn’t get his hand into the gap. Broad man.”

“Broader than the passage?” she asked.

“In spirit.”

Rell called her name. She kept a hand on Durin’s elbow while turning.

“I would like to know what is being removed from my installation.”

“A buckle, Master Rell. If you intend to add it to the inventory, you should be quick. Its ownership has already become hereditary.”

The broker’s eyes moved to Durin’s coat. His contempt was so slight it could have passed as fatigue. Rosceline felt the old man stiffen beneath her hand.

“Let him keep it,” Rell said. “We’re discussing something worth removing.”

Durin’s chin rose. A battle was coming, very probably the largest yet. Rosceline stepped nearer and asked to see the stamped leaves. He took the buckle out and held it toward the lamp. She let him tell her what they meant.

Above them, metal clicked against metal. Briska called for the thinner hook. Pello put it in her hand and held the cloth where she could reach it.

Ilaro had been studying the pillar nearest the throat. He joined Rosceline beneath the walk, speaking quietly enough that Durin leaned toward them.

“The columns are still filling.”

“From Rell’s bracelet?”

“From what it already put in. I don’t think it stops when we stop looking. It moves between them.”

“And when the stone comes out?”

“It may stay there. Briska thinks the stone is a tool joined to the reservoir, not the reservoir itself.”

The Primus drew another breath. Rosceline could feel it taking air from beneath the raised walk. Her skirt moved against her ankle.

“How much more can it hold?” she asked.

Ilaro shook his head.

“Not much?”

“I don’t know. That’s the answer. I can’t give you a safe amount because it would be useful to have one.”

She watched the mouth close.

“I wasn’t asking you to invent it.”

“Good.”

He went back toward the pillar, moving slowly enough to keep his attention on the floor. Durin followed him with his eyes and then looked at Rosceline.

“He doesn’t care for waiting.”

“He has found things worth waiting for unusually rare.”

“I had a captain like that.”

She had heard the captain in three different ranks already. She asked whether he was the broad one. Durin looked pleased that she remembered and began again from the beginning while she watched the stone rise a finger’s breadth from its collar.

Chapter 12 — The case changes hands

The retaining pin came out cleanly until the last third.

Briska felt the shoulder catch through the hook. She stopped pulling. Beneath the grating, something exhaled against her boot.

The old drawing showed a straight pin. This one had been peened at the end by a repairer who preferred a component that could never come loose to a component anyone could remove. She respected the fear and disliked the work.

“The fine file,” she said.

Pello took it from her bag. He kept the flap open while she tested the pin with her other hook. His bandaged fingers made the movement slower. She waited instead of reaching around him.

The Keystone sat in a six-sided collar, supported by three thin arms. At its base a sprung cradle touched the reservoir’s conductor. Two catches held the cradle up. Briska had loosened the first. The second would release only after the pin came clear.

Below, Rosceline was describing something to Durin. He interrupted her to improve it. Rell had begun pacing between the doorway and the nearer pillar. Every time he turned, the little working at his wrist hummed against Briska’s teeth.

She filed the burr in short strokes. Fine bronze dust settled on the cloth. Pello bent to blow it away; she shook her head before he began.

“Into the slot and we start again.”

He took a clean corner of the cloth and lifted the dust instead.

She would have liked to work like this somewhere with an open window and a cup within reach. The mechanism was lovely beneath the neglect. One curve admitted the file at exactly the angle needed. Whoever made it had expected another pair of hands to arrive one day, after their own were gone.

The person who peened the pin had apparently disagreed.

When it moved, Briska almost missed the first fraction. She changed her grip and drew it all the way out. The end remained hot against her glove.

“Capsule.”

Pello set the lower half beneath the stone. The collar opened under Briska’s thumb. She let the cradle descend slowly, keeping the Keystone upright until its unequal faces found the recess made for them.

One corner failed to settle.

Her heart struck once, very hard. She lifted the stone again before it could rest on the wrong edge. A thin ridge inside the new capsule had survived its maker’s finishing. The drawing had been accurate; the fitting was not quite finished.

She put the Keystone back in its collar.

“What happened?” Rell called.

“An adjustment.”

“You’ve had time for adjustments.”

“And I’m using it.”

She did not look down. Rosceline began telling him how expensive a dropped instrument would be to certify. Briska took a scraper from her bag and shaved the pale ridge. Dust collected on her knees. She cleared every grain from the recess before trying the stone again.

This time it seated without rocking.

She put the upper half on and fitted the retaining band. The capsule closed along its stepped seam. A strip of thin paper would not enter the joint. She tested every side.

Pello watched her face until she nodded.

The light under the mounting diminished. Across the chamber, one pillar brightened in response. The Primus’s mouth opened, drew a long breath, and closed.

It had not gone to sleep.

Briska put a palm against the empty mounting. Its conductor still carried warmth from the floor. She took her hand away and wiped it on her coat, irritated with herself for checking something she could have checked with a tool.

“The charge stays,” she told Pello.

He opened his coat. His body sling waited against his ribs, empty since the practice in the workshop. She eased the capsule into it. He fastened the broad retaining strap, then a second narrow tie over the first. She tested both herself before allowing the coat to close.

The altar hid their hands from Rell. It did not hide their shoulders. Briska kept the next movement small: the weighted pottery dummy from her bag into the outer case’s felt lining. She shut the lid and wiped the stone dust from the hinge.

“Ready?” Rell asked.

“The catch needs oil.”

“Does everything?”

“Eventually.”

She put a single drop on the stiff little catch and worked it twice. It was a good catch. She regretted making it so good for a thing somebody would soon throw away.

Pello shifted toward the shutter. The capsule added little to his outline. He was carrying the prize before anyone on the floor had seen it leave the mounting, and Briska felt the first precarious pleasure of success.

She sent the false case down to Rosceline on the short line.

Durin wanted to receive it. Rosceline made him hold the loose cord instead. He performed that service with enough ceremony for a banner.

Briska climbed down to the left lane. The ladder’s missing section required a long step onto Daska’s shoulder. She disliked making the monk take her weight after the fight, but the alternative was falling, which would have given her more of it.

“Shutter?” Daska asked when she reached the floor.

“Still held. There’s an interlock behind the wheel. Something has to move first.”

“Does he know?”

Briska looked toward Rell. “Rosceline knows.”

The monk’s mouth tightened.

Rosceline held the case beneath her folded scarf. She brought it far enough into the lane that Rell could see the handle, then stopped where the nearest pillar interrupted his red-capped man’s aim.

“Your order,” she said.

Rell took the folded paper from his coat.

“We shall exchange them outside. It will be a pleasure to discuss the remainder of the work somewhere I can hear myself being underpaid.”

“Here will do.”

“Your men hold the entrance. Ours have a narrow ladder. I would prefer not to confuse a badly chosen step with an attempt to run.”

Rell looked up at Pello. The young man stood near the shutter, holding the spare rope as though the rest of his task consisted of taking it down. His coat concealed the capsule perfectly. His bandaged hand was not on it.

“Bring it here,” Rell said.

Rosceline did not move immediately. Briska caught her arm as she turned.

“It won’t survive a working. The test will find the pottery.”

“I know.”

“I know that too.”

She said it without annoyance. Briska would have preferred annoyance; it would have meant the warning had encountered something.

Rosceline sheathed her rapier, reached past Ilaro and took Durin’s hammer from where it lay below the pillar. She carried it with both hands, pointlessly furious that the old man had been right to insist on bringing so much iron.

Briska accepted it without looking away from the jack.

“Up,” she said.

Durin climbed first. He knew where to put his feet even where the steps had gone. Ilaro followed, keeping his eyes on the back of the guide’s coat. At the broken section he stopped, found the rope with his hand and waited until the world agreed to hold still for the next movement.

“Here,” Durin said above him.

His grip was strong. Ilaro took it and stepped across.

Chapter 15 — No further negotiation

Rosceline waited beneath the ladder until Ilaro was past the missing section.

He moved by decisions, one hand placed and tested before the next foot followed. Durin stood above him on the narrow walk with his back against the wall. The guide had stopped talking. It made him look smaller and more useful.

Across the chamber, the Primus turned toward Daska’s moving shadow. The monk kept the broken chain between herself and its hand. Once, when she pulled, her left fingers opened without permission. She caught the links against her forearm and moved her right hand farther along.

Briska saw it. Rosceline saw Briska see it. Neither had anything helpful to add.

Pello freed his ankle with a sound that went through Rosceline’s teeth. He got his knee onto the walk and rolled against the wall. The capsule struck stone through his coat, a dull little tap. He checked its straps before looking down at the foot that would not bear him.

“Ready,” Ilaro called.

Briska held the jack ready and watched Daska.

Rell came into the lane below Rosceline.

His face was wet. There was blood on his coat that had belonged to the man in the red cap. He held his short sword low and kept his other hand where she could see it. Behind him, Senn sat propped against the altar step with his injured leg drawn close.

“Your man has it.”

Rosceline put a foot on the lowest rung. “Yes.”

“We could have done this differently.”

“We were doing it differently. You wanted a demonstration.”

The accusation satisfied her less than she had expected. She could still hear her own voice wondering whether his seal would reach through the capsule.

Rell looked up at the service walk. A strip of light showed around the shutter’s lower edge. Durin had his arm in the slot beneath the wheel. Ilaro stood beside him, looking down toward the moving shoulder.

“I have the outer gate,” Rell said. “I have the claim. I can make her purchase troublesome for years. Or I can sign the instrument over, take a share and finish the work of getting us out.”

“What share?”

“A third of what she pays. You have five. I’m one.”

She could almost have liked the speed with which he arrived at a defensible fraction.

Rell lowered his sword until the point rested on the floor.

“We’re both practical people.”

Rosceline looked at the hand he held open. It had the same careful nails as the clerk who sold their introduction. Nothing would be settled by killing the clerk either, though she had enjoyed imagining it on the way down.

“Move a little left,” she said. “I can’t get off the ladder with you there.”

He moved.

She drew the rapier and put it through his throat.

The point entered above his coat collar, at the soft hollow beside the windpipe. His mouth opened. She felt the resistance in her wrist, the small correction required to keep the blade from catching against bone. His sword struck the floor. One hand closed on her sleeve.

He had been willing to bargain. His eyes retained the astonishment of that fact after his knees began to fold.

Rosceline drew the blade free. Blood ran over the back of her hand and into the cuff. She stepped aside to let him fall, keeping clear of the arm that still reached toward her.

Across the lane, Daska was looking at her.

The monk had both feet braced against the altar corner. The chain dragged at her hands. Her face changed without her releasing it.

Rosceline wiped the blade once against Rell’s coat, sheathed it and climbed.

The Primus drew in. Daska pulled the chain around the broken altar corner and let her weight take the turn. Its shoulder pressed back toward the jack. Briska drove the screw the last fraction. Bronze met steel with a hard ringing note.

“Hold,” she shouted.

Durin raised something inside the slot.

Behind the wall, a weight descended with a slow clattering scrape.

Durin counted under his breath. The counts were farther apart than Rosceline wanted. At four, the Primus tried to lift its shoulder. The jack took the movement. One of its broad feet grated against the old mounting.

At five, Briska shouted for Daska to keep the turn.

At six, something struck a stop inside the wall.

“Down,” Durin said.

He took his hand out of the slot. A line of bright dust ran around the wheel and vanished into the shutter’s seam.

For a moment nothing followed.

Then the mountain began to breathe out.

The sound was deeper than the Primus’s breath and far larger. It entered Rosceline through the soles of her boots. The pillars dimmed at their bases. Light climbed within them, found the high connecting channels and ran toward the throat behind the shutter.

The guardian threw its head back.

Briska’s jack began to peel away from the mounting.

“It’s turning,” Pello said.

“I see it,” Briska answered.

She stood below them with Durin’s hammer in her hands. The jack’s screw was still whole. The brace she had filed at the inn had opened a little at the edge. If she backed it out now, she could probably save most of the mechanism.

She put the hammer against the brace, judged the angle, and swung.

The first blow drove it deeper. The second broke something inside the regulator. A small piece of steel flew past Rosceline’s knee and struck the wall hard enough to bury its edge in the stone.

Briska’s left forearm opened from wrist to elbow in a sudden bright line.

She hit it again.

The regulator held. The whole upper shaft roared.

Daska dropped the chain. She could not have held it another moment. Her left hand hung curled at her side. The Primus tried to follow her, but its shoulder stayed drawn against the broken jack, the arm moving only a few inches before dragging back.

Senn was still on the floor.

“Go,” Rosceline called.

Daska went toward him.

The choice was made before Rosceline could improve the instruction. The monk pulled him up on his good leg. He tried to use the cut hand and nearly fell. Briska left the hammer against the ruined regulator, caught his belt with her uninjured arm and helped bring him to the ladder.

Rosceline knelt at the top and took the rope Pello offered her.

“Under his arms,” she said.

Daska looked up at her. Then she put the rope where she was told.

Chapter 16 — The cold coming through

Ilaro kept his palm beside the shutter and counted without moving his lips.

Heat came through the iron, then less heat, then a sudden cold draft that lifted the sweat from his wrist. The sound behind it weakened in uneven surges. It would have been easy to mistake the first pause for an end.

Durin caught his sleeve when he reached for the wheel.

“Wait.”

They waited through another rush of air. Small black flakes came from the lower seam. One touched Ilaro’s knuckle and left a white mark in the skin.

“Now?”

The guide held his own hand at the seam. “Another.”

Below, Briska and Daska were getting Senn into the sling. Rosceline knelt beside Pello on the walk, pulling when he told her. The wounded guard came up with his boots knocking the wall. He bit a fold of his sleeve until the injured leg was clear.

Pello secured the rope around a surviving bracket. Rosceline took Senn by the coat and brought him onto the walk. He recoiled from her hand. She let go as soon as his weight was safe.

The next breath through the shutter tasted of wet stone.

Durin nodded.

Ilaro put both hands on the wheel. It turned heavily, then easily enough to surprise him. The shutter traveled inward along a short track. Beyond it, iron rungs climbed through a steep shaft to a small patch of gray daylight.

Air came down the shaft now, clean enough to make him dizzy.

Pello crawled past them, dragging the injured foot. He tested the first rung, moved up and tested the next. The capsule remained beneath his coat. The shoulder straps had held through the fall; one narrow tie was frayed where it had pressed against the wall.

“Tell me about the rope when you reach it,” Ilaro said.

Pello nodded. He went up with his good foot turned sideways on the rungs and the other held clear.

The Primus was folding below them. Its hand dragged over the altar. A finger caught the broken collar and tore it free. It lay against the black stone, empty and still beautiful. Briska watched it fall with an expression Ilaro could not read from above.

“Leave the bag,” Daska told her.

“There are bandages in it.”

“Then take the bag.”

Briska pushed it up ahead of herself. She had tied a cloth around her cut arm with her teeth and one hand. Blood had already reached the knot. At the top she gave Ilaro the tools and sat with her back against the wall until he could bind it properly.

Daska came last from the floor. She had tried to retrieve Durin’s hammer, but its head had become wedged under the ruined jack. Durin saw the empty hands with which she reached the top.

“It held,” she told him.

He looked down toward the mechanism. His mouth worked once.

“Good head,” he said.

Pello’s voice came from the shaft. “Rope’s here. Anchor’s sound.”

Ilaro did not ask him to repeat it. He wrote nothing down. For once there was only the next person and the distance that person had to cross.

Rosceline went into the shaft after Pello. Durin followed with Ilaro behind him. The guide’s mail caught on a projecting bolt; Ilaro freed it before the old man could pull himself backward. At the narrowest part Durin stopped to get breath. Ilaro stopped too. Nobody asked either of them to hurry.

At the mouth, the wind struck them sideways.

The bars Pello had described were set across the opening, one bowed out far enough to admit a body. He had loosened a smaller brace with Briska’s screwdriver and tied it back to make more room. Beyond lay the cracked slab, their rope and a slope of loose stone dropping toward the lesser track.

There was no bridge, no ladder and no convenient path down from the opening. Pello had wrapped the rope around the broad slab and run the free end through his belt so he could control a descent without trusting his foot. Rosceline held the rope below his hands, ready to take weight.

Durin looked at the slope.

“We used to have steps.”

“We have this,” Pello said.

He put the loop beneath the old man’s hips and showed him where to hold. Durin began a statement about rank. Rosceline laid her palm against his back.

“Sit, my dear. We’ve spent a great deal bringing you this far.”

He sat. They lowered him until his boots found the track. He turned at once and looked up, both hands lifted uselessly toward the next person.

Ilaro went down after him. The rope rubbed against his coat. He kept his eyes on the rough stone in front of his face, refusing the long side view of the mountain. When his foot slipped, Durin caught it and planted it on the path.

Above, Daska and Briska brought Senn through the shaft. The guard could use one leg and his left hand. He had made no attempt to retrieve his knife. They tied him into the sling with his injured knee supported. He kept his face turned from Rosceline until they had lowered him past the opening.

He reached the path and leaned against the rock, eyes shut. Durin offered him the handkerchief Daska had refused. Senn took it without inspecting it.

Briska came next with her tool bag tied separately below her. Daska followed, descending awkwardly because her left hand would not open enough to take the rope. Rosceline lowered Pello with the sling under his arms, then came down last herself, freeing the working end and leaving the long line around the slab. Nobody suggested climbing back to recover it.

As her boots reached the track, a great metal crash came from inside the mountain.

Dust blew from the shaft mouth. Ilaro put an arm over his face. When he lowered it, the opening was still there. The mountain stood where it had stood that morning. Inside it, something large continued grinding toward rest.

Farther down, voices rose beside the counting-house. Two men ran out beneath the canvas. Neither looked up toward the lesser path. A cart horse screamed when one pulled its rein too hard.

“That way,” Pello said, pointing along the contour instead of down.

They moved behind the slab and out of sight of the outer works.

Briska stopped after twenty paces to take Pello’s coat open. Ilaro saw the capsule still in its sling, pale stone darkened by sweat at the lower seam. She checked the frayed tie, replaced it and left the retaining band untouched.

“Sound,” she said.

Rosceline held out a hand to steady Pello while Briska closed his coat. He took it because he had to cross loose stone on one good foot. She kept it until they reached the firs.

Durin walked ahead with Daska’s arm beneath his. He had begun to explain something about the old steps. She bent her head to hear him over the wind.

Part V — Payment

Chapter 17 — The men who came back

Senn covered his injured hand with his sound one when Rosceline brought him a cup.

He sat propped against a rolled blanket in the inn’s room nearest the stairs. His hand had swollen beneath Daska’s wrapping. Across the passage, Pello was still trying to ease his boot off. Neither could manage another flight; Rosceline had paid for both rooms before the keeper finished calling her son to fetch the woman who treated quarry injuries.

Senn watched the cup until Rosceline set it within reach. She went to wash.

Farther along the passage, Ilaro was asking for a room away from the kitchen. Briska had obtained a table and clean water, but was still asking for a needle whose point had not been used to open something else.

Rell’s blood had dried inside her cuff. Warm water loosened it slowly. She had to turn the sleeve inside out and rub the seam between her fingers. The basin changed color. Outside the washroom, the keeper began telling someone there would be no supper on that table until the bandages had been cleared away.

Rosceline washed the rapier next. A thread from Rell’s collar had caught against the guard. She picked it free and put it in the stove. The flame took it so quickly it might never have been there.

When she returned, Daska had brought the true capsule from Pello’s room and put it into Briska’s hands.

Briska examined the seam by lamplight. The passive retaining band had not shifted. The pale stone showed a scrape on one corner where it struck the wall through Pello’s coat. She passed a fingertip over it and set the capsule in the original outer case retrieved from the shed.

“May I?” Rosceline asked.

Briska shut the lid.

“At the Countess’s table.”

“I was going to check it.”

“I’ve checked it.”

There was room for an argument. Rosceline could have filled it for some time. The artisan’s bandage was turning red at the wrist, and she had placed the case where she could hold it with her right hand while the healer attended to the left.

“Very well,” Rosceline said.

Daska watched her go to the fire. She had said nothing about the ladder. That silence had acquired a definite shape since the mountain.

The healer arrived smelling of rain and crushed green leaves. Her bag contained rolled splints, thread, a small bottle of spirits and a knife she asked the keeper to heat. Briska inspected the needle before offering her arm.

“That one will do.”

“I’m pleased,” the woman said. “Keep still.”

Rosceline sat by Durin while the stitches went in. His bowl of stew had cooled. He was trying to tear bread with fingers stiff from holding the bypass tongue.

She broke it and put the pieces beside his bowl. He ate one without comment, then began explaining that his hammer would have survived if modern people knew where to strike.

“It was struck against an older machine,” she said.

“Then they should have chosen the machine more carefully.”

“I shall put that in our report.”

He gave her a sidelong glance, looking for mockery. She kept her face occupied with the fire. After a while he took out the buckle and held it on his knee.

“It was a stores clasp,” he said.

She waited.

“They used them on the heavy aprons. The strap went right across here.” He touched the place beneath his belly where his mail no longer met. “If a corner caught you, it took the apron before the skin.”

“Yours?”

He turned it over. The worn bend had nearly vanished beneath his thumb.

“Could have been.”

Daska came to sit on his other side. Her left hand had been bound around a small wooden roll to keep the fingers from closing too tightly. She looked at the buckle while the old man described how the straps were cut.

“The little tongue,” she said when he paused. “You were telling me.”

“Several times.”

“Yes.” She looked down at her bound hand. “I’m sorry.”

Durin considered the apology with the solemnity he had given every offer of beer since they arrived.

“Hulven never listened either.”

“Don’t make me regret it.”

He laughed, unexpectedly, a small loose sound from deep in his chest. It became a cough. Daska held his cup while he drank, waited until he could speak, and asked whether Hulven had ever learned to fit through the return.

“No. We had to send his dinners another way.”

Rosceline left them to it.

They stayed three nights. Senn’s right hand needed cleaning twice a day. His knee would take longer. He gave the healer the name of a brother who worked at a quarry farther down the valley, and the keeper sent a carter to find him. Rosceline paid the man’s immediate expenses in front of Senn. She did not call them compensation. He watched the money change hands and offered her nothing in return.

Rell’s remaining men came to the inn on the second morning.

They asked for their broker. The keeper called Rosceline down while Daska stood by the inner stair. Rosceline told them he was dead and where his body lay. She did not offer to return for it. One of the men looked toward Senn’s room. Senn had heard the voices and called his name.

The two spoke privately. When the man emerged, he had lost most of his interest in recovering a stone for somebody whose agent might not pay him.

“His papers?” he asked Rosceline.

“Still on him.”

“I stayed,” Senn called down the stair. “If the agent says I ran, you tell him what you’ve seen. I want the whole day’s pay.”

The man looked toward Rosceline before he answered.

“I’ll tell him.”

He left with his companion before lunch. The keeper watched them go, then added another night to the guide’s account. She said she preferred settling what could be settled.

Briska fitted a new strap to Durin’s buckle while the healer checked her arm. The work was awkward with one hand. Pello held the leather while she made the holes, sitting with his swollen ankle on a stool. Durin supervised until they both looked at him.

“It’s good,” he said at last.

“Yes,” Briska answered. “It is.”

They paid his full balance on the morning they left. His lodging and meals were settled for another three nights. He intended to stay longer, he said; there were people in the district who required his account.

Rosceline took his hand before boarding the cart.

“Do try to keep the boy in it.”

Durin’s eyes moved over her face. Then he bowed as far as his belly allowed.

“A commander remembers his people.”

She could have corrected him. The mule chose that moment to bite the edge of Pello’s scarf, and everybody’s attention was required elsewhere.

When they started down the road, Durin stood beside the keeper beneath the red goat. His new strap showed bright against the old coat. He had already begun explaining how he held a mountain together while five young people ran about beneath it.

Daska turned on the cart seat to listen until the road took them out of earshot.

Chapter 18 — The second recovery

The Countess received the stone with everyone at the table.

Briska carried the original outer case into the room and opened it herself. Pello sat nearest her, ankle still stiff inside a boot unlaced above the instep. Daska had removed the roll from her hand but could not quite lay her palm flat. Ilaro kept his notebook closed until Celise had shaded the lamp.

Rosceline wore a green coat. The blue one remained with a woman who had been asked whether the stain could be taken out and had answered, after examining it, that a different sleeve might be cheaper.

Serafine looked at the pale capsule.

“Charged?”

“The release hasn’t been touched,” Briska said. “The mounting opened mechanically. The seam is sound. You can inspect the outside without opening it.”

“My reader will do that.”

“Your reader should know the installation fed on an examination. The capsule doesn’t, as far as we’ve observed. Those are different claims.”

The Countess inclined her head. Briska gave her the case only after receiving the acknowledgment.

Celise took it to a side table. The reader, a quiet woman with ink stains at the roots of her nails, inspected the fitting against the drawing and tested the seam with a fine strip of parchment. She held her hands near the capsule without touching it. A little tension entered the room. Rosceline listened beneath the fountain’s irregular sound and heard no working begin.

The woman nodded once to Serafine.

Five purses were brought, with the advance deducted and the road expenses accounted for separately. The Countess paid for Senn’s treatment without asking that it be assigned to a different category. She read the amount charged for Durin’s extra lodging and smiled beneath the veil.

“He has remained behind?”

“He found a willing audience,” Rosceline said. “I thought it cruel to remove him before he exhausted it.”

“That may take some time.”

“We’ve paid for three days. After that I suggest the district chooses a representative.”

Daska took a cake. It was the first thing she had eaten that morning without testing whether she could hold it properly.

The Countess asked for their account.

Rosceline gave the entrance, the guardians, the broker’s interception and the extraction in that order. She described Rell’s certainty about his seal. The story was accurate as far as it went. She could feel Ilaro beside her deciding how far to let it go.

“Then he tested the case,” she said. “Against our advice.”

“What did you say just before?” Ilaro asked.

She looked at him. “I warned him about the workings.”

“After that.”

Celise stopped writing.

Rosceline could have made him supply the words himself. His head hurt; he had ridden five days with a light that kept moving between the cart’s tilt and the road. She did not want to buy that particular advantage in front of the Countess.

“I suggested his seal might not reach through the capsule. I said commercial seals were made for crates.”

“And then?”

“That I would be interested to see how his agent priced the result.”

Ilaro opened the notebook and checked what he had written at the inn. He closed it again without looking pleased.

“He used it,” Rosceline said.

“Yes,” the Countess answered. “He did.”

Nobody offered her the rest of the sentence.

Daska set the half-eaten cake on her plate.

“He offered terms at the ladder.”

Rosceline looked down at her own hands. She had scrubbed the nail beside her thumb until the skin became sore. No blood remained.

“He did.”

“Did you accept?” Serafine asked.

“No.”

The fountain sounded behind the screen. Outside, a woman was calling to somebody who had left a basket in the rain.

The Countess turned to Celise. “His agent will need to hear that the claim has changed circumstances. Ask for the leaseholders’ objections before writing.”

She took Rosceline’s private agreement when it was placed beside the purses. Beneath the first completed recovery she wrote a second, signed it and let the ink dry before returning the paper.

Two remain.

Rosceline read the words. They had not become less desirable through being written at this table.

Briska waited until the paper was folded.

“Next time the known hazards come to all of us. Together. If you have another letter, or another drawing, we see it before we leave.”

“I gave you the drawings I had,” Serafine said.

“You gave her something after we left.”

Rosceline’s hand closed around the folded agreement.

“Private business,” she said.

Briska looked at her, then back at the Countess. “Keep the private business. The instructions for the job come to the table.”

Celise began writing again.

“Agreed,” Serafine said. “Known hazards and instructions affecting the company’s work. You will all receive them.”

It left room for things neither of them had said. Rosceline saw Briska recognize that and accept the narrower agreement. The artisan took her purse with her good hand and went downstairs with Pello.

Daska followed Ilaro. At the doorway she paused, looking back toward Rosceline. For a moment Rosceline thought she meant to say something about the ladder.

“We’re eating at Briska’s,” the monk said. “After the lamps are lit.”

Then she went.

Serafine brought out Sarem’s book.

Rosceline sat with it on her knees. She chose the mark she had heard before leaving for the mountain. His voice told her not to look so pleased with herself. The laugh followed, breaking in the same place.

She did not laugh with it this time. She listened until the working stopped, then turned the page to see her own small writing beneath his. One word had been scratched out so thoroughly the paper had nearly worn through.

She could not remember what it had been. She closed the book herself.

Briska had set a brass collar and a narrow pipe above the cups on her workshop table. Rosceline recognized the bell’s spring. The rest of the tools had been pushed aside for a fish large enough to make Daska reconsider the position of every dish.

Rosceline put down the wine and the warm loaf. Daska took the bread; Pello took the bottles. Ilaro moved a chair into the space beside him without looking up from his folded account. A scrap of pale canvas shielded his face from the lamp. Through the open windows came canal air and the smell of someone else’s supper.

Rosceline sat. The contrivance stood over the cups with the dignity of a mechanism expecting to be admired.

“What does it do?” Rosceline asked.

“Pours.”

“How fortunate. We have been suffering under the old arrangement for years.”

Briska fitted the neck of a bottle into the collar. “It stops at the rim. The float lifts the arm and closes the valve.”

“Which rim?” Ilaro asked.

“The one under it.”

Pello set a cup beneath the pipe. Briska turned the little release. Wine ran into the cup, rose smoothly to the proper height and stopped.

Rosceline applauded once, softly. Briska looked pleased despite herself. She moved the first cup and set another in its place.

The float stuck.

Wine burst from the pipe, struck the second cup’s edge and spread across the table. Pello lifted the bread before it reached the crust. Daska caught the fish platter with her good hand. Ilaro held his account above the flow and waited while Briska wrestled the bottle free.

The bottle emptied just before she succeeded.

For a moment nobody said anything. Briska stood with the wet mechanism in one hand and the empty bottle in the other. A drop ran from her elbow and struck the floor.

Daska began to laugh. She pressed her bound hand against her mouth, remembered the pain and laughed more carefully.

Rosceline had the one full cup.

She put it in the middle of the table, above the spreading wine, and pushed a dry cloth toward Briska. Pello reached for the second bottle.

“By hand,” he said.

Briska let him have it. Then she sat down, broke a piece of bread and began to eat before anyone could give her a tool.
