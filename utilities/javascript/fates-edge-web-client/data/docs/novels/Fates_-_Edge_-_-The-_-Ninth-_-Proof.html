Chapter 1 — Another Term

The porter remembered the trunk.

“You had that last time.”

“I’ve grown attached to it,” Lerris said.

“The corner took a piece out of the door.”

“Then you’ve something to remember it by.”

The porter did not smile. Lerris put Constano’s signed chit on the desk and stopped trying to improve the encounter.

Behind him, a carter waited with the trunk balanced on the lip of his barrow. He had been promised an easy delivery. His expression suggested that the promise had already been revised several times on the way up from the quay. Lerris handed him a coin before the porter could find another defect in the luggage.

The entry was still there under Levis. He signed beneath it, paid what remained of the term’s charge, and watched the porter sand the wet ink. Beside the ordinary conditions of residence, a shorter note referred to permission for the closed collections.

“That hasn’t changed,” the porter said.

“I wasn’t asking it to.”

“You’ll need a separate order.”

“Yes.”

The new key was brass and had a red thread through its bow. Lerris took it without asking whether his old room was available. He had chosen the north dormitory because it was cheap enough not to require another letter home and because it was close to the public rooms. It stood across a court from the damaged range, not within it. The porter made the distinction twice.

Markus was waiting outside.

He looked thinner. Lerris noticed that before he noticed the new coat, which had been made to fit him as he was now rather than as somebody hoped he would be after a good winter. He stood with one hand on the barrow’s handle while the carter tightened a strap. For a moment Lerris wanted to embrace him with such force that the older injuries might still have mattered. Instead he caught his shoulder.

“You’ve let him bring it all this way.”

“I thought he might know how.”

“I meant you could have met me at the quay.”

“I did. Yesterday.”

Lerris looked at him.

“The boat was late.”

“I gathered.”

“Did you wait long?”

Markus took the key from him and examined the tag. “Long enough to purchase a fish I didn’t want.”

The carter cleared his throat. They went to find the room.

It was narrow, with a bed along one wall and a table beneath the window. Somebody had repaired a crack in the plaster and left the new patch unpainted. The window looked onto a tiled roof, beyond which the upper part of a tree was visible. Lerris stood on tiptoe to see more of the tree.

“Don’t,” Markus said.

“What?”

“The table won’t bear you.”

“I hadn’t intended—”

His brother looked at him.

They got the trunk into the corner without damaging anything. The carter accepted another coin, tested it with his thumb, and departed. Lerris sat on the bed. It gave beneath him with a long complaint.

“Well,” he said.

Markus opened the window. There was a smell of warm tiles and somebody’s onions frying.

“It’s better when the air comes through.”

“You’ve been in it.”

“I asked to see it.”

“Did you try the bed?”

“No.”

“You should have. We might have escaped.”

Markus laughed, and did not have to stop afterwards. He looked out at the tree and asked whether his brother had eaten.

They went through the outer courts to the stalls. Lerris had meant to ask after Alayse at once, and discovered that he was waiting for a place where he could hear the answer without someone pushing between them. At the bread stall Markus stopped to inspect a tray, and Lerris asked there.

“She is well,” his brother said. “Busy. She sent you a message.”

“What message?”

“To write before asking her to send things you’ve left behind.”

“I haven’t left anything.”

“She said you’d say that.”

Markus bought two portions and paid before Lerris found his purse. They ate standing beneath the awning, breaking the crust into the stew. The broth was too hot. Lerris burned the roof of his mouth and tried to conceal it, badly.

His brother had been back for some weeks. He was working on the Valvano papers, in the public rooms, for hours he chose beforehand and mostly kept. He said this without inviting praise, and Lerris was careful not to give it.

“Mostly?”

“The copying takes longer than the estimates.”

“It always did.”

“Yes.” Markus scraped the last of the stew with his bread. “Sometimes I leave it unfinished.”

Lerris looked away before that could become a subject. A child beside the stall was attempting to feed a dog from a spoon. The dog had understood perfectly what was required; the child had not yet allowed for the tongue.

They walked on together. In the catalog hall Aqyl stood behind a desk covered in slips, trimming their uneven edges with a small blade. He wore linen, the cuffs rolled back. His hair was tied out of the way, and a smear of paste marked one wrist.

Lerris had not expected him to look so occupied.

“Another term,” the tutor said.

“So I’m told.”

Aqyl asked after the Valvano legacy. Markus answered briefly. There was no warmth between them that Lerris could identify, but no quarrel either. Aqyl cut a fresh slip, wrote the public collection’s mark on it, and gave it to Lerris.

“For the first consultation.”

“Do I need a sponsor?”

“For this material, no.”

The slip was ordinary cream paper. Aqyl’s name appeared at the foot, followed by the addition he used in formal writing, s. Aqyl. Lerris put it inside his coat. There were other people waiting, and the tutor had already turned to hear a question from the far end of the desk.

Outside, bells began to sound. Markus slowed. Lerris slowed with him, neither pretending that they needed to examine the fountain. When the last note had faded, his brother put a hand briefly on his arm.

“Tomorrow?”

“For breakfast?”

“If you’re awake.”

“I’ve become abstemious and diligent.”

“Then you’ll be hungry.”

They parted at the stair.

That evening Lerris wrote to Constano. He described the room, the food, his brother’s coat. He wrote that Markus was steady, then paused over the word. It would please Constano. It was also what he had seen that day. He left it.

When he shook out his traveling coat, a second slip fell from the inner pocket.

This one was narrower, bone-colored, enclosed in a paper sleeve sealed along one edge. A long number had been written on the outside with a precision that made the numerals look stamped. He could not remember receiving it.

He set Aqyl’s consultation slip beside it. Different paper, different hand.

Someone spoke on the stair, too quietly for him to distinguish a word. Lerris turned. The sound did not come again. He listened until he heard a door close, then looked back at the sleeve.

He ought to return it in the morning. He knew this with no effort at all.

The seal opened easily beneath his thumbnail.

Inside lay a thin bright sliver, colder against his finger than the room could account for. He tipped it onto the table. It held the lamplight along one edge, although he could not see an edge fine enough to catch it.

His letter remained open beside him.

He added that a card had come into his possession, perhaps with the catalog slip, and that he meant to return it. He began describing the metal, stopped, and put the pen down.

In the corridor, footsteps went past his door.

He folded the sleeve around the sliver without touching it again. Then he finished the letter, sealed it, and moved the little packet to the far side of the table before getting into bed.

Chapter 2 — The Other Desk

By morning it looked like something that had broken off a clasp.

Lerris did not open the sleeve to improve the comparison. He put it in his purse, apart from the coins, and took Constano’s letter downstairs to be sent. The porter accepted the letter and asked whether there was anything else.

For a moment Lerris considered the packet.

“Where do mislaid catalog cards go?”

“catalog desk.”

“Of course.”

His brother was already at breakfast. There was a dish of little fried fish between the bowls, and Markus was removing their heads with an attention that seemed excessive until Lerris tasted one.

“You’ve been buying these since yesterday?”

“They aren’t the same fish.”

“I’m relieved.”

He told Markus about the card. He had decided on the way down to say it plainly. Even so, he heard himself make the story amusing: an unearned invitation, a number long enough to be an account of his debts, something metallic where there ought to have been a book.

Markus stopped eating.

“Have you got it?”

Lerris brought out the sleeve. His brother did not open it.

“Take it to the Wardens.”

“Not the catalog?”

“No.”

“Do you recognize it?”

“The description. I gave them a piece of metal yesterday morning. Dark, not bright. It may have nothing to do with yours.”

“What piece?”

Markus laid his knife beside the bowl.

“Something I should have surrendered before.”

Lerris waited. His brother rubbed a bit of fish skin from his thumb.

“I kept it wrapped. Then I kept the wrapping in a box and told myself that was different. Yesterday I took it out.”

“Did something happen?”

“No.”

“Then why yesterday?”

Markus looked tired of the question before answering it.

“Because I could do it yesterday.”

Lerris took the sleeve back. He wanted to ask where the dark piece had come from, whether it belonged to the work in the old notebook, why Markus had not mentioned it while they were examining the damaged pages. The breakfast room was filling. A man carrying a tray waited for the bench beside them.

“I’ll take it there.”

His brother moved his bowl to give the man room.

“Tell them you opened it.”

“I intended to.”

“All right.”

The Wardens’ clerk worked beneath a window protected by fine wire. He had a pair of scales, a row of stoppered jars, and a shallow tray lined with cloth. When Lerris described the object, he drew the tray toward him.

“Leave it in the paper.”

He inspected the number first. Then he opened the sleeve with a wooden spatula and turned the sliver without touching it. He weighed the packet, weighed the metal, and entered both separately.

“Where did you receive this?”

Lerris told him.

“Did Master Aqyl give it to you?”

“I don’t know. He gave me this.”

The consultation slip was examined and returned.

“Any other person handle your coat?”

“The carter. Possibly. Before I put it on.”

“Did you leave it unattended?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

Lerris began to reconstruct the day. The questions were reasonable. By the fifth he wanted to make the clerk responsible for how foolish the answers made him feel.

The man wrote without comment.

At last he put the sliver in a little box. The sleeve remained on the desk.

“Do you know what it is?” Lerris asked.

“I know which keeper must see it.”

“May I speak to the keeper?”

“You may leave a request.”

He did. The clerk wrote a receipt and gave him a copy of the number from the sleeve, together with the number of his deposit. Lerris checked that the two were different.

“Yes,” the clerk said. “One arrived with it. One says where we’ve put it.”

Outside, he felt an unreasonable lightness. The object was behind a guarded door, entered in a book, and somebody else would decide how it ought to be handled.

He went to the public collection and worked until midday.

His subject was a comparison of toll exemptions. He had chosen it partly because the records were available and partly because he could imagine Constano reading a finished account without wondering what use had been made of another term’s expenses. Three villages appeared under two different spellings. A bridge had been charged as a ford for more than a decade. He found the correction and was pleased enough to copy it neatly.

When he packed up, a woman at the next table asked whether he had finished with the weights book.

“I wasn’t using it.”

“No? It came over with your papers.”

A narrow volume lay beneath the last wrapper. He lifted it. On the inside cover was the same long number that had appeared on the sleeve.

He went cold, and then angry at the speed with which he had gone cold.

The woman watched him.

“Is something wrong?”

“Where did this come from?”

“An attendant brought it.”

“Which attendant?”

“I wasn’t looking.”

She had a broad ink stain along one finger and a tiny piece of thread caught in her cuff. Nothing about her appeared prepared for him. That was no assurance. He knew it, and resented knowing it.

He took the book to the desk.

The attendant checked the mark and found it in the delivery list. It had been requested for consultation that morning. The request bore his recorded name.

“I didn’t request it.”

The attendant called for the original slip. They searched one pile, then another. The woman from the next table waited nearby, holding her own papers.

Eventually the slip appeared. Beneath the number, in a hand unlike Lerris’s, someone had written for Levis Fenwood. There was no signature claiming to be his.

“Then someone requested it for you,” the attendant said.

“Who?”

“I couldn’t say.”

The book was a printed catalog of measures, with handwritten additions at the back. He opened it on the desk. No metal fell out. No voice spoke. Between two pages lay a sheet inviting the reader to a comparison of disputed positions after the evening meal, at a house beyond the dyers’ street.

The woman made a small, embarrassed sound.

“Oh,” she said. “That’s ours.”

Lerris looked at her.

“You could have said.”

“I hadn’t seen the paper.”

“Did you put the card in my coat?”

“What card?”

The attendant cleared his throat. There were other readers waiting.

Lerris took the invitation out, left the book on the desk, and went with the woman into the court.

Chapter 3 — A Place at the Table

Her name was Vesrin. She repaired calculations for people who preferred not to be told that this was what they needed.

“Accounts?” Lerris asked.

“Sometimes. Often commentaries. A man divides by something he has not shown to be present and writes six pages about the result. Then he brings it to me because the seventh page has become difficult.”

They stood near the fountain. A group of students occupied its sunny side, sharing a parcel of food. Vesrin shifted out of the spray without interrupting herself.

“Do you play?” she asked.

“Several things.”

“Kon’reh.”

“Yes.”

“Then you may enjoy the evening. Nobody will require you to speak.”

He held up the invitation.

“Someone has gone to considerable trouble to ask me.”

“Sending a book across a room isn’t considerable trouble.”

“The number on the book was on the card.”

She looked at him more carefully.

“Was there metal in it?”

He did not answer at once.

Vesrin tucked her papers beneath her arm. “Have you still got it?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I gave it to the Wardens.”

Her expression changed, but he could not decide whether the change was concern or merely the calculation of an inconvenience.

“Did they give you a receipt?”

“Yes.”

“Keep it.”

“Why?”

“If whoever gave it to you asks for it back, you can say where it is.”

Lerris stared at her. She was making something ugly and inexplicable sound like a borrowed tool.

“Do you know who sent it?”

“I can ask.”

“Ask whom?”

“The person who arranges the invitations.”

“And the pieces of metal?”

“I don’t arrange either.”

A bell began somewhere behind the hall. Vesrin looked toward the sound with immediate annoyance.

“I have to go. Come or don’t. If you come, bring the ordinary slip. They have room for visitors. You don’t need the other thing.”

She left him beside the fountain.

Lerris read the invitation again. It named a time and a house, promised supper at a modest charge, and requested that guests bring no borrowed books. There was no mark of a patron, no threatening flourish, nothing he could show Thorne that would justify more than advice not to go.

He found Istren after her lesson, as she was wiping a board.

“Do you know a woman called Vesrin?”

“Yes.”

“Do you trust her?”

“To do what?”

He helped gather the little wooden counters her pupils had left on the floor. One had rolled beneath a chest. He knelt, reached, and found two more.

“She invited me to a discussion.”

“Then she’ll expect you to listen.”

“Is she good?”

“Better than she can afford to be. People take corrections more kindly when they think of them as assistance.”

He put the counters in their box.

“Would you go?”

“To her discussion? If I had time.”

He hesitated. Istren waited with the damp cloth in her hand.

“There was another invitation. Or something that might have been one.”

He told her about the sleeve and the metal. She listened, then set down the cloth.

“Is this the same thing?”

“The number is.”

“Then ask them why.”

“I did.”

“Before you go, I mean.”

“She didn’t know.”

Istren considered him.

“You’re asking whether I can tell you it’s safe.”

“No.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“I’d like you to be able to,” he admitted.

“I know Vesrin’s work. I’ve never been to that house.”

He thanked her. This time he did not turn the answer into something more convenient on the way out.

He wrote a note to Markus stating where he was going and when he expected to return. He gave it to the porter, with instructions to deliver it before supper. He also told the porter where he would be. The man entered the address without enthusiasm.

“If I’m late—”

“Shall I send someone?”

“Yes.”

“How late?”

They agreed on an hour. Lerris paid for the extra errand in advance, embarrassed by the transaction and steadied by it.

The house beyond the dyers’ street had a green door and a broken scraper. A woman admitted him, took the supper money, and told him the gathering was upstairs. Someone had hung damp stockings near the kitchen fire. He could smell cabbage and hot fat.

Vesrin sat at a long table with four other people. She looked relieved to see him, which he disliked, and then pleased, which he liked before he could prevent himself.

“I asked,” she said quietly as he took a chair. “The host will speak to you.”

“Who is the host?”

“He’ll be here after supper.”

A bowl was passed. Lerris took bread and listened to an argument about whether an unfinished game could be reproduced from a player’s account. One man maintained that the account omitted what the player had considered and rejected. A woman opposite him asked whether he proposed to record every foolish idea that passed through his head before moving.

“You would need a second table,” Vesrin said.

They laughed. The man made a bow from his chair and took more cabbage.

Lerris found himself joining in. The problem was interesting. He had seen Caldris reconstruct a position faithfully enough for Markus to recognize it. He had also watched his brother discover how much vanished when a few leaves were cut from a notebook. He used the first example and withheld the second.

After supper a board was brought out. Its pieces were ordinary, mismatched, with felt on the bottoms to spare the table. Vesrin invited Lerris to take one side while she set a position on the other.

“Whose game?” he asked.

“Nobody’s. It’s a problem.”

He studied it. An apparent escape failed after two moves. Another could be closed if the opponent gave up an advantage elsewhere. He began to enjoy himself.

Half an hour passed before he noticed the man standing near the door.

He was older than Vesrin, plainly dressed, holding a cup with both hands. He waited until Lerris had finished his move before approaching.

“You returned the metal,” he said.

“Yes.”

“That was sensible.”

“Did you send it?”

“No. I sent the book.”

“Who sent the card?”

“I don’t know who put it in your coat.”

“That isn’t quite an answer.”

“No,” the man said. “It isn’t.”

The others were rearranging the pieces, discussing the move Lerris had made. Nobody appeared to be listening to them.

“Why invite me?” Lerris asked.

“Because you’ve watched a record fail someone who knew how to read it.”

He thought of Markus’s missing pages and felt the room recede a little around him.

“Who told you that?”

“Your statement was read at the university inquiry.”

“Not publicly.”

“No.”

Lerris put both hands on the edge of the table. He could leave. He had told people where he was, and the door remained open behind the man. He wanted the man to see him leave.

“What do you want?”

“To show you a distinction your brother was not given.”

“What distinction?”

“Between something that copies an answer and something that requires you to answer. People put them in the same locked room. That doesn’t make them the same.”

“Can you prove it?”

“Some of it.”

Vesrin looked up from the board. She had heard the last exchange.

“Not tonight,” she said.

The man glanced at her, then nodded.

“Not tonight. You should go when you said you would.”

Lerris stood. His disappointment was immediate and humiliating.

He thanked Vesrin for the game, collected his coat, and walked back through streets that were still busy. At the gate the porter looked up from his book.

“Back, then.”

“Yes.”

“I haven’t sent anyone.”

“No need.”

In his room, Lerris wrote down the position while he still remembered it. Then he wrote the man’s words beneath, found that he had improved one of them, and corrected it.

His purse and his name and every ordinary thing he could think to test remained his own.

He read what he had written once more before putting out the lamp.

Chapter 4 — What He Had Kept

Markus had Lerris’s note beside his breakfast.

He had folded it twice more than was necessary. When Lerris sat down, he moved it away from the butter dish and continued eating. There was a streak of yellow along one edge.

“Did you get back?” he asked.

“Apparently.”

“I meant when you said.”

“Yes.”

“What did they want?”

Lerris had rehearsed an answer on the stairs. It had contained the game, the useful discussion, and Vesrin’s work, all in an order that made the evening sound pleasantly uneventful. Faced with the little folded note, he could hear the arrangement too clearly to use it.

“One of them had read my statement.”

His brother put down his spoon.

“Which statement?”

“The inquiry.”

“Did he say so?”

“Yes.”

“And you stayed?”

“I left at the time I’d arranged.”

Markus pushed his bowl aside. Lerris wished he would finish eating. The interruption made what he had done seem worse, and he could not find a reasonable way to object to it.

He described the man, the room, the words he had written down. When he reached the distinction between copying an answer and requiring one, Markus looked at the window.

“It’s possible,” he said.

Lerris stopped.

“That they’re different?”

“That he’s described two different things. It doesn’t follow that he can tell them apart.”

“He offered to show me.”

“Of course he did.”

“You haven’t met him.”

“No.”

There was no satisfaction in the agreement. Lerris took a piece of bread and discovered that he did not want it.

“Would you come?”

“No.”

The answer was so immediate that he looked up.

“If it were in a public room—”

“No, Lerris.”

“I don’t mean you should do anything. Only listen.”

His brother drew the folded note toward him and flattened it with his palm.

“I’m not going to sit there while somebody tells you what happened to me and watch you look across to see whether he’s right.”

Lerris flushed. He had imagined exactly that, though in his imagining Markus had been pleased to correct the man.

“I could ask him to write it.”

“You can ask him anything you like.”

“That sounds as if you’re washing your hands of it.”

Markus looked back at him. For an instant Lerris saw how much his brother wanted to leave the table.

Instead Markus took up his spoon. The porridge had thickened while they talked. He added water from the little jug, stirred, and ate a mouthful before speaking.

“I gave them the metal because I didn’t want it in my room any longer. I don’t want its acquaintances either.”

“Was it Aqyl’s?”

“The piece I had? Yes.”

Lerris waited.

“A key, or near enough. I used it years ago.”

“He took that from you.”

“He sent it back with my papers some years later. He said the lock had been changed.”

“Did you try it?”

“No. I left it wrapped. Then it was put away with things I didn’t look at.”

“Until yesterday.”

“Until before you arrived.”

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

Markus’s spoon struck the bowl.

“Would you have given me yours if I’d kept mine?”

“I did give it in.”

“Yes. You did.”

His brother began gathering the crumbs at the edge of the plate with one finger. Lerris could not decide whether the question had been an accusation or a confession. He was ashamed of how much the distinction mattered to him.

They finished breakfast badly.

In the court, Markus stopped beneath the arch. People went around them carrying books and baskets. Lerris moved aside to let a woman pass with a sleeping child against her shoulder.

“I’ll ask who had the statement,” Markus said.

“I can do that.”

“I know. I’m asking about mine as well.”

“All right.”

“And if you see him again, don’t offer my notebook.”

“I hadn’t intended to.”

Markus nodded. He looked relieved enough that Lerris wanted to tell him he would not go back.

He did not say it.

They parted, and Lerris went to the toll records. He sat before the same columns he had found satisfying the previous day. A bridge, a ford, three villages with two spellings apiece. He read a paragraph four times without retaining it.

At midday he found a mistake in his own copy. He had carried a charge from one year into the next, giving a household a debt it did not owe. It was an ordinary error, easily corrected. He scraped the ink carefully and waited for the paper to dry before writing the figure again.

That afternoon he finished the comparison.

He took it to Vesrin the following morning.

Chapter 5 — The Borrowed Hour

Vesrin worked above a maker of combs.

Fine pale dust lay on the lower stair. On the landing, a boy was using a brush to gather it into a pan. He told Lerris to wait and then, when Lerris did, looked startled by his obedience.

Upstairs, Vesrin had three open books and a headache.

She said so before he sat down.

“If you’re bringing a fourth book, come back tomorrow.”

“Two sheets.”

“Those can be worse.”

He laid down the comparison. She examined the first page, turned to the second, and reached for a clean scrap.

“Do you want reassurance or checking?”

“Checking.”

She named a price. He paid it.

For the next little while he listened to the comb-maker below. The work had a dry, repetitive sound, interrupted by conversations he could not quite hear. There was a pot of something growing on Vesrin’s sill, its leaves gray with dust. She had tied one stem to a pen that no longer had a nib.

She found two places where his conclusion outran his figures and one where he had been too cautious.

“This charge belongs to the whole crossing,” she said. “You’ve divided it between the households and then charged the crossing again.”

Lerris bent over the page. She waited while he followed her finger.

“Oh.”

“Yes.”

“Can I take that out?”

“You should.”

He did. It improved the account considerably, at the cost of the paragraph he had most enjoyed writing. Vesrin read his replacement and put a small mark beside it.

When she had finished, he asked the host’s name.

“Neravel.”

“What’s his work?”

“He brings people together who have pieces of the same question.”

“That must be difficult to put on a bill.”

She smiled despite the headache.

“He has money. Other people send him things.”

“Like statements from a closed inquiry.”

Vesrin’s smile went.

“I didn’t know that when I spoke to you.”

“Did you know about my brother?”

“That he’d been hurt. Most people here know that.”

“Did you know why I’d been invited?”

She moved his two sheets aside.

“I was told you could play and had been among the people brought out. I thought you might ask better questions than some of the others.”

“What others?”

“Men who come because they think keeping an appointment in an ordinary room is beneath them.”

Lerris looked toward the door. He had come through it with a corrected account and a paid receipt; he could leave with those and have gained something real.

“Do you believe him?” he asked.

“About everything? No.”

“About the things that happen in those rooms.”

“I’ve seen something.”

She took a second scrap, wrote a short line, hesitated, and drew it toward herself again.

“What?”

“A woman I worked with couldn’t remember a tune.”

Lerris waited.

“She’d sung it while she copied. For years. It irritated me. Then she went to one of the lower consultations and came back without it. She remembered the words. She remembered being told to stop. She couldn’t find a note.”

“People forget.”

“Yes.”

“So how—”

“Because it came back.”

The comb-maker called up the stair. Vesrin shut her eyes.

“It’s on the shelf!” she shouted. “Where it was when you asked before!”

A moment later the man called his thanks.

She opened her eyes. “They gave her an hour with it. I sat with her. She sang until her throat hurt. Then she stopped halfway through and asked me why I was crying.”

Lerris looked at her hands. They were flat on the table.

“Could you have taught her?”

“I tried.”

“Did she ask for this?”

“She asked to have it back.”

“I mean losing it.”

“I wasn’t there.”

He wanted to hear the story again, with the omissions filled. Vesrin began arranging the books she had moved to make room for his work.

“You can ask Neravel what they did,” she said. “I’m telling you what I saw.”

“Is she still here?”

“No.”

“Where—”

“She went away. I won’t send you after her.”

He sat back. Downstairs, something clattered onto the floor and the comb-maker began laughing at himself.

Vesrin handed over the corrected sheets.

“I don’t think you should bring your brother,” she said.

“He won’t come.”

“Good.”

“But you go.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

She looked at the scrap on which she had begun writing. He could see only three words, none of them remarkable.

“I want to know whether she can have it for longer.”

She folded the scrap and put it beneath the pot on the sill.

Lerris took his work. At the door he asked whether the woman had paid for the hour.

“She paid before,” Vesrin said.

He went down slowly, careful of the dust on the stair.

Chapter 6 — Two Impressions

Neravel agreed to meet in a public room.

He arrived late, with a packet under his arm and an apology that made no attempt to be interesting. Lerris had chosen a table near the attendant’s desk. The windows were open; a woman at the far end was reading aloud to a student whose hand had been bandaged. Nothing could be said quietly enough to be entirely private.

“You’ve asked about your statement,” Neravel said.

“My brother has.”

“I had a copy from a man employed to compare the accounts. I paid for his time.”

“Was he permitted to give it to you?”

“I didn’t ask.”

“You knew.”

Neravel put the packet down.

“I knew he was willing to take the money.”

Lerris felt the urge to stand. He stayed seated, hating the man’s apparent confidence that he would.

“Who?”

“I won’t give you his name.”

“Then why tell me any of it?”

“Because if I tell you I found it by accident, we spend the afternoon discussing whether I’m lying.”

“We might discuss that anyway.”

“Yes.”

Neravel loosened the packet. Inside were two pieces of wax protected between boards, each held in a shallow wooden frame. Their surfaces bore close, irregular marks.

“What are those?”

“Impressions of the same object, taken eleven days apart.”

“By you?”

“One by me. One by a keeper.”

“Where’s the object?”

“With the keeper.”

Lerris did not touch the frames. Neravel held each by its wooden rim, keeping his thumbs clear of the wax. The boards were scarcely broader than his spread hand. As he tilted them toward the lamp, Lerris saw the shallow ridges lift out of a dull, greasy surface; one edge bore the cloudy crescent of a fingerprint.

The marks resembled the teeth of a fine saw, but each tooth had a smaller notch cut into one side. In the second impression several notches faced the other way.

“Somebody altered it,” Lerris said.

“Perhaps.”

“Or you took the wax from something else.”

“Perhaps.”

“Is this the demonstration?”

“No. This is why I’m asking you to come to one.”

The woman reading at the end of the room paused to help her companion turn a page. Lerris watched them for a moment. Neither had looked toward him.

“Vesrin told me about the tune.”

Neravel waited.

“Was that your doing?”

“I helped arrange the return.”

“For an hour.”

“Yes.”

“Why only an hour?”

“Because that’s what we could obtain.”

“From whom?”

“The people keeping what she left.”

Lerris thought of the Wardens’ clerk, weighing metal separately from paper. He disliked the thought and could not make it leave.

“What did she get for it?”

“That isn’t my story to tell.”

“You bought mine.”

Neravel looked down at the wax. For the first time he seemed to have lost the next thing he meant to say.

“Yes,” he said eventually. “I did.”

Lerris let the silence remain. He had expected to enjoy obtaining an admission. Instead he wanted to know about the tune badly enough that he was afraid of what he would forgive to hear it.

Neravel covered one impression, leaving the other in the light.

“A man can keep an object in a locked box and discover that locking it hasn’t ended his dealings with it. He can also believe he’s being called when he isn’t. I don’t know which happened to your brother. I do know that the usual advice leaves both men watching the same box.”

“Have you read his account too?”

“No.”

“Don’t.”

Neravel nodded once.

“That isn’t a request.”

“I heard you.”

Lerris looked at the uncovered wax. A little hair had become trapped near its edge, entirely ordinary, bent where the surface had set around it.

“What would I be asked to do?”

“Watch. Make a record. Check it against another person’s afterwards.”

“No moves?”

“Not at this consultation.”

“No name given?”

“They already know the name you use here.”

“That’s not the same as my giving it.”

“No,” Neravel said. “It isn’t.”

He produced a second paper. This one named the Library of Keys, a time in the afternoon, and a keeper responsible for admitting three visitors. There was a seal. Lerris studied it without pretending he could authenticate it by looking.

“I’ll ask at their desk.”

“You should.”

“And I’ll bring someone.”

“The permission is for three. Vesrin, you, and me.”

“Then get another.”

Neravel considered him. “I can ask.”

“Do that.”

He left the paper but took away the wax.

Lerris remained at the table. He had won none of the answers he had meant to demand. He had, however, made the man leave without a decision, and the small satisfaction of that was enough to keep him seated until his pulse settled.

Then he went to the Library of Keys.

The outer hall was cool. Along one wall, petitioners sat holding requests and little wrapped objects. A woman slept with her head against the stone. Beside her, a man kept a covered bowl level on his knees with both hands.

Lerris approached the desk and presented Neravel’s paper.

The keeper who received it read the seal, checked a book, and asked him to wait. He stood beside a pillar while two petitioners were admitted through a side door. Neither returned before the keeper came back.

“The appointment is entered,” she said. “The terms will be explained before admission.”

“By whom?”

“Keeper Lian’s attendant.”

“May I ask for another person to come?”

“You may ask.”

He gave Caldris’s name, then added that Caldris had not yet agreed. The keeper wrote it down without treating that as a difficulty.

“Is the object at the consultation the one I handed in?”

“Bring your receipt.”

He produced it. She compared the numbers, went away again, and returned more quickly this time.

“No. Your deposit remains where it was placed.”

“Can I have a record of that?”

She made one.

Outside, he read the new slip. The ink was still wet. He folded the paper carefully so the writing would not blot.

He would ask Caldris that evening.

On the way back, he found himself thinking about the woman with the lost tune. Not about her bargain, which he did not know, or her return, which he had not seen. He wondered whether, during that hour, she had chosen to sing it slowly.

Chapter 7 — A Second Chair

Caldris was having a coat fitted.

Lerris found him standing on a low platform while a woman knelt at his feet with pins between her lips. The coat was dark blue, better cloth than anything Lerris had seen him wear at the university. One sleeve remained detached. It lay over a chair like the arm of a defeated man.

“Don’t laugh,” Caldris said.

“I hadn’t.”

“You were preparing.”

The woman removed a pin from her mouth.

“If you turn again, I’ll put this through you.”

Caldris faced the window. Lerris waited beside the door, enjoying himself despite the business that had brought him.

“An appointment?” he asked.

“An examination.”

“Yours?”

“Theirs. I’m to assist.”

“Congratulations.”

“Don’t. They haven’t paid me.”

The woman lifted the hem, considered it, and let it fall. Caldris watched her hands with the anxious attention of a man who had spent more than he meant to. When she asked him to take the coat off, Lerris helped with the unfinished shoulder. He could feel the weight of the cloth through the lining.

Outside, Caldris stopped to look back through the window.

“Do you think the color’s too much?”

“No.”

“Good. What do you want?”

Lerris laughed.

“You’ve never waited through a fitting for my company.”

They walked toward the public rooms. Lerris described the invitation, including the metal and the inquiry statement. Caldris listened without interrupting until they reached the fountain.

“You gave my name already?”

“As someone I’d ask.”

“Ask first next time.”

“Yes.”

Caldris sat on the fountain’s rim, keeping his old coat clear of the damp.

“What am I meant to do there?”

“Watch. Tell me if I’ve missed something.”

“And if I tell you to leave?”

Lerris looked at him. A boy was dangling a length of string in the water beside them, trying to recover a floating leaf.

“I’ll listen.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I can’t promise to obey before I know the reason.”

Caldris nodded, almost approvingly.

“Then don’t bring me as the man who will make you obey.”

“I wasn’t—”

“You were doing something like it.”

Lerris sat beside him. He remembered paying the porter to send someone after him. The address had been useful; the receipt had been real. He had also enjoyed returning safely to prove that he had taken sufficient care.

“I want you to see it,” he said. “And I don’t want to be the only person deciding afterwards what happened.”

“That I can do.”

“You’ll come?”

“If the appointment doesn’t move. I have the examination the next morning.”

“It won’t move on my account.”

“Have that written down somewhere.”

The boy caught the leaf, raised it dripping, and discovered that he no longer wanted it. He dropped it back into the fountain.

Caldris asked to see Neravel’s invitation. He read it twice, then looked at the confirmation from the Library.

“Keep the original,” he said. “I’ll make my own note of the time.”

“Do you think it’s a trick?”

“I think a trick can be performed at an appointment.”

He stood. Lerris caught his sleeve before he could go.

“Thank you.”

Caldris glanced at the hand and then at him.

“Buy me supper afterwards. Somewhere with chairs. I’m tired of standing up with a bowl.”

Lerris released him.

The additional permission arrived the following afternoon. Caldris checked it himself at the desk. They agreed to meet outside the Library early enough to read whatever they were asked to sign.

Lerris spent the intervening morning on his toll comparison. Vesrin’s corrections had left a gap he could fill only by going back to the original account, and the volume was already in use. He waited nearly an hour while an elderly reader copied a passage with a magnifying lens held above the page. Once he would have asked whether he might borrow it briefly. Today he sat with his notes and tried to decide which question he needed answered first.

When the book became available, the answer was disappointingly simple. The charge had changed when a second landing was built. He copied the date and the carpenter’s payment, then closed the volume.

For the first time that week, he had finished something before the bell.

Chapter 8 — Water in a Shallow Dish

Keeper Lian’s attendant had put out four chairs.

There were no cushions, but the seats had been polished smooth by use. Caldris tested his with a hand before sitting. Vesrin was already there, fastening her papers into a wrapper. Neravel stood near the window. He greeted Lerris, then allowed the attendant to continue.

“You may leave before the object is uncovered,” she said. “After it is uncovered, you will remain seated until the keeper covers it again. If you become unwell, say so. We will stop.”

“What if someone stands?” Caldris asked.

“We cover the object.”

“And if the keeper can’t?”

“I cover it.”

The attendant pointed to the cloth folded beside her. Lerris looked for a second door and found one behind the chairs, open onto the passage by which they had entered. A guard stood there. He was looking at their hands, not the covered table.

“No names spoken during the examination,” the attendant continued. “Questions afterwards. You may record what you see, but no direct impression or traced copy is permitted.”

“Why?” Lerris asked.

“Because an impression has remained active after the object was put away.”

Neravel’s wax frames came back to him. He turned toward their host.

“Those things you showed me—”

“Spent,” Neravel said.

The attendant looked at him.

“Inspected and found unresponsive,” she said. “That is what the report says.”

Neravel inclined his head. Vesrin continued folding the end of her wrapper, pressing the crease with a thumbnail.

Lerris asked whether he could read the report. The attendant told him to enter a separate request after the consultation.

Then Lian came in.

She was smaller than he had expected. Her sleeves were tied back, and she carried a shallow dish in both hands. Water trembled in it as she crossed the room. She set it on a stand beside the table and waited while the ripples settled.

There was no greeting. She looked at each visitor in turn, stopping longest at Caldris. He bowed from his chair. Lian touched the water with two fingers.

Writing formed beneath them.

Lerris could read it from where he sat. The letters darkened as if the bottom of the dish had acquired grooves, then moved with the slight motion of the surface. He leaned forward before remembering that he had been told to remain seated, not motionless.

Have you been told what is required of you?

They answered. The attendant recorded each reply on an ordinary sheet. Lian passed her palm above the dish and the marks spread, thinned, and vanished.

The next sentence appeared more slowly.

You are here to observe a return. You are not asked to undertake one.

Vesrin’s foot moved beneath her chair. Lerris saw the toe press hard against the floor.

The keeper uncovered a small object in a wooden cradle. It was not a sliver. It resembled the broken end of an ivory comb, with three short teeth and a broad base stained almost black. A narrow strip of metal had been fitted into a cut along its side.

Beside it lay two tiles, one white and one gray.

Lian placed the gray tile against the cradle. Nothing happened. She moved it away and wrote in the dish.

The permitted interval begins when the second tile touches.

The attendant turned a small sandglass. Lian waited until she had reset it, then laid the white tile against the broken comb.

Lerris heard a man drawing breath.

The sound came from the object. It was close, uncomfortably close, as though someone had leaned beside his ear. Caldris stiffened. Across the table, Neravel kept his eyes lowered.

A voice said, “Lower at the far end.”

There was a scraping noise. The voice cursed, without anger, and another man laughed.

“That’s the end I’ve got.”

“I can see that.”

The scraping resumed. Something heavy struck wood. Then came the breath again, identical in its small catch.

“Lower at the far end.”

Lerris found himself waiting for the laugh. It arrived exactly where he remembered it.

The sand fell. He could see each grain in the narrow part of the glass, the stream dark against the wall. The third repetition had begun when Lian lifted the white tile.

The voice stopped in the middle of a word.

She covered the comb. The silence seemed to expand before the ordinary sounds returned: a cart in the street, somebody clearing a throat in the passage, Caldris’s sleeve brushing his knee.

Lian wrote again.

What did you hear?

The attendant gave them separate sheets. Caldris moved his chair a little farther from Lerris before beginning. Vesrin had written half a page by the time Lerris found his pen.

He recorded the words, the scrape, the blow, the laughter. He wrote that there were two men, crossed out men, and replaced it with voices. He could not remember whether the second laugh had been louder than the first. He left that unanswered.

Their accounts were collected before anyone spoke.

“Who were they?” Vesrin asked.

Lian’s fingers moved across the dish.

The owner identified one as her father. We have not identified the other.

“Is he dead?” Lerris asked.

Yes.

“Was that him?”

Lian watched him. Then she erased the water and wrote a shorter answer.

We do not claim that.

The attendant fetched a ledger. The object had been deposited some years earlier. Its owner had reported losing the memory of an afternoon spent helping her father move a bed. She remembered being in the house that day. She remembered what happened before and afterwards. The missing part had troubled her only when she tried to tell someone about it.

“Why did she lose it?” Caldris asked.

“The circumstances of her deposit are private,” the attendant said.

“But you can let us hear it?”

“She permitted this interval to be used for supervised observation.”

“Did she hear it herself?” Vesrin asked.

“Yes.”

“Did hearing it give it back?”

The attendant looked toward Lian.

The keeper wrote with one hand braced against the stand.

She could report what she heard here. She could not remember being there.

Vesrin put her pen down.

Lerris looked at the covered cradle. The exchange had been so small, so foolishly affectionate. He could imagine a bed caught across a doorway, a daughter waiting with bedding while the men argued about which end to lift. None of that had been in the sound. He had supplied it almost at once.

“Then why call it a return?” he asked.

The water moved beneath Lian’s fingers.

That is the name under which permission was sought.

Neravel lifted his head.

“There have been more complete instances.”

Lian did not look away from Lerris. She erased the sentence with a slow movement of her hand.

The attendant closed the ledger.

Chapter 9 — After the Hearing

Caldris wanted the supper he had been promised.

Lerris had forgotten it. He remembered only when his friend stopped outside a cookshop and asked whether they were continuing to walk for a reason.

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry. Be hungry.”

They took a table near the back, away from the open hearth. Caldris ordered a dish before Lerris had looked at the food. There were chairs, solid ones, and he settled into his with a relief that had nothing to do with the Library.

For a while they ate without speaking. Lerris kept hearing the first man’s breath. He tried to place it among voices he knew and then made himself stop.

“Well?” he asked.

Caldris swallowed.

“I heard two voices. So did you.”

“Did you think it was a trick?”

“I couldn’t see where a man could hide. That doesn’t mean there wasn’t one.”

“The repetitions were exact.”

“They seemed exact.”

Lerris pushed his bowl away. “You can doubt anything.”

“Yes. I can also eat while doing it.”

He drew the bowl back.

Caldris tore a piece of bread, dipped it in the sauce, and thought for a moment.

“It frightened me,” he said.

Lerris looked up.

“The second time through. I knew the laugh was coming and couldn’t stop waiting for it.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t want another demonstration.”

“Because it frightened you?”

“Because I have an examination tomorrow and I would like to be thinking about the candidates.”

Lerris felt an impulse to insist that this was more important. He caught it too late to keep it out of his face.

Caldris set his bread down.

“They’ve worked for years. One of them has pawned almost everything he owns to stay here. Tomorrow matters rather a lot to him.”

“I didn’t say it didn’t.”

“No.”

They ate a little more. The sauce had dried at the edge of Lerris’s bowl.

Afterwards Caldris asked him to describe the wax impressions. Lerris drew the broad shape with a finger on the table, then stopped.

“They said not to trace.”

“They said not to trace the object. You aren’t tracing anything.”

“I know.”

“Then stop if you want to. Don’t make it a rule they didn’t give you.”

Lerris wiped the table with his sleeve. Caldris watched without comment.

They parted outside the cookshop. Lerris paid, wished him well for the morning, and meant it. His friend went away with the practical haste of a man who still had work before bed.

Vesrin was waiting at the gate of the north dormitory.

“I thought you might eat,” she said.

“You could have come.”

“I wanted to be alone.”

“And now?”

“Now I’d rather not.”

They walked along the outer court. The lamps had been lit, but there were dark spaces between them, full of ordinary evening smells: damp stone, stale cooking water, the sweet trace of blossom from a garden beyond the wall.

“Was that what happened with your friend?” Lerris asked.

“No.”

“You’re certain?”

“She sang it. She didn’t listen to something sing.”

“Could she have learned it again?”

“Not in the time. Not like that.”

He wanted to ask what like that meant. Vesrin stopped beneath a lamp and took out a folded sheet.

“Lian’s attendant gave me this. It’s permission to submit another request. Not permission for another return.”

“Will you?”

“Yes.”

“Does your friend want you to?”

“She asked me before she went away. I’ve asked her since. She still does.”

Lerris nodded. The questions had begun to sound accusing even to him.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“For asking?”

“For the way I’m asking.”

She put the sheet away.

“I didn’t like hearing them,” she said. “Those men. I kept thinking she might not have expected strangers to find it interesting.”

“She gave permission.”

“I know.”

A student came through the arch carrying a lamp too close to his books. Vesrin called to him. He moved it away, thanked her, and nearly struck the post with his shoulder.

She laughed, briefly.

“I’m going to ask Neravel for the keeper’s report on the wax,” he said.

“Ask the keeper.”

“I will.”

“And read what she says. Not what he says she means.”

“Do you think he lied?”

“I think he wants you to come back.”

They stood a little longer. Then Vesrin said good night and went down toward the comb-maker’s street.

Lerris found Markus in his room, sitting beside the window with a book unopened on his knee. The porter had admitted him. Lerris was glad to see him and annoyed that he was glad.

“You didn’t have to wait.”

“I came to tell you about the inquiry papers. Then I waited.”

“Did you find out?”

“There were authorized comparison copies. One hasn’t been returned. They hadn’t asked for it until now.”

Lerris took off his coat.

“Neravel said he paid for mine.”

His brother’s face changed.

“You’ve seen him again.”

“In a public room. And at the Library. Caldris came.”

Markus put the unopened book on the table.

Lerris told him about the consultation. He kept to what he had heard, the permission, the statements afterwards. His brother listened without interrupting, though once he rubbed his palms hard against his knees.

“Could that happen to you?” Lerris asked at last.

“What?”

“Something kept. Something they could return.”

“I don’t know.”

“But if it could—”

“Lerris.”

“I know you don’t want to go there.”

“I don’t want you to arrange a hearing for me.”

“I haven’t.”

“Don’t.”

He wanted to say that he was doing this for his own interest. It was partly true. It would also have been the cruellest answer available, and he found that he had to turn away to keep it in his mouth.

Markus rose. At the door he paused.

“What did Caldris think?”

“He was frightened.”

“Were you?”

“Yes.”

His brother nodded.

After he left, Lerris put the Library receipt beside his account of the evening. Beneath them lay the finished toll comparison. He had not thought of it since handing it in for checking.

He took it out, read the conclusion once, and addressed a wrapper to Constano. The work was modest. It was finished. He sent it in the morning before going to request the keeper’s report.

Chapter 10 — The Mark on the Back

The report was shorter than Lerris expected.

He had waited two days to read it, paid for an attendant to bring it, and surrendered his own ink at the desk. Now there were three leaves before him, with much of the first occupied by a description of the containers in which the impressions had arrived.

He read that part twice. It was easy to pass over. Neravel had carried the wax in wooden frames; the keeper had received it in a tin lined with wool. The wax had been warmed and reset before the second examination. A small split near one edge had been repaired.

On the second leaf, the examiner described an impression that changed while resting on a table.

There was no account of a door opening, a voice speaking, or a memory returned. The examiner had put a tiny mark at the back of the wax, turned it over, and recorded the disposition of the notches. After the next inspection, the mark was at the front.

Lerris sat back.

He could imagine a dishonest examiner turning the wax. He could imagine a second piece substituted during the interval. The report anticipated both objections and described the people present, their positions, the closed door. It did not demand that he believe them.

The last leaf was an addendum. The object from which the impression had been taken was no longer in the keeper’s custody. Its owner had recovered it under a limited release. The impression had subsequently ceased to change.

Below that, a smaller hand had written: The direction of dependence remains unestablished.

Lerris asked the attendant what it meant.

She read the sentence before answering.

“They don’t know whether the thing followed the wax or the wax followed the thing.”

“Or neither.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the earlier description. Someone had taken considerable care over the mark, less over what had happened between the first and second examinations. He could not decide whether this was a defect in the work or merely the point at which the work had become difficult.

“May I copy the addendum?”

“The words. Not the figure.”

He used the pen provided.

Outside, rain was coming down hard enough to clear the steps. Vesrin stood beneath the overhang with a parcel inside her coat. She looked up when he emerged.

“They said you were still in there.”

“Have you come about your request?”

“Yes.”

“Any answer?”

“Another question.”

She watched water running from a broken mouth on the gutter.

“They want her present. She says she won’t come without knowing what they’re offering. They’ve asked me to explain that they can’t say until she comes.”

Lerris nearly smiled. Her face stopped him.

“What will you do?”

“Write it as clearly as I can. Then let her be angry at it.”

He showed her the copied sentence. She read it under the shelter of his coat.

“Neravel knows the owner,” she said.

“Of the original?”

“Yes. He makes instruments.”

“Is he part of the gathering?”

“He used to come.”

“Why did he stop?”

“Ask him.”

The rain thickened. Across the street a man was trying to cover a handcart with a cloth too small for it. Vesrin shifted her parcel, ready to leave.

“Will Neravel introduce me?”

“He may. The man owes him money.”

That made Lerris less eager, then more curious about the shape of the debt.

Vesrin saw the change.

“Don’t go as a favor to Neravel,” she said. “He can collect his own money.”

She put up her hood and went into the rain.

Lerris remained beneath the gutter until the worst had passed. Then he went to the green door, carrying his own copy of the addendum.

Chapter 11 — The Instrument Maker

Neravel gave him an address without offering to accompany him.

He also gave him a folded note. Lerris opened it in front of him. It asked the instrument maker to speak freely if he wished, and made no mention of payment. Neravel watched him read it with an expression too mild to be entirely unresentful.

“You could have asked.”

“I could.”

The address led to a workshop below street level. Light entered through a grating at the height of Lerris’s shoulder. Beneath it stood a bench crowded with little metal parts, all laid on strips of cloth. He could hear water dripping somewhere but could see no leak.

The man at the bench was trying to thread a wire through an opening too small for his fingers. He held the wire against his lip while choosing a tool.

“If you’re here about the pump, it’s not mine.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

Lerris handed him the note. He read it, snorted, and put it beneath a weight.

“Neravel’s friends are getting better dressed.”

“I’m not sure I’m his friend.”

“Then there’s hope for the coat.”

His name was Tavren. He kept working while Lerris explained the report. At the mention of the mark on the wax, the wire slipped. Tavren laid down both hands, very carefully.

“Did they show you the thing itself?”

“No.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

“Good.”

He rose and crossed to a cupboard. Lerris expected a locked box. Tavren brought back an ordinary brass tool with a pair of unequal arms and a short toothed edge fitted near the joint.

“It began as this,” he said.

“Began?”

“This is another one. I make them for measuring the pitch of small wheels. The one you read about was mine.”

“Where is it?”

“Back with the keepers.”

“The report said you recovered it.”

“I did. Then I took it back.”

He held the tool by its longer arm, the joint resting against the base of his forefinger, and opened the shorter arm with his thumb. The brass was bright along that reach and brown in the recesses. When he let the toothed edge settle against the other arm, it made a faint scratching click. He set the harmless tool between them. One arm swung when he released it, tapping the wood.

“What happened?” Lerris asked.

Tavren looked toward the grating. Feet passed outside, splashing through water that had collected against the curb.

“I lent it for a measurement. They said the board wouldn’t lie flat. I knew the men making it. Good joiners. I thought they’d been blamed for somebody else’s bad drawing.”

“Which board?”

“A playing board. A large one.”

He picked up the tool and closed the arms.

“The joints were true. The floor wasn’t. Or the floor was true until the board was down. I couldn’t get the same reading twice. They paid me to keep trying.”

“Who paid?”

“Neravel, at first.”

“And later?”

“I was paid from a purse. I didn’t ask whose.”

Lerris could hear the arrangement taking shape: an ordinary commission, a difficulty, more work. He had imagined something more deliberate, with a point at which a man knew he had begun.

“How long did you stay?”

“Three visits. On the third I left the tool overnight. In the morning it measured the same distance differently according to which end I began at.”

“That’s possible if—”

“I know what makes a bad tool.”

Lerris shut his mouth.

Tavren rubbed the closed joint with his thumb.

“I took it to the keepers. They took impressions. They kept it. Then I needed it back, because they wanted payment for further examination and I wanted to finish the job and be paid.”

“Did you?”

“Finish? No.”

“Get paid?”

“For the three visits. Enough to make me go again, not enough to clear what I’d borrowed.”

He returned the ordinary tool to the cupboard.

“What did it do when you brought it home?”

Tavren stood with his hand on the door.

“My apprentice couldn’t find the stair.”

Lerris looked toward the steps behind him.

“Not this stair. My old workshop. He went up with the shutters, came back down, and asked where we’d moved it. I told him to stop being stupid. He cried.”

The cupboard door closed.

“I took the tool back that afternoon. He could find it when I came home.”

“Did anyone else—”

“Yes. I tried. I found the stair. Every time. I made him show me and he walked into the shelves beside it.”

Tavren sat down. He put the wire through its opening on the first attempt and drew it out the other side.

“The keepers wrote that I couldn’t confirm a change in the stair. That’s true. I couldn’t.”

Lerris looked at the neat strips of cloth on the bench. Each held a different set of parts. He had an unpleasant desire to disturb one, to see whether Tavren would notice.

“Is your apprentice still with you?”

“No. His uncle found him work somewhere else.”

“May I—”

“No.”

Tavren bent over the wire. Lerris waited through several movements before accepting that the conversation was finished.

At the door he turned.

“What should I tell Neravel?”

“That you came. I’ll write about the money myself.”

On the street, Lerris stopped beside the grating. Below, Tavren had begun turning a small handle. The instrument answered with a dry, regular click. He worked steadily, unaware of being watched, until Lerris moved away.

Chapter 12 — The Drawing That Would Not Lie Flat

The next gathering met in daylight.

Lerris arrived with Tavren’s account written in his own hand and no intention of surrendering it. He had asked permission to keep notes before leaving the workshop; Tavren had allowed that, provided his former apprentice was neither named nor sought out. Lerris had put the condition at the top of the page.

Neravel read it without reaching for it.

“He was frightened,” he said.

“He had reason.”

“Yes.”

“Do you dispute what happened?”

“I dispute the conclusion that nothing useful was learned.”

“He didn’t say that.”

“Then we may agree.”

Lerris disliked the ease with which the man moved past the injury. He also wanted to see the drawing.

It was spread on the upstairs table, weighted at the corners with cups. Vesrin stood at one end. Two men Lerris recognized from the supper sat beside it, arguing over a line that had been erased and drawn again. There was no board on the floor, no salt, no object hidden beneath a cloth.

Neravel introduced the work as a reconstruction. Lerris interrupted him.

“Of what?”

“A position. From several records made by people who left at different times.”

“The board Tavren measured?”

“One of the records concerns it.”

“Who left?”

“Some people walked out. Some were removed by the custodians.”

“Alive?”

Neravel looked at the men by the table.

“Those represented in this drawing, yes.”

It was a careful answer. Lerris wrote it down.

Vesrin moved a cup so he could see. The diagram had a familiar center and an unfamiliar margin, where several lines continued beyond the drawn boundary. Different inks distinguished the accounts. At first he could make no sense of the contradictions. A piece appeared twice in one record, nowhere in another. One witness had described a move that the adjacent witness denied having seen.

“Why ask me?” he said.

“Because you play,” Vesrin answered. “Because you know a witness can be exact about the wrong part. And because you aren’t committed to our last attempt.”

He leaned closer. Neravel found him a chair.

For an hour they did ordinary work. They separated the accounts, matched the moves that could be matched, and stopped calling the same piece by two names. One of the men became irritated when Lerris asked him to repeat a conclusion. Vesrin made him go back to the line from which he had derived it. The conclusion survived. Lerris apologized.

Then he found something.

The longest account described the position from the far end of the table. The next had been copied as though its writer sat at the near end. Nobody had turned the second diagram. Its lettering faced the reader, and everyone had followed the lettering.

“Turn this,” he said.

“We have.”

“Only this. Leave the first.”

Vesrin lifted the sheet. A cup caught the corner; she freed it and rotated the paper.

Two disputed moves ceased to be disputed.

No lamp flickered. Nothing opened. One of the men said a word of heartfelt disgust and pushed his chair back. Vesrin laughed, once, with relief.

Lerris felt pleasure spread through him so swiftly that he forgot to conceal it.

They worked another hour. The revised position explained why one witness thought a piece had been removed: he had been watching the place from which it was reflected in a polished surface. It did not explain the doubled piece or the missing interval. Lerris marked those separately.

Neravel brought bread and cold meat. They ate at the table, protecting the papers with clean boards. Someone asked Lerris about his toll work, and he explained the landing correction. They listened. He did not have to make it amusing.

Only when he reached for a second piece of bread did he notice how hungry he was.

Vesrin copied the corrected orientation onto a fresh sheet. She left the uncertain margin unfinished.

“This can go to the keepers,” she said.

Neravel nodded.

“Will they use it?” Lerris asked.

“They may refuse to let us use it,” one of the men said.

“That’s not the same question,” Vesrin replied.

She put Lerris’s name beside the correction. He watched her write Levis Fenwood and did not amend it.

Neravel asked whether he would return after the keeper’s response.

“Yes,” Lerris said.

The answer was out before he thought about his brother. He could still qualify it. He could say that he would read the response first, that he had other work, that this was only a comparison of drawings.

Instead he asked for a copy of what they had done.

They gave him one, with the unresolved places left blank.

He walked back carrying it flat beneath his coat. The afternoon had cleared. Water shone between the street stones, and a woman outside a doorway was beating the damp from a rug with enough violence to stop conversation on that side of the road.

At the university gate he paused to enter his return in the porter’s book. Nobody had needed to be sent after him. He had promised nothing except another afternoon.

Upstairs, he laid the drawing beside his receipt from the Wardens. For the first time since returning to Thepyrgos, the thing he most wanted to write about was something he had done himself.

Chapter 13 — The Empty Place

The keepers sent back the correction with a piece missing.

Someone had cut a rectangle from the lower corner of Vesrin’s clean copy. The cut passed through two lines and the edge of a notation. A separate sheet acknowledged the reversal of orientation and asked where the visitors had obtained the adjoining position.

Neravel laid both papers on the table.

“They could have asked without taking it.”

Vesrin lifted the damaged copy against the light.

“Was there an instruction to return it entire?”

“I expected them to.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Lerris found his own copy in his case. It was intact. He set it beside the other, and all three leaned over the little space the keepers had removed.

The notation was one of those he had not understood. It described a piece as being present after its removal. He had assumed a copying error and left it unresolved.

“They want the witness,” Vesrin said.

“Then we should give them the account,” Lerris answered.

Neravel looked at him.

“The account was lent to us.”

“Ask the owner.”

“I have.”

“And?”

“He won’t permit his name to go with it.”

“Will he permit the words?”

Neravel paused. Vesrin put the cut sheet down.

“That would answer what they’ve asked,” she said.

“It may.”

They sent a copy without the name. Lerris watched Vesrin mark the omission plainly, so it could not be mistaken for a complete account. Neravel took it to the Library himself.

The rest of the afternoon went badly. With the corrected orientation, several positions could be reconciled, but they did not form a continuous sequence. Lerris kept trying to move a piece through the gap. Each attempt required him to ignore something a witness had written. By the time the lamps were lit, he had become impatient with people he had never met.

“He can’t have seen that,” he said.

Vesrin put down her pen.

“Then stop for tonight.”

He began to object, saw the ink on his own cuff, and realized he had dipped the sleeve into the pot without noticing. There was a dark streak across the bottom of his page.

They cleaned the table together. Vesrin went downstairs to settle the supper money. Alone for a moment, Lerris held the cut copy over his own. The missing rectangle made a small window onto the line beneath.

He did not see the error until he moved the paper.

The witness had used the same mark for a position occupied and a position that had been occupied. In the rest of the account, a little stroke distinguished them. Here the stroke crossed the fold and had been lost in copying.

He called Vesrin back.

She examined the two sheets, then fetched the earlier copy. The fold had indeed run through that place. She set a weight at each corner and leaned close enough for her hair to fall onto the table.

“I can’t prove it from this,” she said.

“But it could be.”

“Yes.”

“If it’s a place left empty, the next move works.”

“Yes.”

She looked up. He was grinning. He could feel it and could not stop.

They wrote a second note to the keepers. Vesrin made him include the uncertainty before the conclusion. He added it willingly, almost lavishly.

Neravel returned as they were sealing it. He read the note, asked two questions, and put a hand briefly on Lerris’s shoulder.

“I thought you would be useful.”

The pleasure of it lasted all the way back to his room.

He wrote to Markus that the keepers had taken an interest in their reconstruction. He mentioned the corrected orientation and the damaged fold. He did not mention the cut-out rectangle. It would require a paragraph to explain, and he wanted his brother to read the rest before becoming alarmed.

He sealed the letter while that still seemed a sufficient reason.

Chapter 14 — Beneath the Library

The reply required them to come in person.

Vesrin read it aloud twice at the green-door house. This was to be an inspection of the surviving setting, under supervision, to compare the recovered position with marks on the floor. No play was permitted. No loose pieces were to be brought. They would enter and leave together.

The permission named Vesrin, Neravel, and Levis Fenwood.

Lerris asked whether Caldris could be added.

“He can apply,” Neravel said. “But the inspection is tomorrow.”

Caldris could not come. He was examining again, and this time did not apologize for it. He read the permission, asked whether Lerris understood the phrase surviving setting, and returned the paper when Lerris admitted he did not.

“Find out before you go down.”

“I will.”

Lerris did ask. The attendant at the Library told him that the board had been removed, but the marks by which it had been placed remained. He would not be asked to handle an object. He could withdraw before entering the stair.

He sent the address and expected return time to Markus, then went with the others.

The stair was narrow and quite ordinary. Its treads had been worn deeper on one side, and there was a smell of water behind the walls. Two custodians preceded them with lamps. Another followed, carrying nothing except a folded cloth and a short iron bar through his belt.

At the bottom, an attendant counted them through a door and entered the names on a slate. Neravel had to repeat his; the woman had not heard him over the sound of a bucket being filled somewhere nearby.

Lerris found this reassuring in a way he knew was foolish.

The room beyond was larger than the stair suggested. A line of pale stones crossed the floor. Along the far wall, shelves stood empty except for scraps of leather and the gray shapes where books had once kept the dust off. The ceiling was low enough that one of the custodians ducked beneath a beam without seeming to notice.

Lian waited beside a basin set on a high stand. The water was already still.

She wrote that they were to remain on the near side of the pale stones. The attendant repeated the instruction, and pointed out the boundary with her lamp.

On the floor beyond it, a large square had been cut into the stone. Within the square were smaller marks, some filled with plaster. Two empty sockets showed where something had once been fastened down.

Tavren had stood here, Lerris thought. Or in a room like it, with an apprentice somewhere walking into shelves.

Vesrin unrolled the corrected drawing. They put it on a portable stand and began the comparison.

For the first quarter of an hour nothing contradicted the paper. Then one of the custodians pointed with a wooden rod to a plastered mark near the far corner.

“That was filled before the board was removed,” the attendant said. “Your account puts a piece there afterwards.”

“A place,” Lerris said. “Possibly an empty place.”

The woman looked toward Lian. The keeper wrote a question in the basin.

Why retain it?

Lerris looked at the drawing. He could see the next move as plainly as he had at the table upstairs. The position required an opening, but the opening had not existed until something was removed.

“To keep the route of the move,” he said. “If you erase the place as well, it looks as though the piece crossed a closed line.”

Lian studied the paper. She asked the attendant to show them a second mark.

The custodian moved his rod.

Something scraped beneath the floor.

Everyone stopped. Lerris heard a bucket set down in the passage, the water slapping its sides. The scrape came again, farther to the left.

Lian lifted one hand. The attendant told the custodian to withdraw the rod.

As he did, the paper on the stand tilted.

Vesrin caught one edge. Lerris caught the other. Neither had touched the stand. Its near leg had sunk into a join between two stones that had not been wide enough to admit it a moment before.

“Back,” the attendant said.

They stepped away together. Neravel took Vesrin by the elbow, and she shook him off hard enough to make him stumble.

A book fell from an empty shelf.

It struck the floor open. Lerris saw writing, black and close, before the custodian at the rear moved in front of him.

“Toward the door.”

He obeyed. The scrape had become a slow grinding sound. There was no wind, no voice, nothing he could recognize as an attempt to persuade him. The lamp nearest the far wall burned steadily while its light slid across the floor at an angle the lamp itself did not take.

At the door, Lerris looked back for Vesrin. She was behind him, carrying the rolled drawing against her chest. Neravel followed. Lian remained beside the basin while the attendants closed a heavy inner shutter across the far part of the room.

The man with the iron bar put it through the shutter’s brackets. His hand shook once, violently, when he let go.

They waited in the passage while the slate was checked. Three visitors in, three out. Lian emerged last, her sleeve wet to the elbow.

No one had been hurt.

The attendant said this aloud, asking each of them to confirm it. Vesrin answered with obvious irritation. Neravel said yes. Lerris had to swallow before he could speak.

Lian wrote in a smaller dish brought from the room beside them.

Describe the first change. Separately.

Lerris looked at the narrow table where they were to sit. His hands were still remembering the edge of the falling paper.

“Did I do that?” he asked.

Lian’s eyes moved to him. She wiped the surface clear.

We do not yet know.

He sat down to write.

Chapter 15 — The Paper He Had Brought Back

Markus was waiting at the gate.

Lerris had returned before the agreed hour. He noticed that first and almost said it, as though punctuality might answer the look on his brother’s face.

“What happened?” Markus asked.

“Nothing to us.”

He regretted the words before Markus replied.

“Lerris.”

They went to his room. His brother shut the door and stood with a hand on the latch while Lerris found somewhere to put the drawing. The Library had let him retain his own copy, sealed inside a wrapper he was not to open before returning it the following morning. He laid it flat on the table.

Markus looked at the seals.

“Don’t touch it,” Lerris said.

His brother’s hand had not moved.

Lerris sat on the bed. He described the room, the line, the rod, the stand sinking. He could describe the book least well. It had appeared to fall from the shelf, but he had not seen it there before falling. Markus asked him to make that distinction in what he wrote down.

“I already have.”

“Good.”

“I did what they told me.”

“I believe you.”

The answer undid him more than an accusation would have. He bent forward with his hands between his knees and stared at the floor until the feeling passed.

Markus sat beside him. For a while neither spoke.

Downstairs, someone was arguing about a missing towel. A door banged, followed by an indignant denial from another room. Lerris heard every word and retained none of them.

“I was useful,” he said at last.

His brother turned toward him.

“Before. The drawing. They’d copied one account the wrong way round. I found it.”

“I read your letter.”

“And the mark might mean a place that was left empty.”

“Perhaps.”

“They hadn’t seen that.”

Markus rested his forearms on his knees. He looked tired, but he did not look away.

“I know what it’s like,” he said.

Lerris felt anger come back, grateful and hot.

“You don’t know what I found.”

“No. I know what it’s like when someone needs you to have found it.”

He nearly told his brother to leave. The words formed with such clarity that he had to press his lips together. Markus saw something of it and stood.

“I’ll get water.”

There was water in the jug. Lerris let him go.

When he returned, he had also brought bread, two cups, and a cloth. He poured without offering advice. Lerris drank. His throat hurt, though he could not remember crying out.

“Will you stay?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“I don’t mean all night.”

“I haven’t decided how long you mean.”

That made him laugh, once. Markus smiled and sat in the chair, leaving him the bed.

Later, when Lerris had washed and eaten a little, they examined the Library’s instructions. He was to bring the sealed drawing to the public desk at first bell. No one had ordered him to remain in his room. He was to report dreams or involuntary recollections associated with the inspection, but the attendant had cautioned him not to attribute every restless night to it.

Markus read that last sentence twice.

“Sensible,” he said.

“Do you think they’ll let us continue?”

His brother put the paper down.

“I don’t know.”

“You think I shouldn’t.”

“Yes.”

“I haven’t decided.”

“I heard you.”

They stayed together until supper. Markus spoke about a damaged index he was repairing, a task so tedious that Lerris eventually began offering worse methods merely to provoke him. His brother defended the dullest method with increasing conviction. By the time he left, Lerris could imagine sleeping.

He did sleep. He woke once to the sound of footsteps and knew where he was. In the morning he carried the sealed drawing to the Library without opening it.

Vesrin was on the steps.

There was a bruise on her wrist where Neravel had caught her. She saw Lerris looking and pulled her cuff back rather than hiding it.

“I told him not to do that again.”

“Did he apologize?”

“Yes.”

“Are you going back?”

“Not down there. Not now.”

He looked toward the door.

“They’ve asked for us upstairs,” she said. “There’s something they want us to see.”

The something was the book that had fallen.

It lay shut inside a glass-sided case. Lian’s attendant stood beside it, with a second ledger open on the desk. The fallen book bore an old catalog mark on its spine. That mark had been entered in the ledger years earlier, followed by a date and the words not recovered.

“From that room?” Lerris asked.

“From a removal before the floor was sealed.”

“Was it there yesterday?”

“It wasn’t visible when the inspection began.”

Vesrin leaned closer to the glass. Lerris could see the worn corners, the dark crease where someone had opened the cover repeatedly with a dirty thumb.

“Have you read it?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“What is it?”

“A reader’s record. According to the old entry.”

“Whose?”

“The entry doesn’t say.”

The attendant took their sealed drawing and checked its edges before setting it aside.

“You’re not being asked to resume the inspection,” she said. “The keeper wishes to compare your reconstruction with the recovered record. If it can be examined safely, you may be asked to explain your correction.”

Lerris looked at Vesrin. She was still watching the book.

Yesterday he had wanted to be back in his room with his brother and a cup of water. He could remember wanting it. The memory had not diminished. Beside it now was the knowledge that something had come out of the sealed place, and that a line he had drawn might help them read it.

He asked when they would know.

Chapter 16 — A Reader’s Work

They let Lerris read six pages.

The book remained behind glass. An attendant turned the leaves with a flat wooden tool while he sat on the other side of the case and copied into a numbered booklet. Vesrin had been admitted separately that morning. They were not to compare their accounts until the keeper had received both.

He disliked the arrangement until he began reading. Then he forgot the attendant, the glass, and the small ache in his back from leaning forward.

The writer had recorded successive positions in a cramped hand. Between them were complaints about the food, a reminder to send money to a sister, and a sketch of a stool whose legs had been shortened unequally. Lerris lingered over those things. Whoever had made the book had wanted a better seat and had been angry about broth with no meat in it.

On the third page the numbering began again.

Lerris asked to see the preceding leaf. The attendant turned it back. There was no missing gathering. The writer had returned to the same position and made another choice.

That was possible. An ordinary reconstruction did it constantly. But the next few lines described a woman who had left during the first sequence and had not returned. The revised position retained the place from which she had gone.

Do not fill it merely because it remains, the writer had added beneath.

Lerris copied the sentence. He remembered the lost stroke at the fold and felt the first small certainty that he had found more than an error in transcription.

The sixth page ended halfway through a move.

“Next, please.”

“That’s the extent of the permission.”

He looked up at the attendant.

“There’s more writing.”

“Yes.”

“Does it continue?”

“I can’t tell you.”

Lerris sat back. His neck had stiffened while he read. He had a sudden, ridiculous wish to tip the case toward himself and shake the page loose.

Instead he asked to see the first leaf again.

The complaints about food were written over several days. The positions between them might have occupied a single afternoon. He could not establish how the two counts related. He noted this, returned the booklet, and went outside with nothing in his hands.

Vesrin waited in the court. They were permitted to speak now that both accounts had been collected.

“The place left empty,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And the woman.”

“Yes.”

She took his arm so abruptly that he looked down.

“It isn’t the tune,” she said. “I know that. Don’t tell me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

They walked through the gate together. She did not release him until they reached the street.

At the house beyond the dyers’ street, Neravel listened to their reports. He had not been granted permission to read the book. He seemed more angry about this than Lerris had expected.

“They’ll use you to complete what they won’t let you see.”

“We haven’t completed anything,” Vesrin said.

“You’ve shown them a way to follow it.”

“So have you.”

Neravel looked at her, then went to fetch wine.

Lerris stayed at the table. There was a damp patch where a cup had stood, and he moved his sleeve away from it. He could still see the half-finished line on the sixth page. It occurred to him that he had supplied its next mark twice while walking back.

Both times he had chosen differently.

Chapter 17 — The Woman Who Remembered

The woman arrived while they were eating.

She was neither young nor old enough for Lerris to settle on a polite form of address. She wore a traveling dress whose hem was muddy, and carried her gloves inside a hat. Neravel stood at once. Vesrin remained seated, her spoon halfway to her mouth.

“You might have sent word,” Neravel said.

“I did. You were busy.”

She put the hat on an empty chair. Someone brought a bowl. She tasted the broth, asked for salt, and only then looked at Lerris.

“You’re the one who turned the paper.”

“One paper.”

“That can be enough.”

He waited for an introduction. None came. The others seemed to know her, though the men at the far end of the table grew quieter after she sat.

She asked him about the reading. When he described the limits, she nodded as though hearing an old complaint.

“The keeper is afraid of a conclusion.”

“She has reason to be careful.”

“Certainly.”

The agreement made him more wary than contradiction would have. He watched her break bread into her bowl.

“What would you do with it?” he asked.

“Finish reading.”

“And then?”

“Decide.”

“That’s what they say.”

She smiled. “Yes. It’s an agreeable thing to say while somebody else waits.”

Vesrin set down her spoon.

“Can the interval be longer?”

The woman looked at her without asking what she meant.

“It could.”

“At the same price?”

“No.”

“Then don’t tell her it could as if that’s an offer.”

For a moment nobody moved. The woman wiped a little broth from her finger, folded the cloth neatly, and put it beside her bowl.

“You’re right.”

Vesrin looked away.

After supper the woman asked him to walk with her as far as the door. She did not take his arm. They passed the hanging stockings and stood where the kitchen light reached the threshold.

“What should I call you?” he asked.

“What do they call me?”

“Nothing, so far.”

“Then let that wait.”

He disliked it, and she could see that he disliked it.

“Names are useful,” she said. “I have several. I’d rather not begin with the wrong one.”

“That gives you an advantage.”

“Yes.”

He could have gone back upstairs. The kitchen woman was kneading something at the table, her forearms dusted white. The door to the street stood open.

“Do you know what happened to the writer?” he asked.

“Which writer?”

“The book that came back.”

“He was trying to leave room for someone.”

“Did he succeed?”

“For a while.”

“Is he alive?”

She regarded him with an attention that was almost tenderness.

“I wouldn’t offer you that promise.”

“Then what are you offering?”

“A chance to stop making the same refusal in different handwriting.”

She reached into her hat and brought out a small folded paper. She put it on the kitchen table, well away from the flour.

“A list of the accounts the keeper hasn’t shown you. You can ask for them. You can also ask whether she intends to keep the place empty with the man still in it.”

“Who?”

“The one whose book you read.”

He stared at her. She took up her gloves.

“You said you didn’t know if he was alive.”

“I said I wouldn’t promise.”

She left before he found another question.

Upstairs, Neravel was gathering cups. Lerris set the folded paper on the table between them.

“Who is she?”

Neravel’s hands stopped.

“Some call her the Voice.”

“Of what?”

“The Ninth.”

Vesrin shut her eyes. One of the men at the end of the room stood and went to the window.

Lerris looked at the familiar table, the half-cleared plates, the corrected drawing beneath its clean board.

“How long were you going to wait to tell me?”

“I wanted you to see the work first,” Neravel said.

Lerris took his coat. He left the list on the table.

No one tried to stop him.

Chapter 18 — The Petition

He stayed away for nine days, though he did not count them until afterwards.

On the first he told Markus everything he could remember of the woman. His brother listened standing up, one hand on the back of a chair. When Lerris said he had left the list, Markus sat down.

“Good.”

The word irritated him. He had expected relief, perhaps anger on his behalf. He had not expected to feel like a child returning stolen fruit.

“I want to ask Lian about the writer.”

“Then ask.”

“You don’t think I should?”

“I think you should ask without promising anybody you’ll like the answer.”

He did ask. The petition took three days to reach the right desk and another two to return. It contained no name, only a statement that the status of persons associated with the recovered record remained under examination. He could request a further audience.

Lerris requested one.

While he waited, he attended a disputation in which a man defended a measurement he had never made. He ate with Caldris and learned that the examinations had gone well, except for a candidate who had wept from anger rather than relief. He received a brief letter from Constano acknowledging the toll work and asking for a clean copy of one table. He made it. He slept badly on two nights and well on the third.

The world did not narrow immediately to the list he had left behind. He had enough ordinary business to fill his days. This made his return to the Library harder to excuse as necessity, and he went anyway.

Lian received him in a room above ground. Her dish stood between them. There was no object under a cloth.

He asked whether anyone remained beneath the sealed floor.

The writing formed slowly.

There are places we cannot enter without altering what they hold.

“That’s not what I asked.”

It is part of the answer.

“Is the writer there?”

Lian erased the water. He watched her fingers, the slight swelling at one joint, a scar beside the nail.

We have not established that he is elsewhere.

Lerris leaned back. He could hear the Voice asking him whether the keeper meant to preserve the empty place with the man in it.

“What are you doing to find out?”

The attendant laid three reports on the table. Lerris had to sign before reading them. There had been attempts. A lowered lamp had returned without its wick. A custodian had crossed a marked threshold and come back unable to recognize the person waiting for him. He recovered some recognitions over the next weeks. Not all. The latest attempt had produced the empty room they had inspected.

He read the dates. Years lay between the first and last.

“Was he told the risk?”

“The custodian?” the attendant asked. “Yes.”

“And the man inside?”

Lian wrote before the attendant could answer.

We do not know what he was told.

Lerris put down the report. For once he had no accusation to make that did not strike people already hurt by the attempt.

“Can the corrected position help?”

Possibly.

“Will you use it?”

We are preparing an approach that does not require completing the sequence.

“Let me help.”

The words surprised him less than they should have.

Lian watched him. Then she wrote the terms under which she would consider it. He would work only from records they provided. He would undertake no moves on an active setting. He would disclose contact from the people who called themselves the Coterie. He would accept the keeper’s decision to stop an examination.

He read every line.

“I’ve already had contact.”

We know.

He described the woman, the list, his refusal to take it. The attendant recorded this without looking pleased.

Lian gave him nothing to sign that day. She told him to return after he had slept on the proposal.

On the way home he stopped outside Vesrin’s workshop. He could hear her speaking sharply to somebody upstairs, not about him, not about the Ninth. He waited until the voice ceased, then went up.

She opened the door with ink on her wrist.

“I wondered when you’d come.”

“I haven’t come back to Neravel.”

“I didn’t ask.”

He told her about the reports. She listened with her hand still on the door.

Then she let him in.

Chapter 19 — The Place Left for Him

The work occupied the rest of that month.

Lerris went to the Library in the mornings and returned to the ordinary collection after the meal. Vesrin was admitted on separate terms. They compared records, marked uncertainties, and learned how many ways a witness could describe the same room while believing himself precise.

The writer had not been alone. Three others appeared in the earlier pages; one left, one ceased to be mentioned, and one remained as a person addressed without reply. They called that person the listener until Lerris objected that silence in a notebook was not silence in the room. After that they used the initial the writer had given him.

It was slow work, sometimes satisfying. Lian attended twice, wrote a question, and left them arguing over it. The custodians brought food. One knew where the draught entered beneath their table and placed a folded cloth against the gap without being asked.

They found the next part of the sequence in an account the Voice had named on her list. Lerris learned this from Vesrin, who had taken a copy after he left.

He reported it. The keeper did not end the work. She had the account checked against another source and allowed the verified portion to remain.

He had begun expecting every refusal and was relieved to be wrong.

The arrangement they recovered contained a place for an observer who had not made the earlier moves. That observer could describe the position from outside it, enabling those within to distinguish a departure from an erasure. The word outside troubled Vesrin. In three accounts it referred to a seat, not a room.

“We need to know where that seat is,” she said.

A custodian brought the old floor measurements. Lerris recognized Tavren’s corrections in a copy of a copy, the sharp little marks at the ends of lines. The observer’s place lay beyond the pale boundary stones, close to the shutter they had seen closed after the disturbance.

Lian agreed to another inspection with fewer people and no reconstruction aloud. A custodian would go to the seat with the prepared record. Lerris would remain at the boundary and compare what could be seen.

He told Markus in person.

His brother asked whether the keeper knew about the Voice. Lerris said yes. He asked whether Caldris would be there. No. Vesrin would remain above with the comparison record; she had declined to go below again.

“And if the custodian can’t return?” Markus asked.

“They bring him back.”

“How?”

Lerris described the shutter, the marked retreat, the attendants waiting. Halfway through he realized he did not know what happened if the distance changed.

He went back and asked.

The attendant answered honestly: the route had failed before. They had no guarantee. They were using a record of the space left by withdrawal rather than assuming the same door remained available.

Lerris sat with that for a long time.

The next morning he asked to speak to the custodian who would take the seat. The man was older than him, with a short beard and a habit of holding one sleeve while he thought. He had a daughter working in the public catalog and a son who had gone inland. He had volunteered.

“Why?” Lerris asked.

“Because I can read the positions, and because the last man was my friend.”

He meant the custodian who had lost recognitions, not the writer beyond the threshold. Lerris had to ask to be sure.

They talked through the intended moves without moving anything. The custodian understood the diagram but kept reversing one transition. Lerris corrected it, then again. The man frowned and rubbed his beard.

“Show me once more.”

Lerris did.

By evening he had decided to ask for the seat himself.

He wrote the request before telling anyone, while he could still make the words look like a reasonable conclusion from the work.

Chapter 20 — The Salt-Pressed Hour

They refused him first.

Lian wrote that understanding a drawing did not qualify him to undertake an exposure. He replied that misunderstanding it did not qualify the custodian. The attendant stopped writing for a moment. Lerris heard his own arrogance and did not withdraw the point.

The custodian, when asked, agreed with him.

“He sees the change sooner,” the man said. “I can follow it after he points it out. That’s not the same.”

The keeper put both hands beside the dish. For several breaths she wrote nothing.

The conditions changed. Lerris would take the near observer’s seat only. He would identify the current position, not answer a move. If a further choice were presented, he would rise on the attendant’s signal and withdraw. The custodian would stand at the return mark to read the withdrawal. They rehearsed the physical route upstairs with chairs.

It was embarrassing. It also showed Lerris that the first turn was sharper than he expected. He caught his foot twice and learned to place it differently.

He wrote to Markus that he was to observe from within the setting. He explained the rehearsal and the stop signal. He left the letter with the porter before dawn on the day of the inspection.

Markus came before they went below.

There was no time for a private argument, but his brother made one anyway, drawing him into an empty alcove beside the stair.

“You wrote it as though it were decided.”

“It is.”

“Then why tell me?”

“Because I didn’t want to disappear without—”

He stopped. Markus’s face had changed.

“I didn’t mean that.”

“I think you did.”

Lerris looked at the stone between them. He had wanted his brother’s knowledge of the appointment to be another precaution. He had not imagined having to stand before the person who would receive its failure.

“I can still refuse,” he said.

“Yes.”

Neither moved.

From the stair the attendant called his recorded name. Markus heard it and looked toward the sound, but Lerris was already turning.

“I’ll be back before the meal.”

He hated himself a little for saying it. He went down.

The lower room smelled of salt. A narrow dry line had been laid inside the boundary, broken where they were to cross and ready to be closed behind them. Lerris had been told why: it limited one kind of passage. It did not secure every other.

The chair stood on a dark square beyond the shutter. He reached it by the rehearsed route. The room appeared unremarkable from there, smaller than the drawing suggested. He could see the attendant, the custodian at the return mark, the pale boundary, the open door.

He described the position.

The custodian read it back.

At the far edge of the setting a man appeared, seated with his head bent. He was holding a book open on his knees. There was no arrival Lerris could identify. The man was simply present between one glance and the next.

“Remain still,” the attendant said.

Lerris did.

The man raised his head. His mouth moved. Lerris could not hear him.

“Describe only,” the custodian said.

Lerris described the chair, the book, the man’s hands. The custodian began reading a withdrawal from the prepared sheet.

The man shook his head.

Lerris looked at the place beside him. It was the empty position from the notebook, but the return line led through it. If the custodian completed the withdrawal as written, the record would count the place as occupied and remove the passage the seated man had left himself.

“Wait,” Lerris said.

The attendant raised her hand.

“Withdraw.”

“The line’s wrong.”

“Withdraw now.”

He stood. The man at the far edge clutched the book against his chest.

Lerris could still see the door. He could still go.

He put his hand on the empty square.

“Leave this,” he said.

The custodian stopped reading.

Something struck the shutter from the other side. Lerris turned toward the sound and found the return mark farther away than it had been. The attendant shouted. He took a step toward her, then another. His chair scraped behind him though he had not touched it.

The man with the book began to speak. This time Lerris heard every word.

“Don’t take your hand away yet.”

He looked down. His hand was still on the square. He had walked three steps and had not moved it.

The shutter came down between him and the door.

For a moment there was light beneath it. Then that too was gone.

Part II — The Unreturned

Chapter 21 — On the Other Side

Markus heard the shutter fall.

He had been waiting above, where the sound ought to have been no more than a door closing somewhere beneath the floor. It struck through the soles of his shoes. The attendant beside him went still, then hurried to the stair.

He followed her. She turned and put an arm across the way.

“Wait here.”

“My brother—”

“I know.”

She went down. He remained at the top because two other custodians had come out of the side room and because he could see, with humiliating clarity, how little he could do if he forced his way past them.

The first person brought up was the older custodian. He was conscious. His mouth worked without sound. Someone held a cloth around his wrist, where a line of blood ran beneath the cuff.

Markus stood so the man could see him.

“Where is he?”

The custodian looked at his face and began to cry.

They took him away. Markus went down two steps before the guard stopped him.

Lian emerged after a long interval. The dish in her hands was empty. She set it on the table, and someone brought water. Markus watched the water pour.

He remains beyond the return.

“Bring him back.”

We are trying.

He looked past her toward the stair. There were people moving below. Lamps, a rolled cloth, a man carrying a new length of wood. It was not idleness. He wished it were, because then he could have spent his anger on something within reach.

“What did he do?”

The keeper’s fingers hesitated.

He answered.

“He was told to observe.”

Yes.

“What did he answer?”

They showed him the prepared sheet. He could not read it at first. The marks ran together. He had not slept, and he was trying to force meaning out of them before he knew what they represented.

He asked for Lerris’s own account of the correction. It was brought. Beside it, the prepared withdrawal looked wrong in a way he could not yet explain.

“You used his work,” he said.

Lian did not deny it.

By evening they had reopened the near room. It contained the chair and no one sitting in it. The far shutter could not be raised without the floor beyond it altering. They stopped the attempt when a custodian’s lamp returned with its flame burning downwards inside the oil.

Markus sat through the report. He did not remember agreeing to sit.

Vesrin found him there. She had been in the upper room with the comparison record. The paper remained under her arm, bent where she had gripped it.

“It’s my correction,” he said to her.

“No.”

“I started him—”

“Markus.”

He looked at her. She was angry enough to make him attend.

“I need you to read something. Not tonight if you can’t. But don’t turn this into something that happened before he was born.”

She set the paper beside him. Then she sat on the floor because all the chairs were occupied.

He read until the letters steadied.

Chapter 22 — The Man with the Book

The man did not ask Lerris’s name.

He asked whether he could still feel his hand.

“Yes.”

“Then bring it toward you slowly.”

Lerris tried. His fingers came free of the square with the unpleasant sensation of lifting skin from cold metal. He staggered and struck the chair with his knee.

The man watched him from the far edge. His beard was longer than Lerris had first thought, thin in places, with gray through it. One shoe had been repaired with a strip of cloth.

“Where is the door?” Lerris asked.

“Which one?”

“The one I came through.”

“Gone for now.”

He rose with difficulty, keeping the book tucked beneath one arm. Lerris expected him to approach. Instead he went to a shelf that had not been there when the shutter fell and took down a jug.

“Drink before you ask everything.”

The water was cool. Lerris smelled it, then drank because he was thirsty enough that suspicion had become an obstacle he could no longer afford.

The man’s name was Othrel. He had written the recovered book, or said he had. When Lerris described the complaint about broth, he smiled with one side of his mouth.

“It was poor.”

“How long have you been here?”

“I don’t know.”

“Days?”

“Yes.”

“Years?”

“Possibly.”

Lerris set the cup down too hard.

“Don’t do that.”

“What?”

“Answer as though it’s amusing.”

Othrel’s smile vanished.

“I’ve counted days. I’ve counted meals. I once counted the water stains on that wall. They didn’t stay where I put them.”

Lerris looked at the wall. There were stains, brown and ordinary, one shaped like a hand with too many fingers. He refused to count them.

“People are trying to reach us.”

“They have before.”

“Then help me tell them where we are.”

Othrel lowered himself into the chair. For a moment he looked very old.

“First we have to keep the passage to the food.”

It lay beyond a narrow arch. Lerris could see a table and a covered pot. Between them was a band of floor marked with the same lines he had been reconstructing for weeks.

“You have to play.”

“Sometimes. Sometimes you have to wait.”

“Who brings it?”

“People who can.”

“From the Coterie?”

Othrel nodded.

Lerris sat opposite him. He thought of the open street door at the green house, the broth, Neravel’s hand on his shoulder. He had believed choosing to leave was a thing he could keep doing.

Othrel opened the book. Its pages were nearly full.

“Show me what you changed,” he said.

Chapter 23 — Levis Fenwood

The first request came back with no matching entry.

Markus stared at the clerk’s note until Vesrin took it from him. It concerned the hired room beyond the dyers’ street, not the university enrollment. He had asked for every account held by Lerris Fenwood, and the house’s owner had truthfully found none.

“Levis,” she said.

He looked up.

“That’s the name on our papers.”

He remembered the attendant calling down the stair. He had heard his brother’s second name all his life and had still written the one he used.

“Put both,” he said.

They put both on everything after that. It did not solve the search, but it brought back receipts for supper, a request for a book, a payment for copying. His brother had intended to be there next week.

Neravel came to the Library on the third day. Markus met him in the court.

“Can you reach him?”

“Not by the way they used.”

“Can you reach him?”

“I may be able to send something.”

Markus caught his sleeve. Neravel did not pull away.

“Then send something.”

“The custodians won’t let me take the remaining record.”

Vesrin arrived before Markus answered. She took his hand off the sleeve with surprising force.

“Don’t agree to anything out here.”

Neravel spread his hands.

“Ask them yourselves.”

They did. Lian permitted a copy of the last verified position, omitting the unresolved margin. Neravel said it might not be enough. The keeper wrote that it was what she would release.

Five days later a message came back.

The writing was Lerris’s. Markus knew the awkward join in one word, the way he crowded the last line rather than use another sheet. He read it without breathing.

I am alive. There is a man here called Othrel. They have food. Don’t let them close the vacant place. I can explain it when I have the next position.

Below that, in smaller writing:

I am sorry about breakfast.

Markus pressed the paper flat with both hands.

“What breakfast?” Vesrin asked.

“The one he promised to be back for.”

There had been a meal, not breakfast. Markus remembered exactly what Lerris had said in the alcove. He said nothing about the difference yet.

That evening he wrote to Alayse. He began three letters and tore up two. In the third he told her his brother was alive, and that he did not know how to bring him home.

Chapter 24 — The Chair by the Wall

Othrel taught Lerris where to sleep.

The chair near the wall was safe when the lamp could be seen from it. The mattress beyond the arch was more comfortable, but sometimes the arch ceased to lead back. Othrel had lost a blanket that way and nearly lost a hand retrieving it.

Lerris slept in the chair. He woke cramped, furious, and ashamed of having slept at all.

A woman brought food. She was not the Voice. She carried two bowls on a board and told them to keep the spoons this time. Othrel thanked her by a name Lerris did not catch. When he asked whether she could lead them out, she looked toward the marked floor.

“I can come by the serving way.”

“Then we can—”

“No. You can’t.”

He followed her anyway. At the arch he saw a kitchen with a fire and somebody kneeling to scrub a pot. He put one foot through and felt the floor tilt beneath it. Othrel caught his coat and dragged him back.

The woman had already gone. The arch showed bare shelves.

Lerris struck Othrel’s hand away, then apologized. The older man returned to his bowl without accepting or rejecting it.

They worked.

The position altered when a choice was answered. It could also alter while they slept, though Othrel believed this meant somebody elsewhere had moved. Refusing did not freeze the whole arrangement. It left them sitting with whatever routes remained open while other people made decisions they could not see.

Once Lerris delayed a response until a boy carrying water had crossed a side passage. Othrel urged him to hurry. He waited. The boy vanished behind a pillar, and Lerris completed the move. Where the passage had been, there was then a wall.

He did not know whether he had saved the boy. He knew that answering sooner would have changed the floor beneath him.

After that he watched for other people before touching anything.

Neravel appeared during what Lerris thought was the fourth waking period. He brought clean stockings, a packet of paper, and the message Markus had sent.

Lerris seized it before greeting him.

His brother had written plainly. Do not complete anything to prove you can get out. Tell us what you need to remain alive. Tell us what you know, not what they promise.

He sat with the paper for a long time.

Neravel waited beside the empty square.

“You can come and go,” Lerris said.

“Not freely.”

“More freely than I can.”

“Yes.”

“Then take me.”

“I can’t take you through a place the sequence has assigned to you. We have to release that first.”

“How?”

Neravel looked at the book.

“Finish the return.”

Othrel closed his eyes.

Lerris noticed. He did not yet know what to ask about it.

Chapter 25 — The Winter Rooms

By winter Markus had learned which people would answer him quickly and which would answer him at all.

He rented a smaller room so he could afford the copying. Alayse sent money he had not asked for and a letter asking him to come home for a little while. He went, because she had asked and because he had begun hearing Lerris in the movement of carts outside his window.

She met him before he reached the door.

For a moment he could not put down the bag. He had carried it so long, from boat to road to the last hired cart, that his hand seemed to have accepted the handle as part of itself. Alayse took his wrist, opened his fingers one by one, and set it on the step.

Then she embraced him.

He held her too tightly. She made a small sound and he loosened his arms at once, apologizing into her hair. She told him to be quiet. He was so grateful to be told what to do that he laughed, and then could not stop crying.

They stayed on the step until she became cold.

Inside, the room had altered in small ways. A table had been moved closer to the light. There were new covers on two cushions. The cupboard door that had once caught at the top now closed smoothly, and Markus found himself opening it again to understand the repair.

“A man came,” Alayse said. “He planed it.”

“I could have done that.”

“Yes.”

He closed the cupboard. She took his coat and hung it where it had always belonged.

At supper he spoke too much. He described the messages, the differences between the copies, the custodian who had remembered a mark correctly after everyone else dismissed it. Alayse listened. When he paused for breath she asked whether he wanted more food. He realized he had eaten almost nothing.

“What have you been doing?” he asked.

She began telling him about a dispute over a delivery. He listened for several sentences, lost the thread, and had to ask who the man was. Her answer was patient.

“I’m sorry.”

“You’re tired.”

“I asked.”

“Yes. You did.”

She put down her spoon and began again, from the point at which he had stopped hearing. This time he followed. The delivery had been wrong, the man had insisted it was what had been ordered, and she had eventually produced the original measure from a drawer in which he had assured her it could not possibly be. Markus laughed at the right place. Alayse smiled, and the room became easier to sit in. He ate while she finished the account. The peas were sweet beneath the butter, and the pepper caught him by surprise. He coughed, drank, and took another spoonful before it had quite stopped burning.

That night he woke with one hand searching the space beside the bed. He could not remember what he expected to find. Alayse lit a lamp without asking him to explain it.

“Paper?” she said.

“No. I don’t think so.”

“Water?”

He nodded. She gave him the cup. There was a chipped place near the base he remembered from before the university. He turned it beneath his thumb until his breath slowed.

“Is he eating?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you know, or does someone say?”

“He writes about the food. He complains about it.”

She smiled a little. “That sounds like him.”

Markus had learned to doubt a recognizable hand and an ordinary complaint. Here, in bed beside Alayse, he let the resemblance be enough for one moment.

In the morning they walked out together. He had forgotten how much time a household required simply to continue: a hinge, a bill, a person waiting for a decision about where something should be stored. Alayse did not assign him tasks to cure him. She had tasks to do, and sometimes he was the person standing closest to the thing that needed lifting.

He carried a box to the upper room and was surprised by how weak he had become. Halfway up, he set it on a stair and pretended to adjust his grip. She stood below holding the door.

“Leave it there for a moment.”

“I can manage.”

“I know. Leave it there for a moment.”

They waited. Dust floated in the light above the stair. He could hear somebody outside calling for a dog. For several breaths nothing depended on him arriving anywhere.

Later, he helped make a list of household expenses. The figures were small compared with what he had spent on the search. Their very smallness made him ashamed. He had paid for three copies of a useless statement without asking what else the money might do. He did not tell Alayse this as if confession would return it to the household.

“I should send less home for a while,” he said, then stopped. “No. I mean take less.”

“I know what you mean.”

“I’m not speaking well.”

“You’re speaking.”

She continued the addition. He checked it and found a minor error. She accused him of looking pleased. He was, and admitted it.

On the fourth evening he opened his bag.

Alayse watched from the other side of the room. He took out the latest message and read it though he knew almost every word. When he looked up she had gone to the kitchen. He followed and found her cutting bread with unnecessary care.

“I have to go back.”

“I know.”

“I could wait another day.”

“Then wait because you want the day. Not because you want me to say you may leave afterwards.”

He leaned against the doorframe. She kept cutting.

“I do want it.”

She put the knife down.

“Then stay.”

He stayed. They spent much of the extra day on unremarkable things. There was no final conversation that made the separation acceptable. At the door the following morning she held his face between her hands and looked at him for longer than he found easy.

“Tell me when you don’t know,” she said. “Don’t keep sending certainty because you think I need it.”

“I will.”

“Try to.”

He kissed her. He took the bag himself. When the cart moved, he looked back until the turn hid her, then sat with his hands empty in his lap.

When he returned to Thepyrgos, two petitions had advanced and one had been lost.

He found the lost one beneath another name in the receiving book. He no longer felt triumphant when these things were put right. He made a note, paid for another copy, and carried it to the next desk.

Istren refused him at first.

“I have work,” she said. “Work that was waiting before you asked.”

“I can pay.”

“I don’t want to be paid to spend my evenings wondering whether a line I’ve copied will take someone away.”

He stood in her teaching room, feeling every pupil who had once waited outside for his brother.

“I need someone who will tell me when I’m reading what I want.”

“Then you need someone less tired than I am.”

He thanked her and left.

She came to his room three days later with a schedule written on a scrap. Two mornings each week. Public rooms only. No original objects brought to her. If she stopped, he would not ask again in the same conversation.

He agreed.

The first thing she asked to see was the complete record of his own earlier work.

“I’ve given you what remains.”

“What you’ve given the keepers.”

He looked at the box beneath his bed.

She waited.

There were letters, accounts of nights he had omitted because he could not bear them read as evidence, a description of using Aqyl’s dark key after he had already decided to stop. He had retained these as private failures, separate from the work. Istren read them as dates and actions.

“This one matters,” she said.

He knew which before she pointed.

He had returned to a room after leaving an incomplete arrangement and had found his place waiting for him. The old danger was not necessarily Lerris’s, but the record gave them a question they had not asked clearly: did the setting follow a person, or hold a place for whoever returned?

“Don’t make them the same,” Markus said.

“I haven’t.”

He let her copy the passage with its date intact.

Outside, rain struck the shutters. Istren kept writing. Markus sat opposite her and endured the work of being useful.

Chapter 26 — A Year in the Margin

Lerris began a new sheet for each waking period.

At first he wrote a date. After the third impossible correction he left the year and numbered the sheets. Othrel advised him not to number them. Lerris did it anyway, angry at the suggestion that he should surrender even this.

There were good days. He discovered how to preserve the route to the water without advancing the far edge. He found an old instruction whose missing word could be recovered from the next sentence. Othrel laughed at one of his jokes, a genuine laugh, and for an hour the room felt inhabited rather than endured.

There were bad days when the food arrived cold and the same move seemed to have been made without him. He accused Othrel of acting while he slept. Othrel accused him of forgetting. They spent a long silence on opposite sides of the table before comparing the sheets and finding that neither account was sufficient.

The Voice came with a candle.

She set it between them and asked whether they were cold. Lerris nearly thanked her before remembering who she was.

“Where have you been?”

“Elsewhere.”

“Take me there.”

“Would you leave him?”

He looked at Othrel. The older man kept his eyes on the candle.

“Don’t ask it like that,” Lerris said.

“How would you like it asked?”

“As something you can do.”

The woman sat. She did not seem offended.

“There is a completion that lets the place cease to require you. The custodians call it a breach. We call it an opening.”

“And what comes through?”

“What has been kept outside.”

He waited. She offered nothing more.

“That could be anything.”

“Yes.”

The candle flame bent toward the book though there was no draught. Lerris moved the book away. The flame straightened.

“Why me?”

“Because you answered a place that wasn’t yours. Now it is.”

He looked at the square he had touched. He had kept it empty. He had thought that was the opposite of taking it.

The Voice left the candle when she went.

Later a message arrived from Markus. The year at its head was 840.

Lerris read it, laughed once in disbelief, and carried it to Othrel.

The older man looked at the date. He did not look surprised.

Lerris hit him.

It was a short, clumsy blow. Othrel fell against the chair and struck his mouth on its back. Blood ran into his beard.

For a moment Lerris wanted him to hit back. Othrel only sat down.

“You could have told me,” Lerris said.

“I did.”

He fetched water. Othrel took the cloth from him and held it to his mouth himself.

Lerris sat on the floor beside the chair until the candle burned low. Neither spoke again that waking period.

Chapter 27 — The Letter Home

Alayse’s letter arrived folded around a smaller sheet.

She had written to Elyas. Markus read her account of it at the table in his rented room, then laid it down and looked out of the window until the street below ceased to blur. She had not sent him a copy to rebuke him. She wanted him to know who had been asked to help and what she had said. She still spoke of Lerris as a student. There was no ordinary word for where he was.

On the smaller sheet she had written the things Markus had forgotten to answer: whether the money had arrived; whether he was eating at the house or buying food elsewhere; whether the cough mentioned in his last letter was better.

He answered those first.

The money had arrived. He ate at the house three evenings a week, otherwise at the university. The cough was better. He had not mentioned it again because it was better, not because he meant to hide it. He wrote that last sentence and crossed it out. She had not accused him.

Then he told her about the latest message. Lerris had asked what month it was. Markus had answered with the full date, the weather, and an account of the southern court being repaved. He had enclosed a coin minted that year until Istren advised against sending any object whose part in the exchange they did not understand. He took it out. It remained on the table beside the letter.

There was a knock. He folded Alayse’s sheet before admitting Istren.

She had brought the correspondence chart. It was long enough now that they had to move the lamp to unroll it. Messages left by one route returned by another. A reply that referred to the previous letter might arrive before a reply to the one sent months earlier. They had stopped assigning dates from the order of delivery.

“What have you found?” he asked.

“The food.”

She pointed to the references in Lerris’s notes. A bowl with a broken rim. A woman asking for spoons. A meal arriving hot, then another that had stood too long. They had treated these as evidence of condition, things to reassure or frighten them. Istren had marked the descriptions against the route used by each messenger.

“The serving passage holds longer than the other approaches,” she said. “Not always. But it holds when they leave the same position waiting.”

“Neravel said it wouldn’t take him.”

“It may take someone who isn’t assigned a place.”

Markus looked at the chart. He felt hope rise so violently that he had to put both hands on the table.

“Can you prove it?”

“No.”

“Then how—”

“We ask about the passage. Precisely. Before asking permission to use it.”

He nodded. His mouth had gone dry.

They wrote six questions and reduced them to four. Whether the passage opened before the food arrived or with it. Whether the same person always brought it. Whether an empty bowl could be put back through. Whether Othrel had ever seen a server remain.

Markus wanted to add Can you wait? He did not. His brother had been waiting for longer than either of them could count.

After Istren left, he finished the letter to Alayse. He told her there was a new possibility and that he could not yet call it a route.

He put in the coin. She would have no use for it, but he wanted to send something that had been in his hand without making it evidence.

Chapter 28 — The Woman at the Serving Door

The food arrived in a basket this time.

The woman who brought it had a cut on one finger, wrapped so thickly that she could not bend it. Othrel took the basket before she had to ask. Lerris stepped aside, leaving the arch clear.

“What happened?” he asked.

“Knife.”

“Can I help?”

“You can give me the bowls.”

He did. He had learned not to ask all the questions at once.

She came often enough now that he knew she disliked carrying the soup because it slopped against her wrist. She had a son who wanted a dog. She sometimes arrived angry from somewhere else and sometimes with a little news she did not finish before the passage changed. He still did not know where her house was. When he had asked, she had laughed and said he would not like the walk.

“Does the door stay open if you do?”

She looked toward Othrel.

“Sometimes.”

“Have you stayed?”

“Long enough to wash a pot.”

“Would it bring someone else?”

“Who?”

“My brother.”

She set down the empty bowls. For the first time since he had known her, she looked afraid of him.

“Don’t ask me that.”

“I’m not asking you to do it.”

“You will.”

She gathered the bowls against her apron. He moved aside. At the arch she stopped.

“They ask about people I bring.”

“The custodians?”

“No.”

Then she was gone.

Lerris sat beside the basket. Othrel took out the bread and wrapped half of it for later. His lip had healed with a little pale line. Lerris tried not to watch it and often found himself doing so.

“I shouldn’t have asked like that,” he said.

“No.”

“You could have warned me.”

Othrel continued folding the cloth.

Lerris took a breath. “I’m sorry.”

“For which thing?”

The question was neither cruel nor rhetorical. He answered it properly.

They ate. Afterwards he wrote to Markus. The passage might admit a visitor. The server was afraid of those who would question her. He did not know how to promise her safety. He did not ask Markus to make that promise for him.

Before the next meal he moved slowly through the position, preserving the serving arch and the little alcove where a person could sit without being on the marked floor. He left another route closed to do it. Othrel watched him.

“That was where the blankets came.”

“I know.”

“We’ll be cold.”

“I know.”

Othrel considered the alcove. Then he took the blanket from his own chair and folded it over the stone seat.

The woman returned with bread. Lerris showed her the place.

“You don’t have to bring anyone,” he said. “But if you ever need to stay while the passage changes, you can sit there.”

She examined it without thanking him. He was glad of that. He did not trust how he would feel if she did.

That waking period she stayed to eat a piece of bread. She sat on the folded blanket and described the dog her son wanted, which was apparently large enough to pull their table out through the door. Othrel laughed. Lerris listened, and for a little while the food did not have to be eaten in haste.

Chapter 29 — The Terms of Entry

The year turned before Markus was permitted to attempt it.

He wrote 841 at the top of his request and stared at the figure. It made the search look orderly, as though another year had merely joined the first two. His hands had begun to shake when he was tired. He rested them beneath the table until he could sign.

The server had agreed to show a custodian the outer end of the passage. Neravel had arranged the meeting, and wanted acknowledgement that the Coterie had made it possible. Lian gave it in the record. She also required the route to be tested without a person assigned to the setting.

A basket went through and returned. Then a lamp. Then a custodian who remained within sight and came back with the smell of broth on his coat. Beyond him, he reported, there had been a man standing in an arch. They showed him drawings and descriptions. He identified Lerris’s scar at the knuckle, the one acquired long before the university, when he had tried to persuade a horse to do something unreasonable.

Markus wept after hearing that. He went into a storeroom, closed the door, and sat on a chest until he could come out. Nobody followed him. He was grateful to them for the rest of the day.

Lian did not permit him to go alone.

The older custodian who had stood at the return mark would accompany him. His wrist was healed. He had spent months revisiting the failed withdrawal, and Markus had spent some of those months blaming him. They spoke before the attempt, in an empty room where neither could appeal to witnesses.

“Did you see him put his hand down?” Markus asked.

“Yes.”

“Could you have stopped him?”

“I don’t know.”

The answer was unbearable because Markus believed it.

“What should I call you?”

The man told him his name was Tareth.

Markus repeated it. He had read it on statements for two years and had never used it aloud.

They prepared together. No loose diagrams. No answering a request to move. No promise of release to anyone inside. They would observe the route, speak to Lerris, and return before the server’s withdrawal.

The first rehearsal took place in a room so ordinary that Markus found himself resenting it. The custodians had marked the arch with two upright poles and a cloth laid across the top. A bench represented the alcove. A young attendant carried an empty basket through, turned, and came back while Markus and Tareth practiced withdrawing without obstructing her.

Markus stepped aside too late. The basket caught his sleeve.

“Again,” Tareth said.

“I understand it.”

“Then it won’t take long.”

They did it again. This time Markus turned his body, leaving room without stepping into the position marked on the floor. He felt foolish, and then remembered Lerris saying he had rehearsed the inspection. The thought made him cold.

“Did he do this?”

“Something like it.”

“And it didn’t help.”

“It got him to the chair safely. He chose after that.”

Markus looked at the two poles.

They practiced until Markus could make the turn without thinking about his feet. Then the attendant changed the position of the basket. He failed again. They began again.

At midday the server was brought to meet them in the outer room. She looked smaller here, or perhaps the account of her had made her larger in Markus’s mind. She held her apron bunched in both hands.

“You’ll go behind me,” she said. “Not beside me. If the ledge isn’t there, I stop.”

“What ledge?”

She described it. Tareth drew it, and she corrected the drawing impatiently. The edge was lower. The jar stood farther back. There was a stain above it that did not matter until somebody else’s lamp made the whole thing look like a different wall.

“Could you call if it’s wrong?” Markus asked.

“I could. You might already have put your foot through.”

He nodded. They agreed that he would wait for her hand, not merely follow her movement.

The second test used the actual approach. Markus remained in the outer room while Tareth crossed as far as the ledge and returned. The custodian’s face was wet with sweat. He had been gone less than a minute according to the attendants. He said it had felt longer, but would not guess how much longer.

“What did you see?” Markus asked.

“A wall. A shelf. The place where she puts the basket.”

“Did you see my brother?”

“No.”

They waited until the server said the next interval was available. There was no bell to summon it. The kitchen work beyond determined when she could pass, and the custodians accepted her judgment with an attention Markus wished he had known to give it earlier.

On the third test Tareth heard a voice asking whether there was more bread. He did not answer. He returned and said it had sounded like Lerris from the descriptions, which meant very little. Markus thanked him for refusing to make the report more comforting than it was.

That evening they laid out what could be carried. Food in an ordinary wrapping. A clean shirt. Letters inspected for diagrams and then returned unread by the attendant, who took considerable care to show Markus what she was doing. No metal tools beyond those required by the custodian. No loose slivers, borrowed cards, or copied positions.

Markus had brought a little knife to cut the bread. He left it outside when asked. The attendant used it to divide the loaf before packing it, and he watched her finish.

Then they reached the condition no rehearsal could make ordinary. If the return failed, the custodians outside would close the serving aperture rather than risk drawing the marked setting into the inhabited passage.

Markus objected to that last condition.

Lian wrote it again without changing a word.

“How long would you leave us?”

Until we could open safely.

“You left him.”

Yes.

He stood so abruptly that the chair struck the wall. Tareth looked down at his own hands.

Markus had expected a defense. The bare admission offered him nothing to push against. He walked to the window, put his forehead against the cool frame, and stayed there until he could return.

“I don’t absolve you by agreeing,” he said.

Lian waited for the attendant to write that into the terms.

Then Markus signed.

Istren would remain above with the paired records. Vesrin would assist her, not enter. The server’s name would not appear in the circulated account, and a separate arrangement would protect her household if Neravel’s associates retaliated. Markus checked that the household arrangement appeared on the copy going to the outer keeper.

Markus sent Alayse a letter before he went. He did not call the attempt a consultation.

Chapter 30 — His Brother’s Coat

Lerris recognized the coat first.

It was the one Markus had worn when he returned for the term, altered now at the cuffs. His brother came through behind the server with one hand on the wall, stooping beneath the arch. He looked older. Not impossibly older. That was worse.

Lerris remained where he was. The room seemed to lean around him.

Markus said his name.

He went to him so quickly that Tareth stepped back. The embrace hurt both of them. Lerris held on anyway, his face against his brother’s shoulder, and found himself laughing into the cloth while tears ran down his nose.

“You took your time,” he said.

Markus made a sound that might have been a laugh. He could not answer yet.

They stood together until the server asked them to leave room for the basket. Then they moved, awkwardly, still touching one another as though contact were required to keep the reunion true.

Othrel rose from his chair. Markus greeted him. Lerris noticed his brother looking at the scar on the older man’s mouth and looked away.

They sat in the alcove. Tareth remained near the arch. The server began washing the returned bowls, making enough noise that the conversation did not feel overheard.

Markus brought no book, no recovered page. He had bread, a clean shirt, letters from people who had written knowing they might not be delivered. Lerris touched each thing before setting it down.

“How long?” he asked.

“It’s 841.”

“I know the year. How much of it?”

Markus told him.

Lerris stared at the shirt. It had been folded by someone who knew how he liked the collar kept flat. He did not ask who.

“Can we go?”

“Not yet.”

His brother’s face changed with the words.

“The route brings someone unassigned. It doesn’t release your place.”

“I know.”

“We need to understand—”

“I know.”

Markus stopped.

Lerris stood, went to the table, and brought the current record. His hands shook with excitement now, something almost pleasurable beneath the shock.

“I’ve found why the vacant place remains. It isn’t just an exit. It keeps the earlier moves from being final. If we can carry the return through—”

“Lerris.”

“Listen.”

“I am listening.”

“No. You’re looking at me as if I’m ill.”

Markus glanced toward Othrel, then back.

“You’ve been here for years.”

“Then let me have learned something.”

The room went quiet except for water in the basin.

He spread the record on the stone between them. Some of it was careful work. He knew that. He needed his brother to know it before asking him to abandon anything.

Markus bent over it.

For a while they worked as they had over the damaged notebook in the infirmary. Lerris explained a mark; Markus asked what supported it. Twice he found an inconsistency. Twice Lerris showed him where the apparent contradiction resolved. The third time he could not.

“That’s because the last account is missing,” he said.

“Then leave it missing.”

“I can’t. It’s the way out.”

“Who told you?”

Lerris looked toward the serving arch.

Markus sat back.

“The same people who feed you.”

“You wouldn’t refuse the food.”

“No. I wouldn’t.”

“Then don’t speak as though it’s easy to refuse the rest.”

His brother put a hand over his eyes. Lerris heard how harsh he had been and could not yet forgive him enough to soften it.

The server set the last bowl in the basket. Tareth looked at the passage, then at Markus.

“Soon.”

Lerris grabbed his brother’s sleeve.

“Don’t go.”

Markus closed his hand over Lerris’s. He stayed until the last safe moment. Then Tareth led him back through the arch, and Lerris was left holding the clean shirt against his chest.

Part III — The Place and the Person

Chapter 31 — What Could Be Closed

Markus slept for three hours after the first visit.

He woke certain he had left something on the other side. His coat lay over the chair. His shoes were beneath it. The small bag he had taken was on the floor, empty of the bread and shirt. Nothing was missing that he had not meant to leave. He searched it twice before sitting down.

Alayse’s letter waited on the table. He had opened it before going below but had been unable to attend to more than the first line. Now he read the account of an ordinary disagreement over a horse and wept so suddenly that he stained the paper before he could move it away.

There was no one in the room. He was glad and then sorry. He washed his face, drank the cold water in the jug, and went to find Istren.

She listened to everything, including the argument.

“Was his work good?” she asked.

“Some of it.”

“Tell him which part.”

“I did.”

“Tell him again when you’re not asking him to stop.”

Markus sat with his hands on his knees. Istren had spread the new record over their table. She began comparing the changes, occasionally asking him where Lerris had placed a finger or which way he had turned the sheet. The questions made the visit less like a dream.

By afternoon they understood enough to disagree with the proposed closure.

The keepers intended to seal the near setting and preserve the vacant place within it. The recovered record would remain incomplete. No further response would be required. As far as the outer rooms were concerned, the route would cease to be available.

Lerris would remain assigned to the observer’s place.

Markus asked Lian whether that meant leaving him there. She wrote yes.

“And Othrel?”

His assignment precedes this one.

“That isn’t an answer about him.”

The keeper erased the water and wrote again.

He would remain.

Markus wanted to put his hand through the words. Instead he turned to Tareth, who stood near the wall looking ill. He had wanted an ally. The man already understood what was being proposed.

“You agreed to this?”

“To considering it. If the setting begins to open into the occupied rooms.”

“It hasn’t.”

“Not yet.”

Lian asked them to read a report before continuing. A serving aperture had opened for several breaths in a store beyond the Library boundary. The woman carrying the basket had found herself in the wrong building. She had returned without admitting anyone, but a clerk had seen the marked floor through the gap. The clerk’s account contained one of the changes Lerris had made to preserve the food passage.

Markus read it twice.

“Then he can alter it,” he said.

“Yes,” the attendant answered. “That’s part of the danger.”

It was also part of the hope. He could feel himself clutching at it, and knew the keeper could see.

“What would release him without completing it?”

No one answered immediately.

Istren, who had been quiet throughout, laid two sheets beside the dish.

“Your original withdrawal counted an empty place as occupied. His correction prevented that. Why does the closure have to count him as part of the floor?”

Lian studied the sheets. Her fingers moved once above the water without touching it.

Because he answered from that place.

“Then we need a record of him leaving it,” Istren said. “Not a completion from inside it.”

Markus looked at her.

She kept her eyes on the keeper. “You have records made by people who left. That’s how this began.”

The discussion lasted until the lamps were lit. It did not produce a procedure they could safely use. It did produce a list of what they would need to know: which earlier moves required Lerris to remain, which belonged to Othrel, whether an outside record could receive an unfinished departure without supplying the missing conclusion.

At the end Lian drew a line beneath the list.

If the outer boundary changes again, we may have to close before these questions are answered.

Markus wanted to call that a threat. The keeper’s attendant was already arranging an additional watch on the store where the aperture had appeared. The threatened people had names, wages, children waiting for them. He could not make them disappear by loving his brother more.

He took the list back to his room. In his reply to Alayse he wrote that Lerris had embraced him, that he had eaten the bread, and that they had quarrelled. Then he sat for a long time before writing what the keeper intended if the next attempt failed.

Chapter 32 — The Coterie’s Table

Neravel brought wine.

Lerris had not tasted it since the supper upstairs. The smell alone made him remember wet stockings near the kitchen fire, the first ordinary discussion, the pleasure of being listened to. He took the cup and held it without drinking.

“Is this payment?”

“No.”

“Then sit down.”

Neravel sat. Othrel took the other cup. He drank a little, closed his eyes, and swallowed with care.

“The keepers have spoken to your brother,” Neravel said.

“Yes.”

“They’ll close it with you inside.”

“If they believe they have to.”

“Does the qualification help?”

Lerris set the cup down.

“Don’t speak to me as though I haven’t been here.”

Neravel bowed his head. When he raised it, the easy manner had gone.

“I thought you would be better able to leave than the rest of us.”

“Why?”

“Because you saw the empty place before you entered.”

“You arranged for me to find it.”

“I gave you work. You found what I hadn’t.”

“That doesn’t make it innocent.”

“No.”

Othrel watched them over his cup. Lerris had heard the word no from so many people who meant to continue exactly as before that he could no longer decide what an admission was worth.

“What happens if I complete the return?”

Neravel took a folded sheet from inside his coat and laid it on the table.

“The place ceases to hold you. The routes cease to require permission from the outer custodians. What has been withheld can move.”

“People?”

“People. Memories. Things for which those are poor names.”

“And the boundaries?”

“Change.”

“Into what?”

“I don’t know.”

There it was, after all the careful phrases. Lerris looked at the sheet but did not open it.

“Would you do it?”

“I came to help you do it.”

“That isn’t the same.”

Neravel’s mouth tightened. He looked older than he had in the public room, less able to arrange his expression before answering.

“I’ve been waiting for a voice I remember for fifteen years. I don’t know whether it’s still a person. I don’t know whether I would recognize it if it came. Yes. I would open the way.”

The room was quiet. Othrel put down his empty cup.

“You never told me that,” Lerris said.

“It wouldn’t have made my drawings better.”

“No. It might have made me read your offers differently.”

Neravel nodded. He did not ask forgiveness.

Later, after he left, Othrel unfolded the sheet. Lerris almost stopped him. It was a proposed completion, missing the final response. He could follow enough of it to see why Neravel believed it might release them.

He could also see that release was not its only work.

At the far margin a space had been set aside for an answer that no one inside the existing arrangement could give. The word beside it was one he had seen in the recovered book, partly scraped away: bride.

Othrel covered it with his hand.

“Not aloud.”

“Who is she?”

“I don’t know who she was.”

“Have you seen her?”

Othrel nodded.

“What did she want?”

“To be let finish what we’d begun.”

Lerris folded the sheet. He put it where Markus would be able to see it when he came again.

That night he dreamed of the cookshop where Caldris had eaten while doubting. He could remember the sauce and the impatience and the chair beneath him. In the dream Caldris was still waiting for the next morning’s examination. Lerris tried to tell him that it had happened years ago, but his friend had already turned to answer someone else.

Chapter 33 — The Second Visit

Markus returned with Tareth and permission to remain through one change of the serving passage.

This meant he might not be able to leave by the way he had entered. They told Lerris before sitting down. He asked whether the keeper had improved the return or merely agreed to risk them. Tareth said both.

Vesrin had sent a letter, and a scarf because the blankets had not returned. Lerris put the scarf around Othrel’s shoulders. It suited him badly. The older man rubbed the wool between his fingers and kept it on.

Markus began by showing him the part of his work Istren had verified.

“The orientation is sound. The passage left by the boy’s departure is sound as far as these two accounts can establish. Your choice to wait may have kept it open long enough.”

Lerris stared at the marks beside his own writing.

“May have.”

“Yes.”

“That is what I wrote.”

“I know.”

For a moment he could not speak.

Then Markus showed him the closure proposal.

They argued. Not immediately, and not over whether the danger was real. Lerris understood the store aperture. He remembered making the change. What he could not bear was the calmness of the sentence assigning him to a preserved place, as though his endurance were a property the keepers could rely on.

“You signed something that lets them leave you too,” he said.

“Yes.”

“You had no right.”

Markus looked at him.

Lerris heard the sentence return before his brother spoke. He had made his own choice on the square, and Markus had been left to receive it.

“That doesn’t make mine better,” he said.

“No.”

They sat in silence for a little while. Tareth examined the serving arch. Othrel watched him with an expression Lerris could not read.

The proposed outside record gave them work. They began separating Lerris’s changes from the ones he had inherited. Each required an account of what had been open before, what he had done, and what remained afterwards. The pages he had numbered by waking period helped, but not enough. He had omitted meals when they seemed unimportant. He had described the same doorway by three different names.

At one point Markus found a line in Neravel’s proposed completion that made him stop.

“What?” Lerris asked.

His brother shook his head.

“Markus.”

“I’ve seen that comparison.”

“Where?”

“In the pages I burned.”

He reached toward the sheet, then withdrew his hand. Lerris saw the movement and remembered the tray, the little curl of ash beneath its rim.

“Could it be the same source?”

“Yes.”

“Could it help?”

Markus closed his eyes. “Possibly.”

They traced the reference without completing it. It led to a comparison whose original was no longer in the surviving notebook. A later hand had supplied a continuation. Markus recognized the first half. He could not certify the second.

He wanted to read it. Lerris could see that he wanted it badly enough to be ashamed.

“We can mark it uncertain,” Lerris said.

His brother let out a breath.

“Yes.”

They marked it. Tareth returned to say the serving aperture was beginning to shift. The planned change had come earlier than expected.

They gathered the sheets. Markus did not try to leave at once; the route showed shelves where the passage should have been. Tareth sat beside the arch, holding the outside record in both hands.

They would have to remain until the next opening.

Othrel divided the bread into four portions. He did it without comment, and Lerris loved him for it.

Chapter 34 — The Guest at the Lamp

By the time the next opening failed, Markus had been awake too long.

He lost his place in the account, found it, and lost it again. Tareth offered to read aloud. The words did not help. Markus pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and asked for a moment.

Lerris took the paper away. He made a place against the wall, folded the clean shirt beneath his brother’s head, and sat beside him while he tried to sleep. Every time the floor scraped, Markus woke with his hands out before him.

“I used to do that,” Lerris said.

His brother gave a tired, disbelieving laugh.

“Very comforting.”

“I meant you can stop.”

“I understood.”

He shut his eyes again. The lamp burned lower. Tareth and Othrel murmured over the record in the alcove, two older men making themselves useful rather than admitting they were cold.

The woman came from beyond the lamp.

There was no arch there. Lerris knew the wall. He had spent enough hours looking at it to recognize a crack shaped like a hook. She passed in front of the crack wearing a pale dress that should have caught dust from the floor and did not. At her wrist hung a narrow ribbon, frayed at one end.

He did not rise. His hand had gone to Markus’s shoulder before he knew he had moved.

“He needs sleep,” she said.

“Don’t touch him.”

“I haven’t.”

Her voice was quiet enough not to wake him. That frightened Lerris more than a whisper would have.

“What do you want?”

“To be received.”

“Not here.”

She looked at the lamp, the borrowed scarf, the four pieces of bread.

“You’ve received less welcome guests.”

He said nothing. He did not want to discover that an answer counted as something other than speech.

She knelt on the other side of Markus. Lerris caught her wrist. It was warm. She let him hold it, and for a moment the contact was so ordinary that he nearly apologized.

“Let him rest,” she said.

His brother’s breathing changed.

Lerris released her and shook Markus’s shoulder. He did not wake. He was breathing deeply, without the catch that had returned since entering the archive.

“What have you done?”

“Nothing he hasn’t needed.”

“Wake him.”

“Why?”

He could not answer without sounding cruel. She had made that the first difficulty, and he understood it while still wanting the sleep to continue.

Tareth came toward them. He stopped where the lamp’s light met the line of salt he had laid beside the alcove. The woman glanced at it and remained where she was.

“Don’t ask her for anything,” he said.

“She put him to sleep.”

“I can see that.”

The woman smiled, not at Tareth but at Markus.

“He’ll wake when I leave. Unless you’d like the rest to last.”

Lerris looked at his brother’s face. Without effort in it he seemed younger, almost the man at the first breakfast in Thepyrgos, before Lerris had learned to watch him.

“What would you take?”

Tareth made a sharp sound. Too late.

“One waiting place,” she said. “The one you’re keeping empty.”

“And then?”

“You could go.”

“All of us?”

“All who wished to.”

Othrel had come no closer than the salt. He was gripping the scarf at his throat.

“What comes in?” Lerris asked.

She looked at him with something like disappointment.

“I do.”

He had never seen a threat offered with such patience. He could imagine bringing her a chair, apologizing for the cold, waking Markus to say there was a guest. He could imagine doing it gladly.

“No,” he said.

The word hurt. He had expected relief from saying it. Instead he felt as though he had denied his brother water.

The woman stood. Her wrist bore no mark where he had held it.

“Keep him warm, then.”

She went back past the lamp. Markus woke with a startled breath and reached for Lerris.

He held his brother until the shaking passed. He did not tell him, yet, how close he had come to asking for another hour.

Chapter 35 — The Serving Way

Markus remembered warmth.

It embarrassed him to say so after Lerris described the woman. He had expected to remember nothing, or to wake with the residue of a nightmare. Instead he remembered sleeping in a room where he did not need to hear the door. He wanted it back with a force that made him afraid to look toward the lamp.

Lerris sat beside him, waiting.

“You didn’t agree to anything?”

“No.”

“Did I?”

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“I was here.”

Markus looked at his brother’s face. He had come to rescue him and was now asking to be reassured like a frightened child. Lerris answered without impatience. That helped until he wondered how many times his brother had needed the same assurance and received none.

Tareth gave him water. The custodian had replenished the salt from a little packet kept inside his coat. The line was thin, visibly finite. Markus had never been so glad to see a boundary that made no promise of holding everything.

They worked again after he could attend to the page.

The outside record needed the serving interval. It was the only passage that had admitted people without assigning them a move. The server arrived through a route opened by somebody elsewhere, but her departure did not appear to complete the sequence. It was repeated work, a task with an ordinary end: the bowls returned, the basket carried away.

“We could return with the bowls,” Lerris said.

“You tried following her,” Othrel reminded him.

“I know. I mean the record of the departure, not hiding under the bread.”

Othrel grunted. Markus could not tell whether he approved of the distinction or disliked the joke.

They needed the server’s account: what she did before entering and what told her she could leave. Lerris knew more of this than anyone. He knew that she checked a ledge for an empty jar; that she sometimes waited until she could hear a spoon touch a bowl; that she did not cross if the light above the arch appeared blue.

“Why didn’t you put that in the letters?” Markus asked.

Lerris looked ashamed, then angry.

“Because I was trying to explain the part I thought mattered.”

Markus thought of his own inventories, the measurements preserved while he omitted who had brought the lamp. He wrote down the jar.

When the woman came, they did not surround her. Othrel took the food. Tareth stood back from the arch. Lerris asked whether she had time to sit.

She looked at Markus, then at the new salt.

“Something came?”

“Yes.”

She put the basket down.

“Then not long.”

Her hands were red from washing. The bandage had gone from her finger, leaving a raised pale seam. Markus had seen people with hands like that all his life and had never wanted so badly to be worthy of their help.

They asked about the jar. It was set out by the kitchen keeper when a passage could be used. Who was the kitchen keeper? A woman who changed shifts with another. Did either serve the Voice? They served whoever had the kitchen, she said, with sudden weariness. It was a kitchen. Fires needed tending.

Lerris stopped the questions.

“Do you want to stop coming?”

She looked at him sharply.

“I want to go home after.”

“What keeps you?”

“The account. I’m paid against the account. If I leave before it’s closed, they say what I’ve carried is still owed.”

Neravel’s purse, Markus thought. Or another purse behind it. He could not settle that here.

“Would payment outside release it?”

“I don’t know.”

Tareth asked to see the tally she carried. She took it from her apron: an ordinary strip of wood, notches darkened with use, a cord through one end. He did not touch it. He asked permission to describe it to the outer keepers. She considered this for a long time, then agreed.

The arch flickered behind her. Not light, precisely; for a moment the shelf visible beyond it was nearer than the opening. She rose at once.

“Bowls.”

Othrel had them ready. She went out quickly, holding the basket against her hip.

Tareth stepped after her to fix the return mark. He had reached the arch when the shelf swung across it. Markus heard the blow before he saw the custodian fall.

Lerris caught him under the arms. Together they dragged him clear. The arch showed stone.

Tareth’s leg lay at an angle below the knee. He tried to sit up, saw it, and went white.

Markus took his shoulders while Lerris fetched cloth. Othrel broke a length from the unused stool. They did not discuss the proof while they bound the leg. They discussed where to put hands, whether he could feel his toes, whether he was going to vomit.

He was. Lerris held him forward until he had finished.

Afterwards the custodian apologized for losing the route.

“Don’t,” Markus said, with such anger that Tareth flinched.

He softened his grip.

“Please don’t.”

Chapter 36 — The Counter-Record

Outside, Istren received the account of the tally before she learned about Tareth’s leg.

The two notes arrived together in the same basket, but one had been folded inside a cloth. She read the tally first, pencilling a question beside the description. Then Vesrin opened the cloth and made a sound that caused her to put down the pen.

They went to Lian at once.

The keeper read both notes. Her face changed at the injury, though her hand remained steady over the water. She ordered a litter and a healer to the outer serving approach. The opening did not return. They waited with the litter beside a wall that looked incapable of admitting a basket, much less a man.

Istren sat on its edge. Vesrin walked the length of the passage and back. The healer asked how long the leg had been broken. Nobody could tell him.

By the next watch they had an answer about the tally.

It belonged to a household account administered by the Coterie. The food supplied was ordinary food, bought in ordinary markets, but its delivery was entered as service to the closed setting. The keeper could accept the outstanding obligation into a separate account if the supplier consented and someone paid the wages due. She could not erase it merely because its administration was cruel.

Vesrin said she would pay what she could. Istren sent for Markus’s purse and the authorization he had left for necessary expenses. It was enough for the arrears, not enough to compensate the woman for losing the work. They asked the Library to employ her in the outer kitchen until she found something else.

The request produced a dispute about who would answer if she carried something from the lower service into the public rooms. Vesrin asked whether they proposed to leave her carrying it because that was less visible. The attendant told her sharply to lower her voice. She did, and repeated the question.

Lian wrote the conditions herself. Separate entry. No objects taken into the public rooms. A change of clothes, washing, examination, and paid time while they observed whether anything followed. The server would be told what they feared. She could refuse the employment and still receive the wages owed.

Istren copied it in plain language.

Then they turned to the harder matter: how to receive an unfinished departure.

The recovered reader’s book contained one. The woman who had left in its early pages had been recorded by someone outside the active arrangement, in a separate hand. Until now the keepers had treated that as a later addition. Istren laid the physical descriptions beside each other. Different ink, different pressure, a line written across an older crease. It was later, but it had been written while the sequence still mattered.

“The record didn’t have to be completed,” she said. “It had to stop requiring her next move.”

Lian asked what had occupied the place afterwards.

“Nothing. That’s the part Lerris found.”

They followed it through the available accounts. It did not work as a general release. Othrel’s original assignment was older and bound into too many moves. Lerris’s could be separated only if the links made since his arrival were surrendered. His work inside had kept routes open. It had also made his presence useful to the setting.

If they removed it, they would lose the route to the book from which he hoped to recover the final explanation. The Coterie’s completion would no longer be possible by that sequence. The surviving record would be deliberately damaged, with enough retained to preserve the restriction and not enough to repeat the approach.

Vesrin looked at the pages.

“Can he live with that?”

Istren thought of Markus asking her to read what he had hidden.

“We have to ask him.”

The first returning message carried Tareth’s pain through the neatness of Markus’s hand. There was little medicine left. The leg was swollen. He could not be moved through a narrow opening on his feet.

Istren wrote the proposal slowly enough that she could not pretend its omissions were accidental. She included the danger to Othrel. She included the loss of the work. She included the fact that the Library would retain a claim on Lerris’s future handling of any surviving fragment.

Chapter 37 — Othrel’s Book

Othrel read the proposal before Lerris.

Markus gave it to him because his part was the most uncertain. Lerris wanted to object and recognized the objection as his desire to understand everything before anyone else was allowed to decide.

The older man took a long time. He moved his lips over some of the words. Since Tareth’s injury, the room had been cold, and his fingers were stiff inside Vesrin’s scarf.

“What happens to the things I remember?” he asked.

Markus knelt beside him so he would not have to raise his voice.

“They don’t know. The setting has been using your record for longer. Removing it may take parts of what you can recall with it.”

“Parts.”

“Yes.”

“Which?”

“They can’t say.”

Othrel looked toward the marked floor. Tareth slept fitfully in the alcove. Lerris had finally persuaded him to take the last of the powder the healer had sent through. The man had wanted to save it in case someone else was hurt.

“I’ve forgotten enough already,” Othrel said.

Nobody answered.

He opened his book. Some pages were his own writing; others he had copied so often that he no longer knew whose they had been. He found a small sketch of a cup with a chipped handle and touched it with one finger.

“My wife hated that cup.”

Lerris looked up. Othrel had never mentioned a wife.

“Is she—”

“I don’t know.”

They sat in silence.

“She said it caught her finger when she washed it. I kept turning it the other way on the shelf, as though that repaired it.”

His mouth moved. Lerris thought he might laugh. He did not.

“I can’t remember the color.”

“We can write what you do remember,” Markus said.

“So I can read it afterwards and be told it was mine?”

The question stopped him. Lerris thought of the comb in its wooden cradle, the father moving a bed, a daughter hearing an afternoon she could no longer inhabit.

“I don’t know if it would help,” Markus said.

Othrel closed the book.

“Write the cup.”

They wrote for the rest of that waking period. A house with a step worn toward the left. The name of a neighbor, uncertain in its last syllable. A child who had not belonged to him but had come through the kitchen every morning for a piece of bread. The things arrived in no useful order. Sometimes Othrel contradicted himself and became angry when Markus tried to reconcile the accounts. They kept both.

Lerris wrote until his hand cramped. Then Markus took the pen and he warmed Othrel’s cup between his palms.

At the end the older man asked for the proposal again.

“Will I still know how to eat?”

Markus swallowed.

“They think so.”

“Walk?”

“Yes.”

“Will someone tell me if I’m waiting for people who aren’t coming?”

“We will try to find them.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Lerris leaned forward.

“Yes,” he said. “We won’t keep saying tomorrow because we don’t want to tell you.”

Othrel studied his face. Then he nodded.

Lerris wanted to apologize again for the blow. The older man had given him something far more difficult than an occasion for apology. He had to accept it without making Othrel attend to his shame.

They sent his consent out in the next basket.

Only then did Lerris read the part concerning himself.

The unfinished record could be preserved outside the active sequence if he surrendered his right to complete or reproduce it. It would not be an ordinary promise. The place he had answered would remain marked as a departure witnessed by the custodians, and his future participation in an equivalent completion could reopen what they sealed. He would owe the Witness’s keepers disclosure of surviving approaches, and would be called if the boundary changed.

“They mean to keep me,” he said.

Markus sat opposite him.

“They mean to keep hold of what you could open.”

“That sounds better when you say it.”

“It doesn’t feel better.”

Lerris looked at his numbered sheets. The work that had kept him alive would be used once, damaged, and put beyond him. He had no finished answer to carry home. He had corrected a drawing, endangered a custodian, and spent years he could not remember as years.

“I want to see the next page,” he said.

His brother did not tell him it was foolish.

“I know.”

For a long time they sat with that.

Chapter 38 — The Last Comparison

Neravel came before the final withdrawal had been prepared.

He had learned enough from the server’s departure arrangements to understand what the keepers intended. He did not conceal his anger. He stood inside the arch with a packet under his arm and looked at the bedding around Tareth as though the injury had been committed to inconvenience him.

Then Tareth woke and asked for water. Neravel fetched it. He held the cup while the man drank, and the resentment left his face for a moment.

Lerris watched him. It would have been easier if the kindness were false.

“You can go out with her,” Neravel said. “The woman carrying the food. You understand what that costs us.”

“She has been paid.”

“I don’t mean the wages.”

“She wanted to go home.”

Neravel put the cup down.

“And the ones who can’t?”

Lerris had no answer that satisfied both of them.

The packet contained a copy of the comparison Markus recognized. It was more complete than the earlier sheet. Neravel laid it on the table and stepped away.

“I didn’t write the missing part. You can examine the hands. You can ask the keeper whether she has it. You can decide it’s false. But don’t pretend no one offered you the means.”

Markus approached it slowly.

Lerris watched his brother read. The first change in his face was recognition. The second was grief. He had not understood until then how much Markus wanted the destroyed work back. It belonged to a time in which he had trusted his own mind more easily.

“The line’s right,” Markus said.

“Which line?”

“The one I remember.”

“And the rest?”

His brother did not answer.

Beyond the lamp a pale edge showed against the wall. Not a whole figure. A hem, perhaps, or a trick of light. Tareth saw it and reached for the salt.

Neravel kept his eyes on Markus.

“You don’t have to give it up twice.”

The sentence struck so precisely that Lerris felt it in his own body.

Markus lifted the sheet. For a moment he looked as though he would fold it into his coat. Then he set it beside the current record and compared the final marks.

“This supplies the answer from outside the position,” he said.

“Yes.”

“We don’t know who’s answering.”

“We can find out.”

“By letting it answer.”

Neravel said nothing.

Markus drew his own surviving notes toward him. He marked the reference as excluded, with enough description that Istren would know what he had refused to carry across. His pen shook. He waited, steadied it, and finished.

“Take it away,” he said.

Neravel looked at Lerris.

“Is that your answer too?”

Lerris thought of Othrel’s cup, the unknown color, the handful of scenes they had written down. He thought of Markus on the floor, sleeping as if nothing had ever harmed him. He could not make those wants cease by recognizing what was being asked.

“Yes.”

Neravel gathered the packet. At the arch he turned.

“I hope the people keeping your record remember that you were a person.”

“So do I,” Lerris said.

The man left. They did not see him again before the sealing.

The pale edge remained near the lamp. Tareth pointed out the break in the salt line, and Lerris filled it under his direction. The hem withdrew past the lamp; its light still reached the empty floor.

Chapter 39 — Before the Opening

They had one full basket in which to send their decisions.

The server set it on the table and waited while Markus wrapped the papers. She had already agreed to the outer arrangement. Her household would be told where she was; the wages were to be paid whether she accepted the new work or not. She had asked that her son be brought to see her as soon as the keepers judged it possible. That request was written separately, in words she had approved.

“Not when it’s convenient,” she said again. “When it’s possible.”

Markus read it back exactly.

Lerris had written his consent beneath Othrel’s. The act had not made him peaceful. He kept looking at the numbered sheets, imagining how easily one could be folded into the lining of his coat. No one had searched him. No one had even asked whether he intended to keep something back. Their trust felt like another difficulty laid in his way.

He told Markus.

His brother looked at the coat, then at him.

“Which sheet?”

“The one with the serving passage before it changed.”

“Why that one?”

“I don’t know. Because I understood it.”

Markus sat beside him. They read it together. It contained a description of the boy with the water, the delay, the wall appearing after he had gone. Below that were the positions necessary to repeat the change.

“Keep the account,” Markus said. “Give them the working.”

“Will they allow it?”

“Ask.”

They sent the question with the consent. Lerris copied out the boy’s passage without the diagram, then laid both versions side by side so no one could mistake what he had removed.

He kept looking at the blank bottom half of the new sheet. At last he folded it once and left it visible on the table.

After the basket went, they prepared Tareth for moving. His splinted leg had swollen beneath the bindings. Markus loosened them under his direction, one at a time, while Othrel held the boards steady. Lerris fetched water and cloth. The custodian’s breath became quick and shallow, but he did not tell them to stop until the second binding was free.

They stopped at once.

“I’m sorry,” Tareth said.

“You haven’t delayed an appointment,” Othrel told him.

The remark was so dry that Tareth laughed, then groaned. Lerris had to look away because he had begun to smile at the wrong part of the sound.

They used the unoccupied chair to make a carrying seat. Its back was sound; the front stretcher had loosened. Othrel knew how to brace it with a strip cut from the broken stool. He worked slowly, refusing help until he needed a second pair of hands to hold the joint. Lerris held it while he bound the wood.

“You’ve done this before.”

“Furniture breaks.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant. Hold that end.”

They tested the chair with Markus sitting in it. He was heavier than Tareth now, though Lerris remembered a time when the difference would have gone the other way. He and Othrel lifted it a few inches. The binding held. They carried it to the alcove and back, discovering where they would have to turn sideways.

The reply arrived after a period of darkness in which the lamp burned but gave little light.

Lian accepted the separated narrative if it remained free of the reproducible sequence. Istren had added a note in her own hand: The boy should stay in the account. Don’t make it cleaner by taking him out.

Lerris sat down with that sentence for longer than he meant to.

The instructions for departure were longer. The outer end would be held by the kitchen account, now transferred under the keeper’s authority with the server’s consent. A pair of external witnesses would record each departure while the inner arrangement remained incomplete. The active working sheets would be left within the marked setting; the corresponding outside record would retain the gaps. Only after the people were across would Lian close the final correspondence between the two.

If anyone completed the offered next move, the whole procedure would fail. If the inner record crossed with them, it might carry the assignment out. If the outer record were destroyed, the empty place would again require an answer.

Tareth read the instructions twice, then asked Markus to read them aloud. They sounded like people explaining exactly where they could still be wrong.

Othrel asked whether he could take the pages about the cup and the house.

“Yes,” Markus said. “Those are yours.”

The older man tucked them inside Vesrin’s scarf.

When everything was ready, Lerris found he had nothing useful to do. He sat with his back against the wall and watched his brother mend a tear in the carrying cloth. The stitches were ugly. Markus had never been good at sewing. He pulled each one tight with the determined concentration he gave to a task he resented being unable to perform elegantly.

Lerris wanted to tell him he could do it better. Instead he held the lamp.

Chapter 40 — The Outer Table

Istren had asked for two copies of the departure instructions and received three.

She checked them against one another because by now she trusted no number of copies to prove agreement. On the second sheet, a word had been omitted. It was an ordinary copying error, the sort she corrected for pupils every week. She made the correction and had the attendant witness it. The terror of the work did not improve anyone’s handwriting.

The outer passage had been cleared. The litter stood ready. The healer had laid out his tools where he could reach them without passing through the watchers. Two guards kept everyone else back. There were no spectators, though several people had asked to be present and one had tried to invoke a title.

Vesrin sat at the second table. Her task was to read the inner record’s reported changes against Istren’s account of who crossed. They had practiced the sequence with empty chairs until both could distinguish a pause from a missed response. Now the chairs were stacked against the wall, and the absence of them made the room seem underprepared.

Lian came with the dish, salt, and a small copper weight whose base had been scored. She placed the weight on the outer record. She did not explain it as an instrument anyone could use. The rite belonged to her custody and to the authority she served. Istren had asked what the others needed to do if the keeper became unable to continue. The answer was to hold the current record, close the serving aperture if they could, and summon the other keeper waiting above. They were not to improvise the final act.

It was the first instruction all day that made Istren breathe more easily.

The server came out first.

She carried an empty basket. At the threshold she stopped, looked back, and began to cry without making a sound. Vesrin rose. The woman waved her away until she had set down the basket herself.

“My boy?”

“He’s with the woman you named,” the attendant said. “She has been told where you are.”

“When can I—”

“As soon as the examination permits. We wrote it.”

The server nodded. She let herself be led to the adjoining room, looking back once at the basket as though she had forgotten something inside it.

Her departure was not counted as one of the assigned people. The route remained under the transferred account, but the kitchen beyond the arch had already changed. Istren could see a table at an angle, then a wall, then a darker opening farther away.

She put her finger beneath the first line.

Lian touched the water.

The marks on the copper weight darkened. Istren felt a pressure in her ears, like the beginning of a descent through mountain air. Vesrin swallowed and kept her pen over the page.

“Visible,” the attendant said.

The arch opened onto the room they had known only through descriptions. Istren saw the lamp, the patched chair, Tareth on the carrying seat. Then Markus stepped into view, and she had to grip the edge of the table to keep from rising.

He looked toward her. She could not tell whether he could see her face clearly. He raised one hand: not a greeting, a sign that he was ready.

Lerris stood behind him. He was thinner than the young man she remembered leaving her teaching room. His hair had grown unevenly. He held a folded sheet against his chest as though afraid to put it down.

She looked back at the record before relief could make her miss a line.

Chapter 41 — Four Departures

Tareth went first because he could not go quickly later.

Lerris and Markus lifted the chair. Othrel steadied the splinted leg as they turned beneath the arch. The opening was broad enough, but its lower edge rose where the floor should have continued. Markus had to lift his end higher. Tareth caught the chair’s back with both hands and made a low sound through his teeth.

“Wait,” Lerris said.

They waited. The outer attendants took the forward weight. For several breaths the chair hung between the rooms, held by people who could not all see one another’s hands.

Then the far end lowered. Tareth was through.

Lerris heard a woman say the custodian’s name and another voice answer that he had been received. The sound steadied him more than the sight of the doorway. Someone was there to take him.

Markus returned to the inner side. He had not crossed completely. His sleeve was wet where it had brushed the edge of the opening, though there was no water to be seen.

Othrel stood beside his chair, holding the scarf at his throat.

“Now you,” Lerris said.

The older man looked at the room. His book lay on the table, open at the last incomplete position. The pages about the cup were inside the scarf. Lerris could see their corner.

“I don’t know where I’m going.”

“To people who will help.”

“Will you be there?”

“Yes.”

Othrel took a step, stopped, and looked back at the chair.

It was only a chair. Lerris had hated it, slept in it, watched the old man draw it closer to the lamp.

He offered his arm. Othrel took it.

They crossed the inner marks slowly. At the edge the older man’s grip tightened so sharply that Lerris nearly cried out. He looked at him and saw bewilderment erase the intention from his face.

“What is this?” Othrel asked.

“A doorway.”

“Where’s—”

He stopped. His eyes moved from Lerris to Markus, then to the scarf.

Markus took his other arm. They led him through without hurrying him. Outside, someone spoke his name. He did not answer at first. Then he turned toward the sound, and the attendant guided him away from the opening.

Lerris remained at the inner edge until the outside voice confirmed receipt. He wanted to follow and ask what Othrel still knew. Markus put a hand on his shoulder.

“We have to finish leaving.”

The next change came while they turned back.

The lamp brightened. Beyond it the woman in the pale dress stood with one hand on the table. Beside her lay the packet Neravel had taken away.

“You needn’t leave him empty,” she said.

Lerris looked toward the arch. Othrel was out of sight.

“You don’t know what he lost,” Markus said.

“I know what I can return.”

Her fingers rested on the packet. The familiar comparison lay uppermost, the one Markus had recognized. Beneath it was another sheet, covered in a hand Lerris knew as his own.

He saw the next position.

Not enough to read. Enough to know how close he was to seeing it. The arrangement had been waiting through all the cold meals and doubtful mornings, and now it lay in reach, no more protected than a page on a public desk.

The outer attendant called. Markus answered. Lerris did not move.

The woman watched him with sympathy that might have been real.

“You can leave knowing,” she said.

He imagined Othrel’s cup restored to its color. Markus sleeping. Himself able to write a finished account that explained what he had done with the years. He wanted all of it. The wanting filled him so completely that there seemed no room left for the plain fact that she had asked to enter.

His brother took his hand.

Lerris looked down at their joined fingers. Markus’s nails were broken at two edges. Ink had settled into the lines beside his thumb. He had come through the serving way carrying a shirt.

“I can’t read it,” Lerris said.

“You can,” the woman answered gently.

He turned his face away.

“No.”

They crossed to the arch together. The floor dragged at his feet as though he were lifting them from deep mud. Markus went first, still holding him. At the threshold Lerris felt his hand pulled in two directions: toward his brother and toward the place he had answered.

Outside, Istren began reading the departure.

She used the name on the record. Levis Fenwood. Then the name Markus had asked her to add. Lerris. She described the unfinished place and the man withdrawing from it, the work retained within, the narrative permitted out.

He had to answer that the withdrawal was his choice.

The words caught in his throat. Saying them meant leaving the page unread.

Markus could not answer for him. He stood on the outer side with tears running down his face and waited.

Lerris answered.

The pressure released. He fell through against his brother, and the attendants caught them both.

Chapter 42 — What the Keeper Held

Istren did not look up when Lerris fell.

She heard the impact, the hurried voices, Markus saying he had him. She kept her finger under the final line. Vesrin read the corresponding gap in the inner account, then stopped before the mark that would have supplied its answer.

The aperture remained open.

Through it, the woman in pale cloth approached the boundary. Istren saw her from the corner of her eye and had an absurd thought that the room should have been made ready for a wedding. The thought arrived complete with flowers, bowls, a place for the guests. She gripped the table until the nails of her other hand hurt her palm.

Lian moved the copper weight onto the unfinished line.

The water in the dish rose against its rim without spilling. The keeper’s arm shook. Her attendant brought the second salt vessel, but Lian stopped her from pouring it across the aperture. The people were out; the correspondence between their places had not yet closed. Cutting it now would leave the inner assignment without an outer departure.

Vesrin repeated the recorded omission. Her voice cracked halfway through. She began the sentence again from its start, slower this time.

The woman reached the inner edge.

“You have them,” she said. “Let the rest come.”

Lian wrote in the raised water. Istren could not read the marks from where she sat. They were not intended for her. The attendant watched them and moved the salt at the keeper’s direction, sealing the two outer corners first.

The pale woman looked toward Lerris.

He had turned on the floor to see her. Markus tried to pull him away, then stopped. The choice had to remain his, and Istren hated that anything still had to be asked of him.

“Don’t make it a farewell,” Vesrin said, very softly.

Lerris shut his eyes.

The inner light changed. For a moment Istren could see the table, the scattered papers, the chair Othrel had left. Then the room seemed to draw away along a distance too long for the building. The woman remained near, one hand extended, not pleading and not threatening.

Lian pressed the copper weight down.

The dish cracked.

Water ran over the stand and onto the floor. The keeper bent with it, her hand still on the weight. The attendant caught her shoulder but did not lift her. Istren read the last sentence exactly as it stood: departure witnessed, completion withheld, record retained in custody.

The aperture closed.

No one moved for several breaths. Then the healer pushed past the guards to reach Lian, and the ordinary room broke into ordinary demands. Cloth. A chair. More light. Someone to keep the crowd outside the door.

Istren lifted her finger from the page. There was a crescent of blood beside the last line where her nail had cut her palm.

Lerris sat against the wall with Markus beside him. He was looking at the place where the opening had been.

“It isn’t destroyed,” he said.

“No,” the attendant answered.

“It could open again.”

“Yes.”

He nodded once. Istren could not tell whether the answer frightened him or merely allowed him to stop pretending it had to be otherwise.

Vesrin gathered the loose records without stacking them together. They had agreed beforehand which belonged in which wrapper. Her hands shook too badly to tie the first cord. Istren took it, finished the knot, and handed it back.

Across the room Othrel asked for water. Someone brought it. He thanked her.

Part IV — The Returned

Chapter 43 — The Morning Bell

Lerris woke before dawn and waited for the lamp to change.

There was no lamp burning. A faint gray square marked the window, and somewhere beyond it a bird called twice. He lay still, trying to place the sound. For a moment he thought the serving woman had brought something alive into the kitchen. Then he remembered the bed.

He moved one foot beneath the blanket. The other followed. His knees did not strike the underside of the chair. He could turn onto his side without falling.

He did so and began to cry.

There was room for his legs. He could put his arm where he wanted. The pillow smelled faintly of soap, and he had no one to thank who would not make the gratitude sound excessive.

Markus stirred in the other bed.

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

His brother waited. Lerris tried to breathe quietly and failed.

Markus got up, crossed the small room, and sat beside him. He did not ask again. He put a hand between Lerris’s shoulders, warm through the borrowed shirt, and remained until the sobbing had become merely embarrassing.

“I’m sorry,” Lerris said.

“For waking me?”

“Yes.”

“I wasn’t sleeping much.”

“That isn’t better.”

“No.”

They stayed that way until the window lightened. The morning bell began. Lerris counted without meaning to. At the end he kept waiting, and his brother’s hand tightened once against his back.

“That’s all,” Markus said.

“I know.”

He did not know until several breaths later.

Breakfast came on a tray. Lerris ate too quickly, then could not finish. He wanted to keep the bread beneath his pillow. Instead he asked whether it would be taken away if he left it on the table. The attendant said it could remain. He asked whether she was certain. She looked at Markus, and Lerris hated her for that before hating himself.

His brother answered neither for him nor to her. He moved the plate closer to the bed.

The days that followed were not orderly. Some mornings Lerris wanted to walk the whole city. Others he could not leave the room until someone had gone through the door and returned. He was examined, fed, questioned, allowed to sleep. His hands had small cracks around the nails that he did not remember acquiring. The muscles at the backs of his thighs hurt when he stood straight. He had become used to living folded into places that did not fit him.

The healer spoke of movement and food. He did not promise to restore lost time. He helped with the pain in Lerris’s feet, and told him that gaining strength would still require using them. The first walk ended halfway down the passage, where Lerris sat on a bench and pretended to examine a window catch until the trembling passed.

Markus did not remain every hour. He had his own examinations, letters, and an exhaustion he could no longer postpone. Once he fell asleep in a chair while Lerris was speaking. Lerris stopped mid-sentence, offended before he understood. He put a blanket over him and sat quietly on the bed, unable to remember what had been so important to finish saying.

Tareth occupied a room nearby. The leg had been set again. He bore the necessary work badly, which he admitted with a shame Lerris found unbearable. On the day he could receive visitors, Lerris brought him fruit and discovered he had no appetite for it.

“Leave it,” Tareth said. “My daughter will eat it.”

“Is she here?”

“She comes after work.”

Lerris sat. The custodian’s splinted leg lay on a raised support. One hand rested on the blanket as though holding himself in place.

“I’m sorry.”

Tareth looked tired.

“I know.”

“I should have—”

“Don’t tell me which move you should have made. I can’t follow it today.”

Lerris closed his mouth.

For a while they listened to someone sweeping the passage. Tareth asked whether the outer room had been damaged. Lerris told him about the dish, the water, Lian being helped into a chair.

“Her hand?”

“I don’t know.”

“Ask.”

“I will.”

The custodian nodded. Then he asked for the small comb on his table. His beard had become tangled, and lifting both arms tired him. Lerris offered to help. Tareth considered the offer, then gave him the comb.

It was difficult work. The knots pulled. Lerris went too quickly once and was told so with considerable force. He slowed down, feeling the old man’s irritation return him to something he could actually do.

When Tareth’s daughter arrived, she found them arguing over whether a section should simply be cut off. She took the comb from Lerris and settled the matter in less than a minute.

He left the fruit for her.

Chapter 44 — The Color of the Cup

Othrel knew Lerris on the first visit and did not know him on the second.

On the third he said, “You’re the one who writes,” which was true enough to hurt.

He had been given a room with a chair by the window. The chair bore no resemblance to the one beneath the sealed floor. Lerris had asked for that, then wondered whether he had deprived him of a familiarity that might have helped. Nobody could answer. Othrel seemed comfortable in it, and that had to stand until he said otherwise.

The scarf lay across his knees. He remembered that it belonged to someone else. He asked whose, and Lerris told him Vesrin. The name did not settle anywhere Lerris could see. When Vesrin visited, however, he touched the scarf and smiled as though recognizing the connection before the person.

They read the pages about the house together. Sometimes Othrel interrupted with a detail not written down. Sometimes he denied a thing he had dictated days before. Lerris learned to put the paper aside rather than use it to defeat him.

The cup was blue on one afternoon and brown on another.

“It doesn’t matter,” Lerris said, too quickly.

Othrel looked at him.

“Then why did you write it?”

He had no good answer. He sat through the silence until the older man asked for water.

The search for his family was more successful than the search for his memory. A name in an old account led to a lodging, the lodging to a former employer, and finally to a daughter living inland. She had been young when he vanished and had children of her own. His wife was dead.

Lerris was present when they told him. He had promised not to let people keep saying tomorrow. The promise seemed much easier to have made inside the room.

Othrel listened. He asked the date. The attendant gave it, and he repeated the year softly, trying it against some private count.

“Was she ill?”

“For a while.”

“Did someone stay?”

“Your daughter.”

He nodded. Then he asked whether the woman they meant was the one who disliked the cup.

“Yes,” Lerris said.

Othrel looked down at his hands. He wept without apparent surprise, as though his body had recognized a grief his mind could not yet place. Lerris sat beside him. He did not read the pages.

The daughter came eleven days later.

Lerris met her outside because he did not know how to leave them the room once she had entered. She carried a bag and wore traveling dust on the lower part of her dress. Her first question was whether her father could walk. Her second was whether he knew she was coming.

“He’s been told.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No,” Lerris said. “I don’t know whether he remembers.”

She shut her eyes briefly.

“Thank you.”

He showed her the door and remained outside. Through it he heard a chair move, a voice he did not recognize, and then Othrel saying something too softly to understand. He went down the passage before he could make himself a witness to more than they had offered.

When she emerged later, she asked for the pages. Lerris brought them. She read standing up, her bag still over one shoulder.

“This neighbor’s name is wrong,” she said.

“He wasn’t certain.”

“I can see that.”

She pointed to the notation preserving both versions. Then she reached the cup and made a sound that was almost laughter.

“It was green.”

Lerris looked at her.

“An ugly green. My mother hated that too.”

For a moment he saw the household as something larger than the fragments Othrel had given them: a child watching her parents dispute a chipped cup, finding both of them tiresome, then growing old enough to miss the argument. He almost told her, then looked at the pages in her hands.

She folded the pages.

“I’ll leave a copy when I can.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know. I’d like there to be one.”

She took her father inland when the healer judged him strong enough. Lerris helped carry the bag to the cart. Othrel sat beside his daughter, looking uncertain but not distressed. The scarf was still over his knees. Vesrin had told him to keep it.

“Will you write?” Lerris asked.

The older man looked at his daughter before answering.

“If I can.”

“That’s all right.”

Othrel held out his hand. Lerris took it. He could not tell whether he was being thanked, forgiven, or merely greeted, and for once did not try to discover which.

After the cart had gone, he went back to the empty room. There was a cup on the table, plain clay, with both its handles sound. He carried it to the washing room and left it with the others.

Chapter 45 — What Had Gone On

Caldris had acquired a pupil who believed him infallible.

He complained of this while walking Lerris slowly around the public garden. The pupil had begun repeating his casual remarks as if they were conclusions reached after study. Once Caldris had said the air was too damp for clear thinking, and the young man had used it to excuse an entire page of mistakes.

“Have you tried being wrong in front of him?” Lerris asked.

“Repeatedly. He treats it as a test.”

Lerris laughed. It felt different outside, as though the sound had somewhere to go.

The garden had changed. A tree had been cut back after a storm. The bench where students used to leave cups had been moved beneath another wall, and the path beside the fountain had been widened. He remembered the old arrangement so clearly that each new thing looked briefly like an error.

Caldris did not explain every change. He let him discover them.

“Istren has more pupils,” he said after a while.

“So she told me.”

“She has an assistant now.”

“Good.”

“She wanted you to know before you came, in case you expected the old room.”

Lerris stopped beside a bed of herbs. A bee worked among the flowers with such patient attention that he watched it until he could answer without making the answer defensive.

“I don’t expect anything.”

Caldris looked at him.

“I expect too much,” Lerris corrected. “I know.”

They continued.

Dasken had left the copying rooms for steadier work elsewhere. He sent a note, brief and friendly, asking whether Lerris had finally learned the proper unit for the granary measures. Phene still worked, but supervised fewer people. Orest had written from a bridge repair far inland. He was sorry not to be there. He had enclosed a sketch of a failed pier because Lerris had once asked what the water did behind the facing.

Lerris kept the sketch beside his bed. For several nights he looked at it without trying to turn it into anything else.

The changes he could not easily name were in people’s manners. They were glad to see him and careful with him. They waited for him to finish sentences he would once have been interrupted in. Sometimes he wanted to shake them. Sometimes the smallest ordinary impatience made him so relieved that he nearly cried.

At supper with Istren, he knocked over a cup.

“Oh, move the papers,” she said sharply.

He did, laughing while he blotted the table. She stared at him, then began laughing too, though she had no idea why. The assistant watched them with polite incomprehension.

Later, alone, he was ashamed of how much he had needed to be treated as inconvenient rather than damaged. The shame did not prevent him from sleeping better.

Markus was preparing to go home.

He had delayed it twice already. Lerris knew this without being told. There were letters from Alayse on the table, packed and unpacked papers, a list of things to finish before departure that grew whenever it became too short.

“You should go,” Lerris said.

His brother kept folding a shirt.

“I intend to.”

“When?”

“When the last account is settled.”

“That’s what I used to say.”

Markus put down the shirt.

It had been an unfair thing to say. Lerris knew that and did not withdraw it, because it was also true enough to do the work he wanted.

“I can remain with the keepers for another few weeks. I have appointments. I have Caldris and Istren and people who are paid to notice whether I’ve eaten.”

“I don’t want to leave you with people paid to notice.”

“Then leave me with people who are tired of you finding reasons not to.”

His brother looked toward the window. Lerris could see him trying to decide whether anger would make departure easier or merely hurt them both.

“I don’t know what happens when I’m not here,” Markus said.

“No.”

“I knew what happened last time.”

“Not because you left.”

Markus sat on the bed. The shirt lay half folded between them.

They did not settle it that evening. They talked, ate badly, quarrelled about something minor, and went to sleep. In the morning Markus wrote to Alayse with a definite route and date. Lerris saw him seal the letter and did not ask to read it.

On the day he left, they embraced carefully but without fear of broken ribs. Markus smelled of soap and the cedar lining of the borrowed chest. Lerris held him long enough to feel his brother begin to tremble.

“I’m here,” he said.

“I know.”

“I’ll write.”

“I know.”

He did write that night, before there was anything new to report. He told him the food had been poor, that Caldris had beaten him at a game he had not meant to play, and that he had walked farther than yesterday. He left out nothing merely because it sounded small.

Chapter 46 — The Witness’s Keeping

Lerris had expected the obligation to be written in language he could not argue with.

Most of it concerned things he understood. He was to disclose any surviving copy of the working sequence. He was not to reconstruct the excluded completion, whether from memory, recovered papers, or another person’s account. If he encountered an active fragment, he was to report it to a custodian rather than test it himself. The keepers would retain the paired record of his departure and could call on him if it changed.

There were obligations on their side as well. He insisted on hearing those read aloud.

His account would not be circulated as a curiosity. The names of those who had helped him would not be used to solicit further approaches. He could request to see the record of his own custody, though some parts would remain restricted. If the keepers altered the terms, they had to notify him and provide a reason. They could not promise that he would agree with it.

“What happens if I refuse now?” he asked.

Lian sat opposite him with her hand bandaged. The cracked dish had been replaced by another, deeper and less convenient. Her writing took longer.

The departure has already been accepted. Refusal would not return you to the room.

“Would you confine me?”

If you attempted to reopen it, yes.

“And if I simply leave?”

The keeper looked at him for a long time.

You would leave with an obligation you had declined to understand.

He disliked the answer. He could also see that she had not made it stronger than the facts allowed. His body had crossed because other people held the unfinished account.

Tareth was present, his leg supported on a stool. Istren had come at Lerris’s request. Vesrin waited outside, having business of her own with the keeper later.

He asked about the condition of the record. Lian showed him the wrappers, closed and marked, and the place where the copper weight would remain. The original working sheets were beyond the seal. The outer account recorded their gaps without supplying the missing moves. No single surviving set could recreate the whole approach without further choices and risks.

“So somebody could try.”

Yes.

“Even you.”

The keeper did not erase the word.

He looked at Istren. She gave no sign of approval or warning.

“What does the Witness require of me beyond this?”

Lian’s fingers moved more slowly.

What you undertake.

“I don’t understand.”

The attendant explained that the recorded obligation was specific. Service was another matter. Those who had been touched by such places sometimes worked with the custodians, carrying accounts, examining reports, accompanying recoveries. Some did none of those things. A rescue did not make a person competent to perform another.

He laughed at that, without pleasure.

“No.”

Tareth shifted his splinted leg. The room waited while he found a less painful position.

“I’d like to know how people survive them,” Lerris said. “Not only what is supposed to hold them shut.”

“Then begin with the people,” Istren said.

It was the first advice she had offered that day.

He accepted the obligation. The attendant read each clause, including his objections and the keeper’s answers. Lerris spoke his consent where required, using both names so the account would not divide him by a clerical convenience. Lian set the copper weight against the closed wrapper while he rested his hand on the table beside it.

For a moment he felt the old pull, the pressure from the place beneath the Library. He did not see the room. He felt only that something which had once expected him had been given another means of remaining unfinished.

Then it passed.

He lifted his hand. There was no new mark on it. The knuckle scar remained, ordinary and familiar. He had expected a visible sign and was relieved, then strangely disappointed, not to have one.

Tareth saw him looking.

“Keep the copy,” he said. “It’s less easy to admire.”

Lerris smiled. He put the document in his case.

Outside, Vesrin stood beneath the covered walk. She had a letter open in both hands. Lerris asked whether it was from her colleague.

“Yes.”

“Will she come?”

“No.”

“Not now?”

“She says no. She knows what the keepers have offered. She doesn’t want to travel for another interval that might leave her worse afterwards.”

Vesrin folded the letter carefully.

“What will you do?”

“Write back. Tell her I understand.”

“Do you?”

“No. But I can understand that she means it.”

They walked together toward the street. At the gate Vesrin stopped.

“I wanted you to prove it could be done,” she said.

“What?”

“A return that stayed. Something the keepers couldn’t put back in a box.”

He looked at her. She had not asked him to forgive her, and he did not know whether he could have answered if she had.

“I wanted that too.”

“I know.”

She touched his sleeve. Then she went to the desk to have her letter sent, and he continued toward the public rooms with the obligation in his case.

That afternoon he made his first request for a survivor’s account. It was refused. The person concerned did not wish to speak to another scholar.

Lerris wrote a reply acknowledging the refusal and did not send a second question inside it.

Chapter 47 — Ordinary Play

Caldris set up a position Lerris knew.

“You don’t have to finish it,” he said.

“That makes it sound worse.”

“I can make it sound worse still, if that helps.”

They sat in the public garden, where there were too many sounds for any one of them to command the afternoon. Water ran in the fountain. A gardener dragged a sack of cut branches along the path. Somewhere out of sight two students were attempting a song and disagreeing over the words.

The board was the old one. Lerris recognized the chipped piece and the replacement wood. He had played on it before the disappearance, before the stacks, before anything he could now call a warning. He rested his hands on his knees.

Caldris waited.

At last Lerris moved a piece. Nothing happened except that his friend considered it, made an answering move, and reached for a fig.

He played three more turns before the feeling came: an expectation that the empty place was not empty, that some necessary person had been omitted from the account. He knew it was the garden. He could see the sack of branches. He could hear the students laughing. Knowing did not immediately release his hand.

“Stop,” he said.

Caldris put down the fig.

“All right.”

“I know where I am.”

“I didn’t ask.”

Lerris stood, walked to the fountain, and put his hands in the water. It was cold and full of leaves. A child stared at him. He withdrew them, shook off the drops, and came back.

“I don’t want to make this impossible.”

“Then don’t decide about every game from one afternoon.”

They left the board in place while they ate. Caldris talked about his troublesome pupil, who had finally disagreed with him and had chosen a point on which Caldris was demonstrably right. This pleased him more than it ought to have. Lerris listened until he could laugh without using the laugh to conceal something.

Afterwards he sat down again.

“Two moves.”

“Each?”

“Yes.”

They played two moves each. Caldris had the better position. Lerris could see how to improve his own and deliberately did not attempt it. His friend began putting the pieces away without comment.

He left Thepyrgos some weeks later.

There were permissions to arrange because of the obligation, but no keeper accompanied him. He carried sealed contact instructions, a copy of the terms, and the allowed narrative of the boy with the water. The working diagrams remained in custody. His bright sliver remained deposited. He had asked for confirmation before leaving and received it without being invited to inspect the object.

The porter returned a small balance from the room charge. Lerris checked it, thanked him, and had an absurd desire to ask whether the room would remain his if he did not come back on time.

Instead he gave the key.

He traveled by ship, sick for part of the crossing, bored for more of it. The sea did not make allowances for what had happened to him. He discovered that this was sometimes a comfort and sometimes merely unpleasant. He wrote descriptions of weather because he had promised Markus letters and because not every page had to preserve a danger.

In Viterra he found a country altered while he had been counting meals in a room. People spoke of the queen with admiration, calculation, resentment, often in the same conversation. Roads and households had changed hands. Men he remembered as junior officers had responsibilities they discussed without asking whether he understood them.

He listened badly at first. Every account of the intervening years seemed to contain a reproach, though no one intended it. Gradually the stories acquired people other than himself.

He saw Markus and Alayse together before beginning the next journey. His brother looked less watchful in rooms where somebody else knew where the cups were kept. Alayse was affectionate to Lerris without treating his return as permission to forget what the search had cost. Once, when Markus left the room, she asked him whether he intended to go back to Thepyrgos.

“Not soon.”

“That isn’t quite an answer.”

“No.”

She looked at him for a moment, then nodded.

He stayed long enough to become an ordinary guest. He was sent to fetch things. He was told when he left a door open. He learned which chair was most comfortable and lost it whenever somebody older entered the room.

At night Markus sometimes spoke in his sleep.

Lerris had heard it before, but now he understood more of the words and wished he understood fewer. Once he woke to a story about a traveler who left a cup outside a tent and found it full each morning, though everyone in the camp denied filling it. The traveler drank until he recognized what the cup was taking from the people who slept around him.

Markus did not tell the ending. He woke while Lerris sat beside the bed, asked for water, and remembered nothing in the morning.

Lerris wrote the fragments down. He told himself he was keeping them in case his brother wanted to know. He did not ask whether he did.

Chapter 48 — A Name in the Glass

The mirror belonged to a woman who repaired neither mirrors nor people, as she told Lerris when he first called.

“I ask a question,” she said. “Sometimes something answers. If you want a guarantee, buy a bell.”

She had been recommended by a man whose brother had survived an encounter on a road Lerris meant to examine. He had begun collecting such accounts, with permission where he could obtain it and silence where he could not. Most contained less than he wanted. Some were full of explanations that contradicted the events described. A few held details he could not stop thinking about: a creature refusing to cross a line it had crossed before, a person allowed to leave after doing nothing the witnesses considered useful, a voice that sounded familiar only until someone tried to name it.

He wanted a method. He knew what wanting a method had already cost him. The knowledge slowed him. It did not stop him.

The woman asked what he was looking for.

“Someone who knows how to live after such things.”

“Everyone alive after them knows something of that.”

“Someone who can tell me more.”

She gave him an unflattering look.

“About your own trouble?”

“About a thing in my father’s work. An old account. My brother began with it.”

He had brought a permitted extract from the family material, ordinary words rather than the excluded proof. The woman read it and asked whether he knew what his father had meant by the passage. He did not. Braedyn’s scholarship was not a convenient map of the consequences his sons had found in it.

Still, he put the extract beside the mirror.

He notified a custodian of the Witness through the contact he had been given. The reply distinguished the proposed question from the sealed work and warned him not to use the prohibited sequence as a means of asking. He had not intended to. He resented the warning, then copied it into the page where he recorded the appointment.

The room was small and smelled of old incense. The mirror stood on a table draped in plain cloth. Its frame was blackened wood, with one repaired corner. Lerris inspected the back when invited. He found nothing that made him understand the front.

The woman set a bowl of water nearby and lit the incense. This was not Lian’s rite. She said so when he asked about the resemblance. Water was water, she told him; he should not make every basin the same basin because he had been frightened beside one.

He sat.

The question was narrower than the one he had first proposed. What person, connected to the old account, could answer him now? He did not ask for an answer from the dead. He did not offer his brother’s name. He gave his own because he was the one asking, and felt the old discomfort as he did it.

The glass clouded.

For a while nothing else happened. His reflection remained visible through the dimness: tired eyes, a line beside his mouth he did not remember having before, the collar he had straightened twice while waiting. He thought of Caldris’s coat fitting and nearly laughed.

Then a name appeared below his face.

Not cut into the glass. Not written in breath on its surface. The letters seemed to stand a little in front of it, too near to belong to the reflected room.

Asselin.

He knew the name. A baron whom people placed differently depending on whose maps they trusted. A household spoken of with elaborate reluctance. A man whose continued existence, if the stories were true, was itself an account of surviving something that ought to have ended him.

“Do you see it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

The woman did not sound pleased.

“What does it mean?”

“It means that’s the answer you received.”

“Is it true?”

She extinguished the incense.

“You should decide what would establish that before you travel.”

The letters thinned. His own face remained, bewildered and disappointed by how little a name could settle.

He left with the extract, the record of the question, and no promise of protection. Outside, the street was loud with carts. He stood for a moment in the doorway while the ordinary light rearranged the room behind him into something less extraordinary.

He wrote to Markus that evening.

His brother’s reply came before he had completed the travel arrangements. It did not forbid him to go. It asked what he wanted to learn that he could not ask in writing, whether he knew anyone who had returned from the baron’s house, and who would know where he was.

At the end Markus had added a shorter paragraph.

I cannot keep giving you the frightening parts of what happened to me. Some of them are all I have left of an ordinary day. Ask me about other things too.

Lerris read it several times.

He wrote back about the sea crossing, the man who had tried to sell him a horse too old for its teeth to support the claim, and the garden he had seen that morning. He also told him he was still arranging to go north.

He did not mention the story he had written down from his sleep.

Chapter 49 — The Price of a Story

Dusana was mending a strap when he found her.

He had expected someone to announce him, or at least to ask why a stranger had come among the wagons. Instead a boy pointed him toward a woman sitting on a folded blanket beside a wheel. She had the strap braced over one knee and a needle in her hand. When Lerris asked whether she was Dusana, she looked up long enough to decide he was not carrying anything useful.

“Yes.”

He introduced himself. She returned to the stitch.

“I was told you know the roads north.”

“Some of them.”

“I need to reach Baron Asselin.”

“No.”

The answer was so brief that he waited for the rest. She pulled the thread tight with her teeth and examined the seam.

“I can pay.”

“I assumed so.”

“Is it the route?”

“It is several things.”

“Could you tell me which?”

She set the strap down.

“Do you intend to improve my answer until you like it?”

He felt himself color. Nearby, two people were loading sacks onto a cart while arguing over which had been left in the damp. A woman shook out a blanket and sent dust across the sunlight. Nobody appeared interested in rescuing him from the conversation.

“No,” he said. Then, because it was not quite true: “I hoped there might be a way.”

“There are ways. I haven’t agreed to take you.”

He stood awkwardly beside the wheel. She picked up the strap again.

He should have left. He knew that. He had received refusals before and had learned to keep them separate from the work of asking. Yet Asselin’s name had followed him through letters, maps, the memory of the glass. He had begun thinking of reaching the baron as the next necessary thing, and necessity made other people’s decisions feel like obstacles.

“Would you hear why?”

Dusana sighed and pulled the thread through the strap.

“I’ll hear a story,” she said. “Then I’ll give you my answer. Once.”

He could have told her about the mirror. He had prepared that account, including its limits. He could have described the room beneath the Library and his uncertainty about the people who survived such places. Those were his experiences to spend, however poorly he chose the occasion.

Instead he thought of the cup outside the tent.

The story had frightened him because of the way Markus had spoken it, without waking, in a voice that did not seem to know it was being heard. Lerris had tried to identify a source and found none. He had kept it because it felt important. Now the importance became something he could use.

He began with the traveler.

Dusana continued sewing through the first few sentences. At the mention of the full cup each morning, her hands stopped.

“Where did you hear that?”

Lerris should have stopped then.

He said, “My brother.”

“From whom did he hear it?”

“I don’t know.”

He continued, more quietly. The people in the camp denied filling the cup. The traveler drank because thirst was ordinary and the gift appeared harmless. Then a child forgot the sound of her own mother’s laugh, and a man could no longer remember which road had brought him home.

Dusana put the needle through the strap and left it there.

“Enough.”

He stopped.

Her face had changed. The irritation was gone, and he disliked what had replaced it so much that he wanted the irritation back. She looked past him toward the wagons, checking who had been close enough to hear.

“Did he tell you this while awake?”

“No.”

For a moment he thought she would strike him. She did not move.

“Does he know you’ve brought it here?”

“No.”

He heard the answer as she must have heard it: permission he had never sought because it might have been refused.

Dusana rose. She was not much taller standing, but the change made him step back.

“Wait there.”

She went to the nearest wagon and spoke to someone inside. Lerris remained beside the wheel. The unfinished strap lay where she had left it, needle standing upright. A fly landed on the leather and walked around the thread.

He had achieved something. He could feel the terrible small satisfaction of that even while shame spread through him. He wanted to say he had not known what the story meant, but ignorance did not explain why he had chosen it instead of his own.

Dusana returned with a cup of water. She drank, then set it on the wheel hub.

“We’ll take you.”

He opened his mouth.

“No more stories,” she said.

“I didn’t mean—”

“I know what you meant. You wanted me to take you.”

The exactness silenced him.

She told him when they would leave, what he could bring, and how the journey would be paid. He was not buying authority over the route. He would not leave the company because a map showed a shorter way. If she told him to put something away, he would put it away before asking her to explain. There were other travelers and other business; his destination would not make their needs incidental.

He agreed to each condition. It felt inadequate.

“Is there something I can do about what I said?”

“Yes. Stop saying it.”

He bowed, an automatic courtesy that seemed absurd beside the wheel, and went back toward the town.

That evening he took out Markus’s letter. The short paragraph at the end was worse now than when he had first read it. His brother had asked for something small and clear. Lerris had found a way to treat it as though it concerned only future questions, not words already in his possession.

He began a reply.

I used something you said in your sleep to persuade a guide.

He sat with the sentence. He could explain the mirror, the refusal, the urgency he had felt. He could make a longer account in which the choice appeared to have emerged reluctantly from circumstances. He had written such accounts before.

He added Dusana’s name, the intended route, and the fact that she had told him not to repeat it. He said he was sorry.

The wax had begun to set by the time he pressed his seal into it.

Chapter 50 — North

The company left later than Lerris expected because a wheel needed work.

He stood with his bag packed while the repair was made, trying not to look impatient. Dusana noticed anyway.

“Road will still be there,” she said.

He nodded.

“If it isn’t, we’ll want the wheel.”

That was all she said to him for most of the morning.

There were wagons carrying goods and bedding, people who walked for part of the day and rode when the pace or their bodies required it, a child entrusted with a task she took seriously until a dog distracted her. Lerris found that joining a company meant being inconvenient before it meant belonging. His bag was in the wrong place. He stood where someone needed to pass. He offered to carry something whose owner preferred to know where it was.

By midday he had been given a job simple enough to be useful: keep a covered pot upright while the wagon crossed a badly rutted stretch. It required both hands and made conversation impossible. He was grateful for it.

They stopped near water to eat. Lerris waited to be told where to sit, then realized nobody was arranging the meal around him. He found a place clear of the work and accepted the bread passed his way.

The landscape was familiar enough to make him careless. He recognized a kind of tree, a shape of roof, the way a field had been divided around old stones. He began to point out a road on his map and stopped when Dusana looked at him.

“It’s marked as passable,” he said.

“It is.”

“But we’re not taking it.”

“No.”

He folded the map. Later another traveler told him the bridge had been damaged and the far approach was deep in mud. There was no mystery in that choice. Lerris was relieved and embarrassed by how readily he had expected one.

Toward evening they came within sight of the Black River. The water carried a cold brightness under the sky. He watched it while the others discussed the crossing and thought of the dish on Lian’s stand, the crack opening beneath her hand. He had written to the custodian named in his travel papers, giving the route and the baron’s name. The reply had not arrived before departure. He had left instructions for it to follow.

He was not free of the record beneath Thepyrgos. He could feel that most clearly when he tried to imagine reconstructing the last position. His thoughts went to the omitted place and stopped against a resistance that was partly oath, partly fear, and partly the knowledge that he had chosen to leave it. He did not test which part would fail first.

At the first camp he opened his case. There was the copy of his obligation, the narrative of the boy with the water, Orest’s bridge sketch, letters, blank paper. He had left the prohibited working behind.

Dusana passed while he was arranging the pages.

“Writing home?”

“Not tonight.”

She looked at the case, then at him.

“Keep the things you can’t replace out of the dew.”

He moved them beneath the cover. She continued toward the fire.

He wrote a few lines about the day’s route, the damaged bridge, the place where the wagon wheel had needed care. He included who had told him each thing. Then he stopped. It was enough for one day. The people whose names he had written were nearby, eating, quarrelling gently, looking for a misplaced spoon. He could go and sit with them rather than continue describing them from a distance.

He closed the case.

Across the fire, Dusana shifted to make room for someone carrying a pot. Lerris rose to help, waited until he was asked to take the other handle, and carried it with the woman to the stones prepared beside the flames.

Beyond the camp the road ran north. Asselin waited somewhere ahead, if the glass had given him anything more than a name. He would reach the baron’s door as a guest. What he would be when he left it was not something the mirror had shown.

For now the pot was heavy, the handle hot through the cloth. The woman told him to lower his side a little.

He did.
