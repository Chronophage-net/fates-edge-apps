Part I — The old address

Chapter 1 — A paid journey back

The man who came to collect the cargo had brought six men to help him count twelve rolls of canvas.

Sariel watched them spread across the landing. One stayed by the gate. Two went toward the wagon, and the man with the account made for the horse’s head, where a person wanting to help generally made himself useful by being somewhere else.

“You can count from there,” she said.

He stopped. He was young enough to have grown the beard expressly for this appointment. Rain had made little points of it.

“We are taking delivery.”

“Then fetch the woman who ordered it.”

“My aunt has entrusted me with her affairs.”

Behind Sariel, Benjamin moved his foot from the hub to the ground. The motion was small. The man beside the gate noticed it and began looking at the river.

They were at Three Willows, where the western carrying road met a canal wide enough for freight boats. Silkstrand lay a day’s journey beyond the low rise. Already the houses had colored shutters and arguments conducted from upper windows, and already anyone bringing goods toward the city was expected to make something of its inhabitants’ lives easier at his own expense. The canvas buyer’s nephew had suggested a delivery charge, a weighing charge and an allowance for damp. He had not suggested bringing the buyer.

“She’s at worship.”

“We’ll wait.”

“The gate closes.”

“It’s a gate. It does that.”

Elena came round the wagon carrying a loaf and looked at the six men.

“Are they with us?”

“Apparently we’re with them,” Sariel said.

“Oh.” Elena tore off a piece of bread. “Do we feed them?”

The nephew’s mouth moved. Sariel thought he was deciding which insult he could safely understand.

The rolls had been loaded dry at the last staging house. Thane had hung a little covered cup beneath the tilt and marked the water level at each halt; any damp that reached the middle of the load had first to get past a man who resented damage to canvas almost as much as damage to wood. He now climbed down from the driver’s board with his measuring rod under his left arm.

“Which roll?” he asked.

“The consignment as a whole.”

“Then you’re going to be here a while.”

He put the rod across the open back of the wagon. His right hand rested against his coat, its two stiff fingers hooked loosely into the leather tab sewn there. The nearest of the hired men glanced at the hand, then at the rod, and smiled.

Sariel took one step sideways. Benjamin took another on the opposite side of the wagon. The smile disappeared.

They could have beaten these men. It would have taken longer to keep the horse from breaking its legs in the traces afterward, and longer still to establish who was paying for the canvas with a magistrate who bought his own supplies through the nephew’s family. Sariel had a whole afternoon available. She did not have that kind of week.

She sat on the rear board, took the bread from Elena and began eating.

“Your aunt wanted awning cloth,” she told the nephew. “The yard’s open. It’s raining. She’ll probably be pleased if you send for her.”

He sent the man at the gate.

The aunt arrived before Sariel finished the crust. She wore a green shawl over a mourning cap and carried a covered dish. At the sight of her nephew’s companions she stopped beneath the gate arch, regarded all six and asked whose funeral she had missed.

The men went away. The canvas was unloaded. Its buyer paid the remaining escort fee without an argument and made her nephew hold the empty dish while she counted.

There were honey pastries in it. She gave the five one each. The nephew got the dish.

Their room above the ferry kitchen held two beds, a pallet and a table which Thane had already expressed an opinion about without touching. Sariel put her pack on the floor by the wall. The window looked toward Silkstrand. A pale band lay across the horizon where the city gathered the late light; above it were the masts, the dark line of the Heights, and a crane whose long arm moved so slowly she could not see it moving until she looked away and back.

Kestra sat at the table with a letter.

“Tavia’s?” Sariel asked.

“She says the weather is better where she is.”

“She always says that.”

“She says we’ve owed her a visit for four years.”

“She always says that too.”

“It was three years last time.”

Benjamin put his wet coat over the back of a chair. Gray showed in the hair above his ears when he bent to pull his boots off. He could still take a stair two at a time. In the morning he sometimes went down one at a time and pretended to be attending to something behind him. Sariel had stopped mentioning it. She had her own arrangements with mornings.

“What does she want?” he asked.

“For once, nothing for herself.”

Kestra passed the letter. There were two enclosures. Tavia had folded her own words around them, leaving a patch of grease beside the signature. She had written while eating, probably standing, with somebody telling her she was wanted elsewhere. Sariel knew the shape her letters made when she was being hurried.

Merrit Sann is a correspondent of my employer’s. I have done three jobs through him and been paid for all three, though the last involved a goat which he had described as freight. I attach his request for an introduction. It came through two people before reaching me; I have verified that he sent it. Ask what he calls freight before agreeing to anything.

You are near enough to consider it. That is the only recommendation I am making.

The next sheet was in a small, orderly hand. Sann wanted a discreet company for private enquiries in Silkstrand: verifying claims, finding people, accompanying clients who had reasons not to approach the ordinary offices. He would advance expenses against an agreed engagement. Replies could be left with Bastien Rove at the lower canal workshops, who knew where to find him when he was in the city.

Sariel read that name twice.

“He still has the boat?”

“He may have a different boat,” Kestra said. “People do.”

“Bastien liked that boat.”

“You’ve liked six coats since we last saw him.”

“I liked one coat. The others were what I could get.”

Elena sat on the pallet and put her feet against the warm wall over the kitchen flue. She read Sann’s sheet without asking to have it passed to her, leaning over Sariel’s arm as she had leaned over a hundred worse things.

“What sort of claims?”

“He doesn’t say.”

“Then we ask before we quote.”

“We aren’t quoting,” Benjamin said.

Nobody answered at once. Below them the kitchen door opened, admitting a gust that made the window rattle. A servant shouted for the door to be shut. Another voice shouted that someone had to bring the wood in.

Thane picked up the remaining enclosure. It was a note on good paper, embossed at the corner with the seal of a guild correspondence office. It concerned the reestablished Silk Coin, its current council and the manner in which former members might apply to have earlier work recognized.

He laid it flat and held the curled edge with his good hand.

“Sera’s people.”

“Sera’s clerk,” Kestra said. “Nobody’s offering us the keys.”

Sariel could remember the key. Its bow had been split, wrapped with wire and split again. Hest had insisted she was going to mend it properly when everyone stopped needing the door. Sariel had once dropped it down the gap beside the kitchen step and spent an hour with a candle and a hook while somebody on the other side shouted that a locksmith ought to be able to get into her own house.

She folded Tavia’s letter over the embossed seal.

“It says we can apply.”

“I can read,” Benjamin said.

He said it too quietly. Elena took her feet off the wall.

“Nobody has decided.”

“I know how we sound when nobody has decided.”

He had Orren’s sword across his knees. The blade was older than any of the boots in the room; the binding on the hilt was not. Benjamin renewed it when it needed renewing. The first time Sariel had watched him cut the old leather away, she had felt an absurd impulse to stop him.

“We can ask about the work,” she said. “And ask what the office requires. Those are two questions.”

“In the same city.”

“Yes.”

“Under the same Countess.”

“Yes.”

He bent and set the sword beside his pack. When he straightened, he looked tired rather than angry, which was harder to answer.

Thane took a folded advertisement out of the pocket on his sleeve. He had kept it dry under his coat. The top showed a small engraved screw press, not well drawn; Sariel could see the places where he had corrected the engraver in pencil.

“There’s work,” he said. “A room with a bench and a power shaft. Short leases. I had the notice through Linett’s people. I was going to write.”

“You have a bench,” Benjamin said.

“I know. I made it.”

“In Millhaven.”

“It’ll be there when I get back.”

Elena reached for the advertisement. “How short?”

“A season. Less if you take the room with the broken shutter.”

“You would.”

“It’s only a shutter.”

She smiled, finally. “Of course it is.”

Sariel let them discuss the shaft. From the window she could see the city beginning to disappear into its own lights. They had worked in port towns since the fire, in houses grander than the Coin had ever been, in villages which let them stay because their work was good and they were there when someone needed it. At Millhaven a woman would stop Thane in the square to complain about a hinge he had repaired fifteen years before. Benjamin was invited to supper by people whose sons had grown up while he taught them to stand properly. There were hooks in a room for their coats.

She wanted to walk into Silkstrand and give her name without watching what the listener did with it.

“Five days to find out,” she said. “We hire rooms we can afford. We don’t borrow for it. Nobody goes into the Heights alone.”

“And if the work lasts six?” Elena asked.

“Then we discuss it on the fifth.”

“Before we spend the money.”

“Before.”

Benjamin rubbed his ankle through his stocking. “I am not spending another night in wet boots.”

Thane looked at the boots beneath his chair. “Then we should have stopped yesterday.”

Sariel laughed. Benjamin looked at her, then at Thane, and shook his head with the slow displeasure he reserved for jokes he wished he had made first.

“Five days,” he said.

Elena held up Sann’s letter. “We hear his terms. After that I won’t promise what I haven’t examined.”

Kestra drew a clean sheet toward her. “And I would like to speak for myself in the office.”

“You always do.”

“You sometimes speak over me while I’m doing it.”

Sariel accepted the correction. Thane fetched the ink from his pack and passed it across without setting it on the bad corner of the table.

They ate downstairs. The kitchen had fish in brown butter and onions cooked until they came apart under a spoon. There was one good piece of the crisp skin. Sariel watched Benjamin turn the platter so Elena found it first, then watched Elena take half and push the platter back. Kestra asked whether Tavia’s goat had been delivered. Nobody knew. They wrote to ask her after supper.

In the morning Sariel put Hest’s pick case into the inside pocket of her coat. The letter with the lock drawing lay behind the tools, in a new wrapper protecting the old one. She felt the case once to make certain it lay flat, then went down to the landing, where the others were disagreeing about which boat would get them into Silkstrand driest.

They chose the one whose owner helped with the bags before naming his price.

Chapter 2 — Names off the page

The clerk gave Kestra a clean pen. It was such an ordinary kindness that she had written her first name before she realized how tightly she was holding it.

“Company?”

“The Silk Coin.”

The clerk reached toward a shelf, stopped, and looked at the paper again.

Outside the hiring office a cart was unloading bottles. Each time the carter put down a crate, glass knocked against glass with a sound like a roomful of people about to make a toast. Kestra had been listening to it while she waited. Now she could not make out the individual clinks.

“You have an introduction from the current council?”

“I have their instructions for applying.”

“That isn’t quite—”

“No. I understand the difference.”

She made herself put the pen down. The clerk was not the woman who had occupied this desk when Kestra was young. She had a small fresh burn on her wrist, the kind made by lifting a pot without looking at the handle, and a ribbon tied too tightly round her sleeve to keep it out of the ink. Behind her, someone had drawn a bird on a discarded timetable. Its feet were much too large.

Kestra set the application note on the counter, then the references. Linett’s letter came first. Below it were confirmations from two carriers and a physician with whom Elena had worked for a flooded summer. The sheets had traveled separately and arrived with different creases. Nothing about them looked like a set bought from a man at a street table.

The clerk read each. At the physician’s seal she gave a little nod.

“These establish employment.”

“That’s what we’re seeking.”

“They don’t establish standing here.”

“No. That is why I’ve brought them.”

The clerk glanced behind her. The second desk was empty. Kestra wished she had not looked. Until then she had been able to imagine that the interruption was something one woman might choose to finish.

They had arrived the previous afternoon, found Bastien’s workshop, and discovered that its upper room still required a brush and a bucket before anyone sensible would sleep there. Bastien had supplied the brush. He had also supplied the names of three inns more expensive than his room, just in case they wished to complain. By sunset Benjamin had repaired a shutter catch, Elena had opened every window, and Thane had accused a folding bed of having been made by someone with a grudge against sleepers. It had almost felt like a visit.

Sariel had offered to come to the office. Kestra had declined. Now she wanted her there, and resented wanting it.

The clerk carried the papers to a cabinet with wooden drawers. She took a key off a hook, opened the second drawer and searched among folded packets. The longer she searched, the more hopeful Kestra became. A missing paper would be ordinary. It could be found or replaced.

The clerk returned with a narrow card.

“Your earlier affiliation has an unresolved suspension.”

“The house was burned.”

“Yes.”

“There were people in it.”

“I see that.”

Kestra looked down. What the woman saw was a reference number in brown ink. The card would have fit inside Hest’s hand.

“Which obligation is said to be outstanding?” she asked.

“It doesn’t list one here. The attachments are held separately.”

“Then I would like the attachments.”

The clerk’s mouth tightened. “I can request them.”

“Please do.”

She had meant to sound grateful. She heard the command in it too late.

The clerk did not rebuke her. She took out another sheet and began writing a request. The carter outside dropped something. This time the glass did not sound like a toast. Both women turned toward the door, and Kestra found herself smiling before she could stop it.

“That would be the cordial,” the clerk said. “The cheap ones don’t break.”

“I can come back.”

“You will have to. There are two offices involved.”

The small interval of companionship closed. Kestra waited while the woman finished, copied the reference number, sanded the page and shook the sand back into its pot. She paid the request fee and took the receipt. Her name was spelled correctly. She checked twice.

“For work before the review,” the clerk said, lowering her voice, “a current local member can sponsor a limited engagement. A named client, named service, definite end. It isn’t a restoration.”

“Would that let us use our name?”

“On that engagement, with the sponsor’s countersignature.”

Kestra gathered the letters. “Thank you.”

The woman hesitated. “You could apply as a different company.”

“Would these five people cease to have a suspension?”

“No.”

“Then I should prefer to keep the name.”

She managed to say it without sounding as though the clerk had burned the house herself.

At the foot of the office steps a woman sat with a basket between her boots. There were no goods in it. She was rubbing a groove in the handle with her thumb, over and over, until Kestra’s own thumb began to ache in sympathy.

“Did they find your attachments?” the woman asked.

Kestra stopped.

“The window carries,” the woman said. “Sorry.”

“They are requesting them.”

“They requested mine in the spring.”

She lifted the basket. It left a pale rectangle on her skirt where the sun had not reached. Fine white grit had worked into the folds around her nails; when she rose, some fell from a cuff.

“Nera Pellan. If you go again, ask for the answer by hand. It can sit in the outgoing tray until the next collection. If they say they’ve sent it, ask who signed for it. Be polite. She’ll help you if she can.”

“You make her sound imprisoned.”

“She gets dinner.”

Kestra laughed once, despite herself. Nera had not smiled.

“What did they keep of yours?”

“Tools. In the store behind the polishing hall. I’m allowed to collect the personal things, which apparently don’t include the things I work with.”

She turned her hand palm up. The thick skin at the bases of her fingers was splitting. Kestra could distinguish the lighter spots where old cuts had healed. A polisher’s hand, though the nails were longer than those of anyone presently earning by it.

“I mixed an abrasive wash,” Nera said. “The approved seller had none. I bought elsewhere. The batch passed. The purchasing didn’t.”

“And that suspends your work?”

“It seems to.”

There was more anger in the last three words than in the rest. Kestra felt suddenly intrusive, standing over her with the portfolio against her ribs. She shifted aside so a man carrying a parcel could pass, and Nera came down the remaining step beside her.

“The sponsorship works,” Nera said. “If your sponsor pays into the right hall. I had one for a month. Don’t let them make it open-ended. The guarantor can withdraw whenever he likes, and then you have to explain why you’re halfway through the job.”

“Do you charge for this advice?”

“I would if you looked richer.”

Kestra did not offer her a coin. Nera would have seen pity before she saw money, and Kestra had no work to offer in its place. She asked where messages would reach her. Nera named a wash yard behind the public baths, whose owner let her do finishing work in the evenings.

“Not the front bath,” she added. “They’ll sell you something.”

Then she crossed the street with her empty basket. Kestra watched her avoid the cordial spreading through a crack between the paving stones. It was red and smelled of almonds. Two children had found sticks with which to investigate it; the carter was defending his remaining bottles with a broom.

At the canal, Kestra stopped beneath a striped awning and opened the portfolio. She put the request receipt in the outer pocket, where she would find it without disturbing the letters. Beside it she wrote Nera’s name, address and the words ask who signed. The ink from the borrowed office pen had stained the side of her middle finger. She rubbed it against her thumb and made the stain worse.

When she reached the workshop, Sariel was sitting on the quay with Bastien. He had one leg stretched in front of him and was doing something elaborate with a knot while telling Sariel why she would never have been much use on a boat.

“She falls in,” Kestra said.

“Once,” Sariel said.

“In my sight.”

Bastien pulled the knot through. “That’s what I told her.”

Sariel looked at the portfolio. Kestra had intended to give a concise account. Instead she sat on the edge of a crate and said, “They have a card.”

Bastien stopped working. The canal slapped twice against the brick below them.

Kestra explained the card, the missing attachments, the letters that proved they had worked without proving they were entitled to work here. Sariel listened. When Kestra finished, she said, “We could go to Sera’s council ourselves.”

“We could. They would still have to answer the suspension.”

“We didn’t suspend anything.”

“I know.”

“We’ve paid every due anyone—”

“Sariel. I know.”

The knot lay in Bastien’s lap. He put his palm over it, as if it were something that might interrupt.

Kestra took a breath. “I asked for the papers. In the meantime Sann may be able to sponsor a private engagement. His hall, his commission, an end date. We would have to make the work quite specific.”

“He’ll be here this afternoon,” Bastien said.

Sariel studied Kestra’s face for a moment longer, then reached into the covered bowl beside the crate and handed her a piece of fried dough. It had gone cold. There was coarse salt caught in the blister on top.

“You went without breakfast.”

“There was a queue.”

“There’s always a queue.”

Kestra ate the dough. Bastien went back to his knot. She let Sariel sit beside her without giving the receipt back for someone else to carry.

Chapter 3 — The woman brought in

“If you call me dear again,” the woman said, “I shall have you dismissed.”

Elena moved the cup out of reach of her elbow.

“I called you thirsty.”

“You were thinking it.”

“I was thinking you would knock the cup over.”

The woman stared at it. She was young, at least in the ordinary ways Elena had learned to judge: the skin beside her eyes, the small firm joint of the jaw, a freckle that had not spread across her cheek. There was a raw scratch below that jaw and a little powder left in the hollow of her throat. She had been brought up the workshop stairs between Bastien and Benjamin, objecting to the manner of the ascent while using both men’s arms.

Now she sat on the bed with one shoe off. The ankle above it had swollen beyond the stocking. Her other hand pressed the right side of her ribs.

“I must speak to Sann.”

“He is downstairs. I’m Elena. May I look?”

“It requires cooling.”

“It may require several things.”

“I have sprained an ankle before.”

“This one?”

The woman went still.

Elena waited. Merrit’s messenger had brought a particular request that morning, rather less ordinary than the enquiries mentioned in his old letter. She had heard the claim before the patient arrived: Dame Olyne’s mind in an actress’s body, another woman wearing her clothes and opening her correspondence, a performance that had not ended when the music did. Sariel had supplied the outline with a face that invited neither belief nor laughter. Elena had spent the last half hour cleaning a basin.

At length the woman took her hand off her ribs.

“No,” she said. “Not this one.”

“Then tell me if I hurt it.”

She let Elena lift the foot. The stocking had a good heel and a bad darn, white thread on blue. Its wearer had pulled it on inside out. Elena rolled it carefully past the swelling, watching the patient’s face; the little lines of pain arrived before the breath did. She stopped until it passed.

“How did you fall?”

“A stair.”

“Up or down?”

“Down.”

“Someone push you?”

“Not with a hand.”

Elena put the foot on a folded blanket. “I’ll need more than that.”

The woman looked toward the door. It was shut, but footsteps and voices came readily through the boards. Thane was downstairs asking Bastien about a timber he had found under the bench. Bastien was saying it was not for sale. They had been having the same exchange for several minutes.

“I was leaving,” the woman said. “They told me to wait. I did not wait. I was not familiar with the stair.”

“You struck your side on the way down?”

She nodded.

There was no wound beneath the gown, only a bruise spreading along the lower ribs and the body’s quick protest when Elena asked her to draw a full breath. She spoke steadily through most of the examination. At the end, when Elena helped her back against the pillows, her teeth chattered.

“I’m not cold.”

“You can have the blanket anyway.”

Elena tucked it round her shoulders. She drank then, three small mouthfuls, holding the cup with both hands. Afterward she spread her fingers against its sides and stared at them. Elena waited until she looked up.

“What should I call you?”

“Olyne.”

“And the woman whose body this is?”

“Zélie.”

“Does Zélie take medicines? Has she ever been ill after a particular food, or—”

“How should I know?”

“Then I won’t give you anything strong until we can ask.”

“Ask her?”

“If we can.”

Olyne pushed the cup away. Elena caught it this time. Water darkened the blanket, spreading toward the injured foot.

“She knows precisely what she has done.”

“Perhaps. I still need to know what this body can safely swallow.”

She changed the wet blanket. The patient had closed her eyes, but she was not asleep. Her mouth worked once, shaping words without letting them through.

Elena put the cup on the floor, out of reach, and brought in Merrit Sann.

He had the kind of careful shoes that made a man conspicuous in a canal workshop. Elena watched him avoid the damp patch by the door. His coat was plain and well brushed; a thread had come loose at the cuff and been clipped rather than pulled. He bowed to the young woman on the bed and seemed immediately embarrassed by the bow.

“Dame Olyne?”

“You took your time.”

“I have had to verify—”

“You have had to ask your partners whether I was worth the embarrassment.”

“One partner,” he said. “The other is away.”

She looked at him properly for the first time.

Elena drew the chair up beside the bed. Sann sat without taking off his gloves, noticed them and removed them. Sariel came in after him, with Kestra carrying her portfolio. Benjamin stayed outside the door. There were enough people in the little room already, and the patient could see the exit from where she sat.

Sann brought out a folded paper. It was not an account. He held it by the blank edge and asked the patient to tell him which of the listed shipments she had disputed.

She read it quickly. “The second. The destination was correct; the payer wasn’t. You’ve brought the earlier copy.”

“What happened to the later one?”

“Your nephew put it in a parcel of samples. I sent you the parcel unopened because the wax smelled of fish.”

Sann’s lips parted. Olyne used her thumbnail to notch the lower left corner of the folded sheet before turning it over. The motion was sharp and unthinking. Sann watched the corner for longer than he had watched her answer.

“You could have taught someone that,” Sariel said.

“I could,” he said.

Olyne made an angry sound. “Ask me something else.”

He did. The next answer came easily. The one after that did not. Olyne named the wrong vessel, corrected herself, then told him he was confusing two seasons. He took out his own old letter, read it and apologized.

“There,” she said. “Will that do?”

“For me to continue,” he answered.

“For you to recognize me.”

He looked at Elena. She refused the invitation to make his decision comfortable.

“I knew your hand on that paper,” he told the patient. “Before you turned it over. I would not swear before a hall on that alone.”

She thrust the paper back at him. In her hurry she caught the blanket with her thumb and pulled it off her foot. Elena replaced it.

“I don’t want an oath,” Olyne said. “I want my own rooms.”

Sariel moved the second stool nearer. “Then begin with the room where it happened.”

The account came in pieces. A fitting, a private rehearsal at Ravenswood, a mask whose edge had been adjusted to the shape of her face. Courtesy required a little participation. It would amuse the guests. She had been told where to stand and what gesture to make. Zélie stood opposite her. There had been a light too close to the eyes, and afterward a skirt that hung wrong, an unfamiliar pressure in the mouth, someone addressing her from across the room in the voice she expected to hear inside her own skull.

“She cried,” Olyne said.

No one wrote that down at first. Kestra did when Olyne looked at her.

“Who explained what had happened?” Sariel asked.

“Sel Virelle. She arranges the troupe’s sponsors. She said it could be put right. She said I must not alarm anyone.”

“And then?”

“Then she explained how much easier it would be if I agreed to a few things first.”

Olyne’s hands were shaking again. She trapped them beneath the blanket. The next part was delivered as though she were describing a disputed consignment: people who had come and gone, a servant who accepted the older body’s instructions, a door she could no longer order opened. She had been accustomed to being obeyed and knew exactly when it ceased. Elena could see her trying to place the moment earlier, somewhere she might still have prevented it.

“I left,” she finished. “Eventually someone let me leave.”

“Someone?”

“A maid. I paid her with Zélie’s brooch. Are you going to tell me I stole it?”

“I’m going to ask if anyone followed you,” Sariel said.

Olyne did not know.

They let that answer stand. Downstairs Thane had stopped asking about the timber. Through the floor came a slow scrape as somebody moved a heavy object out of the way.

“The fitted things,” Kestra said. “Who made the clothes?”

“Mestrel’s shop. The old measurements were sent there. The new ones too, for the entertainment.”

“Could she tell them apart?”

“She told me my left shoulder was lower than my right. She was extremely pleased with herself.”

“We should see her,” Sariel said. “And find someone who can hear the claim privately before it reaches your household.”

“I require more than a hearing.”

“Yes. We need somewhere to start.”

Sann had been turning the gloves in his lap. He laid them on his knee.

“I can sponsor a specific engagement through my hall. Not a general protection of the company.”

Kestra looked at him. “Would you put that in writing today?”

“If we agree the terms.”

Sariel glanced at Elena, who held up two fingers: two minutes outside. They stepped onto the landing. The door stayed ajar, with Kestra inside and Benjamin at its hinge. They were not leaving Olyne to hear five people decide whether she deserved a bed.

“She cannot be hurried on stairs,” Elena said. “She can walk a little on level ground if someone supports her. No running. No carrying over a shoulder. If you intend to take her to a meeting, hire a chair.”

“You believe her?”

“I believe she’s hurt. Sann believes enough to risk money. I haven’t seen this happen before.”

“Neither have I.”

Sariel leaned against the rail. Years had thickened the wood with paint; beneath her hand a chip showed three different greens. Elena wanted, suddenly and fiercely, to be in a room where a broken ankle was only a broken ankle and the person paying did not have to prove she belonged to herself. She pressed her fingers into the fold of her apron until the impulse passed.

“Ask what she expects us to do if the hearing fails,” she said.

They asked. Olyne expected them to go on. Sariel expected payment for going on and the right to refuse an unsafe attempt. Sann expected a clear account of what he was guaranteeing. The argument took longer than the examination and did the patient less visible harm.

They settled at six hundred marks, including expenses: one hundred and fifty advanced by Sann, the rest due when Olyne could reach her funds again. Protection, an attempt at identification, and assistance toward a return; no promise that magic they did not understand would obey them. A first private meeting would give the engagement its immediate limits. Further work would require the company to agree.

Olyne tried to strike out attempt. Sariel put it back.

“You will not keep my money for trying badly.”

“Then don’t hire us.”

The woman stared at her. Elena could hear Bastien beginning to climb the stair, left foot first, right foot coming up to meet it. He stopped below the landing when no one called him in.

At last Olyne signed. The name came out in an unfamiliar hand. She stared at it until the ink began to dry, then covered it with her palm before Kestra could lift the page.

Elena turned to the basin and rinsed the cloth slowly. When she looked back, the hand had moved.

Sann counted the advance onto the little table. Kestra wrapped it in a separate purse and recorded the terms. Sariel left to hire transport for the morning. Bastien finally came up with broth and an expression of injured surprise at finding the bed occupied by a paying customer.

“I’d have shaken the mattress harder,” he said.

Olyne looked at the bowl. “What is that?”

“Supper.”

“What’s in it?”

“At this price, an answer costs extra.”

Elena took the bowl before the conversation could become a professional difficulty. She tasted it herself, then gave Olyne the spoon. The woman ate sitting rigidly upright. After a few mouthfuls her shoulders lowered; when Elena shifted the pillow, she let her.

Below the open window, Bastien’s boat knocked softly against its fender. Elena waited until the spoon kept moving without encouragement, then went downstairs to find out how much of her own supper the others had left.

Chapter 4 — The wrong cloak

Mestrel’s apprentice had chalk on her cheek and no intention of being helpful before the shop opened.

“Collections at eleven.”

“We’re bringing something,” Sariel said.

“Deliveries round the back.”

Behind her, Olyne drew herself up between Benjamin and Elena. The effort hurt. Sariel saw her grip tighten on Benjamin’s sleeve.

“Dame Olyne’s fitting,” she said before the patient could speak.

The apprentice looked at the clothes, looked again at the bandaged ankle and opened the door. Sariel had come yesterday afternoon to arrange this. Apparently Mestrel had remembered to tell somebody after all.

Inside, dress forms stood along the wall in white covers, their heads gathered into knots. A woman without her face beneath a cloth was not what Sariel wanted to see that morning. She looked past them to the clear space behind the screen, where a chair and a low stool had been put out.

Elena settled Olyne, folded a gray cloak beneath the foot and told her how long she could sit upright before they would need to take her back. Olyne objected to discussing her limits with a dressmaker present. Mestrel, arriving with a cup of something steaming, said she had spent thirty years discussing ladies’ limits and was generally the last to be believed about them.

Elena liked her at once. That worried Sariel a little; Elena was perfectly capable of liking someone who would help them into trouble.

The records were in a long drawer behind the counter. Mestrel took out a ledger and two folded slips, found the entries and laid them beside the corresponding descriptions of cloth. She had measured both women. She remembered them separately, though she remembered most clearly which one had been difficult about paying.

“Stand,” she told Olyne.

“I have an injured ankle.”

“I need your shoulders.”

Elena helped her upright, keeping the weight on the sound leg. Mestrel put a tape across her back, checked the slope and wrote a number. Then she asked her to turn her head.

“That’s Zélie’s fit.”

“I know,” Olyne said.

“You asked.”

“We asked whether you would say so at a private meeting,” Sariel put in.

Mestrel measured the other shoulder. “I can say whose measurements these are. If you want someone to tell you who’s inside them, find a priest.”

“Would you provide a signed copy?”

“Of the measurements.”

“Yes.”

The dressmaker nodded. Sariel felt the small inward loosening of a plan which had survived its first ordinary request. Sann had arranged a private appointment for the next morning. They would have his evidence, the fitting record and whatever Olyne could answer before people accustomed to hearing a claim without laughing at the first sentence. It would not get her body back. It might get them through the household’s door.

Mestrel made a working copy on a narrow buff slip and set it beside a parcel wrapped in faded blue cloth. Two little mask outlines stood above the names. She copied the earlier change of delivery address as it appeared in the ledger, crossing out the first destination, then rubbed her thumb over the correction before it dried.

“These were copied for the troupe,” she said. “The face fittings came through with the clothes. Keep that with this until I finish the clean copy.”

Inside the blue cloth were the alterations Olyne had brought back: a sleeve that had pinched and a hem already muddied during her escape. Mestrel would work on them between customers. Sariel tucked the slip into the outer fold and tied the parcel loosely enough to show where it belonged.

Benjamin and Elena had to go back for the medicine kit and the borrowed footrest. The carrier who had brought Olyne had another hire. Sariel went to find a chair for the return journey. It was a short errand; Mestrel agreed to keep the patient behind the screen. Olyne agreed to this only after being told the alternative involved waiting in the street.

At the hirehouse Sariel rejected a chair with a high step and another whose poles shifted at their sockets. The owner watched her inspect the third.

“You could walk.”

“She can’t.”

He shrugged. “That one costs more.”

She paid, then added the price of taking the longer way round. Elena had been particular about that too: no sharp steps beside the canal, no short route over the broken paving. The porters complained until she showed them where to lower the pole and how to support the ankle inside. Then the older one nodded as if he had thought of it himself.

“Blue ribbon,” he said, tying one to the forward pole. “If we move along, that’s yours.”

She was wearing Bastien’s brown coat. It gave her the shape of a household servant sent on an errand, broad shoulders and roomy elbows, nothing expensive enough to invite a second glance. Before borrowing it she had moved Hest’s tools from her own coat into the flat pouch beneath her belt. She checked the pouch now, more from habit than need, and took the blue parcel back into the shop.

The room had filled while she was away.

A woman was being pinned into a dress worth more than their advance. Another had a new shoe on one foot and looked delighted with it. A woman by the masks had taken issue with a piece of brass. The man beside the baskets watched Sariel’s route rather than her face.

“I’m collecting the alterations.”

“They are not—” Mestrel began.

“For the lady at the back.”

She put down the parcel. Beside it lay a cream invitation with violet wax, and in the wax a velvet flower: Serafine’s household mark.

Sariel’s hand stopped on the blue knot.

The woman in the dress watched her through the mirror. She was not pretending otherwise. Beautiful clothes, a composed mouth, eyes that kept nothing of the mirror’s flattering amber light: Sariel had met enough of the Countess’s people to know that none of those things proved anything.

The seal did.

“Have we met?” the woman asked.

“I’d remember.”

“One hopes. I have occasionally put a great deal of effort into being forgotten, but never in this dress.”

Sariel moved toward the screen. The quiet man shifted his basket. She noted the help and the position it left him in, between her and the door, then let Mestrel meet her halfway.

“Your friend is being difficult.”

“She has had a difficult morning.”

“It began yesterday.”

For a moment Sariel nearly laughed. It would have been easier if the woman in the dress had not been watching. She touched Mestrel’s elbow and asked, quietly, “Can she leave in these?”

The dressmaker looked behind the screen and nodded.

Olyne had both hands pressed to the seat. A man stood near her with a closed notebook. He looked tired and annoyed by the light; Sariel could not tell how much he had heard.

She knelt, lifted the foot and drew out the cloak. “We’re going.”

“My clothes—”

“Later.”

She put the cloak round Olyne’s shoulders, took off Bastien’s coat to keep its bulk out of the way and passed it to Mestrel. There would be time to collect it. She hoped there would be time to collect a number of things.

When she stepped back to the counter, the woman in the expensive dress held the buff slip.

The parcel’s outer fold had come open. Sariel could see where she had dragged it loose with her wrist when the seal caught her attention. The paper had slid beneath the gloves. She had managed to bring evidence directly to the Countess’s people without their having to ask.

“That belongs with the parcel.”

“Then you should have wrapped it.”

“I did.”

The woman’s offer of a chair was courteous enough to sound innocent. Sariel told her they had transport. Olyne announced from behind the screen that she would not be carried like a parcel through a shop.

“You can walk to the door,” Sariel said.

She said it gently because the distance was going to hurt. Olyne heard the other part and stopped arguing.

“What is Ravenswood fitting you for?” the woman asked.

Sariel looked at the slip. Ravenswood had been written at its foot. She had given them that too.

“Ask your employer.”

“I haven’t accepted any employment.”

“You’ve accepted the clothes.”

Mestrel brought Olyne round the screen. Sariel got her arm beneath the cloak and felt the woman’s weight settle against her. It was less than she expected. Whatever Olyne imagined of the body she had been put in, it had already spent most of its strength.

The porter appeared. There was a brief confusion over whose chair waited outside. Sariel pointed to her patient, and Mestrel moved between the women with Bastien’s coat. The step was shallow. Olyne crossed it as if it were a ditch.

Outside, Sariel helped lower her into the chair. The older porter had remembered the footrest. Behind them someone swore. She did not turn to enjoy it.

The quiet man came to the door, touched the blue ribbon, then looked farther along the street. He knew the chair was hers. He stood aside.

At the corner Sariel looked back. The fitting slip was still in the woman’s hand. She could return, ask again, embarrass Mestrel, try to take it. She knew the exact reach required. The woman was pinned into an unfinished dress.

The chair rocked. Olyne made a sound that was nothing like an order.

Sariel caught the pole. “Easy. Stop here.”

The porters stopped. She checked the ankle, moved the footrest an inch and held the curtain aside until Olyne nodded.

“The longer route,” she reminded them.

“We know.”

She went with them.

Benjamin met the chair at the workshop with the missing footrest under one arm. He looked at the one already inside it, looked at Sariel without her coat, and decided to ask about neither until Olyne was upstairs.

Elena did not grant the same courtesy.

“Was the step clear?”

“Yes.”

“Did they take her over the broken stones?”

“No.”

“Then why is she white?”

“Because someone measured her while she was standing, and then we went into a shop with the Countess’s invitation on the counter.”

Elena’s face changed. She helped Olyne lie down before looking back.

“Was it meant for her?”

“For a woman having a very expensive dress fitted. Five of them, I think. I left the working slip in her hand.”

Sariel heard how flatly she said it. She had spent the walk imagining the Countess reading the names, looking amused, putting a fingertip on the one she wished to keep. She could not stop supplying the room in which it would happen.

Kestra brought the sponsorship paper out of her portfolio. “We still have tomorrow’s appointment.”

“Until somebody tells them not to see us.”

“Then we go before that happens.”

“And Mestrel can send the signed measurements,” Elena said. “You left a working copy. You didn’t burn her ledger.”

Sariel sat on the stair. She had intended to speak to Bastien, warn him that the address might become unsafe, make arrangements before another thing went wrong. Instead she sat while Elena drew Olyne’s curtains and Benjamin put the unused footrest on a shelf.

“I can fetch the coat,” he said.

“Not yet.”

He lowered himself onto the stair beside her. Their shoulders touched. Down below, Thane was making Bastien’s loose bench stop rocking, which Bastien had insisted was an improvement nobody needed.

“You got her out,” Benjamin said.

“I put her in there.”

“You didn’t invite the other five.”

She knew that. It helped to hear him say it anyway.

After a while she stood and went to tell Bastien what had happened. He listened, asked whether she had lost his coat, and then showed her the rear loading door and the boat kept beneath it. They agreed on where to go if somebody came asking. He charged her for the unused berth as well as the room, and she paid without bargaining.

Chapter 5 — Someone else’s lease

The front door was yellow.

Sariel had remembered it black, though she knew that was wrong. It had been brown before the fire, darkened with oil and years of hands, with a pale streak at shoulder height where a delivery hook had scraped it. There was nothing left to scrape now. The new door had been planed smooth and painted a yellow so cheerful she almost kept walking.

An awning hung above it. Blue and white stripes, straight at the edges, not patched. Through the open half she could see a woman on a table reaching across a great spread of canvas. A younger man stood at the other end with a measuring cord in his teeth.

“You’re pulling,” the woman said.

He took the cord out of his mouth. “It’s caught.”

“Then uncaught it.”

Sariel stopped in the doorway.

The room smelled of hot wax and new rope. For a moment the smell was so wrong that she could not see properly. The old table had stood to the left, beneath a window that was now bricked halfway up; Hest had used the good light there, and once when Sariel had been working late she had put a pear beside her hand without interrupting the particular, glorious difficulty of the lock. Sariel could remember the grit of the pear more clearly than the shape of the room.

“Delivery?” the woman asked.

“No.”

“Come in or shut the lower half. You’re letting the draft under it.”

She meant the canvas. Sariel came in and closed the door.

The woman stepped off the table. She had taken off her shoes to stand on the cloth and now put them on without bending, working each heel down with the opposite toe. A heavy needle had been pushed through the cuff of her sleeve. Sariel watched it catch the light when she reached for a cup.

“Can I help you?”

“I used to live here.”

The woman looked toward the stairs.

“Before,” Sariel said.

“Ah.”

She set the cup down. The younger man began measuring again, very quietly, as though the cloth might be offended by ordinary work.

“Hanne Sarret.”

“Sariel.”

There was no recognition. She had not known how much she was hoping for it until it failed to arrive.

Hanne looked round the room. “They kept the outer walls. Most of the rear had to come down. If you mean the people I rent from, they’ve been the owners since before I took it.”

“I’m not here about your lease.”

“People generally say that first.”

The younger man stopped pretending not to listen. Hanne gave him a small shake of her head and he went back to the cord.

Sariel uncurled her hands. She had walked here after lunch without telling the others where she was going. It was not far from Bastien’s; she knew every turning even where the houses had changed. At one corner she had found herself slowing for a loose paving stone that had been replaced so long ago its replacement was worn hollow.

“I wanted to see it,” she said.

Hanne studied her a little longer, then pointed with the hand holding the cup.

“You can look. Keep clear of that end. The wax is hot.”

Sariel went past the table. There had been a bench here, too close to the stair. Benjamin had caught his heel on it during practice and Orren had refused to move it until he could explain what the bench had done wrong. Thane would have been on the floor with the table legs. Kestra on a crate. Somewhere behind all that, a kettle which everyone expected somebody else to fill.

Now there were three women sewing a hem and an empty stool with a coat over it. One of the women had a small boy asleep beneath her chair, screened from the draft by a bolt of cloth. His mouth was open. Every few stitches she touched the top of his head with the side of her foot.

The rear stair had been moved. The old passage was a cupboard; the new one ran along the opposite wall and ended at a door with a recently fitted hasp. Sariel reached toward it before remembering herself.

“Does it still lead to the yard?”

“Yes. We had it widened for the frames.”

“May I?”

Hanne nodded.

The yard was smaller. An extension had taken the corner where they had kept broken things which somebody was always going to mend. In its place stood a drying rack covered in narrow strips of blue cloth. A woman on the far side hauled them straight with both hands and swore when one slipped. Light fell across her wrists.

Sariel had expected to feel the fire here. What came instead was the memory of wanting to leave: being young, bored with another damp afternoon indoors, furious that Hest had given her a lock to clean when she was quite capable of opening it already. There had been a whole city outside and people who could walk through it without asking permission. She had wanted work worth telling someone about.

Behind her Hanne said, “The council gave us a month to put the yard right. Then changed which things counted as putting it right. That’s why there’s a drain in the wrong corner.”

Water lay beside the drying rack. The woman shook one blue strip hard enough to spray both her neighbors.

“It used to drain that way,” Sariel said, pointing.

“It still wants to.”

She could see how to clear it. Lift the new sill, cut beneath the paving, give the water the old path. Thane would have liked the problem. She almost offered him before stopping herself.

Hanne saw the hesitation. “Have you a claim on the place?”

“No.”

“An old one?”

Sariel looked back through the doorway, past the cutting table to the yellow door. The boy under the chair had woken. His mother was giving him something from a folded cloth, keeping her needle between her lips while she did it.

“No claim I can ask you to answer.”

Hanne took a breath she had been holding. “I’ve three years left on the lease. Seven people work here when the orders come in. I can’t afford another move.”

“I didn’t mean to frighten you.”

“You didn’t, at first.”

Sariel winced. Hanne looked at her without either satisfaction or apology.

“Would you like to see upstairs?” she asked.

For a moment Sariel would have given almost anything to hear the offer in a different voice. Hest, impatient with her dawdling; Tavia, calling down that somebody had left a wet coat on her bed; even Kes shouting about a misplaced paper while everybody pretended to look. She had not expected this woman to be kind, and did not know what to do with kindness from someone guarding the place against her.

“No,” she said. Then, because the first word sounded ungrateful, “Not today.”

Hanne accepted it. They went back through the workshop together. The younger man had finally freed the measuring cord. It lay straight across the cloth, and he was staring at the marks with the dismay of someone whose equipment had stopped providing an excuse.

“Measure from the other end,” Hanne told him.

“I have.”

“Do it where I can see.”

Sariel reached for the door. The latch rose smoothly beneath her thumb. Nothing caught. Someone had done good work here.

She let it fall again and took an ordinary scrap of paper from her belt pouch. Not one of the old letters. On it she wrote Bastien’s address, her name, and the description of the drain before the extension.

“If your builder asks,” she said. “I remember where the outlet was.”

Hanne read it. “Thank you.”

“I’m not promising my carpenter.”

“I didn’t ask for him.”

Sariel smiled. It surprised them both.

Outside, she walked half a street before stopping at a shop window. The glass showed her face over a display of cheap combs. She waited until it stopped looking like someone who had been turned away. Then she bought a comb she did not need, because the shopkeeper had seen her looking and because for once she did not want to explain what she had come for.

By the time she reached the canal the preliminary supper at Ravenswood had become their evening’s work. The estate was hosting a reconciliation between the Gemcutters and Alchemists over the fouling of the city’s washing water. There would be speeches, followed by a visiting Thepyrgosi troupe. Their director, Vaudrin, had fitted six pairs of patrons and players for the entertainment at the closing gala.

Kestra had found a retired fitter who knew enough of the troupe’s preparations to confirm that personally fitted masks mattered. The woman’s directions were folded beside Mestrel’s clean measurements, which Benjamin had collected with Bastien’s coat. The Countess’s invitation was not among the things returned. Nor was the buff slip.

“Mestrel says the woman still has it,” Benjamin said.

“Which woman?”

“The one she had to move the knife for.”

Sariel hung the coat on Bastien’s peg. She had expected to be relieved at its return. Instead she resented how easily a coat could come back when everything else required a petition.

Olyne’s mask would be at Ravenswood. So would the apparatus used in the private rehearsal. Sann had a way to bring his petition to the hall in the morning, but the examiner he had approached had asked whether the company could produce anything beyond statements about an entertainment. He had not required theft. He had not offered to obtain the objects himself either.

“The mask is hers,” Kestra said. “Fitted to her original body. If the people at the house mean to help her, they can hardly object to our asking for it.”

“Do you mean asking?” Benjamin said.

“I mean we begin there.”

Elena sat on the bottom stair. “She stays here tonight.”

No one argued. Above them Olyne had begun to quarrel with Bastien about the broth. That sound was a better report than Elena could have given.

Thane unrolled his tools. He had been to look at the service entrance that afternoon, with Benjamin passing for a man interested in hiring a wagon. An upper gallery, a broad stair, doors that admitted working people more readily when they carried something. He had drawn only the route he had seen. The rest of the paper was blank.

“If we have to bring the whole thing down,” he said, “I need to see how it sits.”

“And if we can’t?”

“We leave it sitting.”

Sariel looked at the empty portion of the drawing. She could feel Hanne’s workshop under her feet: the altered stair, the good latch, the woman touching the boy with her foot. For an hour she had been walking through rooms that would not become hers again by recognizing them. The evening offered doors she might actually open. She was aware of being glad, and of not wanting to examine the gladness too closely.

“We take the fitted face if we can,” she said. “We learn what else is there. Nobody stays to win an argument.”

Benjamin picked up his sword belt. “That last part for me?”

“For all of us.”

“I’ll remind you.”

She fastened her own coat. Hest’s case went back into its inside pocket. The new comb fitted beside it and caught on the flap; she moved it to the other side, annoyed by the small clumsiness, then laughed when Thane held out his left hand without looking.

“No,” she said. “I’ll manage.”

Outside, the first supper boats were moving up the canal. Lamps shone beneath their awnings. A man in a pale waistcoat leaned over a rail to object to the smell from a neighboring kitchen, and the woman cooking there told him to hold his breath until the next bridge.

Sariel waited for Benjamin at the stair. He came down with the parcel they meant to carry into Ravenswood, and she took one end before he asked.

Part II — What the city will hear

Chapter 6 — The mask taken

The linen smelled of hot starch, and someone had folded a fork into it.

Sariel found the fork when she put the stack against her hip. She kept walking. A servant with his hands full of pastry was coming the other way, looking past her toward a woman who wanted to know why there were no plates. Sariel lifted the top cloth, laid the fork on his tray and went through the door he held with his heel.

The borrowed apron pulled across her shoulders. She had tied it too high. Lowering it would have meant putting down the linen, and people who put things down in a busy house were liable to be given different things to carry.

Beyond the ballroom doors, a man was objecting to a fish. Sariel let the dispute carry on without her.

She had already passed the sponsor’s suite once. The door had been open a hand’s breadth. Inside, a maid shook out a blue dress while an older woman’s voice asked for the other shoes. Olyne’s voice, used by someone who did not have Olyne’s impatience with getting dressed. Sariel had almost stopped. Then the maid had looked up, and she had gone on with the linen.

They could not get both women out tonight. Elena had made her describe the journey, from the injured patient’s bed to the place of return, before permitting even an argument about it. Olyne could walk a little on level ground. The ankle swelled when she sat with the foot down; coughing brought both hands to her ribs. Whatever happened upstairs, she was staying with Bastien.

There were five of them, a supper full of guards and an instrument none of them had operated. Sariel had repeated these facts to herself in the lane. They had not become more agreeable indoors.

Thane waited on the service landing with a broken brass fastening in his good hand. He had acquired it from a genuine repair beneath a sideboard and intended to remain on that errand for as long as it was useful.

“They gave me a second one,” he murmured.

“Fix it tomorrow.”

“I told them we might be busy.”

She put the linen on a chest. He looked toward the gallery. The musician below drew a long, wavering note from a string. Somewhere farther off Benjamin was carrying two cups and allowing people to think he had forgotten where he meant to go.

Kestra had gone ahead. Sariel disliked losing sight of her in a strange house, a feeling to which Kestra had given the consideration it deserved by crawling beneath the performance frame without asking.

The screen at the gallery entrance showed a painted mountain whose lower slopes were worn away by passing furniture. Sariel slipped behind it. She saw glass first, two dark wings reflecting a slice of the room. The red face hung between them, small in its elaborate mounting. Its mouth was a pale cut in a surface that seemed darker whenever she looked directly at it.

Kestra’s shoe showed beneath the base. She withdrew it when Sariel touched the upright.

Across the frame, another woman was already working.

Sariel recognized the solid shoulders, the cropped hair and the tool case from the fitting shop. The Countess had sent somebody who knew what she was doing. The toolwoman had a bar against a catch and was watching the glass rather than the doorway, which Sariel appreciated for exactly the wrong reason.

Thane stopped just inside the screen. Sariel pointed him toward the side of the frame. He looked at the glass hinge, then at her. The slight movement of his mouth meant that he had found three objections and would save two.

The paired faces stood in a shallow cupboard against the wall. Sariel had seen the attendant shut it earlier. The lock was better than the cupboard. Its tongue ran into a thin brass plate held by screws that someone had painted over without tightening.

She chose to open the lock. Pulling the plate would leave a noise and a splinter, and the hand that knew this kind of ward had been taught before the guildhouse burned.

The second pick found the spring. She turned it, felt a little resistance and eased back. Hest would have made her begin again for that first push. Sariel changed the angle. The tongue withdrew. As the cupboard opened, she saw the toolwoman turn her head.

Inside, pale noses projected from padded divisions. There were masks with curled painted mouths, sober half-faces, a gilt brow behind cream silk. None of them looked like the people who had paid to wear them. Labels were tucked beneath the padding. Sariel found Olyne’s name beside the blue-and-white face and checked the small marks along its lining against the drawing Kestra had made from their notes.

She wanted the red one. Taking it would end whatever the troupe intended for the next supper, and it might give them a way to insist on a return. It might also leave two women waiting while Thane and Kestra learned how to carry a dangerous object through a door. The blue one was fitted to Olyne’s body. Vaudrin could not finish this business without it.

She put it into the flat canvas bag.

Behind her, the toolwoman moved.

Then a man reached for the toolwoman’s case. A second was coming out of the sponsor’s doorway with a short staff.

Benjamin’s cup broke against the man reaching for the case.

Sariel crouched over the bag as the other guard’s staff struck. Tools scattered on the boards. Thane had moved to the far side of the frame; he held a little wedge in his left hand, poised between putting it to use and getting out of the way. Glass rasped behind the toolwoman’s shoulder.

“Mind that,” Thane said.

Kestra slid out from under the base, her sleeve dusty, and passed close enough to touch Sariel’s elbow.

“The marks travel with it,” she whispered.

Sariel drew the mouth of the bag together. Kestra was already past the screens. She had the drawing, and there was nothing here she could help by being hit.

The toolwoman and the staff moved across Sariel’s view. Benjamin caught the guard at the rail. A broad woman arrived and stripped the weapon from the man’s grip with an abruptness that made Sariel grateful she had not chosen that entrance herself.

For a moment there was room to reach the red face. The bronze fork sat in its mounting. Sariel could see where a hand would turn it.

“Leave that,” the toolwoman said.

“You leave yours.”

Thane stepped between them. He was looking toward the public stair. More feet were coming, quickly now.

“Thane,” Sariel said. “Go.”

He backed into the narrow passage. She followed. Behind the arriving guards she caught Sel’s black dress and the Countess’s woman in the expensive sleeve.

“The blue bag,” Sel said. “Take the blue bag.”

Benjamin came into the passage after them and drew Orren’s sword. Sariel knew from the way he placed his feet that he intended to hold the turn, and from the way he left his rear heel free that he intended to come away from it.

The broad woman wanted through. He denied her. Sariel heard wood ring against steel as she went down the service stair.

Thane was one step below her. He had his right arm close to his body; his left hand ran lightly along the rail. At the bottom they found two servants trying to decide whether the disturbance entitled them to leave the plates where they stood. Sariel passed between them and the wall. A bowl of cream trembled on the uppermost plate.

She looked back at the landing.

No Benjamin.

She waited two breaths, counted a third and started up one step. His boot came round the turn. He paused until she moved, making an impatient little motion with his sword hand. She hated him briefly for being able to see her waiting.

Elena stood at the far end of the wharf beside the empty cart. Blankets covered the bed; a lidded bucket stood beneath its side rail.

“Not the upper lane,” Elena said. “They’ve shut the gate for a delivery. Go past the wash yard.”

“Kes?”

“Ahead.”

Thane put his left hand through the leather loop on the shaft. The cart started with a small squeal that sounded, to Sariel, louder than everything behind them.

She heard a shout from the loading door. Turning, she saw the crossbow. The people from the other company scattered toward the baskets. Sariel caught Elena above the elbow and hauled her round the corner before Elena could finish telling her she had seen it.

Benjamin stayed at the angle until they had passed. Then he came after them, sword low, his breath loud enough for Sariel to hear over the cart.

They took the wash-yard lane. Hot water ran along the gutter in a steaming strip. Sariel tucked the bag between the blankets and walked beside it with one hand under the folded cloth. A woman at a door looked at Benjamin’s sword. He put it away. It had no blood on it.

Halfway down the lane Kestra came out from beneath a drying rack. She had a streak of dust across her forehead and was trying to fasten her cuff with fingers that would not quite manage the button.

Sariel did it for her. Neither spoke until the cart had passed the next turning.

Bastien made them take their shoes off at the foot of his stair.

“If there’s trouble after you, it’ll find the room without the footprints.”

He took Benjamin’s wet coat and hung it where it would drip into a bucket instead of the shavings. Above them Olyne demanded to know who had come in. The young voice carried easily through the boards. It sounded stronger than it had that morning, which pleased Sariel until she remembered how often strength was spent in sounding strong.

Elena went up first. Sariel followed with the wrapped mask.

Olyne lay propped on two pillows, her hair caught back in an ugly knot. The scratch beneath her jaw had dried dark. She looked at Sariel’s face, then at the parcel.

“Is it the instrument?”

“Your fitted face.”

“Then where is the instrument?”

“Still in the house.”

Olyne shut her eyes. Sariel let her keep them shut while she unwrapped the cloth on the little table.

Blue paint curved over the cheek. The lining was pale and clean except for a tiny dark mark where a fitting pin had caught. Sariel had thought it would seem ordinary away from the frame. Instead she disliked the empty shape more. It belonged against an absent face, and the woman who owned that face was in the bed behind her.

“Let me see.”

Sariel held it up. Olyne reached, then stopped with her hand halfway out from the blanket.

“The brow is too broad,” she said.

Elena looked at her.

“For this.” Olyne touched the young forehead. “I know whose it is.”

Sariel lowered the mask. For the first time since they had brought the woman here, Olyne seemed embarrassed by their presence.

“Put it away.”

Kestra came in carrying a cup. She had spoken, before supper, to the retired fitter who had checked their account of the painted workings. She repeated the distinction carefully: removing a fitted face from its box would not force a change or spoil the return. The central working directed the passage. The personal mask still belonged to the living body for which it had been made.

“She has not examined this instrument,” Kestra added.

“You said she knew them.”

“She knows fitted work. She watched the troupe rehearse last season. I told you what she could tell us, and what she couldn’t.”

Olyne accepted the cup without thanking her. After a moment she asked for the cloth to be put over the mask’s mouth as well.

Downstairs, Benjamin had taken off his shirt to inspect the mark on his sword arm. Sariel stood in the doorway while Elena pressed along the muscle. He endured that, but flinched when she lifted his wrist.

“Bruised,” Elena said. “Tell me if the hand goes numb.”

“It won’t.”

“You may tell it that yourself.”

She went back upstairs. Thane was sitting with his tools open, his bad hand resting palm-up on his knee. Sariel took a loose shirt button from the bench beside Benjamin. She found thread in Elena’s basket and sat to sew it back on.

He watched the needle for a while.

“You were coming up again.”

“Only a step.”

“I had room.”

She pulled the thread through too hard. The button tilted. She loosened it and began the stitch again.

Benjamin moved closer, bringing the lamp with him. His shoulder touched hers. They sat that way until she finished.

Kestra came down with the hearing papers, and Bastien cleared a patch of workbench for her. Sariel wrapped the mask a second time and put it beside her own bedroll. Before lying down, she checked the window catch and the door to the rear gallery. The neighboring store’s stair stood empty beyond it. Their paid right to use that way lasted until morning.

She went downstairs to ask Bastien to renew it.

Chapter 7 — What the guild wants heard

Nera Pellan had brought an empty sack for her tools.

It lay across her knees in the Gemcutters’ waiting room. There was a patch where something hot had burned through the weave, neatly sewn with string of a different color. Kestra remembered her carrying an empty basket at the hiring office. Apparently she had reconsidered how much she would be allowed to take home.

“They’ve moved you upstairs,” Nera said.

“Someone has. I haven’t thanked them yet.”

The room’s windows faced the canal. One had been painted shut. A porter tried the other, gave up when the latch bent in his hand, and put down a tray of glasses without opening it. The smell of damp coats remained where it was.

Merrit Sann sat beside Kestra with his hat between his shoes. He had dressed for his firm, a heavy good coat with a small repair under the arm. When he reached to straighten the papers on his knee, Kestra saw where the lining had been replaced.

“You can still speak to her alone,” she told him.

“I have spoken to her.”

“I mean before you answer questions.”

He regarded the closed inner door. “I know what she does with the corner of a paper. I know what she said about a consignment that never arrived, and who took the second payment. I also know a clever person could have been told those things.”

“That is what I want you to say.”

“It won’t be what they want.”

Kestra had slept badly. The grain of the bench pressed through her coat at the same place whenever she shifted. In her case were Olyne’s account, the letters establishing Sann’s business with her and a request that the two women be questioned separately. There was also the company’s request for temporary sponsorship. She had put that beneath the others.

The clerk who emerged from the inner room took the papers in the opposite order.

“Your company is represented by five persons?”

“Today by me. The witness will come when transport and the manner of questioning are settled.”

“The claimant.”

“She is also a witness to what happened.”

He gave her the patient look of a man who could afford to make a distinction repeatedly because he would be paid until the office closed. “Your company employed her?”

“She employed us.”

“That is among the things to be established.”

Nera made a small sound beside the window. She was pulling a loose thread from the mouth of the sack. Kestra wished she would stop. The wish was unreasonable and did not make the sound easier to hear.

The clerk admitted them to a room with a long table, three occupied chairs at the far side and no place laid for the patient. Kavra, the Gemcutters’ guildmistress, sat in the middle. The two others were a guild examiner and a man responsible for commercial admissions. Kestra had expected an independent witness from the Alchemists. There was an empty chair where one might have sat.

“He has asked to receive any written account,” Kavra said when Kestra looked at it.

“Will he attend?”

“He is making inquiries about what his presence would acknowledge.”

Kestra put her case on the table. “This was to be a private identification.”

“It is private.”

Sann sat. Kestra stayed standing until Kavra gestured toward the other chair. Then she sat too, because her back hurt and standing would not improve their position.

The admissions officer opened a narrow book.

“Silk Coin.”

Kestra watched his forefinger travel down a page. He found the place without searching. Someone had prepared it for him.

“There is a guild trading under that designation.”

“We know. Its rebuilding did not include our personal reinstatement. That was made clear at the hiring office.”

“Then you understand why we cannot proceed on a company authority.”

“I came to establish a woman’s identity.”

“On behalf of a company claiming to hold her commission.”

“On her behalf.” Kestra opened Olyne’s statement. “Here is her account. Here is Mr. Sann, who has done business with her for years. If you find the account insufficient, ask another question. You have not asked the first one yet.”

The examiner reached for the statement. The admissions officer put two fingers on it before he could lift it.

Kestra looked at Kavra. Kavra looked at the fingers.

“Let him read,” she said.

For several minutes nobody spoke. Through the wall came the muffled scrape of a tray being moved. Kestra could hear a woman outside protesting that the goods had been sound when accepted. Nera’s voice was low and stubborn. It made the room feel less private than its occupants preferred.

The examiner asked Sann about Olyne’s instructions. Sann answered plainly. He named the shipment, the missing goods, the peculiar correction Olyne had made to his estimate. When asked about the paper-folding habit, he demonstrated it with his thumbnail.

The examiner copied the notch.

“Like this?”

Sann waited while the man turned the sheet over.

“You did it after you decided to show me,” he said. “She did it while she was angry about something else. I don’t claim that settles it.”

Kestra almost smiled. She held herself still.

Kavra asked how the young woman had come to be injured. Kestra gave the limited account they had and the assessment Elena had permitted her to repeat. She asked again for the assurance that no one would remove the patient to Ravenswood while the claim was being heard.

“You wish a guild escort?”

“For the journey here and back.”

“Back to what address?” the admissions officer asked.

“That can be given to the escort when it is appointed.”

“You are asking us to acknowledge custody while withholding the place in which the person is confined.”

Kestra felt heat creep into her neck. “She is not confined.”

“Then she can present herself.”

“With an injured ankle.”

“Carriage can be arranged.”

“By whom?”

He opened a second folder. The paper inside carried Ravenswood’s mark. There was an assurance signed in Olyne’s name, and an offer from the hostess’s chosen companion to receive the distressed performer for care.

Kestra pulled her own hand off the table. She had begun reaching for the paper as though she could prevent it existing by taking it away.

“Have you asked the woman who signed that who she is?”

“Dame Olyne’s affairs are well known to this office.”

“I did not ask whether you knew her affairs.”

Kavra’s chair scraped. “We have requested a private meeting.”

“And been refused?”

“Her selected companion is to be present.”

Kestra made herself look at the examiner. He had read the whole account. There was still a way for him to say he wanted to hear the person who had given it.

“If I bring her here with Elena,” she said, “will you give your word she leaves with us?”

The examiner glanced at Kavra. Kavra did not answer at once.

The outer door opened. A clerk brought in another folder and apologized. The admissions officer took it with visible relief, as though a simpler wrong had arrived.

“The specimen you requested,” the clerk said.

He laid a signed commercial order beside the assurance. Kestra saw Olyne’s name. Beneath it, in the same hard hand, was a direction excluding Nera Pellan from approved purchasing and deliveries pending recovery of costs.

“That’s hers,” Kestra said.

The clerk looked at her. “Her signature?”

“Her order.”

It covered a batch of abrasive wash purchased outside the permitted suppliers. The finished work had been accepted. There was a notation admitting that much. The loss was calculated from purchases diverted from a bonded seller; Nera’s tools were held against it at the guild store.

Kestra read the signature again. No one had compelled Olyne to make it with an unfamiliar hand. The date preceded the troupe’s arrival.

“We have had enough attempts,” the admissions officer said, “to recover property by asserting that an order was given under some unprovable influence.”

“This is not such an assertion.”

“You know the woman waiting outside.”

“I met her at your hiring office.”

“Where she has continued to obstruct business.”

Kestra stood so suddenly the chair caught the back of her knee.

Sann put a hand on the table. He did not touch her. The small restraint gave her a moment in which to decide what she was going to say.

“Olyne may have signed every word of that order. You can ask her why when she is in a position to answer. It does not tell you whether the woman at Ravenswood is the one who signed it.”

The admissions officer shut his book.

“The petitioners’ commercial standing must be resolved before the guild assumes responsibility for their proceedings.”

“Then have Mr. Sann petition.”

“His principal is outside Silkstrand.”

“Let me petition in my own name.”

“Your own name is among those at issue.”

Kestra sat down again. The chair had moved farther back than she thought; she had to catch its edge with her hand.

Kavra told the officer to leave the papers. He protested that the entries were active. She told him again. He went, taking the book but leaving both signatures and Olyne’s account.

The examiner stayed. Kestra watched him straighten a glass that had been square with the table already.

“I cannot promise the escort you require today,” Kavra said.

“Then the hearing is canceled.”

“Deferred.”

“To when?”

Kavra had no date. She offered to pursue a meeting before the final entertainment, with representatives who would agree beforehand what they had come to hear. Kestra wanted to strike the table with the whole weight of her arm. Instead she took out a clean sheet and wrote the conditions she had asked for.

“Mark which ones you refuse.”

“I have not refused all of them.”

“Mark those you will provide, then.”

Kavra took the pen. She confirmed receipt of the identity claim and agreed that the women must be questioned separately if the meeting could be secured. She did not authorize removal of the patient. She did not give Kestra a right to work under the company’s old name.

Kestra dried the ink before she folded the sheet. Her hand was shaking, and she refused to let it leave a smear they could blame for anything later.

Nera was still outside. Her sack was empty.

“Did they hear you?” she asked.

“Parts.”

“You were louder than I was.”

Kestra sat beside her. Sann stood at the window, giving them what privacy the room offered.

“I saw the order,” Kestra said.

Nera stopped working the loose thread.

“I bought what I could get. The approved seller wanted half again as much and couldn’t deliver for eight days. There were men waiting for the pieces. They wouldn’t pay me for waiting.”

“The work was accepted.”

“Some of it was rough. I redid it. That isn’t why they’ve got my wheels.” She folded the sack along its old crease. “She said if they let me choose suppliers, everyone would want to.”

Kestra looked toward the closed inner door.

“The woman we’re helping is Olyne.”

“I could hear that much.”

“I didn’t know about this.”

Nera gave her a tired, baffled look. “Would it have got my things back if you did?”

Kestra had no answer that belonged in the room.

Nera stood, her knees stiff. “Bring her back if you can. I’d like to put my question to the person who signed.”

She took her empty sack downstairs. Kestra stayed until she could breathe without wanting to say something cruel to a person who had not earned it. Then Sann collected his hat and offered to carry her case.

She let him, as far as the street.

At the bookseller’s corner she found a ribbon seller willing to look for a man with a black portfolio. She paid him and waited beside a table of damp books, keeping Olyne’s statement under her coat. When Ilaro Selci arrived, he had to be made to notice her.

“You’re Selci.”

“Yes.”

“I’m not giving you the address.”

He wanted to sit down before they spent the time she thought she was saving. She chose an eating room facing a brick wall. His companion chose the quiet table.

Kestra put the statement between them. It took less effort than she had expected to let go of the paper, and more to watch him read it. She stayed until he had marked the permission she gave: all five of his company could see it; their employer could not.

When she came out, there was bread in her handkerchief. She had eaten one piece without tasting it. She stopped beneath the awning, broke the other in half and chewed slowly this time before taking the news back to the patient.

Chapter 8 — Her afternoon

The costume worker made Elena wait while she finished taking pins out of her mouth.

They were very small pins. She put each one through the felt band round her wrist, examined the last between thumb and finger and said, “No.”

Elena stood beside a basket of worn stockings. “I haven’t asked yet.”

“You came from the shop with a woman who needed help walking. Last night someone ran off with a face. Now you want a private word with someone I’m paid to dress. I can make a fair attempt at the question.”

The worker was mending a padded shoulder in a room off Ravenswood’s eastern service passage. A charcoal iron stood on a tile beneath the window. Its hot metal smell mingled with boiled starch and the faint sourness of clothes that had dried without being properly washed.

Elena had come carrying a parcel of bandages to be delivered to the housekeeper. It was a real parcel. She had paid for the cloth and had given the name of an acquaintance who occasionally did dressings here. If she failed to reach Zélie, the house would at least get the bandages.

She disliked the expense more now that Kestra had returned from the hearing. Kestra had gone out again to find the Countess’s investigator. Elena had taken the other direction, reaching Ravenswood before the household began admitting its midday visitors.

“Has anyone asked whether she’s well?” Elena said.

“Everyone asks. They ask on the way to asking for something.”

“I need to know whether she wants to leave.”

The woman pulled her thread through the shoulder. “She has a carriage whenever she wants one.”

“Then it should be easy to ask.”

For a while there was only the soft rasp of thread through wadding. The worker looked toward the doorway. Elena moved her basket off the chair and sat where she could be seen from the passage.

“Who gets blamed?” the woman asked.

“If you send me away, no one will hear that we spoke.”

“And if I don’t?”

“Tell her I’m here. If she chooses to come in, you can go and complain about my basket being in your chair.”

The woman examined the basket. “It is in my chair.”

She went out. Elena waited with her hands flat on her knees, resisting the desire to count the minutes by the sound of shoes passing. Someone laughed outside. A servant pushed open the door, saw her and apologized. Elena asked whether the water in the jug was drinking water. The servant said it had been brought from the well and poured a cup before leaving.

She was drinking when Zélie came in.

The older body filled the doorway differently from the one Elena had been tending. Zélie held one hand beneath her breast, where a seam had worked itself into an uncomfortable place. Her hair was pinned too severely at the temples. The ring with the blue stone shone on a finger swollen around it.

“She said you do dressings.”

“I do.”

“For whom?”

Elena set down the cup. “For the woman who is in your body.”

Zélie went pale. She gripped the door edge and looked behind her.

“Shut it,” she said.

Elena did, leaving the latch raised so the worker could enter without knocking. When she turned back, Zélie had taken the good chair. She did it without looking at the other one, and then seemed to notice what she had done.

“You can sit there.”

Elena took the stool. One of its legs was short. She folded a scrap of cloth and pushed it beneath the foot.

“Is she dead?”

“No.”

“Is she dying?”

“I haven’t seen signs that make me think so. She’s hurt. Her breathing needs watching, and she must keep weight off the ankle.”

Zélie pressed both palms against the arms of the chair.

“Which ankle?”

Elena told her.

“I twisted that one coming off a wagon. Years ago. It was all right afterward.”

“Does it trouble you when you’ve been standing?”

“Sometimes. The outside of it.”

Elena took that in. Olyne could not have told her. It was useful in a small, ordinary way that left both women sitting in the wrong places.

“I’ll watch for that.”

Zélie began to rub the ring. She pushed it upward, met the swollen knuckle and stopped.

“Don’t force it,” Elena said.

“I’m not.”

Elena waited. After a while Zélie took her other hand away.

The worker came back for the iron, carrying it out with a folded cloth. She left the door as Elena had left it. Zélie watched her go.

“She used to tell me to come back when she wasn’t busy.”

“Does she now?”

“No.”

Elena did not fill the silence. She could hear a cart at the canal landing, the scrape and thump of barrels crossing boards. Zélie’s feet were planted wide beneath her skirts. She kept shifting one of them, searching for a comfortable place.

“I didn’t know they weren’t going to finish,” she said at last.

Elena had expected a denial prepared for her. This sounded like the end of a conversation the woman had been having alone for days.

“What did they tell you would happen?”

“The demonstration. Then we would go into the garden. People would be allowed to ask questions. We would come back before the company ate.”

“You and Olyne?”

“Yes.”

“Did she tell you she agreed?”

Zélie’s eyes narrowed. “You know she didn’t.”

“I know what she remembers.”

“They said she’d agreed to all of it. The dinner, the questions. I thought she was being generous. People are, sometimes, when there are enough people there to see.”

Elena watched her swallow. The muscles in the older throat did not move as the young one’s did; Zélie touched the hollow beneath her chin afterward, as if clearing something that had remained there.

She had been offered money for the demonstration. More than she could earn from the ordinary work waiting for her after the troupe left. Vaudrin had already taught her the movements. She could find her mark without looking down, turn the silver tongue, wait for the glass and answer the invitation. During rehearsals the central face was covered. The repeated motions had become dull enough that she stopped being afraid of what they represented.

Then Sel offered the real afternoon.

Zélie described the first moments badly. She could remember the ceiling seeming closer, a weight across her middle, one shoe pressing a toe she had never felt before. She had looked at the woman wearing her own face and burst into tears. Then Olyne had begun shouting from the other side of the glass. There were people laughing because they thought that was part of what they had paid to see.

“I laughed too,” Zélie said. “At first.”

She took a breath through her nose. Elena could see she wanted to make the sentence smaller and had decided against trying.

“Then she tried to stand, and they took her out. Sel said somebody accustomed to giving orders could become upset when everyone stopped obeying them. I thought—”

She reached for the cup Elena had used, stopped and asked whether there was another.

Elena fetched one from the shelf. She poured water and gave it to her.

“I thought she should try it for a little while,” Zélie said.

They sat with that while the sounds of work continued outside. Elena had heard Olyne tell Bastien that his chair was unsuitable, as though the man had arranged to give her a young body and then furnished it badly. She could imagine the satisfaction. She could also remember the swollen ankle when she first took the stocking off.

“When did you ask them to bring her back?”

“After the garden.”

“How long?”

“I don’t know.”

Elena waited.

“Long enough to eat,” Zélie said. “Long enough to be warm. I had a room. There wasn’t anybody’s washing hanging over my bed. They brought a tray and asked where I wanted it. They stayed until I’d answered.”

She looked angry now, and Elena could not tell which of them she wanted to spend it on.

“I had seen the rooms before. You carry things into them. You don’t sit down.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’ve changed dressings in rooms where they covered the chair before they let me use it.”

Zélie’s anger eased a little. She looked toward the costume worker’s empty place.

Sel had come later with the assurance that the demonstration was a success and the public performance should proceed. Zélie had asked for the return first. Olyne was too agitated, Sel said; the instrument had been put away. Signing would prevent the household being embarrassed by a minor delay.

Zélie had refused, and Sel had stopped discussing embarrassment.

“She said Olyne could have me locked in a room while they decided what to do with me. That it wouldn’t matter which face I had. There were places they could keep someone who didn’t know her own name.”

“And you signed.”

“Yes.”

“Did you sign anything else?”

“Notes. Flowers for the house. The invitation to the last dance. She put the name in front of me to copy.”

Elena asked her to remember the papers separately. Zélie grew cross, then frightened, then cross again. The assurance was one sheet. The notes were small folded cards. She had not signed a sale or a bank order that she could remember, but Elena could see that she no longer trusted her own recollection of being pleased to hold a good pen.

“I’ll come back to that,” Elena said.

“Will you?”

“If we can arrange it.”

Zélie put her water down. Her thumb had left a wet mark on the cup.

Elena moved the basket out of the way of the door.

“You can walk out with me now. Say you want me to look at your hand in better light. There are people outside who will let you pass because of the face you’re wearing.”

“Sel will know.”

“By then you’ll be outside.”

“And where do I go?”

“Somewhere separate from her. Somewhere we can keep you until we bring both of you to the frame.”

Zélie stood. The quick movement surprised Elena. She reached toward her and stopped herself. The woman was steady enough; she had stood because she wanted to go.

Then she looked down at the skirts.

“She’ll have me arrested.”

Elena did not answer quickly enough.

“You think so too.”

“I think she’ll be angry. I think she’ll say you helped them. I haven’t asked a magistrate what happens next.”

“But you’ll put me in front of her.”

“For the return.”

“And after that I will be a player again. In a room full of people who listen when she speaks.”

Elena wished she had brought someone who knew the law. Kestra would at least have known which promises were useless before she opened her mouth. But Kestra’s morning had ended on a bench outside a hearing that never began.

“We can find someone to speak for you,” she said.

“Who?”

“I don’t have a name yet.”

Zélie sat down.

There was no dramatic change in her face. She settled into the chair, pulled her skirts clear of the leg and waited for Elena to understand that the distance to the door had grown too great.

Elena put both hands on her knees. “I will take you if you change your mind.”

“Will you tell Olyne I wouldn’t come?”

“I’ll tell her what I asked and what you answered.”

“She’ll hate me more.”

“I don’t know.”

Zélie leaned back. For a few breaths she looked exhausted enough to sleep where she sat.

“The woman in the good dress,” she said. “From last night. She wants something.”

Elena knew which woman she meant.

“Probably.”

“She had the fitting slip. She kept showing it. I thought she wanted money to keep quiet.”

“I don’t know what she’ll ask you.”

“She knows people. People who decide things.”

Elena could offer no such acquaintance without making another woman into shelter she had not agreed to provide. She told Zélie that if anyone offered an advocate, she should ask whether the advocate would hear her alone, and who would be paying. Zélie listened very carefully. It hurt to see how much more useful she found that than the open door.

Before Elena left, she asked where the yellow bird face was kept.

“Upstairs. In the sponsor’s room. The green box.”

“Can you get it?”

“If she sends me for it.”

“Don’t take a risk just to show us.”

Zélie looked at her with the first faint amusement of their meeting. “You asked me to walk out of the house.”

“Yes. I’d have been with you.”

The worker returned with the cooled iron. She took in the two seated women and the unused bandages without comment. Zélie rose and asked whether the seam under her arm could be let out. When the worker went to find a chalk, Zélie touched Elena’s sleeve.

“Will my ankle be worse because she’s walking on it?”

“I’m keeping her off it as much as she’ll let me.”

“She should let you.”

“I have mentioned that.”

Zélie held her sleeve a moment longer. Then she released it and went to stand by the window where the worker could see the seam.

Elena left the parcel for the housekeeper. Outside, the canal light hurt her eyes after the dim room. She stopped beneath an awning to let them settle, feeling the absence of the woman’s hand on her arm. She had offered to bring back another patient without knowing where she would put her. She would need to speak to Sariel about a second room, and to Kestra about an advocate, and to Olyne about what she meant to say.

First she went to buy more clean water.

Chapter 9 — An address withheld

The men arrived with a folded authority and a carrying chair.

Elena saw the chair through Bastien’s open shutters: polished handles, a hood against the weather, two bearers waiting with the offended patience of people hired to stand still. She had been watching Olyne breathe and did not immediately understand what was wrong. For a moment she thought the hearing had been arranged after all.

Then Bastien said, from downstairs, “Whose authority?”

The answer was too quiet to hear.

Olyne lifted her head. Elena put a finger to her own mouth. The patient settled back, watching the doorway.

Kestra sat beside the table. She had been making a clean copy of the petitioner’s conditions, crossing out the phrases Olyne insisted on adding about her social position. She capped the ink and put the sheets into her case.

Benjamin had gone round the front to meet the water seller. Sariel was out. Thane had taken the laundry invitation to the other company’s workshop. Elena could hear none of them now. There was the canal slap under the shutters, and Bastien speaking again.

“She’ll come down if she chooses. You can wait here.”

Elena moved to the top of the stair.

The man nearest Bastien wore a carrier’s short coat with no wear at the shoulder. His hands were empty. Beyond him a second man held the folded authority away from a damp patch on the bench. Neither looked up until Elena spoke.

“Who sent for a chair?”

“Mistress Virelle. We are to take the young woman to the household.”

“She hasn’t asked to go.”

“Then ask her.”

He sounded reasonable. He had come prepared to sound reasonable in front of people, and there was something in the stillness of the second man that made Elena move one step down before she knew why.

Bastien shifted across the bottom of the stair. His left knee would not straighten completely. It had made him look drunk the first time Elena saw him walking, until he explained what a loaded barge could do when the quay moved beneath a man’s foot.

“Wait outside,” he said.

The first man reached past him.

Bastien caught his sleeve. The second man dropped the folded paper and came forward. Elena saw a blade low beside his thigh, almost hidden by the bench. She shouted. Bastien turned into the warning, raised his arm and was cut across the forearm.

The first man shoved him into the stair rail.

Elena came down the last three steps too fast. She caught a bowl off the wash shelf and flung its contents into the second man’s face. The greasy water would not stop him long. It stopped him long enough for Bastien to put a foot against the bench and drive it sideways into his knees.

Upstairs, Kestra slammed the room door.

Elena got an arm under Bastien’s good one and pulled him behind the worktable. Blood ran over his knuckles. He pressed the cut with his other palm and said something furious about the chair.

“Can you hold that?” she asked.

“I am holding it.”

She tore a strip from the cloth at her waist. He let her wind it, impatient with how little pressure she could put on while he kept moving.

The man in the carrier’s coat had started up the stair. Elena reached for the long boat hook beside the table. Bastien caught the shaft before she could lift it.

“There’s a pin.”

He pointed with his bloody fingers. Beneath the stair, a fitted crossbar held a rack of punting poles against the wall. Elena pulled the pin. The bar dropped, and the poles rolled across the bottom steps with a noise that filled the workshop. The man above them cursed and kicked one away. His companion had to climb over the rest to follow.

Bastien pushed Elena toward the narrow space at the wall.

“Go up. Use the gallery.”

“You too.”

“I know where it is.”

She waited until he put his foot on the first step. Then she went past him along the side, braced one hand against the wall and climbed, hearing the men work themselves free below.

At the room door she called Kestra’s name. The door opened just far enough to let her in. Olyne was out of bed, holding the back of the chair. Her face was gray around the mouth.

“The gallery,” Elena said.

“It’s open.” Kestra had the patient’s shoes in one hand. “I checked.”

Olyne looked at Bastien coming through the doorway. His sleeve was wet to the elbow.

“Where is the cloth?” she asked.

“On the table. Bring the roll.”

It was the first useful thing anyone had given her to do all afternoon. She took it and let Kestra put her own arm beneath the young woman’s shoulder. Elena snatched the little dressing wallet from the bedpost. The medicine chest stood under the window, its lid open, bottles nested in wool. She bent for it.

The door struck Bastien in the back.

He turned, drove his good shoulder against it and shouted at her to move.

Elena let go of the chest handle.

The rear gallery connected the upper floor to the neighboring store. Three ordinary steps crossed a gap between roofs. They were dry, narrow and painfully bright after the shuttered room. Kestra went first with Olyne. Elena followed with Bastien, whose bad leg had begun trembling.

At the store door he took the key from his belt. He missed the lock twice. Elena steadied his wrist and kept her other hand against the dressing. The cuff of her sleeve pressed into his blood. He got the key home on the third try.

They went inside among empty packing crates.

Behind them wood cracked. Elena did not look back. Bastien locked the store door, put the key in his pocket and shook his head when she reached for it.

“They’ll break it,” he said. “I still want my key.”

The store stair turned once before reaching the alley. Olyne could not take it standing. Elena made her sit and move down one step at a time while Kestra held the injured foot clear. It was slow. Every time Olyne lifted herself, her breath caught and she paused until it eased. Elena could hear the assault on the door above and the small sounds the patient was trying to swallow.

“Keep going,” Bastien said.

Elena looked at him. He was leaning against the crates, his good hand over the wound, allowing them room on the stair.

“I’ll come after you.”

She believed him because there was no better use for the next breath.

The alley door opened before they reached it.

Benjamin stood beyond, sword in hand. The water seller had abandoned his cart across the alley mouth. One wheel lay at an angle. A man in a short coat was retreating toward the canal, holding his empty hand against his ribs.

“Clear,” Benjamin said. “For now.”

He took Olyne beneath the knees and waited for Elena to support her back. Together they lifted her onto the cart. Kestra folded her coat beneath the injured foot. The patient stared at the sky and kept both hands around the cloth she had been told to carry.

Elena went back for Bastien.

He was halfway down, seated on a step, moving by lowering his bad leg with his good hand. The dressing had slipped. She put it back and sat beside him, fitting her shoulder under his arm.

“You could have waited for me,” she said.

“At the top?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

She almost laughed. Then someone hit the upstairs door hard enough to shake dust from the beam, and they went down together.

The woman who owned the bakehouse yard allowed them fifteen minutes and stayed beside the gate to make certain of it.

Elena worked on Bastien with a bowl of water balanced on an overturned bucket. The wound was ugly and bleeding freely, but his fingers moved and the hand remained warm. She could do what was needed to get him to a proper room. Afterward she would have to look again.

He watched her use the last of the clean cloth from her wallet.

“There’s more upstairs.”

“I know.”

“In the blue box.”

“I know where I left it.”

Bastien looked away. Elena bound the arm, tested the wrap and lowered his hand onto his knee.

“I’m sorry,” she said.

“You didn’t bring the knife.”

She wanted to tell him exactly what she had brought: a woman people wanted back, an argument he had no stake in, enough money for a room and not enough to pay for what had happened in it. He was staring across the yard toward his own canal, and she made herself stop.

Benjamin came back from the corner. Kestra had gone to find Sariel; the cart waited beside them with Olyne lying under a borrowed sack. One of the chair bearers had followed far enough to complain about his unpaid hire before realizing whom he was asking.

“We can take you with us,” Benjamin told Bastien.

“Where?”

“Out of the city if you want.”

Bastien shook his head.

“I’ve a boat being paid off and three people who owe me for work. The woman next door has a key to my cellar. If I go, she’ll put her barrels in it before breakfast.”

“They know your house.”

“It’s my house.” He looked at Elena. “She can’t come back to it.”

Elena nodded.

“I mean it. You’ll pay for the door. I’ll tell you what it cost when I know. But she can’t come back.”

“She won’t.”

His face relaxed, a little. He had been waiting for her to make refusal difficult.

He named a neighbor with whom he would spend the night. Benjamin went to fetch the man while Elena stayed. Before Bastien left, he asked her to check the arm tomorrow. She agreed to come without the patient. He gave her an impatient look.

“I assumed that much.”

Sariel found them as the bakehouse woman began reminding Elena about the fifteen minutes. The rooms Sariel had inspected on Netters’ Lane were still available. Belotte, who let them above the cooper’s store, wanted payment before anyone took a patient upstairs.

They paid for the night. The longer arrangement could be settled in the morning.

The stairs were broad enough for two people to support Olyne. Elena was so grateful for this small fact that she put her forehead against the doorpost after they had got her into bed. She stayed there until Sariel touched her back.

“Go and wash,” Sariel said. “I’ll sit with her.”

There was not enough water left in the basin for her sleeve. Elena cleaned her hands, spread the contents of her dressing wallet on a towel and looked at what remained. A few needles. Two folded pieces of cloth. The little jar she had taken to Ravenswood, nearly empty.

Sariel came back from the workshop with a dented tin and two unopened packets. The men had gone. Someone had emptied the medicine chest onto the floor while searching it; the bottles had broken beneath boots and the wash-water bucket. Sariel put what she had recovered beside the wallet. Elena opened one packet, found it wet through and closed it again.

Thane arrived with news that the invitation had been delivered and that the other company’s lifting tool might be made easier to hold. Elena listened until he mentioned their boat.

Then she put the wallet away and asked for the address of the workshop.

It was dark when Pello opened the door. Elena counted the people beyond him before entering. She had spent the afternoon discovering how much could happen in a familiar doorway.

“I need to know about your boat.”

“Which one?”

“The one you expect to carry her in.”

He looked at her cuff. “Is she worse?”

“This isn’t hers.”

Rosceline drew out a chair with her foot.

“Sit down before you inform us how inadequate we are.”

Elena sat. The water they gave her was cool enough to hurt her teeth. She drank the whole cup, then explained what the injured body could manage. She did not tell them which roof it now slept beneath.

Pello had a drawing of the canal steps. His boat was too small for what she required. He said so without trying to make the patient smaller, and she found herself listening more carefully to his next question.

“How flat?”

She showed him with her hands. Long enough to support the ribs and the injured leg; broad enough that the person carrying the foot would not have to grip the foot itself. They discussed the weight of a person being lowered, the angle of the steps and what happened when the tide gave them another one to cross.

When she left, she had the beginnings of a transport plan and no promise that the right boat had been hired. Outside she stood in the lane and loosened the cuff where it had dried against her skin.

Then she walked back to Netters’ Lane with the workshop’s clean water still cold in her stomach.

Chapter 10 — The names offered back

Sel had chosen a bookbinder’s shop beside the little bridge. Its owner was still working in the back. Sariel could hear him tapping the folded sections into line, an irregular dry sound that stopped whenever he listened to the room in front.

The invitation had reached her in the afternoon, before she knew about Bastien. A boy outside the guild office had given it to her. She had put it away intending to consult the others. By the time she found them, Elena’s sleeve was dark and the patient’s bedding lay on a hired cart.

She had told Benjamin where she was going. He waited across the canal, close enough to see the door.

Sel sat beside the counter. She wore the same restrained black as at the supper and had taken off her gloves. In front of her lay a leather folio with a brass clasp. One corner had worn pale where the cover folded.

“Your carrier was hurt,” Sariel said.

“I employed men to bring a frightened performer back to her company.”

“You employed men with a knife.”

Sel looked past her toward the door. “I gave instructions that she was not to be injured.”

“Bastien was.”

“I can pay for his treatment.”

Sariel remained standing. She wanted Sel to try her hand, or her shoulder, some little possessive touch she could answer with a reason the bookbinder would understand. Sel kept both hands on the folio.

“Sit,” she said. “You came because you wanted to see these.”

Sariel pulled the chair away from the counter before taking it. Sel noticed the changed distance. She opened the brass clasp.

The top sheet was old. Sariel could see where a binding thread had passed through its edge and been cut. A narrow attachment carried the names of the surviving company, with a number beside each and an office mark stamped partly across the joint.

Her own name stood third.

For a moment she could not make herself read the rest. The small dark letters had the obstinate familiarity of a door she had used daily and then found unexpectedly in another town.

Sel turned the sheet. Beneath it were the payments that should have accompanied the old entries. The stamps were clear. The final payment had been acknowledged before the exclusion for unpaid assessments was entered.

“May I?” Sariel asked.

Sel slid the pages closer.

Sariel examined the edges and the binding holes. The paper was of the right age. The damaged corner of one stamp matched a mark she had seen on other documents when the office still expected her to use it. A clerk had put the month in the wrong place, corrected it and initialed the correction. She remembered Kestra complaining about that clerk’s forms long before they had reason to want them back.

“Where were these?”

“Attached to the company’s entry. The exclusions were carried forward against the individual names. No one brought the two together.”

“No one chose to.”

“I won’t argue the distinction.”

“You could have given them to Kestra this morning.”

“Yes.”

Sariel looked up.

The newer papers were introductions. Two serving officials had put their names to undertakings to sponsor a review. One dealt with admissions. The other could have the old entries presented without requiring the petitioners first to prove the standing they were trying to recover. There were limits in the wording. Sariel read those twice.

“This doesn’t reinstate us.”

“No.”

“It doesn’t return the building.”

“The building has a lawful tenant.”

Sariel disliked hearing Sel say what she had learned in Hanne’s workshop.

“It doesn’t say who burned it.”

“It concerns commercial entries, Sariel. I have not offered you a finding against the Countess.”

The binder tapped his folded sections again. Sariel could smell paste warming at the back of the shop.

“And Sera’s guild?”

“Keeps its own standing. These are your names. Yours and the four people working with you.”

Sariel spread the sheets without lifting them from the folio. Kestra’s entry had a little stain beneath its ruled line. Thane’s was cramped at the end, the last letter pushed against the margin. Whoever had written them had been bored that morning, or hungry, and had wanted to finish. Sariel could have paid such a person now without thinking much about the amount. She had spent the morning unable to make his successor listen.

“What do you want?”

“Take the performer out of Silkstrand. You already have work elsewhere. Take her somewhere she can be cared for, and stop intervening in the entertainment.”

“You know who she is.”

Sel’s fingers rested on the clasp. “I know what you believe.”

Sariel pushed the pages back far enough that Sel had to look down to stop them slipping.

“Say it while we can both hear you.”

Sel aligned the attachment with the larger sheet.

“Take Olyne, in the body she now occupies, out of the city. Withdraw the petition and return the fitted face. You will receive these papers and the introductions. The review will have fees. Those have been arranged.”

“And Zélie stays where she is.”

“She will be provided for.”

“Until she signs something badly.”

Sel waited for Sariel to continue. When she did not, Sel said, “Both women are frightened. You cannot remedy every decision they regret by making this a public spectacle.”

“You can bring them to the frame.”

“I can bring people to the frame when they will cooperate with what follows.”

Sariel leaned back. The chair’s lower rail pressed against her ankle. She moved her foot and found herself thinking of Olyne’s bare heel resting on Elena’s rolled coat.

“Will you put the return before our departure?”

“No.”

“Then we aren’t discussing the same work.”

“You could be discussing the next twenty commissions. Some here. Some with introductions that travel farther than a letter from a village. Your craftsman could lease a bench without someone else putting his name on the agreement.”

Sariel said nothing.

“You don’t all have to live here,” Sel continued. “People can be allowed into a city without being required to make it their home.”

Sariel wanted to hear the rest. That angered her more than the simpler lies. She had seen the two signatures. She could find out whether the officials meant what they had signed. She could stand before a hiring desk with her own papers and let the clerk discover how little opinion he was entitled to have.

She picked up the old attachment once more. Sel allowed it. The paper was lighter than she expected, the ink faint where too much daylight had reached its upper edge.

“These belong in the office.”

“They can go back there with your names.”

“Let me take them to my companions.”

“Tell them what you’ve seen.”

There would be no copying. Sel said it without apology. Sariel put the attachment back, taking care over its weakened corner.

“I need to talk to them.”

“Of course.”

Sel closed the folio. The clasp made a small, firm sound.

Sariel stood. She had not given the answer she had expected to enjoy giving when she entered.

At the door Sel asked, “Does the woman employing you make better use of her power?”

Sariel turned with her hand on the latch.

“You can put that question to her when she’s able to answer in her own face.”

She crossed the bridge to Benjamin. He took one look at her and fell into step without asking until they had left the shop behind.

Thane was trying to get a splinter out of Kestra’s hand when Sariel returned. They sat by Belotte’s lamp, the bowl of clean water between them. Kestra was correcting his account of where the splinter went. He had stopped listening several minutes earlier and was proceeding with the work.

Elena came in soon afterward, carrying her damp cuff away from her wrist. She told them about the workshop before anyone asked her to listen to anything else. Pello understood the carrying problem. He needed another boat. Nothing had been hired yet.

Sariel moved a chair for her. They had a few minutes while the patient slept in the other room.

She described every paper she could remember. Kestra asked about the attachment, then the stamps. Thane asked who had signed the new introductions. Benjamin wanted the order of delivery: papers before departure, or once Sel was certain they were gone.

“Before,” Sariel said. “The introductions would be used afterward.”

“And we take Olyne by force?”

“She didn’t call it that.”

“I am.”

Kestra drew her hand away from Thane. He caught her wrist gently and turned it back toward the light.

“I’ve nearly got it.”

She waited. When the splinter came free, she looked at the tiny dark thing on his nail as though surprised it could have occupied so much of her attention.

“We paid those assessments,” she said.

“I know.”

“I took them. There were questions afterward. Not from you. From people who’d heard we’d left money unpaid and thought perhaps that was why they hadn’t helped.”

Sariel had not known about those questions. Kestra washed her palm in the bowl and dried it carefully.

Thane asked whether they could obtain the papers without the bargain.

“I asked. She kept them.”

“Buy them?”

“With what?” Benjamin said.

“I’m asking whether she gave a figure.”

“No,” Sariel said. “She wants the performance left alone.”

Thane sat back. “I would like my name cleared. I don’t care for her knowing how much.”

“Neither do I.”

The answer came out roughly. Sariel looked at the lamp until the bright circle spoiled her view of the table.

Elena put her empty wallet beside it.

“Olyne can’t travel far tomorrow. We could wait until she could. She would still ask where we were taking her, and why.”

“She might prefer being alive somewhere else,” Benjamin said.

“She might. Then she can choose it. But that’s not what she hired us to do.”

Sariel looked toward the closed inner door. Their client had shouted about the stairs, complained about the bed and then clung to Elena’s sleeve while they got her onto it. Sariel had wanted to leave the room for a while and have none of the woman asked of her.

“I thought about it,” she said. “Taking her somewhere quiet. Letting this finish here. Getting our own business heard.”

No one hurried to tell her she hadn’t meant it.

“Could we ever bring her back?” Kestra asked.

Sariel shook her head. “I don’t know.”

Elena rubbed the mark the dried cuff had left on her wrist.

“Zélie wanted to come with me. She wouldn’t because I couldn’t tell her what Olyne would do afterward. I can’t ask her to trust us if we’re agreeing she stays there.”

Benjamin leaned forward, elbows on knees. “I want those papers as well. But if we take the woman because Sel says so, I’m the one carrying her down the stairs. She’ll ask me to put her down.”

Thane reached for his tool roll and stopped before touching it.

“We can ask for the papers again after they’re returned.”

“She won’t give them to us then,” Sariel said.

He held her gaze. “Probably not.”

They let the lamp burn for a while. From below came the hollow sound of the cooper setting hoops onto a barrel. Someone dropped one, and it rolled in diminishing circles until he stopped it with his foot.

Kestra asked Sariel to describe the old attachment once more. Sariel did. She could remember where every name stood, but not the number beside her own. She had read it several times and could no longer bring it back.

At last Elena rose to check the patient. Thane gathered the little tools he had used for Kestra’s hand. Benjamin took the bowl away.

Sariel remained by the table. Kestra stayed with her.

“Was mine legible?” Kestra asked.

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“All of it.”

Kestra nodded. She put her hand beside Sariel’s, palm down, the small dressed puncture hidden against the wood.

They sat until Elena came back and asked which of them intended to take the first watch.

Part III — The people kept out

Chapter 11 — One company’s money

Elena put the chipped bottle on the apothecary’s counter and waited while he looked at it.

He lifted it toward the window. A little of yesterday’s medicine clung to the bottom, brown against green glass. There was a print of her thumb on its shoulder. She had carried it out of Bastien’s workshop in the pocket where she ordinarily kept a clean handkerchief.

“I can fill it,” he said. “I can’t mend it.”

“The chip is above the shoulder.”

“It will run when you put the stopper in.”

She knew that. She had hoped he had a stopper which would require less pressure, or a cheaper bottle, or some useful fact which had not occurred to her between the cooper’s yard and his shop. He placed a sound bottle beside the broken one. The difference between them was small and payable.

Behind her, Benjamin took a step sideways to let a woman with a basket reach the counter. The basket smelled of bruised mint. The woman wanted something for her husband’s stomach, then explained several reasons why her husband deserved the stomach he had. Elena listened to the apothecary ask what he had actually eaten.

When the woman went, Elena laid out the rest of her purchases. Clean wrapping cloth. A packet of bark. Needles in a waxed fold. Enough of the thicker salve to cover ordinary trouble without purchasing the great jar from which it was measured. She had lost her second small basin at Bastien’s; Belotte would lend one provided it came back without blood dried under the rim.

The apothecary began to wrap the goods.

“Leave the sleeping draught,” Elena said.

He stopped. “You asked for it.”

“I have changed my mind.”

Benjamin’s reflection stood broad and uncertain in the shop glass. He had learned not to interrupt her purchases. That did not prevent his face from asking questions.

Outside, he took the parcel before she could fit it beneath her arm.

“Did we not need that one?”

“We might. We need these now.”

“I have money.”

“So have I.”

She showed him what remained in her hand, then closed her fingers over it. He looked away first. Across the lane a woman was shaking a piece of blue cloth from an upper window. Dust came down in the sunlight and settled on the shoulders of people who could not see her.

Elena had put aside the eighteen marks for a coat. Nothing elaborate. Wool that did not let the weather through at the elbows, and sleeves made to her own length instead of turned up until the cuff became a shelf for everything she touched. She had seen the cloth in Millhaven. The draper had agreed to keep a piece until their return.

She took off her old coat as they walked and inspected the stained cuff. Cold water had lightened Bastien’s blood without removing it.

“I am being repaid,” she said.

“I hadn’t suggested otherwise.”

“You were arranging your face.”

He considered this. “I’ll carry it behind the parcel.”

She laughed once and pushed his elbow toward the corner before a handcart could take it off.

At the cooper’s store, Belotte was counting empty hoops. She did it by touch, lifting each iron circle and setting it in the next stack, without looking down from the conversation she was having with Sariel.

“Seven nights,” Belotte said. “From today. The rooms back on the morning after the seventh. If you stay through dinner, you pay another day.”

Sariel looked at Elena. Elena nodded.

“And no tradesmen at the upper door after dark.”

“We may need a physician.”

Belotte moved another hoop. “Physicians can knock at my door like anyone else. I’m not having a man wandering among the stock saying he is a physician.”

She had already agreed to the sick woman. She wanted to know who would empty the slops, where the linen would be washed, and whether the tall man was likely to put his foot through the landing. Benjamin looked up at it.

“It held me last night.”

“It held six casks before you. I’m asking about your habits.”

Sariel asked her to hold the weekly price until they had finished their accounts. Belotte returned the upper key and told them which shutter had to be lifted before it would shut. Elena followed the others upstairs, grateful for a woman who could distinguish a dangerous tenant from a dangerous shutter.

Olyne slept in the inner room. The young face had relaxed enough to lose some of its pinched appearance, though the scratch beneath her jaw remained red. Her injured ankle rested on a folded blanket. Elena checked the warmth of her foot and drew the curtain across the morning light.

In the other room Thane had found a level place on the table by moving it half a hand’s breadth toward the window. Kestra was copying a list onto the back of a freight notice. Beside her stood a pot of lentils which had gone cold while somebody waited for somebody else.

“I told you to eat,” Elena said.

“We did,” Thane said.

She lifted the lid. Someone had eaten a considerable portion. That pleased her more than obedience generally did.

Benjamin put the parcel on the table. Sariel came in behind him carrying the bread and a twist of salt. She shut the door quietly, then checked the latch with the side of her thumb.

Elena unwrapped the purchases one by one. The needle packet caught on the paper, scattered its contents and made her swear. Thane caught the nearest needle before it reached the edge. He laid it on a piece of dark cloth where she could see it.

“Eighteen marks,” she said. “Mine. Write it down before any of you start calling it something else.”

Kestra found a clear space on her working sheet.

“Supplies for the company,” Elena added. “To be returned when Olyne pays. If she does not pay, we discuss it then.”

Sariel nodded. She had the small purse open before her, but her hands rested on either side of it.

They had accepted six hundred marks including expenses. The hundred and fifty advanced by Merrit had paid for more of Silkstrand than Elena would have chosen to see. There were fittings and journeys, a lost room, clean cloth left behind a door she could no longer safely open. The remaining four hundred and fifty depended on a woman getting back into a body which could command her own money.

“We can keep this lodging,” Sariel said. “We can eat. We cannot keep replacing everything every two days.”

“The week costs less than four separate nights,” Kestra said. “Even if we leave early.”

“I’m not moving her tonight,” Elena said.

Benjamin and Thane agreed to the week. Sariel set the rent apart, then looked around the table. “We still decide when we’re going. Paying for the room doesn’t decide for us.”

Elena nodded. She would take Belotte the money after they had eaten.

“Bastien says we aren’t to try,” Elena said.

“How is he?” Kestra asked.

“Angry. The cut is clean. He says his left knee has made sufficient arrangements with the city that he refuses to start again elsewhere.”

Benjamin nodded as if this were a sound commercial reason. Elena was glad he did not offer to bring Bastien away by force.

Thane turned the needle packet so its folded end faced Elena. There was a paper beneath his tool roll. He had taken it out twice since she came in and put it away without unfolding it.

“Show us,” she said.

He looked up.

“Whatever you’re using to prop up your tools.”

He drew the paper free. The roll settled with a small clatter.

“There’s a bench available,” he said. “Near the east repair yards. One of Linett’s contacts put me onto it. The owner lets space to mechanics between contracts. There’s a power shaft, and a lathe I could hire by the day.”

Elena waited. Benjamin stopped tearing the bread.

“For how long?” Sariel asked.

“A month to begin. Longer if we get on.”

“Will he take our name?”

“He will take mine. He suggests I register a different concern for the work.”

“Does he know why?” Benjamin asked.

“Yes.”

“And he calls that a suggestion.”

Thane smoothed the paper with his left hand. The two restricted fingers of his right lay against its edge without quite straightening.

“He is putting his own lease at risk. I don’t need to like his condition to understand it.”

“What work?” Elena asked.

That brought him nearer the table. He described catches for warehouse shutters, then a foot treadle someone wanted adapted for a man who could no longer stand through a full working day. The arrangement would put his bench near two people who cut fine gearing. He had seen their work in a repaired hoist. He took a crust and drew the shape of a joint in the crumbs, failed to get the angle he wanted, and fetched his pencil.

Elena watched him become impatient with the smallness of the paper. He still loved an opportunity to make something fit. The feeling survived poor light, untrustworthy employers and people telling him how remarkable his left hand was whenever they saw him pick up a tool.

“The work at Millhaven?” she asked.

“Waiting. The shutter fittings, certainly. They can get someone else for the press if I send word soon.”

“Do you want someone else to do it?”

“No. I want to do both.”

Benjamin sat back. “Then say that.”

“I thought I had.”

“You told us about the bench.”

Thane looked at him for a long moment. “I want to work there. I don’t yet know whether I want to stay here.”

Outside, the cooper struck a hoop into place. Four blows, a pause, two lighter taps. Elena could hear where the fit had improved. Thane would hear more.

Sariel picked a loose thread from the table’s edge.

“If you take it,” she said, “is it yours, or do all five of us have to become somebody else?”

“Mine.”

“Then it is your decision.”

Benjamin’s jaw tightened. Thane saw it.

“You can say what you think.”

“I think I don’t like this city doing it again.”

“Giving me a bench?”

“Making you grateful for space with someone else’s name over the door.”

Thane put down the pencil. “I rent space in Millhaven.”

“I know.”

“You’ve helped me carry things into it.”

“I know that too.” Benjamin rubbed his forehead. “I want you to be able to take it without all of us standing around looking hurt.”

For a little while nobody found anything useful to say.

Elena reached for the bread. The loaf had a hard black patch underneath where it had sat too close to the oven wall. She cut it away and gave Benjamin the sound half. He accepted it without noticing.

“The man can wait until after tomorrow,” she said.

“He can wait several days.”

“Then tell him you’re interested. Ask if he would hire you the bench for shorter visits later. You’ve made arrangements across a worse distance.”

Thane looked down at the little drawing. “I could ask.”

He folded the offer and put it in his own pocket, away from the company papers.

Olyne called from the inner room. Elena went to her with water. The woman had woken sharply and was trying to sit without putting weight through the injured side.

“My correspondence,” she said.

“Here.”

Elena gave her the folded sheet from Merrit. Olyne turned it over, notched its lower corner with her thumbnail and winced when the movement pulled at her ribs.

“I want a physician at Ravenswood,” Elena said. “Or one ready to receive you afterward.”

“You are treating me.”

“I can keep doing what I have done. I can’t promise what neither of us has needed yet.”

“I am already paying your company.”

“For this work. Your care afterward will require its own arrangements.”

Olyne’s mouth set in the expression Elena had learned to recognize even on the wrong face.

“What if I cannot get to my money?”

“Then Merrit will have to decide what he can advance you. I will tell him what is needed. I won’t promise his answer, either.”

The young hands tightened on the paper. Elena lifted the cup and waited. After a moment Olyne drank.

“Bring him here when he can come,” she said.

Elena adjusted the blanket beneath the foot.

When she returned, the others had warmed the lentils on Belotte’s small stove. Benjamin was holding the pot with a folded rag. Kestra had put the medicine away from the food. Sariel poured Elena a cup and sat beside her without pretending there was more room than there was.

Thane pushed the needle packet toward her.

“You missed one.”

“Where?”

He pointed at her coat. It had caught in the stained cuff, bright and straight across the dark cloth.

Elena pulled it free. For once, the cuff had earned its keep.

Chapter 12 — The laundry

Kestra discovered the missing stool before the Irregulars arrived.

There were five. She had counted them while Benjamin carried them in, and then been occupied by the floor, which sloped toward a drain that no longer accepted water. Five people on one side, five expected on the other. The laundry owner had said they could use what was there. What was there consisted of washing troughs and a trestle with one leg shorter than the others.

“We could fetch more,” Benjamin said.

“And leave someone alone here.”

“Two of us.”

She looked at the light above the high window. They had already spent too long deciding which entrance could be seen from the bridge. Sariel was outside checking the rear alley. Thane had settled at the folding table, making a correction to his drawing. Elena was rubbing the table clean with a damp rag because anything put down on it came away powdered with old soap.

Kestra turned the nearest stool toward the door.

“Let them sit on what we sit on.”

Benjamin glanced at the five stools. She knew what he could have said. He went to check the last door instead.

She took out the freight notice on which she had drawn the frame. Its other side advertised passage for machine parts packed against damp. There was a crude little vessel at the bottom, doing implausibly well in heavy seas. She put the drawing face upward.

They had made their arrangements without putting the new lodging on paper. Olyne had a watcher until Elena returned. Merrit knew where to send a physician. The blue mask lay wrapped in Sariel’s bag. Every separate precaution seemed small beside the fact that they were coming voluntarily into a room with the Countess’s people.

The first through the door was Pello. He went around all three exits, testing catches with the ease of a man who disliked finding them interesting later. Benjamin followed him. Neither objected to the other’s work.

Rosceline saw the stools.

“A generous arrangement,” she said.

Sariel moved two to the far side. Benjamin offered his to Ilaro and settled on the stone lip of a washing trough. Daska sat beside him. The stone held them, as it had held a great many loads before anybody had hired it for suspicion.

Kestra remained standing. She could see the whole table from there.

Sariel unwrapped the edge of the blue face. Rosceline wanted tea; the water was off. The familiar shape of an argument began to settle over the room, and Kestra found herself impatient for the first wound. She wanted to say something that would take Rosceline’s ease away. Not merely contradict her. Make her discover what it was to come to a city carrying work she knew she could do and be treated as an inconvenience someone had survived.

Briska opened a drawing and bent over it.

“We need to establish the move downstairs,” she said. “Does it take the return with it?”

Kestra showed her the feet and the boards joined to them. She had been able to see underneath while other people were trying to put blades through one another. The moving room mattered less than the marks carried with the frame. When Briska asked how she knew, Kestra told her where she had looked.

“You were having the fight as well,” Briska said.

“I was looking under it.”

Thane supplied the carpenter’s order. The carrying bars fastened to the whole base. Nothing in the agreed move required breaking the painted line.

Briska leaned closer. Kestra disliked the relief she felt. She had wanted these people less useful.

Then Ilaro asked about the women, and relief went.

They told him enough. The rooms, the hands that had opened doors, Bastien’s workshop: those remained outside the account. Elena described what the young body could bear. Rosceline repeated Zélie’s account of the afternoon she had accepted. The paid agreement. What she had been told about Olyne’s willingness. Her fear of arrest.

Elena’s mouth tightened. She and Rosceline exchanged several quiet sentences. Kestra kept her attention on Ilaro, who was writing slowly enough that she could see him choose which words needed to survive the meeting.

“She has not actually returned a pair herself,” he said when they came to Zélie’s knowledge of the instrument.

“She has practiced the operation with the face covered,” Kestra answered. “She has watched Vaudrin use it. That is more than anyone else we have.”

“Then we should say exactly that.”

She wanted to ask whether the Countess paid him for such scruples by the hour. He had used none of his own discoveries to get Olyne out of Bastien’s doorway. He had slept somewhere while Elena washed another man’s blood from her hands.

She knew nothing about where he had slept.

If they went into the gala believing Zélie had already returned living people, they would expect confidence she had not earned and punish her for its absence.

Kestra pressed her lips together and nodded.

Ilaro altered his note. He did not look pleased with himself. She found that harder to bear than triumph would have been.

Rosceline proposed bringing the women together, making the return and taking the central face to her employer. Sariel refused.

The room changed without anyone moving. Elena folded the rag she held. Benjamin lowered the cup he had been turning in his hands.

Kestra had considered this argument in a dozen forms since Sariel brought Sel’s offer back. They could secure the instrument. They could make its use conditional. They could ask somebody respected to hold a key. Every version brought her to a door with one person outside it and another person deciding whether to let her through.

Rosceline spoke of custody. Sariel asked whose house. At last the name was said.

“And who guards her?” Sariel asked.

Kestra watched Rosceline choose her answer. It was careful, accomplished, nearly persuasive until it reached the woman whose roof had burned above them.

“You know what she did to you.”

Sariel’s hand stopped on the mask cloth.

“I know what she did to Hest,” she said. “And Orren. And the people who were still inside when the roof went.”

Benjamin put down the cup.

Kestra could remember Hest correcting a spring held too long against a file. She had no use for the memory just then, yet it came with its own light and the exact position of a shoulder. Sariel spoke of apprentices who had apprentices. She spoke of Orren making them carry properly, of a safe placed where a person would kneel, of heat entering a room arranged for them.

The laundry drain gurgled behind the wall.

“I am not asking you to forgive her,” Rosceline said.

“You’re asking us to leave her this.” Sariel touched the cloth. “The next time she wants someone to say yes, she won’t need to put his daughter in a room first.”

Kestra could feel the edge of the freight notice beneath her fingernail. It had begun to split. She moved her hand before she tore through the drawing.

Kes’s daughter was grown. Kestra had no right to tell the room what the woman’s life had become. She wanted no one here to discuss her as though she were still a frightened child kept somewhere useful to Serafine.

Rosceline had gone silent.

There. Kestra had wanted that silence. She let herself look at it.

It did not give her the pleasure she had expected. The woman across the table still had her own five people, her own reasons to want wages paid. Making her hurt had not settled who would carry Olyne around the stair.

Benjamin lifted the jug.

“Water?”

People accepted. Cups moved along the table. Kestra took hers with both hands and drank too quickly. The cold caught a tooth she had been meaning to have looked at since before they left Millhaven.

Beside her, Thane found an error in the clearance on Briska’s drawing. Briska gave him her pencil. Neither softened the correction into a compliment. Elena and Pello bent over the corridor plan, moving the treatment place clear of the turn. Kestra added the alcove to her own sheet.

Rosceline spoke again when she was ready. They could not agree over the object. They could agree that neither woman would be taken away in the other’s body, that the fitted faces would survive until the return, that no one would leave with the central face and make the others’ efforts useless.

Sariel looked at them. Kestra nodded.

They wrote two copies. Kestra checked the words against the doors they actually meant. Ilaro caught an ambiguity about which face each company would bring. She changed it. He sanded the ink and waited until she had finished her own copy before he folded his.

The patience irritated her less than it had at the beginning.

Outside, the lane smelled of boiled cloth. Thane carried three stools, the legs nested against his good forearm; Benjamin carried the other two and the empty jug. Sariel had the blue face. They walked far enough for the laundry’s doorway to disappear behind a stack of bundled reeds before anyone spoke about the meeting.

“She hasn’t agreed to break it,” Benjamin said.

“No,” Sariel answered.

“Will she keep the part she did agree to?” Elena asked.

Thane changed the stools’ position. “Briska will want the people clear of the thing before she moves it. That much I believe.”

“She isn’t the one giving orders.”

“She can put a tool down.”

Kestra looked at the copy in her hand. One corner was damp from the cup.

“I think Rosceline means to keep the women here for the return,” she said. “I think she also expects us to become more reasonable afterward.”

Benjamin gave a short, tired laugh.

Sariel stopped at the cooper’s corner. “Did you tell Ilaro less because he is hers?”

Kestra’s first answer was ready too quickly. She did not give it.

“I wanted to.”

Sariel waited.

“I gave him the operation as we know it. He was right to correct what we were assuming.”

The street narrowed around a wagon. Benjamin stepped ahead with the stools, making room for Elena. Sariel stayed beside Kestra until they could follow.

“I wanted her frightened,” Kestra said.

“I know.”

“I’m not asking you to tell me I was right.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

They walked around the wagon together. At the door, Kestra folded the practical agreement again and put it where she could find it quickly.

Chapter 13 — The woman who will not attend

The maid at Estrenne’s front door asked for a card.

Sariel gave her a folded paper with her name on it. The maid looked at the name, then at Benjamin, then behind them to see whether a carriage would improve the introduction.

“It concerns tomorrow’s entertainment,” Sariel said.

“Madam is resting.”

“Then her attendant.”

The maid hesitated. That was promising. She had a particular person in mind who would resent being kept ignorant of callers. She took the paper and left them beneath an awning whose rain chains ended in two very clean stone bowls.

Benjamin looked at the bowls.

“Don’t,” Sariel said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You always wash your hands when someone puts water near you.”

“Not always.”

The door opened before they could enumerate exceptions. A woman in a plain gray gown came out and shut it behind her. She had dark hair pinned close to her head and the tired, direct look of someone who had spent a day answering questions intended for another person.

“I’m Riette. What has happened?”

Sariel glanced at the door.

“You can tell me here,” the woman said. “Or you can tell everybody inside. They’ll be listening.”

“Your employer should not wear her fitted mask at Ravenswood.”

Riette’s eyes moved to the bag beneath Sariel’s arm.

“She said it hurt her.”

“It may do more than that.”

“Who sent you?”

“No one from Ravenswood.”

“That’s what I meant.”

Sariel could not offer an affiliation which would make the warning easier to hear. She gave the company’s name, then Olyne’s. Riette listened without interruption. When Sariel said that the woman at Ravenswood was inhabiting a body she had been promised for an afternoon, Riette put her hand on the door latch and did not open it.

“Wait here.”

She went inside. The maid came back and stood in the opening. Benjamin moved one foot to keep the door from closing on Sariel’s shoulder. He gave the maid a courteous nod. She decided not to mention it.

They were admitted through a passage with painted hunting panels. Several of the painted dogs had human expressions Sariel disliked. At the far end, Estrenne sat beside an open window with a folded shawl on her lap. Her traveling shoes stood beneath the chair. Somebody had put them there instead of the soft slippers which sat out of reach beside a chest.

“You have been at the house,” Estrenne said.

“Yes.”

“Are you one of Serafine’s?”

“No.”

The answer came out hard. Estrenne looked more closely at her.

“I was given a warning,” she said. “It was short of an explanation.”

“I can supply the part I’ve seen.”

Sariel put the wrapped blue face on the table. She did not hand it over. Estrenne leaned forward, then stopped before touching the painted cheek.

“That is Olyne’s.”

“It fits her body. The woman in that body has another name.”

She described the injured younger woman, her claim, what Merrit had recognized and what could still be doubted. She told Estrenne what she had seen beneath the glass. She left out the way they had escaped, the names of safe doors, and the offer in the leather folio. Benjamin stood by the window, keeping the path to it clear without pretending it could serve as a comfortable exit.

Estrenne asked practical questions. Could Sariel show her Olyne? Not safely today. Had a guild heard the claim? It had declined to reach it. Was the blue face sufficient to prove that such a thing could be done? No.

“I don’t require a judgment,” Sariel said. “You can decline to stand in a machine without proving what it will do to you.”

Riette let out a small breath beside the door.

Estrenne looked down at the traveling shoes. “I declined this morning.”

“The chair has been prepared,” said a man in the doorway.

He was well dressed in the unremarkable way of someone whose clothing was always ready when he wanted it. Sariel recognized the family likeness about his eyes. His attention went first to Benjamin, then to the mask on the table.

“Who are these people?”

“Callers,” Estrenne said.

“You were not receiving.”

“I am receiving them.”

He came into the room. Riette stayed where she was until he looked pointedly at her. Then she stepped sideways rather than leaving.

“The arrangement has been made,” he told his mother. “You have agreed to take a patron’s part. Father’s partners are attending. There is a supper in your honor.”

“Then they will have enough to eat without me.”

“Mother.”

Sariel began to wrap the mask. She could hear how much work had already been done with that one word.

Estrenne put her feet on the floor. “Send for my carriage.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Now.”

He glanced at Riette. “She has been upsetting herself. You know what the physician said about excitement.”

Riette’s face closed. “The physician said she needed to sleep.”

“Which is why strangers should not be brought into her room.”

Sariel fastened the bag. “We can wait in the street for her.”

“No one is going anywhere.”

Benjamin left the window.

The son saw the movement and changed his tone. “Until I understand what is being proposed.”

Sariel gave Estrenne room to answer.

“I intend to sleep elsewhere,” she said. “I intend not to attend Ravenswood. Those are two things, neither especially complicated.”

“You cannot take household servants with you on an impulse.”

Riette went to the small desk near the hearth. She took out a sheet which had been folded twice, then opened it so the son could see its contents.

“He wanted my name on this,” she told Estrenne.

“I wanted you to witness her agreement.”

“It says she wishes to attend. She told me she did not.”

“It records the agreement already given.”

“It has today’s date.”

Estrenne held out her hand. Riette gave her the paper. The son reached for it and found Benjamin between himself and the chair. Benjamin had not touched his sword.

“My wages are with the steward,” Riette said. “He says I receive them when I finish the engagement. He says leaving before the gala would be abandoning you.”

“I did not authorize that.”

“He told me you had.”

Sariel watched the son decide which denial would cost him least. Before he chose, Estrenne folded the paper and put it inside her gown.

“Bring your own things, Riette.”

“Mother, you cannot—”

“Ask the steward for my small case. The one with the brass corners.”

Riette did not move immediately.

“I will help you leave,” she said. “I am not agreeing to come back here afterward.”

Estrenne looked at her. Sariel could see that the distinction was new and unwelcome.

“Very well.”

The son walked out. His footsteps hurried after he passed the door. Benjamin looked at Sariel.

“Go with her,” she told him, meaning Riette. “Only what she says belongs to her.”

They left together. Estrenne bent to pick up her shoe and could not reach it without putting an elbow painfully against the chair arm. Sariel knelt and gave it to her.

“You have done this before,” Estrenne said.

“Shoes?”

“Removed someone.”

Sariel held the other shoe ready. “People are generally heavier than they expect.”

Estrenne almost smiled. Then voices sounded in the passage, and she held out her foot.

They found the front door bolted and the household porter holding the key.

The son stood beside him. An older man with the same eyes had joined him from the stairs. Both looked less sure of their dignity with Benjamin carrying Riette’s small trunk between them.

Sariel had not opened a lock. She considered whether that would remain possible.

Estrenne asked for the key.

“The carriage has gone,” her younger son said.

“There are others.”

“Madam,” the porter began.

“You know who pays you,” Estrenne said.

The man’s gaze moved toward her sons. She saw it and flushed. Sariel kept her hand away from her tools. Picking the lock would take time; asking the porter to step aside gave him a chance to become more firmly employed against them.

“Benjamin,” she said. “Put the trunk down.”

He lowered it to the tiles. Riette caught his sleeve before he let go.

“I can carry that.”

“I know. I’m going to need both hands.”

He turned toward the service passage. The porter moved to stop him. Sariel stepped into the space the man left and put her hand on the bolt. It was an ordinary good lock, fitted badly to a door which had swollen at the lower hinge. If the door took weight, the bolt would bind.

She leaned against it.

Estrenne’s older son caught her wrist. She let the pressure turn her until his forearm lay across the door handle, then folded his fingers back around it. He made a surprised sound. She did not have to break anything. Benjamin had taken the porter by both upper arms and moved him far enough from the lock to make the key available.

“Give it to her,” Benjamin said.

The younger son came at his back. Riette put the trunk in his way. He struck it with his shin, went sideways into the wall and grabbed at the lid to keep himself upright. One hinge tore from the wood.

Something small broke inside.

Riette’s face went white. She pulled the trunk away and set it upright with a care which frightened Sariel more than a shout.

“Leave it,” she told the man.

Estrenne took the key from the porter. He allowed her. Her hands shook sufficiently that Sariel had to release the older son and help her find the keyhole.

The man rubbed his fingers. “I will have you charged.”

“Open it,” Estrenne said to Sariel.

She did. The door stuck against the pressure she had placed on it; she eased it back, lifted the handle and drew it inward. Evening light reached across the tiles. A carter passing with sacks looked straight into the house.

The younger son stopped advancing.

Estrenne stepped out. Riette followed with the damaged trunk. Benjamin brought the brass-cornered case which had somehow acquired the weight of a household during their passage downstairs. Sariel stayed in the doorway until all three were on the pavement.

“You are being manipulated,” the older son told his mother.

She turned.

“Send my correspondence to me when I ask for it.”

They walked to the hired-carriage stand at the corner. Estrenne could manage the distance, but she put her hand on Benjamin’s arm before they reached it. He slowed without drawing attention to the change.

Riette carried her trunk in both hands. The lid had opened enough to show a cracked looking glass wrapped in a stocking. Sariel offered to take it. Riette shook her head.

“It was mine,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I know what a replacement costs.”

“I didn’t mean to tell you it doesn’t matter.”

The woman looked at her, then gave her one end of the trunk.

They rode to an inn Estrenne chose, a quiet place with a night porter and rooms opening onto an inner court. She had enough in her small case to pay. Sariel watched the innkeeper recognize her, accept her instruction that no room number was to be given to callers, and take money for doing it. She did not mistake recognition for a guarantee. Benjamin checked the court and the street door while Sariel went upstairs.

Estrenne asked Riette to stay.

“Until morning,” Riette said. “Then we settle what you owe me and decide what the work is.”

“I have always paid you.”

“The steward has. When he wished.”

Estrenne looked toward Sariel. Sariel examined a warped shutter catch with absorbing interest.

“Weekly,” Estrenne said at last. “From me.”

“I will think about it.”

When they left, Riette was setting the unbroken half of her looking glass against the washstand. Estrenne had taken off her traveling shoes herself.

In the street Benjamin flexed his hands.

“Have we kidnapped somebody?”

“She paid for her own room.”

“That may not be the distinction her sons use.”

Sariel adjusted the blue bag beneath her coat. They had used an afternoon which ought to have gone to their own preparations. Her shoulder hurt where the older son had twisted her arm. There was a small tear in Benjamin’s collar, and he would pretend not to know how it had happened until Elena found it.

“I’ll see her tomorrow before we go to Ravenswood,” she said.

Benjamin nodded. “Take someone.”

“I can check a room.”

“I know. Take someone anyway.”

She took his arm for the walk to the bridge. It was partly to prevent him inspecting his collar in every shop window, and partly because he was there.

Chapter 14 — A route that can be walked

The young man assigned to their guild escort read all five names before he would let Kestra examine his paper.

He pronounced hers correctly. She had not expected to care.

“This admits us for the meeting,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And the inspection.”

“With the other named people.”

“It does not admit us to employment.”

He looked troubled. “I wasn’t told that it did.”

“No. You weren’t.”

She read the conditions again. Kavra had obtained two guild witnesses and a private identification meeting before the closing dance. The claimant could arrive with her own company. The host had retained the right to object to property leaving his house. No one had struck through the old exclusions beside their names. Someone had written a limited permission on a separate sheet and expected the two to coexist.

For today, they could.

The escort watched her fold it. He was young enough to expect either gratitude or a complaint he could take to a superior. Kestra gave him the paper back.

“Who carries the second copy?”

“The other witness.”

“Good. If anyone sends you away, find her before you go.”

He nodded and tucked it inside his coat.

On the walk to Ravenswood, Benjamin carried the board on one shoulder with the rolled blanket lashed to it. He had sanded a rough edge himself. Elena had made him run a loose piece of linen along it until nothing caught. Sariel carried the blue bag beneath her arm, and Thane had the small bundle containing his altered glass catch. None of them looked dressed for an entertainment.

The house was being remade for one. White flowers arrived at the front in shallow baskets. A man on a ladder argued with somebody who wanted a hanging cloth lowered without obscuring the window behind it. Down by the water a cook emptied a bucket with a furtive glance toward the wash drawn farther along the same channel.

Kestra watched the cloudy water spread. The guilds would still have that to answer for if every person at the gala went home in the proper body.

Inside, the stage frame stood on its full base. The upstairs room had not followed it. The marked boards had. A carpenter tightened a fastening while another man carried away the bars used to bring it down. The crimson face was hidden. Kestra could see the new iron strap on the left hinge and the narrow painted line that remained clear of it.

Sariel answered Briska’s question about the patient. Olyne would come with the other witness. Elena would not bring her earlier merely to make the arrangements look complete.

Thane opened his bundle. Briska found a projecting corner on the altered catch, and he took it back to file it blunt. Kestra left them considering the exact amount of metal which might prevent a frightened hand from doing its work.

At the rear stage turn she took out her chalk.

On the back of yesterday’s notice she had drawn a route beginning below, carrying through the stage corridor and out toward the boats. In a quiet room it had looked possible. Here a servant came down the narrow stair with a hamper at his hip and had to turn sideways to get past Benjamin. Another waited above him, breathing loudly through his nose. Beyond the loading door the water moved beneath the steps. An opened gate would change the path; a closed door would stop a body. No working of hers would change that.

She put the paper away.

“We need to walk the route,” she said.

“It’s thirty paces,” Briska answered.

“Then it won’t take us long.”

Thirty paces from the turn to the treatment alcove. A fixed step. Two corners at which a person might follow a lamp’s reflection toward the wrong doorway. The alcove sat clear of the stair’s line. Elena had moved its place on Pello’s drawing with her finger, and now Kestra could see the room in the decision: enough ground to set someone down without obstructing the next person trying to reach them.

Benjamin took one end of the board. Pello took the other. Daska tested the turn with a shoulder against the wall. Briska followed carrying the case she would actually have to move. Kestra walked beside them and put chalk where it could be seen by a person who had to keep looking at their feet.

The board struck the wall at the second turn.

Pello stopped. Benjamin backed a fraction without being asked.

“Here,” Thane said.

He showed them how much sooner they needed to begin the turn. They tried it again. Kestra moved a chalk mark the width of two fingers. If she guided a tired bearer along the first line, the board’s far end would still catch. She rubbed that line out with her cuff until nobody would mistake it for the useful one.

At the alcove Pello asked about duration. She gave him the ten minutes, the renewal at the start, the need to inspect anything that had changed. He asked about the door. The door still required an ordinary person to open it.

“Good,” he said.

She had prepared herself for disappointment. His approval made her look at him more closely.

He went to the loading door and worked its latch. He did not want a road which depended on him believing the latch would be somewhere else when they arrived. He wanted to know what he had to do with his hands.

Briska asked about the bronze disc. Kestra touched it through the blue wool. The first had been Mareth’s loan; she had returned it. This one had come through Three Oaks.

“Does it work as well?”

“I do it as well. Some days.”

The answer felt more exposed than she had intended. Briska accepted it with a small movement of her head and turned back to the carry.

Thane would not be satisfied with an empty board. They had the blanket, the case and the width of the turn; they needed weight. Daska volunteered by sitting down before anyone asked her. Benjamin looked at Pello, who lowered his end so she could lie back.

She folded her hands on her stomach.

Kestra had helped carry Benjamin when he had been scarcely able to remain conscious. She did not want that memory in this corridor. Daska’s instruction that he keep her feet away from the expensive guests gave her something else to hold. Benjamin took up his end and carried her as though she were a woman who might object to being knocked against a wall, which she was.

At the alcove Daska sat up and compared the service favorably with funerals she had known.

Vaudrin arrived before anyone could put the board away. Rosceline appeared behind him and supplied a reason to expect fainting. He surveyed their equipment, the blanket and Daska’s cheerfully horizontal claim on his passage. Then he left.

Kestra watched his hand close on the head of his cane. He had opened the house for a public demonstration. He did not like people making preparations for its consequences.

Rosceline told them not to antagonize him unnecessarily. Briska looked at her until she repeated the last word.

When they had gone back toward the ballroom, Kestra set her small glass on a stable ledge.

“I need another minute,” she said.

Pello looked toward the door. “Now?”

“After I check the marks.”

“Then take the time. We should see it before we need it.”

She had already spent the better part of ten minutes walking and marking, setting the direction into places she could touch. She went back through them once more. The corner of the step. The fixed wall beside the pantry opening. The treatment alcove’s inner edge. There was a fresh smear where somebody had brushed a coat against the lowest chalk, so she restored it.

She could have started below. She could have included the entire stair and remained within the hundred and twenty ordinary paces she could reliably mark. But people climbing it would be watching the person carrying their shoulder, not searching the darkness for a choice between ways. The useful uncertainty lay beyond the turn, where light from the loading door competed with lamps in the other passage. She would mark that uncertainty and leave the stair to hands.

At the start she put her thumb against the branching road and began.

A servant called to her. Benjamin moved into his way and answered quietly. Kestra kept the words in order. The house worked around her: plates set down, a board creaking under a carpenter, two musicians attempting the same opening until one told the other to listen. She did not need silence. She needed to give the minute to one thing.

When she finished, the seam lay pale along the marked ground.

It was slight in daylight. She could see it at the foot of the wall, steady where the reflected window light trembled. Daska stood at the beginning, looked down and asked whether she had to say anything.

“Only mean to follow it.”

“I do.”

She walked. Pello went with her, then stopped at the step to look behind him. Kestra told him the direction again. He turned forward and continued. The invitation began.

She had spent much of her life sorting the useful parts of a working from the flourishes people liked around it. Here she could not separate them with confidence. Loude held the invitation’s place; Zélie knew when to move; the glass had been fitted and marked for these bodies. Kestra listened to the returning names and watched the small motions of the feet.

A sound went out of the room. For an instant she could see a guard’s lips moving without hearing him.

Olyne’s younger hand reached away from the board. Sariel caught the elbow and kept the heel in place. Elena told her to stay.

Then the women drew breath.

The younger one sagged. The older one stepped backward with both hands at her face. Kestra moved the chair the last little distance with her foot.

The instruction to stop came in Zélie’s own young voice.

Kestra had known what they were attempting. She had helped arrange it. Even so, the voice startled her into looking at the speaker as if someone new had entered by a door she had failed to watch.

Zélie pulled the bird face up. She found her injured ankle, the scratch below her jaw. Her crying changed into a laugh and back again. Elena got her into the chair.

Olyne held the blue face at arm’s length. She looked for her ring.

Neither woman looked toward Kestra. She was glad of it. Her own throat had closed painfully, and she needed a moment to recover the use of it.

The men had to wait while Elena settled Zélie’s foot.

Havret stood in Orun’s older body with the splinted hand held against his chest. The man whose hand it was watched from the actor’s uninjured face. Kestra had never seen a person contemplate returning to pain with quite that expression.

Havret asked whether it would hurt.

“The hand will still hurt the person whose hand it is,” Elena told him.

No one offered to make the answer kinder. Orun said to do it.

Benjamin brought Zélie’s chair close to the frame. His left arm had begun to stiffen. Kestra saw him set the chair down with more care than its weight required, then move his hand out from under it before it could be trapped. She would have gone to take the other side if he had looked toward her. He was already clear.

Zélie showed Havret the gesture with his good hand. The other arm stayed in its sling. Sariel fitted the fox to the younger body; the fish went over Orun’s older face.

This return took longer.

Havret spoke too soon. They stopped, waited, and began the invitation again. Kestra kept her attention on the heel marks until the quiet of the room deepened beyond ordinary listening. A shoe shifted. She was ready to call out, then saw Elena correct the position with two fingers against the man’s sleeve.

The older body groaned. The sling pulled; Elena caught it. On the other board Havret stared at two sound hands.

Elena asked their names. The voices answered from their own chests.

Kestra let the hand she had braced against the table loosen. Her fingertips had left damp marks in the dust.

Ilaro began the short questions they had agreed would be enough for now. Kestra returned to the unused faces with the fitting list. She asked the people who had worn them to identify their places. One man was angry that she would not accept his answer on behalf of his partner. She waited until the partner spoke for himself.

Loude stood through it all with her prompt sheet folded small between her fingers. When Kestra gave her the last name, she nodded and said that no one else had stood on the boards with the central face uncovered.

Kestra put down her pen.

At the frame, Briska lifted the red face free.

Sariel stood in the way to the canal. Rosceline held the face in its cloth.

Kestra kept one hand on the rack of unused masks. All four patients were back. The passage was open. From the doorway she could see Pello waiting beside the loading key. Nothing prevented the Countess’s people from carrying out the object they had been paid to acquire, except the five people who had agreed in the laundry that they meant to prevent it.

She watched Rosceline’s companions answer her.

Their disagreement was quieter than Kestra expected. No blade came out. Briska knew exactly how much money they would lose. Daska did not allow the signed agreement to end the discussion. Ilaro remained where Rosceline could see his face.

Kestra did not know what any of them had said in their employer’s rooms. She knew how the Countess could arrange a lamp so that a threat looked like a comfortable place to sit. She kept her hand on the rack.

At last Rosceline asked Briska about breaking the face.

Kestra answered only when there was something she could add.

“Break the connected lines. Then the fitted faces and the rims. Keep the fragments apart until we have checked them.”

The red face passed into Sariel’s hands.

She took it as carefully as she had taken Olyne’s fitted mask from its wrapping. Briska laid it on the bare boards and placed the punch. When the others stood clear, Sariel brought down the hammer.

The first blow bent the metal. The second interrupted the painted junction. The lamps guttered together.

Kestra had been ready for a cry, a pressure in her head, even a movement from one of the returned people. The small failure of the flames frightened her more. She glanced at Zélie. The young woman sat with her hands clasped around the blanket and watched.

Sariel struck again.

Briska turned the pieces with tongs. The work continued past the point at which it could have looked decisive to someone standing at the back of the room. They had to find the connections, expose them, break them, and take the connecting strips out. The glass rims required a different pressure. Thane held a cloth over one scored edge while Briska worked so that the fragments would not fly toward the chairs.

Kestra asked a witness to move closer.

“I can see,” the man said.

“Then see this.”

She pointed to the removed strip and the gap it left. He came close enough to inspect both. She gave him the names of the parts as Briska gave them to her. The words would have to do later, when someone wanted to call this an accident or a stage illusion. She made the witness say that he understood which object had been in the frame.

The fitted linings were cut through. Olyne watched the blue face go beneath the hammer. Zélie turned hers once in her hands before surrendering it. She touched the inner cheek with a finger, then placed it in the waiting cloth.

Kestra examined each broken run of marks. She could certify what they had interrupted and what had been removed. She would not certify that no person in any distant place had ever copied part of a design. When the secretary asked for that promise, she made him cross it out.

“What shall I put instead?”

“What you saw.”

He bent to write.

By the end, the stage smelled of dust and hot lamp oil. Sariel stood with the hammer hanging from one hand. A curl of cut lining had caught on her sleeve. Kestra took it off without comment and dropped it among the ruined faces.

Elena took Orun back to the treatment alcove while the boats were made ready. Zélie waited near the stage turn on the carrying board, with Olyne seated beside her. Twice the young woman reached toward the doorjamb and failed to find it. The second time she drew her hand back under the blanket as if someone had reprimanded her.

Kestra watched her settle again. She had a use for the route they had walked that morning after all.

First she sat beside Ilaro long enough to put bread into his hand and get the women’s refusal entered properly. Olyne could say her part to the witnesses when she was ready. Kestra would have preferred to see it written that instant. He was right to distinguish what he had heard from what she had told him.

Across the corridor Sariel read the safe-conduct. Six days, under their own names, for the lodging, the offices and necessary movements about this case. Kestra watched the secretary add a permitted wharf to it. A thin place to stand, but one the five of them could use before it closed.

She went back to the stage turn.

Fallen plaster lay across the start mark. She brushed it clear, checked the turn and the end at the treatment alcove, and came back over the ground she had walked earlier. The floorboards had not moved. The chalk had survived beneath the dust. She could use the route she had prepared.

Daska stood in the opening with the curtain pole.

“A minute,” Kestra told her.

Daska nodded.

Kestra took the bronze disc in her hand. Its road branches were warm from her skin. This one had come through Three Oaks after the borrowed disc went home. The wool at its neck had been replaced more than once. She set her feet at the beginning and spoke.

A servant tried to enter. Daska stopped him without a word that Kestra could hear. A chair scraped beyond the wall. Kestra kept the invocation whole through the distraction, giving the route its start, its turns, its one direction. She did not reach beyond the marks.

The pale seam appeared along the boards.

It was less bright than the lamplight. It did not need to be brighter. She saw Zélie’s gaze find it as the bearers raised the board; saw the young woman follow it toward the turn instead of searching the uncertain spaces beside her. Her hands relaxed on the blanket.

Kestra walked near enough for Zélie to hear her.

“It ends before the steps.”

“Can I keep looking at it?”

“Until then.”

At the alcove the light ended. Pello still lifted the latch. Daska warned the bearers about the worn stone, and they took the first step slowly, while Rossa held the lighter against the landing.

Kestra went aboard after the board had been settled down the middle. There was a place for each of them if the tools stayed clear of feet and nobody decided to lie sideways. Olyne sat with her knee supported. Orun kept the broken hand close. Havret asked where he could go, then went there without offering to help with work he did not understand.

Pello counted them. Five of the Coin, the four returned people, himself and Daska. Rossa had the stern. Twelve aboard, and the lighter sat low enough to make Kestra grateful for every argument Pello had spent on choosing it.

Briska looked at the remaining space and took her case toward the smaller boat.

Sariel sat beside Kestra. Her left trouser knee was wet through. She stared at it until Kestra touched the seat between them.

“Here,” Kestra said. “You’re sitting on my coat.”

Sariel shifted. Kestra pulled the coat free and laid it across both their legs.

The lighter went out first. The pale route remained somewhere behind the loading door, with no more travelers to guide. Kestra let it spend the rest of its small allotted time on the empty boards.

At the physician’s wharf, Rossa called loudly enough to make an assistant appear before Pello finished tying up.

Clean linen replaced the smell of the canal. Orun went inside first. Elena described the hand and the dressings to the apprentice, then the ankle and ribs. Kestra held the bag while she did it. There was always one more pocket Elena needed, and always a wrong side to hold toward her.

Olyne wrote her message in her own hand. When the cramped finger stopped her, Kestra finished the address. Merrit Sann would be fetched after daylight. The message did not go to the Countess.

In the waiting room Benjamin sat with his sword across his thighs while a fresh dressing was made ready. Kestra put a cup near his good hand. He looked at it with suspicion.

“Water,” she said.

“That was what I feared.”

She went to find something better and came back with weak broth. He accepted it as a major improvement. Thane took the empty cup before Benjamin could balance it on the sword.

By the time Sariel had arranged the onward transport, the upper window was gray. She returned a watermarked agreement to Ilaro, spoke briefly with Rosceline, and waited by the outer door. Elena came with her bag and food the assistant had wrapped for her. Kestra asked one last question about who would remain with Zélie until she woke.

Then she followed the others out.

The street smelled of wet stone and the first bread ovens. Sariel checked that all five were there. No one complained about the counting.

Chapter 18 — What Olyne will sign

Elena slept through the cooper’s first delivery and woke when Benjamin dropped a spoon.

He was trying to eat sitting on the edge of his bed. The bowl balanced on his knees; the wounded arm lay against his side. He had put his boots on before attempting any of this, which was how she knew he hoped to be considered fit for work.

“Leave it,” she said.

“The spoon?”

“The performance.”

He looked at his boots. “My feet are cold.”

She crossed the room, picked up the spoon and exchanged it for a clean one from the tray. Belotte had charged for breakfast and sent enough porridge to make the charge reasonable. Elena sat on the adjacent bed and drew back his collar to inspect the dressing.

“No fresh blood,” he said.

“I know.”

“You could look pleased.”

She kissed the top of his head. He went still beneath it, then leaned his shoulder against her knee while she checked that the cloth was dry.

“Better?” she asked.

“Considerably.”

Sariel came in with Merrit Sann’s note. She had slept less than Elena, or had chosen a less flattering way to arrange the results. A crease from the pillow ran across one cheek.

“He has the authorization. Olyne wants us at the physician’s after noon.”

“Then she can have us after noon.”

“It is after noon.”

Elena looked at the window. Someone had drawn the curtain against the light.

“Who did that?”

“Thane.”

“I may kiss him as well.”

Benjamin returned to his porridge. “He’s downstairs. Don’t give him mine.”

Olyne had rejected the physician’s smallest room because its window faced a wall. She received Elena and Sariel in a larger one with a chair beneath her supported knee. Her own cloak lay over the back, and her ring rested in its linen beside the writing things.

Merrit stood at the table. He had two documents ready, a small purse to weight them against the draft, and the look of a man who had spent a morning finding out how much of yesterday the city intended to believe.

“The immediate sum has been released,” he said.

“How much else?” Olyne asked.

“For the present, what you asked me to secure for this payment and your care.”

“I asked you to secure access to my accounts.”

“And I shall continue doing that tomorrow.”

She pressed her lips together. Elena recognized the effort needed to keep a complaint from becoming an instruction to people who had already finished the task they could do.

Merrit placed the acknowledgment before her. Olyne turned it over, making the familiar small notch at the lower left with her thumbnail. This time there was no doubt about whose hand made it. The bruised finger did not bend easily. She stopped, shook it once, and continued reading.

“Six hundred in all,” she said.

“One hundred and fifty already advanced,” Sariel answered. “Four hundred and fifty outstanding.”

“Including the expenses.”

“Yes.”

Olyne glanced at Elena’s bag. Its replaced clasps did not match. “You lost supplies.”

“Yes.”

“Your figure does not change?”

“No.”

For the first time since they entered, Olyne looked directly at her.

She signed. Merrit moved the paper out of reach of the wet ink and opened the larger purse he had kept beneath his coat. Sariel counted the money. Elena watched the client’s hand close over the linen containing her ring.

“I shall need someone to go through my household’s visitors,” Olyne said. “And find which of them carried messages to Sel. You already know the circumstances.”

“We can hear a proposal,” Sariel said.

“I was giving you the next task.”

Elena moved the signed acknowledgment nearer the edge of the table.

“This one is finished.”

Olyne’s knee shifted against its support. Pain crossed her face before she suppressed it. “You may find continued employment useful while your own position is reviewed.”

“We might,” Sariel said. “We would still agree the work first.”

There was no heat in her answer. Elena had heard that tone before, usually just before somebody discovered the door they had been leaning against was locked.

Merrit laid a second page in front of Olyne. It gave the five names, the company name, and a plain statement that the agreed recovery had been completed. Elena had asked him for it while Sariel was finding transport before dawn.

Olyne read it twice.

“This will not get you a charter.”

“It will tell the next person who asks whether you paid us for satisfactory work,” Sariel said.

“What next person?”

“Whoever asks.”

Olyne lifted her pen, hesitated, and signed.

Elena put the letter away before anyone could make it carry more than it said.

“There is another thing I want you to look at,” she said.

Olyne sat back. “You have just explained that the agreement is complete.”

“This isn’t part of it. Nera Pellan.”

The broker’s expression changed by a small degree.

“Who has been telling you about her?”

“Her exclusion came up at the hearing. She bought abrasive wash from an unapproved supplier during the shortage. The batch was accepted. Her tools are still being held.”

“The supplier was unapproved.”

“Yes.”

“That requirement exists for reasons. An accepted batch does not establish where the next one will come from.”

“Then let someone hear that with her in the room. She needs a date on which the tools and the suspension will be considered.”

Olyne looked at Merrit. He did not rescue her from the request.

“I cannot settle a guild suspension from a physician’s chair,” she said.

“I’m asking you to answer for your signature.”

The pause was long enough for someone in the passage to pass twice, once carrying a basin and once empty-handed.

“Have her send the notice to my commercial office,” Olyne said finally. “Merrit can acknowledge it. I will say that I am available to be questioned about what I signed.”

“Put that in writing.”

“Elena.”

“Your clerk may not have heard this conversation.”

Olyne pulled a small sheet toward her with visible irritation. Her finger cramped before she finished the address. Merrit asked whether he should complete it; she told him she could manage.

Elena waited until she had.

As they stood to leave, Olyne said, “You brought me back.”

Sariel stopped at the door.

“Yes,” Elena said.

Olyne looked down at her own hands. Then she asked Merrit to open the window a little farther.

Zélie sat on the edge of another bed with a woman Elena did not know. Her ankle rested on a stool. A loose strand of dark hair kept falling across the scratch beneath her jaw, and she pushed it back each time as if pleased to recognize the annoyance.

The woman introduced herself as the advocate Rosceline had undertaken to pay. Her satchel held none of a physician’s tools. She asked Elena whether Zélie could sit through a conversation.

“For a little while. She needs rest between questions.”

“Then we shall have a little while.”

Zélie watched Elena put her bag on the floor. “Did Olyne send you?”

“No.”

“Has she told them I signed?”

“The assurance is being kept,” the advocate said. “We can discuss what you understood when you signed it. First I want you to tell me what you have already said, and to whom.”

Zélie’s shoulders rose. “All of it?”

“Start with the part you are afraid to tell me.”

Elena took out a clean strip of cloth and asked permission to examine the ankle. Zélie agreed. Beneath the loosened wrapping the swelling looked much as it had at dawn. The return had given its owner back the task of living with it.

“Olyne will want me put away,” Zélie said.

“She may ask for charges,” the advocate answered. “She does not decide the whole proceeding.”

“Will you be there?”

“When you are questioned formally, yes.”

“Even if I tell you something you don’t like?”

“Yes.”

Zélie looked toward Elena, as though this were another treatment whose prospects she should confirm. Elena drew the clean cloth into place.

“She has told you what she will do.”

“Are you staying?”

The question reached farther than the room. Elena felt the desire to answer it carelessly, with something that would let the young woman sleep.

“We have our own lodging for several more nights. I’ll come tomorrow if you want me, and give the physician everything I know about the injuries. After that we’re arranging to go home.”

Zélie looked down at her hands.

“Tomorrow, then,” she said.

“Tomorrow.”

The advocate asked the assistant outside for a quiet room for the next conversation. Elena finished the bandage. At the door she heard Zélie begin with the afternoon she had been promised, and stayed only long enough to be certain no one interrupted her.

In the rooms above the cooper’s store, the money took up less space than Elena expected.

Sariel laid it on the table with the signed acknowledgment. Thane moved his tools aside. Kestra read the reference while Benjamin tried to find a comfortable way to sit in a chair whose back had evidently been designed for another species of shoulder.

Elena’s eighteen marks came out first.

“You could have left those until we divided it,” she said.

“No,” Sariel answered.

Elena put the coins into her own purse. She had missed them more than she had admitted. A good winter coat had been deferred once already, and she wanted to buy it before the seller decided the same cloth was worth more in cold weather.

The remaining payments took longer. Belotte’s rooms were paid until the morning after the sale. Medicines, transport, food, replacement cloth: Sariel had the actual bills and Kestra made her stop adding the same journey twice. What remained belonged to the five after the company’s agreed costs. It was useful money. It would not rent their old house back even if Hanne wished to leave it.

Sariel folded the safe-conduct and put it beside the new reference.

“We agreed five days before we decided again,” she said. “We’re deciding.”

“Before or after you tell us what you want?” Benjamin asked.

“After. I want to wait until the equipment sale, settle the witness visits, and leave the next morning. Before this expires.”

Thane rested his left hand over the sale notice. “Yes.”

“I want another night without getting into a boat,” Benjamin said. “Several, if they’re available.”

“They are.”

“Then yes.”

Kestra wanted the destruction account copied. Elena wanted to see the wounds once more before any road travel. Both agreed to the same morning.

Sariel looked around the table. No one offered to make the decision larger. She drew the departure date beneath their remaining appointments.

Thane unfolded the sale notice. Benjamin asked whether the clamp was very heavy.

“Yes,” Thane said.

“I was hoping for a better answer.”

“It comes apart.”

“That is a better answer.”

Elena put her feet on the empty stool. For the first time since their arrival, she knew how many nights they meant to stay.

Chapter 19 — A borrowed tool

The handcart had one wheel that preferred the canal.

Sariel brought it back toward the wall with a shove of her hip. Bastien watched from the stool outside the warehouse, his bad knee stretched before him and his injured forearm resting on a folded sack.

“Hold the right handle higher,” he said.

“I am.”

“Higher than that.”

She raised it. The cart traveled straight for three paces, then found a hollow in the stone and turned again.

“You charge people for this.”

“Usually they buy lighter things.”

Sariel parked it against a mooring post and put a wedge under the wheel. The wrapper for the clamp lay inside it, along with cord, two short boards to protect the casting and a strip of old sailcloth that Bastien had lent them on the condition that it came back. He had spoken of the sailcloth twice. He had not mentioned the room above his workshop.

Across the water, someone was washing the last painted decorations off a boat. The crimson color diluted to pink beneath the brush. Sariel watched it spread over the canal until Bastien asked whether she would sit down.

“I’m all right.”

“You can be all right sitting.”

There was room on the edge of the warehouse step. She took it.

The auctioneer’s voice carried through the open door, offering a pair of lamps nobody seemed to want at the starting price. Sariel had been inside long enough to see Thane reach the clamp and Briska reach Thane. Kestra was reading the conditions. Benjamin had arranged his carrying strap on the shoulder that would bear it. None of them needed Sariel leaning over the purchase.

“Where’s Elena?” Bastien asked.

“Replacing the last things. She said she’d come to look at your arm afterward.”

“She looked at it yesterday.”

“She’s thorough.”

“So am I. I washed it.”

“With what?”

He gave her a look she recognized from every man she had ever accompanied to Elena’s door.

“The water she told me to use.”

“Then she’ll be pleased.”

He shifted his bad leg a little farther into the sun. “Are you leaving tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“All of you?”

“Unless that thing inside changes somebody’s mind.”

Bastien nodded toward the sale. “The clamp?”

“The city.”

He looked at her, then let his gaze go to the water. A loaded skiff came close enough to the wharf for its pole to scrape the steps. The woman handling it apologized without slowing down.

“I’m staying,” he said.

“I know.”

“Thought I would save you arranging to take me.”

“We couldn’t fit you with the clamp.”

“Then I’m fortunate.”

She laughed. He smiled and began picking a loose fiber from the sailcloth with his sound hand. The ordinary small task made the cut along his forearm harder for her to look at.

“We’ll send the rest for the broken fittings when you know what they cost.”

“I’ve told you what they cost.”

“You told me what you’d charge.”

“And I’m entitled to decide that.”

She let him finish pulling the fiber. “Yes.”

“If you come back, tell me first. I can find rooms. Someone else’s.”

“Yes,” she said again.

He put the fiber down and grunted as he stood. Sariel offered her arm. He used it for the first step, then released her and went to check the cart’s axle.

The clerk at the door wanted the permit before he let them load anything.

Sariel gave it to him. He held the paper so that the seals caught the light, then looked at the handwritten addition beside the permitted wharf.

“Rove?”

“His landing. If we need the water route.”

“Who added it?”

“The secretary whose initials are beside it.”

He looked again. She waited. The old impatience came up quickly, familiar enough that she could feel it in her teeth. He had the paper in his hand. He could read it or fetch someone who could. She did not have to tell him who had burned a house before he was born.

At last he handed it back.

“Keep the cart out of the doorway.”

“It is out of the doorway.”

“Keep it there.”

She folded the paper carefully. It was much newer than the sheets Sel had shown her. It promised much less. The fold had already begun to soften at the corner where she opened it most often.

Inside, the auctioneer called a figure. Sariel heard Briska’s voice, then Thane’s. There was a pause long enough for another bidder. She found herself leaning toward the door before the hammer fell.

Bastien watched her.

“Twelve,” she said.

“Good clamp?”

“I hope so.”

“For twelve, I hope it carries itself.”

Thane did not emerge immediately. There was something to remove before the bench would fit. Sariel could hear ordinary discussion over the machinery and saw Briska’s narrow spanner pass into Thane’s left hand. Benjamin steadied the seat. Sariel remained outside. Thane had asked for help getting it home; he had not asked to be watched earning the right to own it.

When the three finally came through the warehouse door, Kestra carried the stamped receipt and Benjamin held the wrapping clear of the wheel. Thane turned to lift his hand toward Briska. The spanner rose in reply.

Then the cart struck a loose stone.

Sariel caught the left handle while Benjamin braced the bench. Bastien said something unfriendly about the wharf’s maintenance. The clamp rocked, settled and stayed upright.

“Good morning,” Thane said.

“You bought an awkward thing,” Sariel told him.

“I’ve been told.”

“By someone who wanted it.”

“That too.”

He bent to adjust the padding. A corner of the sale notice, torn off and covered with measurements, lay on the seat. He tucked it into his coat before Benjamin pulled the sacking over the casting.

Sariel had heard the hammer, seen the money change hands and watched him bring the purchase out. She still found herself waiting for a decision she could not see.

“Which way?” Bastien asked.

“To Netters’ Lane,” Thane said.

They began to push.

The lane narrowed beside a warehouse whose awning drained into a barrel. Kestra went ahead to make sure they would not meet a brewer’s cart at the bend. Benjamin walked on the sound side of the load, one hand ready to steady it. Bastien had left them at the wharf after making Sariel repeat where his sailcloth would be returned.

Thane stopped to tighten a cord. Sariel held the handles.

“Do you mean to stay?” she asked.

He kept his left hand on the knot. “In Silkstrand?”

“No. In this lane until the cord rots.”

He looked up. She had meant it to sound lighter than it did.

“The workroom,” she said.

“I know which one.”

A woman emerged from the warehouse carrying a bucket and asked them to move far enough for the door. They moved. Thane checked that the cord would clear the pedal arm, then stood beside Sariel.

“I’m taking it home,” he said.

She looked down at the wrapped casting. “Millhaven.”

“Yes.”

His face gave her time to hear it. He had not said it to reassure her. She knew the difference, though she had sometimes found reassurance useful enough to accept without examination.

“You wanted the other bench.”

“I still do.”

“The power shaft.”

“That particularly.”

They watched Kestra persuade a delivery boy to bring his empty barrow down the wider side of the lane. Benjamin waited beside her, doing nothing strenuous with such studied attention that Sariel knew Elena had threatened him with a consequence.

“I could make some things there that take me a week at home,” Thane said. “I could come for a season with the right orders. I could learn what the men already using it know.”

“And put another name over the door.”

“For now, that was the offer.”

She shifted her grip on the cart. The handle was rough where generations of palms had worn away the smoother wood.

“We could ask again. After the inquiry.”

“We could.”

“Without Sel’s pages.”

“Yes.”

It came out almost angrily. Sariel looked at him.

He rested his restricted right hand on the sacking. The two fingers that would not close lay curved against the cloth. “I know the pages mattered. I’m not saying we can walk into the same office tomorrow and get the same thing. I’ve had enough people tell me I can do anything if I’m patient.”

She let go of one handle.

“I wanted them,” she said.

“I know.”

“I keep seeing the sheet against the post.”

He stayed quiet. Behind them the warehouse woman emptied her bucket into the gutter, carefully avoiding their boots. Sariel waited for the water to pass.

Thane touched her wrist with his good hand.

“I have work waiting where my tools are,” he said. “And a place for this. I want to finish it before I hire another room somewhere else.”

Sariel nodded. It was a sufficiently ordinary desire that she could at last understand why she had been frightened to ask him about it.

“The city will still be here,” she said.

“Probably. We should send ahead next time. See what they intend to do with the water.”

She laughed softly. He left his hand on her wrist until Kestra called that the turn was clear.

They took the handles together.

At Netters’ Lane, Belotte came out to inspect the purchase before she let it across her threshold. The bench had to stay in the cooper’s store below; the stair would not bear a struggle with it. Thane agreed at once, which disappointed her enough that she found fault with the sacking.

Elena arrived while they were loosening it. She stood in the doorway with a paper parcel tucked beneath her arm and looked at the clamp.

“Is this the one?”

Thane pressed the pedal. The jaws opened. The spring returned them with an unnecessarily forceful snap.

“It will be,” he said.

Elena put down her parcel and crouched to see what his foot had done. Sariel found herself crouching beside her. Thane demonstrated it again, more slowly, explaining where he would change the spring and how the leather would keep it from marking the work.

For several minutes they stayed there, watching him show them the thing he wanted.

Chapter 20 — The room they can leave

Elena found a rolled bandage beneath the bed and a stocking that belonged to no one willing to claim it.

She held it up by the toe.

“Benjamin.”

“Mine have heels.”

“This had a heel.”

He came to look. His left arm moved more freely than it had the day before, though he still held it away from the weight of his coat. Elena watched him bend, waited to see whether he flinched, and let him discover that she was watching.

“Not mine,” he said.

“Thane?”

From the other room came a refusal too prompt to be trustworthy.

Elena put the stocking on the windowsill. Belotte would know whether it had arrived with the room. The bandage was still clean inside its wrapper. She added it to the top of her bag and checked that the clasp had caught.

Below the window, a cart waited with their bundles and the wrapped bench. Thane had gone down twice to change how the clamp was secured. He would go again before they left. The carrier had begun asking him for advice about a troublesome fastening on his own seat, and Elena expected that the two activities would occupy one another until it was time to depart.

Their room looked larger with the blankets folded.

Seven nights had left small traces: wax beside the lamp, the pale square where Kestra’s papers had kept the table free of dust, a dark thumb mark on the wall near the washstand. Elena wiped the last with a damp cloth. It lightened but did not disappear. She showed it to Belotte when the landlady came to inspect.

“That was there,” Belotte said.

“I don’t think it was.”

“I do. Have you counted the cups?”

There were the correct number. One had a chip at its handle, which Belotte also claimed as older than their visit. Elena suspected she was tired of looking at them all packed and wished them gone before the next tenant arrived.

“Leave the water,” the woman said. “The girls will use it for the floor.”

Elena set down the basin. It was harder than she expected to stop finding one more task that would justify remaining in the room for another minute.

Sariel and Kestra sat at the table with the papers for departure. The safe-conduct remained valid. The carrier’s route reached the permitted road out. Beyond the city they would be travelers with paid places and ordinary work to do at home.

Merrit had sent a short reply agreeing to forward proposals addressed to the Silk Coin at Millhaven. He had repeated that he could forward them, not answer for their acceptance. Sariel had written beneath it that this was what she wanted. The copy for him lay beside her elbow.

“Is that our last message?” Elena asked.

“Nera’s goes with it.”

Olyne’s promise to attend questions about the exclusion had been copied for Nera. The worker’s response was folded beneath it. She wanted the tools brought to whatever hearing was fixed, so that no one could decide what they were worth without seeing them. There was a stain from abrasive wash at the edge of her thumbprint. Elena had been glad to see the practical demand. It gave the promised hearing something to be made to do.

“Nothing for us to carry there first?”

“Merrit will take both.”

Sariel sealed the packet and put it near the door for the messenger. She did not leave an invitation for every displaced person in Silkstrand to follow them to Millhaven. The address was enough for someone who had work to propose, or news they ought to hear.

Kestra drew the client’s reference from beneath the safe-conduct and read it once more. Elena knew the words now. All five names, the company, the completed commission. A useful page with no old seals attached.

“Put it away before she changes her mind,” Benjamin said.

“She has signed it,” Kestra replied.

“I have seen people change their minds after signing things.”

“So have I.”

She put it away anyway, between two stiff sheets that would protect it on the road.

Sariel remained at the table a moment longer. The hand with which she held the wax had a faint scratch across the knuckle. It was almost healed.

“Nothing left at the physician’s?” she asked Elena.

“Nothing of ours.”

“And Zélie?”

“She has the advocate’s next appointment. The physician knows what I gave her and when. She knows how to send to us.”

Elena had visited the previous afternoon. Zélie had been annoyed by the need to keep the ankle raised and had asked whether she could at least mend the torn hem of her own skirt while she waited. Loude had brought it with some of her other clothes. The young woman’s first stitches had wandered; she had picked them out and tried again. Elena had stayed until they found an easier way to support the fabric.

“Did she ask us to stay?” Sariel said.

“She asked when I was leaving. I told her.”

Sariel nodded, and set the sealing wax aside.

The carrier brought up a proposal with the final departure arrangements.

A merchant had two wagons going out by the same road. He wanted armed company as far as their routes agreed. The money would come out of the cost of their passage, with something additional if they took turns at the night watch.

Benjamin listened from the chair near the window. Elena could see that he was calculating how much of a watch required his injured arm.

“Which wagons?” Sariel asked.

The carrier described the loads. He had not met the other hired men. He knew the merchant’s clerk, who had asked him to mention it.

“When do they leave?”

“Today, if the second axle is ready.”

“Or tomorrow.”

“Possibly.”

Sariel looked toward Elena.

“No fighting work for that arm yet,” Elena said.

“I can stand a watch,” Benjamin told her.

“You can. You can’t promise what will be asked while you stand it.”

He looked down at his sleeve. The dressing no longer showed through the cloth, which had evidently encouraged him.

Thane came in from the stairs, having heard the last of the proposal. “I want to get back to the orders I already promised.”

“How far along are they?” the carrier asked.

“Far enough that people will ask that politely for one more week.”

Kestra smiled. “I want to go home while they still ask politely.”

“So do I,” Elena said.

Benjamin moved his shoulder once, carefully. “Passage, then. No watch.”

Sariel told the carrier they would keep the places they had paid for. He accepted the answer without trying to improve the offer, and went down to finish loading.

Elena had expected to feel extravagant. Instead she found herself thinking about the clean sheets stored in their rooms at Millhaven, and whether the window catch Thane had repaired before they left would still keep the rain out when it blew from the wrong direction.

“There will be work,” she said.

Thane looked at her. “There had better be. I’ve bought a clamp.”

Benjamin laughed, then got up to fetch his sword.

They made the final inspection together because doing it separately had produced three assurances and a missing towel.

Kestra checked behind the door. Thane put his left hand into the gap beside the washstand and recovered a small wooden wedge he had made to stop it rocking. He began to put it in his pocket, considered the matter, and returned it beneath the short leg.

Sariel looked under both beds. She found nothing Elena had missed, except the source of a loose thread which had annoyed her for seven nights. She cut it off the blanket.

Benjamin retrieved the unclaimed stocking from the windowsill.

“I thought it wasn’t yours,” Elena said.

“It may be useful.”

“For what?”

“Something with no heel.”

He put it in the outer pocket of his bag. Thane watched him with deep suspicion, as though the stocking might ultimately become part of a repair he would have to undo.

Elena went down last with the key.

Belotte stood behind the cooper’s counter, arranging the papers for the next tenant. The room payment was settled; the wash charge had been added the previous evening. Elena put the key on the counter and waited while the woman made sure it was the right one. There had been a mistaken key at some earlier time in Belotte’s life, apparently, and she would not let it happen twice.

“The upper room may be let if you come back,” she said.

“We’ll write ahead.”

“Do. People arrive with five bundles and expect a staircase to produce beds.”

Elena thanked her for the breakfast on the morning after Ravenswood. Belotte said it had been porridge and they had paid for it. Then she reached beneath the counter and brought out the little cloth they had used to wrap the extra bread.

“You left this downstairs.”

Elena took it. It smelled faintly of the cooper’s shavings.

Outside, the others waited beside the loaded cart. Sariel had sent the packet. Kestra was explaining to the carrier which bag contained papers and should stay under the cover. Thane inspected the clamp’s cords a final time, then stepped back with a reluctance Elena recognized from her own hand on a fresh dressing.

Benjamin held out a hand for her bag. She gave him the light bundle instead.

“What have I done to deserve this?” he asked.

“Been wounded.”

“I’ll try to make a habit of it.”

“Don’t.”

He tucked the bundle beneath his sound arm and smiled at her.

The market at the end of the lane had begun to open. A woman was setting out the little ginger cakes Elena had smelled the day before. She remembered then what she had meant to buy: enough to share, with none of them assigned to a patient’s appetite or saved against a journey they could not yet name.

“Wait,” she said.

Sariel looked up at once.

“The cakes. From the woman by the green awning.”

“How many?” Benjamin asked.

“More than you bought last time.”

“You said a few.”

“I had not realized what you thought that meant.”

He handed the light bundle to Sariel and went down the lane. Elena stood in the doorway’s morning sun while they waited for him. The room key was inside, on someone else’s counter. Her bag was closed. There was nothing she needed to go upstairs for.

Benjamin returned with a warm paper parcel and an expression of exaggerated care. Thane asked whether he had eaten any on the way.

“One broke.”

“Unfortunate,” Kestra said.

“Very.”

Elena opened the parcel. There were enough. She passed them around while the carrier helped Sariel up, then took the place Benjamin had left beside him. The cake bent softly beneath her fingers. Ginger caught at the back of her throat.

As the cart began to move, Thane turned once to make certain the wrapped bench had not shifted. Kestra held the parcel against the jolt. Sariel reached back for another cake, and Elena gave her the largest one left.
