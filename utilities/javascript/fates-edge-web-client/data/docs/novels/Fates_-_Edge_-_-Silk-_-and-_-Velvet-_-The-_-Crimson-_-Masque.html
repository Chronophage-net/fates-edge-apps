The Countess’s Irregulars — Book Five

Part I — Two sets of invitations

Chapter 1 — The fitting

The dressmaker had no objection to the knife. She objected to where Rosceline intended to put it.

“You will spoil the sleeve.”

“Only if I have to use it.”

“You will spoil it standing still. Lift your arm.”

Rosceline lifted her arm. The woman caught the small bulge beneath the silk between two fingers and held it toward the mirror, as if presenting an unsuitable suitor.

Morning light came through the amber glass above the shop door. It improved most complexions and made the bruise-colored lining look costly. Rosceline had chosen that lining before she knew what the Countess wanted. The invitation to a fitting had arrived with an appointment for the afternoon, an assurance that the necessary clothes would be paid for, and no useful description of the work. She had spent the intervening time discovering what could be considered necessary.

“There is room at the hip,” the dressmaker said.

“I have something at the hip.”

“Naturally.”

She knelt without asking what. Her name was Mestrel, and she had once spent three days persuading a magistrate’s wife that a concealed pocket should not be placed where the magistrate habitually rested his hand. Her professional discretion was excellent. Her patience cost extra.

On the other side of the room Daska was trying a shoe. She stood on one foot, rotated the other, then rose onto her toes with such easy pleasure that Rosceline felt an unreasonable resentment of the months she had spent making her own hip bend comfortably again.

“Can you fight in those?” she asked.

“Can you dance in that?”

“That is a discourteous response to a practical inquiry.”

Daska turned once. The skirt flared, showing her ankles and the plain linen beneath. She laughed at the mirror. Mestrel looked up from Rosceline’s hem and smiled before remembering how much work remained.

Briska had declined a fitting. She stood beside a display of theatrical masks with her measuring cord stretched across a brass support. Her hair had caught on a projecting screw. She freed it, examined the screw’s point and looked personally affronted.

“Someone has cut a wood thread onto this.”

Mestrel’s apprentice came forward. “Is that bad?”

“For the wood? No. For this piece of brass, very.”

Pello moved a basket before the apprentice backed into it. He wore his ordinary coat. Rosceline had offered to have it altered. He had looked at the coat, looked at her, and kept both hands in its pockets.

Ilaro had gone behind a painted screen to escape the glare. A voice came from there now.

“You might fetch the proprietor instead of standing over me.”

It belonged to a woman. A young voice with an older woman’s certainty about being obeyed.

“I was looking for somewhere to sit,” Ilaro said.

“There is a chair.”

“You are in it.”

“Then find another. And ask her why my clothes have been sent to the wrong address.”

Mestrel’s hands stopped at Rosceline’s hip.

“Who is that?” Rosceline asked softly.

“A customer.”

“How reassuring. For a moment I thought you were keeping a duchess behind the scenery.”

The dressmaker took a pin from her mouth and set it in the cushion at her wrist.

“Do not move.”

She went behind the screen. Rosceline moved enough to see through the gap between its panels.

A woman perhaps half Mestrel’s age sat with one foot on a folded cloak. She had dark hair, a pinched face and a scratch beneath her jaw. Somebody had wound a bandage around her ankle. Her gown was inexpensive but recently made, and she wore it with visible disgust. Ilaro had stepped away from the chair. His notebook remained shut in his hand.

“They were collected,” Mestrel said. “I have already told your friend.”

“My friend is not entitled to receive my clothes.”

“Dame Olyne’s household collected them.”

The young woman’s mouth opened. For an instant she looked as though she would spit.

Then the doorbell rang.

Rosceline watched the newcomer in the mirror. A woman in a workmanlike brown coat carried a package wrapped in faded blue cloth. Gray showed in the hair she had pinned hastily; the lines beside her mouth deepened as she surveyed the shop. She glanced at the door behind her before she greeted anyone. One boot was damp to the ankle.

“I’m collecting the alterations.”

“They are not—” Mestrel began.

“For the lady at the back.”

She laid the parcel on the counter. Her eyes passed over the room, pausing at Rosceline’s gloves. The Countess’s cream invitation lay beside them, its velvet flower impressed in violet wax.

The pause was short. Rosceline knew she had seen it because the woman looked at her next, directly, and did not bother to smile.

“Have we met?” Rosceline asked.

“I’d remember.”

“One hopes. I have occasionally put a great deal of effort into being forgotten, but never in this dress.”

The woman moved toward the screen. Pello shifted the basket farther from her path. She noticed him, too.

Mestrel came out with a length of purple thread caught on her sleeve. “Your friend is being difficult.”

“She has had a difficult morning.”

“It began yesterday.”

The newcomer’s expression warmed a little at that. She touched the dressmaker’s elbow, asked a question too quietly for Rosceline to hear and received a reluctant nod. Then she went behind the screen.

Rosceline reached for her gloves.

Under the invitation was a narrow buff slip she had not put there. Two small faces had been drawn at its head. Beneath them, in a careful commercial hand, were a name and two different sets of measurements.

OLYNE. ZÉLIE.

The lower line had been crossed through. A wet thumb had smeared the correction.

The woman in the brown coat emerged while Rosceline was reading it.

“That belongs with the parcel.”

“Then you should have wrapped it.”

“I did.”

Rosceline glanced at the blue cloth on the counter. Its fold had come open beside her gloves. Quite possibly it had slid out when the package was laid down. Quite possibly the woman had meant to take the invitation under cover of collecting it. Neither possibility obliged Rosceline to return something interesting.

“You have an injured companion,” she said. “I have a chair hired outside. Perhaps we can spare her a walk.”

“We have transport.”

“Then this has become an unusually fortunate street.”

Behind the screen the seated woman said, “Sariel, I will not be carried like a parcel through a shop.”

Sariel did not turn.

“You can walk to the door.”

Her tone was gentle enough to surprise Rosceline. It was also final.

Daska sat on the fitting stool to undo her shoe. Ilaro came out from behind the screen and stood beside the counter. Nobody had drawn anything. Rosceline was conscious of the pin Mestrel had left at her waist and the advantage that gave every person in the room who was dressed.

“What is Ravenswood fitting you for?” she asked.

Sariel’s eyes flicked to the slip.

“Ask your employer.”

“I haven’t accepted any employment.”

“You’ve accepted the clothes.”

That was well placed. Rosceline disliked it.

The young woman came around the screen leaning on Mestrel. She was pale beneath a flush that rose along her throat. She held herself stiffly, keeping weight off the bandaged foot. When Sariel offered an arm she took it without another protest.

Ilaro asked her name.

“You heard it.”

“I heard two.”

She stared at him. The doorbell rang again, and a porter put his head inside to ask whether the chair was ready. Rosceline had hired two men; this was the taller, with a white patch in his beard.

“In a moment,” she said.

“It’s for her,” Sariel said, pointing to the woman beside her.

The porter looked from one to the other. Rosceline had not yet decided how to correct him without sounding mean when Mestrel moved between them carrying the brown coat.

“Mind the hem. And that pin, madam.”

The young woman reached the door. Sariel steadied her down the shallow step. Beyond them the hired chair had its little curtain tied back, and the second porter was already kneeling to keep the carrying pole low.

Rosceline stepped after them. The pin at her waist bit deeply.

She stopped, swore and caught the dress before the unfastened side opened to the street.

Mestrel had placed the brown coat over the back of a chair. The woman going out wore a dark gray cloak now. Rosceline had last seen that cloak beneath the injured foot. She could not recall when Sariel had put it around her companion’s shoulders, nor when she had removed her own coat, nor why the porter was helping without asking who would pay.

Pello reached the door. A blue ribbon had been tied around the chair’s forward pole. He touched it, looked along the street, and let his hand fall.

The porters lifted. Sariel walked beside the chair with the blue parcel under her arm. At the corner she looked back once.

Rosceline still held the fitting slip.

For a moment she thought Sariel would return for it. The chair rocked on uneven stones. The woman inside made a small sound. Sariel caught the pole, said something to the porters, and went on.

“Well,” Rosceline said, holding the dress closed. “That was ill-mannered.”

“You were keeping her paper,” Ilaro said.

“I was establishing why she wanted my invitation.”

“She didn’t take your invitation.”

“She took my chair.”

Pello returned from the door. “Ours is farther down.”

“What?”

“Same hirehouse. Different men.”

Rosceline looked at Mestrel. The dressmaker had resumed kneeling beside her, apparently satisfied that the morning could now proceed.

“Did you know?”

“About your sleeve? I told you.”

Daska laughed into the stocking she was pulling straight. Rosceline waited until the pin had been removed before she bent to pick up her gloves. The buff slip went inside her bodice, against bare skin. Mestrel found the second knife, considered it, and began moving the seam.

The brown coat stayed on the chair. Its elbows had been carefully patched. Rosceline could see, now that nobody was wearing it, that it was too broad for the woman who had come in.

Chapter 2 — The people who survived

Ilaro chose the chair nearest the closed curtain. The Countess’s maid brought him water without ice and set it where his right hand would find it. He remembered having asked for that once. He could not decide whether being remembered here made him comfortable.

The afternoon had turned close. Beyond the shutters, the Heights rang with hammering from a house being given a second balcony. Somebody had decided that the first was insufficient to display the wealth required for it. Each blow reached the drawing room softened by fabric and distance.

Rosceline laid the fitting slip on the low table. She had changed into her own dress and was behaving as though the change had been a free choice. Daska ate a little almond cake. Briska declined one, reconsidered, and took the one without icing. Pello remained standing until the maid indicated a chair clear of the door.

The Countess came in with Celise. Her veil was the color of watered wine. She greeted them by name, sat and asked whether the fitting had been satisfactory.

“We encountered another party,” Ilaro said.

Rosceline gave him a look. He waited while she supplied the woman’s description and the name Sariel. She did not mention the pin.

The Countess picked up the buff slip by its clean edge.

“Where is the young woman now?”

“We don’t know.”

“Was she calling herself Olyne?”

“She did not answer the question.”

Celise leaned far enough to read the names. Her hands remained folded in her lap.

“You were going to tell us about these people,” Ilaro said.

“Yes.”

The Countess set the paper down. “Five of the old Silk Coin have returned to the city. Sariel is one. Sera Vesper has rebuilt the Coin. The exclusions against these five still stand. They have made inquiries about the entertainment at Ravenswood.”

Daska stopped brushing crumbs from her fingers.

Ilaro remembered the fire. He had been working then, properly working, with a desk that belonged to the office and a superior who wanted things signed before supper. He had seen an order to avoid the Dye Yards while a private dispute was resolved. The order had used the phrase unnecessary exposure of personnel. He had not been one of the men sent there.

“You burned it,” he said.

The Countess looked at him.

“I ordered it burned.”

Outside, the hammer missed. Metal struck stone with a flat, penetrating ring. Ilaro lost the beginning of the question he had been preparing. He looked down at the fitting slip, waited, and found it again.

“Were they supposed to get out?”

“No.”

No one reached for the cakes.

“Is this a commission to bring them back?” Daska asked.

“It is a commission to take an object from Ravenswood.”

“I’m asking about the people.”

“They are not part of the delivery. I will put that in writing.”

Celise drew a small writing case toward her. Its catch clicked in the still room. She passed Ilaro a sheet listing the five survivors by name, with descriptions beneath. Rosceline leaned over to read it; he left it where the others could see.

Ilaro watched her hands. He knew something of the Court’s methods and less of Celise’s part in them than he would have liked. She was capable of choosing a useful cruelty without moving her voice. She had also, on another evening, insisted that a servant with a fever be sent home before the household began counting how inconvenient that would be.

The Countess brought out a drawing.

It showed a face without a mouth. The cheeks were narrow, the brow broad and smooth. Two small holes stood where nostrils would have been. Behind it the artist had sketched a wooden frame, a pivot and a lower support.

“The Crimson Masque,” she said. “The original. The paint on its inner surface must remain intact.”

Briska bent over the drawing. “Is that a bearing?”

“I expect you will tell me.”

“I can tell you now if someone has drawn it correctly.”

“Vaudrin keeps it in the performance frame. He is the troupe’s director. They are giving the final entertainment at the guild reconciliation in three nights.”

“And you want to own it,” Rosceline said.

“Yes.”

The Countess opened a second sheet. This was a letter with the address removed. She put it before Ilaro.

He read a description of a private performance given the previous season in Thepyrgos. A man had answered to another man’s name while his own body went on speaking elsewhere. The writer had believed it accomplished by ventriloquism until the two men were separated. One had wept and asked for his wife. The other, wearing the first man’s face, had asked where the money was kept.

“Who wrote this?”

“A supplier who wants his advance returned. Celise has spoken with him.”

“Did he see them afterward?”

“He saw them returned,” Celise said. “He was close enough to hear one man being sick. He does not know how it was done. He knows that when the red face was carried out, the demonstration could not be repeated.”

Ilaro reread the letter. The words were awkward in useful ways. A man inventing a wonder for sale would probably not have spent a whole paragraph explaining why he had remained in the room after deciding to leave.

“What does the owner say?”

“That his supplier saw a rehearsal.”

“Have you asked the guilds to stop the performance?”

“I have sent the warning. Kavra’s secretary requested a name and a sworn appearance. The supplier will give neither while Vaudrin holds his money. Orun’s office believes a rival is attempting to have the alchemists’ workshops closed for another week.”

“Are they?”

“Quite possibly. That does not improve the masks.”

Briska turned the drawing sideways. Pello leaned forward to see it. Daska sat back, her hands flat on her knees.

The proposed entertainment was a reconciliation between the Gemcutters and the Alchemists. Their quarrel had begun with tainted water and grown through three closed workshops, an overturned handcart and a fight in which a gem-polisher had lost the use of one eye. Sel Virelle had arranged the public evening. Her singers had also helped quiet the last disturbance. She had acquired introductions from people who were grateful that somebody knew what to do with a crowd.

Ilaro knew the name. He had heard Sel sing outside a closed washhouse. The women waiting for clean water had stopped quarreling until the song ended. Then they had quarreled more quietly, which was not nothing.

“How many people will wear the fitted masks?” he asked.

“Six pairs,” Celise said. “Patrons and players. It is described as an exchange of stations.”

“A fashionable evening,” Rosceline murmured. “One gets to be poor without waiting for one’s husband to become interesting.”

The Countess did not smile.

“Madam Estrenne has accepted a part. She has been foolish enough to believe me once before. I would prefer her to have the opportunity to do it again.”

“Then tell her not to attend.”

“She has received my letter. She has not answered it. Her sons believe this is an excellent opportunity for the house.”

Ilaro placed the supplier’s account beside the fitting slip. Two names. Two measurements. He thought of the young woman’s furious refusal to give him a name he could safely put beside her face.

“Taking this object away prevents another exchange,” he said. “What if one has already happened?”

Celise looked at the Countess before answering.

“The witness saw the return completed while the object remained in its frame. We have no report of anyone left displaced.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No,” she said. “It is what we know.”

He wrote it down.

The fee was two thousand marks, five hundred advanced, approved expenses paid separately. The balance required the original face with its working surface intact. The Countess would not accept a painted copy, a report that someone else had destroyed it, or the person who happened to be wearing it. She said the last for Daska’s benefit and allowed Daska to hear that.

“The advance?” Pello asked.

“For the work of attempting the recovery. It remains yours if the recovery fails. Expenses require evidence of the expense.”

“Boat hire.”

“Agreed in advance, yes.”

“Damage?”

“Not unlimited.”

He nodded. That seemed to be the answer he had expected.

Rosceline had not touched the drawing. She was looking at the completed terms Celise had written. Ilaro could see her reading the part about the people, then the part about ownership of the recovered object. There was no reference to a previous bargain. No numbered recovery. Sarem’s book was in Rosceline’s room, inside the box Briska had made for it.

The Countess rose.

“Take time. The smaller sitting room is available. If you refuse, the fittings are mine to regret.”

She left with Celise. The maid followed, taking the empty water pitcher but leaving the cakes.

For a while nobody spoke.

“I want to know why she wants to keep it,” Daska said.

“Because she expects to find a use for it,” Briska answered. “It isn’t especially ambiguous.”

“I mean a use we can live with.”

Rosceline unfolded the corner of the terms. “At the moment it belongs to a man preparing to use it on twelve people.”

“So steal it,” Daska said. “Don’t deliver it.”

“We have been invited to discuss a paid recovery. If we intend to do something else, I would rather we admitted it before taking the advance.”

Daska looked at her sharply. Rosceline held the look. Neither mentioned Rell.

Ilaro felt the beginning of a headache, a thin warm pressure above the ear. He moved his glass out of a patch of light.

“We can accept the attempt,” he said. “We cannot promise the supplier’s account is the whole thing. If we find people already crossed, we stop and report before moving the face.”

“To her?”

“To one another first.”

Pello was looking at the rough plan on the back of the drawing. “There is a canal door. We need to see whether it opens.”

“You’re taking it?” Daska asked.

“I’ll look at the door.”

Briska set a fingernail against the drawn pivot. “If they’ve mounted this as badly as the shop stand, someone is going to drop it before we get there.”

“That would save a great deal of trouble,” Rosceline said.

“It would create an uninspectable broken working with twelve people around it.”

Rosceline let the paper go.

They asked for a clause allowing consultation before removing an instrument that held a completed exchange. Celise returned and wrote it in. The Countess agreed to it. Daska made her confirm, aloud, that the Coin’s survivors were not included in any demand for delivery. Then she signed.

Ilaro signed next. Pello took time over the name Canaletti. Briska’s pen caught on a rough place in the paper, and she scraped it clean with a knife before finishing. Rosceline read the whole thing once more. When she set her name beneath theirs, the Countess drew five small purses from the drawer beside her chair.

Ilaro took his. A hundred silver marks made a satisfactory weight. He put them inside his coat and felt the notebook press against them.

The Countess offered him the supplier’s original account.

“A copy,” he said. “Keep the original where we can find it.”

Celise reached for fresh paper.

Chapter 3 — A removable face

Ravenswood had no ravens and very little wood. Someone had planted six dark trees along the canal and clipped them until they resembled the brushes used for cleaning bottles. Beyond them stood a house of pale stone, extended twice by men who had disagreed about the height a ceiling ought to have.

Briska came in through the delivery gate with Pello carrying the longer case. Their introduction described her as a specialist in concealed fastenings. She had objected to the word concealed. The steward had objected to the cost. They had arrived at fastenings and an acceptable hourly rate.

The steward led them along the east side of the ballroom. Workers were laying a shallow stage at its southern end, nearest the canal. The room had windows to the west and a main entrance at the north. A service corridor ran behind its east wall. That corridor would have been a perfectly useful route if the household had not filled it with chairs.

Briska stepped over an upholstered arm.

“These have to move.”

“They will be in use by then.”

“And this door?”

“What about it?”

Pello put the case down and opened the canal loading door. It stopped halfway against a stack of trestles outside. He shut it, moved one trestle and tried again. This time he could see the narrow wharf below and a mooring ring sunk into the stone.

“That has to move too,” Briska said.

“We have an event this evening. I cannot have the entire staff occupied with doors.”

“Then choose the ones you expect to use.”

The steward drew breath. Pello shouldered the case again. Briska followed the man up a broad stair from the service corridor to the gallery above the ballroom.

Here the musicians had laid claim to a strip along the western rail. Chairs faced inward toward a row of temporary fitting booths. At the far end, screened from the public stair by folded scenery, stood the performance frame.

It was taller than a man. Dark wood carried two wings of glass, each set in a narrow brass rim. Between them, mounted at the height of a face, hung something red.

Briska had expected paint. This had depth. Light entered the surface and remained there, darkening toward the brow. The face had no mouth; its lower edge tapered to a blunt chin. From one angle it seemed to have closed eyes. From another the eyes were open and directed a little to her left.

She moved right. The impression stayed.

“Does that trouble you?”

The man behind her wore a coat the color of dried cherries and a white linen neckcloth without a stain. He was old enough to have paid for his posture with pain. A narrow cane rested against his wrist. Its silver head had been rubbed smooth.

“The mounting does,” Briska said. “Is this yours?”

“Vaudrin. I am responsible for the performance.”

“That was not quite my question.”

He smiled. His teeth were good. Expensively, individually good.

“The face is entrusted to me.”

“Then whoever entrusted it should have paid for a better lower support.”

She knelt before the frame. The red face sat on two hooked pegs, with a small bronze saddle beneath its chin. A forked lifting tool clipped into the outer wood. Its square stem fitted a socket in the saddle. Turning it eased the lower edge forward so the face could be raised from the hooks without scraping the painted back.

The tool was ordinary bronze, rubbed bright at the grip. The face was something else. Briska felt warmth against her forehead, although the room was cool. A groove worn into the fork’s handle suggested someone had pulled hard when a turn would have done.

“When did this last come out?”

“After the demonstration.”

“Before or after someone twisted the tool against its stop?”

Vaudrin leaned on his cane. “Can you repair it?”

“I can tell you whether it needs repairing if you stop answering every question with another one.”

Pello looked at the glass wings. He did not touch them. His reflection appeared beside Briska’s, thinned by an imperfection in the pane.

Vaudrin crouched, more slowly than he would have liked. He pointed to a line of small dark characters on the back of the central face.

“Nothing touches that surface. Nothing covers it. If you need to remove the face, I will be present.”

“Then you will be present.”

“And the glass remains open only during inspection.”

Briska looked at the hinges. “Why?”

“Because people put their fingers on things.”

That answer, at least, was sound.

He let her examine the lower support. Pello held a shaded work lamp while she checked the saddle’s travel. There was a shaving of wood in the socket. She lifted it out with a narrow pick, set it on her palm and showed it to Vaudrin.

“Your carpenter has cut the recess after fitting the metal. The shaving caught behind the turn. The bronze itself is not bent.”

“So it is safe.”

“The obstruction is removed. Those are different statements.”

He stood, clearly tiring of her. Across the gallery a man was moving a wooden screen with his left shoulder against its edge. His right hand remained close to his chest. One of the lower feet had snagged in a gap between boards, and he was maneuvering the screen backward rather than trying to lift it.

Briska watched for three breaths before saying, “Turn the foot across the gap.”

“I know.”

“It doesn’t look as though—”

The foot came free. He moved the screen two paces, lowered it and leaned against it while a younger worker carried away a coil of rope from behind.

“The rope was under it,” he said.

She returned to the frame.

Vaudrin had gone to reprimand a woman painting silver scales onto a mask. Briska measured the distance between the hooks. Pello held the cord. The left-handed worker came nearer with a trestle under one arm.

“Take it from the shoulders,” he said.

“I am taking it from the shoulders.”

“You’re taking it from the decoration. The hooks are a quarter-finger lower behind it.”

She checked. He was right.

“Who built this?” she asked.

“Somebody who expected never to pack it.”

His beard was graying at the chin. His right fingers were bent and stiff. He used that hand to steady the trestle while lowering it with the left. Briska had seen men attempt to conceal a damaged hand beneath industriousness until it bled. This man had made a small leather loop on the trestle expressly for the two fingers he could use.

“You should look at the glass catch,” he added.

“I have.”

“Try releasing it from this side.”

She did. The catch required the thumb to press inward while two fingers lifted. It worked neatly from the right and poorly from the left. She could redesign it in an hour. She did not say so, because he had not asked.

Pello’s attention had moved beyond the man to the gallery’s south end. There was a narrow passage behind the screens, leading back to the broad service stair. A small door opened off it into the sponsor’s dressing suite.

“That one?” he asked the steward, who had returned with an impatient expression.

“Private.”

“Does it open into another stair?”

“It opens into a dressing room.”

Pello waited.

“And a little room beyond that. No stair.”

He nodded and moved aside for a woman bringing a tray of cups.

Vaudrin returned. “The frame goes down to the stage on the morning of the reconciliation. Four men will carry it. Does your inspection prevent that?”

“No. Carry it with the glass secured. The foot bolts need removing before anyone lifts.”

“They are experienced men.”

“Then they will appreciate the instruction.”

She put the bronze fork back in its clip. Vaudrin shut both glass wings and turned a key. The left catch, when released, made a little double sound. Briska stored it in her head.

On the way down she stopped at the stage. Its front stood only three ordinary steps above the ballroom floor; its back had been built over an older storage recess, reached by a steep stair from the service side. A carpenter below was complaining that somebody had sent all the good shelves upstairs.

Pello looked down the stair.

“Exit?”

“If you’re a rat,” the carpenter called. “Bring a shelf if you’re anything larger.”

Outside, Briska opened her smaller case on the wharf. She drew the mounting while it was still clear in her mind. Pello leaned on the rail and watched a barge go past. Its wash lifted a strip of green dye-water from the steps and spread it across brown.

“The narrow passage behind the screens,” she said. “Can you keep it clear?”

“Until someone puts a screen in it.”

“That is the point of asking you.”

He turned and took the pencil she offered. On her drawing he marked the canal door, the broad stair and the gap behind the screens. Then he marked a small cross halfway up the wharf.

“Loose stone.”

“We’re not repairing the wharf.”

“Don’t step on it with the face.”

Briska added a note. He watched until she had finished, then went to tell the boatman that they would need him after supper.

She looked back up at the gallery window. The man with the damaged hand was standing there with a mask in his good one. He had taken off his work coat. Beneath it she could see a narrow tool roll fitted diagonally across his chest, where he could reach every pocket with his left hand.

He saw her looking and stepped away.

Briska closed the case. She had not asked his name.

Chapter 4 — The preliminary supper

Rosceline entered Ravenswood wearing the new gown and carrying no knife in either sleeve. The dress moved beautifully. She intended, if possible, to keep it that way until it had justified its cost.

The first supper was a smaller occasion than the public reconciliation to follow. Silkstrand’s important people had come to determine which other important people intended to attend. A string quartet played beneath the western windows. The musicians’ last notes trembled against the glass and dissolved into conversation.

Daska smelled the food before they reached the doors.

“There are quails.”

“You are here as my companion.”

“Then accompany me to the quails.”

Rosceline surrendered her wrap and gave the doorman their names. Beyond his shoulder she saw Ilaro being shown into the receiving room with a sheaf of seating amendments. The Countess’s household had supplied the introduction. He was a man inspecting particulars on behalf of guests who paid to have particulars inspected. The role required little deception.

Briska and Pello were still officially repairing fastenings. Rosceline intended to give them the time to repair one in a direction its owner had not requested.

At the ballroom’s far end stood a woman in green, her hair dressed high and her neck glittering with small dark stones. The men beside her leaned in to hear what she said. She did not raise her voice to help them.

“Kavra,” Daska murmured. “Gemcutters.”

“You know her?”

“I know the man whose hand is almost touching her waist. He owes a friend of mine money.”

“Do not collect it during supper.”

“I wasn’t going to. I wanted to see what happened to the hand.”

The hand withdrew of its own accord when Kavra turned. Rosceline accepted a glass from a passing servant and went toward the woman at the center of the room.

Sel Virelle wore black with a thin border of white at the throat. She had chosen no jewel that could compete with her face. Rosceline admired that restraint in people to whom it had not occurred to wear more.

Part II — The wrong woman

Chapter 6 — Who bought the dress

Mestrel had already heard three accounts of the disturbance at Ravenswood. In one, somebody had stolen the guild jewels. In another, two women had fought over a musician. The third concerned a trained bear and was her favorite.

“No bear,” Ilaro said.

“A pity. I have a deposit here for a bear’s coat and the customer has never returned.”

She set a cup beside him. He had asked for hot water. There was a slice of lemon in it, thin enough to read through. On the counter lay Rosceline’s damaged gown, delivered by Pello before the shop opened. Mestrel had put a strip of paper through the tear so that no one could mistake its extent.

Ilaro laid the buff fitting slip beside it.

“Which of these women did you measure?”

“Both.”

“Together?”

“No. Dame Olyne on the first day. Zélie on the next.”

“And the woman who came yesterday?”

Mestrel looked toward the rear screen. “Zélie’s face. Zélie’s clothes, though she seemed to have taken against them. I have never heard a woman be so angry at a pair of inexpensive stockings.”

“Did she speak as Zélie had spoken?”

“People sound different when frightened.”

He liked the answer. He did not write it as evidence of either thing.

Mestrel brought out her book. The entries showed the original purchases, the fittings, the payments and the address to which each garment was to go. “Five days ago.”

“The same stage servant. He carried the faces in shallow boxes. He told me not to put pins through the lining. I told him I had been doing this since before his mother discovered why a lining mattered.”

She turned a page. Beneath the descriptions of fabric, each woman had signed to acknowledge the return of a fitting deposit.

Zélie looked down at her skirt. The older woman’s hands spread over costly cloth. She seemed unable to decide whether to smooth it or push it away.

There had been a private demonstration, patrons and a supplier, a promise of payment greater than her last three engagements together. Vaudrin had shown her the return sequence long before. She had performed it in rehearsal with another player while the central face was covered. Glass, positions, the turn toward the plain side, the spoken invitation and answer. It had been part of the theatre until Sel asked whether she wanted to experience the thing the theatre described.

“They said Dame Olyne knew.”

“Did you ask her?”

“She was talking to people. I was told not to interrupt.”

“You could have.”

Zélie looked at her with naked dislike. “I know.”

Rosceline set down the knife.

For the first hour, Zélie said, she had been delighted. Servants answered. People waited when she had not finished a sentence. Someone brought a cushion without being asked twice. She went into the garden, summoned a plate of food and did not first find out whether the musicians had eaten. Her own body was being taken upstairs because Olyne was making a scene inside it. Sel said that happened sometimes, that a person who had never occupied another station found it difficult to surrender the habits of the first.

“I believed her for a little while.”

“And then?”

“She asked me to sign something.”

The paper declared that the demonstration had been successful and the sponsor wished the public entertainment to proceed. Zélie had refused. Sel had asked whether she intended to remain a penniless player after admitting that she had helped steal a broker’s body. She had also described the rooms in which a magistrate might keep a body while deciding who owned it.

“She didn’t threaten to hurt me.”

“She appears to have been doing quite well without it.”

Zélie looked down again. “I signed. Badly. She said Olyne’s hand was tired.”

From the courtyard came a sudden clatter and an indignant male voice. Daska had probably found something to occupy herself. Rosceline could not see beyond the trees.

“Can you return?” she asked.

“If she comes back. With the blue face. The bird face is still in the dressing room.”

“Yours.”

“My face’s. The one fitted to me.”

“Explain it to someone who has a reason to get it right.”

Zélie took the fruit knife. With its blunt point she drew two marks in the condensation on the table’s stone rim. She placed a peach stone between them.

“The red face stays here. The two glasses turn inward. The first passage is toward the painted face. The return is toward the plain one. You turn the lower silver tongue before the glasses move. You mustn’t turn it halfway.”

“What happens if you do?”

“I don’t know. Vaudrin made me do that part again until I could do it without looking.”

“How fortunate that he was tiresome.”

Zélie did not smile. She set down the knife.

“There are words. They don’t do it alone. You have to be in the place you were given and hold your hand here.” She touched Olyne’s throat, startled at its soft fold, and made herself continue. “Then answer in your own name. Your own. He says it’s the one thing you mustn’t perform.”

“Can someone speak for an injured person?”

“I haven’t seen it done.”

Rosceline stored that uncertainty with the rest.

“When the red face is taken away?”

“Nothing changes. That was why I couldn’t go back after the demonstration. He carried it out and said we would finish when she was calmer.”

“And he did not finish.”

“No.”

The maid came toward them with a pot wrapped in cloth. Zélie’s hand went flat against the stone, wiping the little plan away. She missed one wet mark. Rosceline put the peach plate over it.

The housekeeper recommended a paste made with chalk. Rosceline thanked the maid and asked whether she could bring a small parcel of it to the front desk. The girl went with the relief of a person finally given an errand she could complete.

“Come now,” Rosceline said.

Zélie stared at her.

“Walk to the door with me. You are Dame Olyne to everyone in the house. Tell them you are taking the air.”

The longing was so visible that Rosceline thought she had won. Zélie put both feet under her and shifted forward. Then she stopped.

“Where will you take me?”

“Somewhere Sel cannot close the door.”

“To the woman in my body.”

“Eventually.”

“And after she gets hers back? What will she tell them I did?”

Rosceline could have said she would manage Olyne. It was the kind of promise she made beautifully. She could probably have made it sound like shelter.

“I can arrange an advocate,” she said. “I cannot promise what another woman will say.”

Zélie sat back. The short distance made the offer she had nearly accepted seem farther away than the front door.

“I need to think.”

“You have already been thinking for several days.”

“They have not been my days.”

Rosceline looked at the ring. A bead of blood stood where its edge had broken skin.

She asked for soap. This time she kept the maid in the room. She worked the ring down gently, waited for the finger to ease, then used the soap and a slow twisting pressure to bring it over the knuckle. Zélie gripped the chair with her other hand and made no sound.

The ring came free. Rosceline laid it beside the peaches.

“Keep it where she can find it,” she said.

Zélie touched the pale band on the finger. She looked older for a moment, though the face had not changed.

At the door Rosceline accepted the chalk paste and paid for it. Outside, Daska was leaning against a gatepost, eating a peach she had obtained by some means of her own.

“Did you get her?”

“No.”

“Did she want to come?”

Rosceline took her rapier back. “Very much. That was not sufficient.”

They walked down to the canal. Rosceline carried the unused fruit in the crook of her arm. At the first bridge she gave the basket to a woman who had been waiting with three empty water cans. The woman looked at the peaches, then asked whether there was anything wrong with them.

“No,” Rosceline said, more sharply than she intended. “They are good peaches.”

Chapter 8 — Paid for the whole object

The man at the Gemcutters’ desk would not look at Ilaro’s question until he had inspected the paper on which it was written.

“This is a household introduction.”

“Yes.”

“For Ravenswood.”

“The event is sponsored by your guild.”

“Jointly.”

“I have not asked you to sponsor it again. I am asking where the identification hearing is being held.”

The clerk set the introduction down. His desk stood in a passage through which porters kept bringing wooden trays covered in cloth. Something on one tray clicked and slid. The porter carrying it froze, shut his eyes and waited for the sound to stop before continuing.

“There is no identification hearing on today’s calendar.”

“There was one this morning.”

“Then it would be finished.”

Ilaro placed one hand flat on the desk. He had slept poorly. His ear still held the crossbow’s little snap from the previous night, separated from the other sounds as though somebody had put it in a box for him.

“Please get the calendar.”

The clerk looked at his hand, at the introduction and finally at his face. He went into the office behind him.

Pello stood by the wall. Through the open doors Ilaro could see a woman sitting on the public stair with a bundle in her lap. People stepped around her. A boy beside her was trying to mend a string sandal with a bit of cord. He put a knot in the wrong place, undid it and began again.

Guildmistress Kavra came out instead of the clerk.

“Selci,” she said. “You were at the entertainment.”

“At the supper.”

“I am told you left quickly.”

“I was being shot at.”

“An accusation best made with particulars.”

“I have particulars.”

She led them into a room with a stone table and a high window. He was relieved by the absence of polished surfaces. Kavra gestured for him to sit, then sat opposite with enough distance between them to make a conversation feel like an appointment.

“The proposed hearing has been deferred,” she said. “There was a dispute over the petitioners’ standing and the manner in which the supposed witness was to be presented. I am not going to rehearse that dispute for someone employed by the Countess.”

“I am asking you to hear an account of an active working.”

He had left Kestra’s statement locked in Briska’s cupboard. He told Kavra what he had heard from Rosceline: the woman publicly known as Olyne had identified herself as Zélie, described an exchange, explained the return and refused immediate departure because she feared prosecution. Rosceline was willing to give that account under her own name.

Kavra listened. Her thumb moved over her signet once, then stopped.

“Dame Olyne has signed an assurance that she is well.”

“Which woman signed it?”

“You understand the difficulty.”

“I am trying to have you act on it.”

She stood and took the assurance from a cabinet. He recognized the shape of Olyne’s name from Mestrel’s book. This version had been made by a hand copying the shape rather than repeating a habit. That was his judgment, not something he could prove merely by pointing at it.

“Let Mestrel show you the acknowledged fittings,” he said. “With someone from your office present. Keep both papers.”

Kavra looked at the signature for a long time.

“And then?”

“Ask the woman the questions privately. Without Sel. Without the director.”

“I have requested it.”

“Demand it.”

“We do not own Ravenswood. Nor does the Countess, though she dislikes being reminded of it. The hosts will receive Olyne’s business associates in the presence of her selected companion. The selected companion is Sel.”

Pello moved his weight from one foot to the other. Ilaro heard the scrape and was grateful that he had not spoken.

Kavra returned the assurance to its folder.

“I can obtain a private meeting before the final dance, with named witnesses from both guilds. I can have an escort bring the claimant. If either woman will give the account you describe, there are grounds to stop the performance. If they both deny it in front of the witnesses, we will have a different problem.”

“It is possible to be frightened into signing a denial.”

“I know.”

There was impatience in it, but something else too. She pressed the folder flat, aligning its corners exactly.

“Half my members want the Alchemists’ closed works to remain closed. Orun thinks the allegations come from them. The proprietor of Ravenswood has spent a considerable sum getting two hostile guilds into his ballroom. He will not permit his guests to be seized because my people have found a woman who says she is somebody else.”

“Your mask is already fitted,” Ilaro said.

Kavra’s hand went to the hollow beneath her throat before she stopped it.

“I will not wear it,” she said.

“Tell the others why.”

“I will tell them that the fitting has given me a rash. If I tell them I suspect the performance, Sel will hear it before the hour ends. I would prefer her to remain available for questions.”

It was an answer he disliked and could work with. He asked that the private meeting be made early enough to matter. She agreed. He asked for permission to inspect the unused faces while her witnesses were present. She said she would include it in the request, which was not permission.

On the way out he passed the woman on the stair again. The boy had finished his sandal. The knot held while he pulled at it. Ilaro wanted, briefly and foolishly, to be leaving with that much accomplished.

The Countess received them without the drawing-room cakes.

Rosceline had come ahead with her own account. Daska sat beside the window, arms folded. Briska’s right forearm was wrapped in a clean bandage more to cushion it than to contain blood. Pello put the damaged scowman’s demand on a side table and did not ask for an answer until the larger conversation was finished.

“The return must happen first,” Ilaro said.

“Then it must,” the Countess answered.

“At Ravenswood. With both women and the fitted masks.”

“You have the time until the final entertainment. Take the face afterward.”

Daska leaned forward. “Where do you intend to keep it?”

“Here.”

“Who will be allowed to use it?”

The Countess looked at her. “That is not part of the commission.”

“It has become part of whether I want to finish it.”

For a while Ilaro could hear the water clock in the adjoining room. The little cup filled, tipped and began filling again.

“Would you prefer that Vaudrin keep it?” the Countess asked.

“No,” Daska said. “That isn’t the other choice.”

Rosceline moved in her chair. She did not interrupt.

The Countess’s voice remained level. “I pay people to obtain things I consider worth owning. Sometimes they refuse. You have refused me before.”

“Not after meeting the people you burned out.”

“They have every reason to hate me.”

“You say that as though it makes the rest simpler.”

Celise looked toward Daska. The Countess raised one hand, barely.

“I do not expect you to settle their grievance. I expect you to tell me whether you intend to deliver the object you agreed to recover.”

Ilaro felt the conversation tightening around that answer. Briska had taken a piece of cloth from her pocket and was rolling its edge between two fingers. Pello watched the Countess’s face.

“We intend to attempt the recovery,” Ilaro said. “We have learned a hazard that changes how. We are telling you before we act. I am not going to promise a result while four people I trust are still arguing about it.”

“Five,” Rosceline said.

He looked at her. She gave a small, irritated nod.

The Countess accepted a delay. She did not amend the delivery requirement. The advance remained earned; the balance remained conditional. The agreed expenses would be paid whether the instrument arrived or not, provided they were expenses she had actually agreed to.

Pello slid the scowman’s demand across the table.

“Ten marks for being fired at,” she read.

“He brought the boat back.”

She looked at him, then at Rosceline. “So I understand.”

She marked the demand for payment. Pello took the endorsed paper, folded it once and put it inside his coat.

As they rose, Ilaro asked whether the warning to Estrenne had received an answer.

“Not from her,” the Countess said. “Her eldest son sent a courteous reply assuring me that her affairs were in excellent hands.”

“I would like her address.”

The Countess gave it to him.

At the outer door Daska waited while Rosceline fastened her wrap. Neither woman spoke. Ilaro went down the steps slowly, letting his sight settle before he trusted the next one. Pello stayed beside him without offering an arm. Behind them the house doors closed, and the air outside smelled of somebody frying onions for a meal that would cost less than the repairs to Rosceline’s hem.

Chapter 9 — Other people’s hands

Briska’s workshop door stood open when she got back.

She had locked it. She remembered locking it because the lower bolt had stuck and she had promised herself, again, that a person paid to repair other people’s locks ought to repair her own.

The door had not been forced. The top bolt lay withdrawn. The bottom had been lifted with the exact narrow pressure she used when it was troublesome.

Inside, a man sat on the stool beside the cooling furnace. Her furnace. Her stool.

Thane stood when she came in.

“I was told you would be back.”

“By whom?”

“A woman who said she lent you this place.”

“I rent it.”

“She seemed to believe the arrangement left her some rights.”

Briska put down her case. “It does not leave you any.”

“She opened the upper door. I didn’t want to leave it unsecured.”

“So you opened the lower one.”

“It sticks.”

She looked at his right hand. The fingers were curled close enough that the thin brass wedge he had left on the table had clearly been worked with the other. Her annoyance found no useful purchase there and returned to his face.

“What do you want?”

“A meeting. I was told the woman who talks would come here.”

“That description will not narrow your search in Silkstrand.”

“The one who caught the spoon last night before it fell, then let the actor pretend he had dropped it.”

Briska had not seen that. She did not like learning a new thing Rosceline had done from this man.

He saw the bandage on her forearm. “The staff?”

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You weren’t holding it.”

“No.”

He left the apology there. She found that harder to dismiss than an explanation.

The bronze lifting fork lay wrapped in her case. She took it out to examine the stem and immediately regretted giving him something interesting to look at. He came nearer, keeping his hands away.

“You took it.”

“You took a whole mask.”

“I took a cart down a lane. Sariel took the mask.”

“How carefully you divide the work.”

“It helps when somebody asks where things are.”

She set the fork in a padded vise. Its square stem had a burr that she intended to remove before using it again. Thane watched her lay out the fine files.

“You’re going to lengthen the handle,” he said.

“It needs better leverage.”

“It needs a broader grip.”

“For you.”

“For anyone who has had to hold that glass wing while somebody works behind it.”

He laid his good hand against the handle without touching it. The original design required the wrist to remain turned inward under strain. Her longer handle would improve the force but preserve the awkward angle.

“A strap here,” he said. “Or a crosspiece. Something you can put the heel of your hand against. Otherwise you have a very good tool for someone who isn’t frightened, isn’t hurt, and has both hands free.”

“I had both hands free until you arrived.”

“That seems unlikely to remain the case.”

She wanted him out. She also wanted to see whether the crosspiece could fit without fouling the glass. The two wants occupied an irritating amount of the same space.

“Draw it,” she said.

He drew with his left hand, slowly, making the long lines first and returning to the short ones. She could see where he would once have moved more quickly. He did not look at her while she watched.

By the time Pello arrived with the others, she had rejected the strap and improved the crosspiece. Thane had accepted the improvement without becoming less annoying.

Rosceline stopped in the doorway. Daska looked past her, saw him and said, “Where is the large one?”

“Benjamin? Busy.”

“Tell him his foot was too far back.”

“He said you would say something about his feet.”

Daska laughed. Briska looked up from the drawing and wished, briefly, that people would stop enjoying themselves in her workshop while she was trying to be offended.

Thane gave Rosceline a folded message. She read it without offering it to anyone else, then read it aloud. A meeting the following afternoon, at a laundry closed for repairs. Three entrances. No extra men. The women’s identities and return to be discussed. Nothing on the paper promised a truce beyond the meeting itself.

“What an enticing invitation,” Rosceline said. “Should we bring clean linen?”

“Bring what you mean to say.”

She studied him. “You are very confident that we mean it.”

“No. That is why we’re asking you to say it where everyone can hear.”

He left before she could decide whether to admire the answer. Briska watched him pull the lower bolt clear from outside, ease its crooked plate with a small wedge and shut the door. It closed without sticking.

She went over and opened it again. The plate had not been repaired. A strip of leather had been tucked behind the loose corner to hold it true until the screws could be reset.

“Temporary,” she said.

Nobody had asked.

They ate around the workbench because Rosceline had bought food and insisted that it be eaten while it was worth the price. The flatbread was warm, with chopped herbs and cheese folded inside. Pello brought a jar of olives from his boat. Daska chose the cracked ones first because, she said, they had absorbed more of the good oil.

Ilaro sat on the one sound chair. The stool was too low for his headache. He had placed Kestra’s statement where all five could read it and had told them precisely what she had permitted him to do with it.

They read while they ate. There was no need to repeat the whole thing aloud.

Rosceline left a piece of bread untouched beside her plate. Briska saw her looking at a description of the shoes that had not fitted the woman after she crossed. Then Rosceline turned the page and asked Pello how much sound canvas cost.

“For what?”

“Your boat. You mentioned a cover.”

He gave her a figure. She frowned.

“That is less than I expected.”

“That’s the canvas.”

“And the rest?”

He began naming paint, tar, oil, a damaged rowlock and the small fees charged by people who owned the right to let a boat remain still. Rosceline took her pen from its case. Pello stopped.

“I have it.”

“I was going to add it.”

“So did I.”

She put the pen down. After a moment she slid the untouched bread toward him. He took it.

Daska had threaded a thick needle through the torn shoe. She drew the leather edges together, tested the stitch with her thumb and made a dissatisfied sound.

“It was hired,” Briska said.

“Yes.”

“You’ll still have to pay.”

“I intend to wear it again before I pay.”

She pulled the thread hard enough to close the split. The repair was ugly and strong. Briska could respect one of those qualities.

After the food, Briska lit the smaller forge. She touched the flawed little valve casting that hung beside it, no longer pausing to decide whether anyone was watching, and put the new handle blank into the heat. The first color came along its edge. She liked this part: the short period in which heat made a piece willing to do what its cold shape would resist.

Rosceline moved her plate out of range. Ilaro began a question as Briska lifted the first hammer.

Three blows rang through the workshop. He stopped speaking. She finished the bend before looking up.

“What?”

He pressed two fingers to the closed notebook before him.

“I’ve lost it. Could you stop a moment?”

She put the hammer down. The iron would cool too far and need heating again. It annoyed her. She kept the annoyance to herself and waited.

Outside, a wheel rattled along the lane. Daska pulled another stitch through the shoe. Ilaro looked at the message Thane had left.

“The glass,” he said. “The position of the two people. You said the frame moves downstairs.”

“On the final morning.”

“Are we certain the return can happen after it moves?”

Briska looked at the drawing. They had been speaking as though a frame and a room were interchangeable because the frame could be carried. She set the cooled handle blank aside.

“No,” she said. “I need to ask that.”

Rosceline leaned forward. “Zélie described places marked on the floor. I assumed they went with the performance.”

“We should stop assuming it,” Briska said.

She added the question to the drawing. Ilaro nodded, satisfied enough to leave the rest until they had something to answer it with.

The knock came after dusk.

Pello opened the door. Elena stood outside with her sleeves rolled up and one cuff darkened by drying blood. She glanced past him, counted faces and came in only when Daska moved away from the doorway.

“I need to know about your boat.”

“Which one?” Pello asked.

“The one you expect to carry her in.”

He looked at the cuff. “Is she worse?”

“This isn’t hers.”

Elena did not explain whose it was. Rosceline waited a moment, then drew out a chair with her foot.

“Sit down before you inform us how inadequate we are.”

Elena sat. She took the offered water and drank all of it.

The woman in the young body had an injured ankle and a worsening bruise along her ribs. She could manage a short level walk. She could not safely climb down a long ladder, jump into a boat or be swung under the arms by someone who believed speed was a form of treatment.

“If that’s your plan, change it.”

Pello looked at Briska’s sketch of the canal steps.

“Two steps at low water. Four at high. She’ll have to be carried.”

“On something flat.”

“There is no room in my boat.”

“Then not your boat.”

He rubbed a spot of oil from his thumb, considering. Briska had seen people mistake that silence for agreement. Elena did not. She waited until he looked up.

“How flat?”

She put the empty cup down and began showing him with her hands.

Chapter 10 — The laundry

Rosceline had once been entertained in a bathhouse whose owner kept a trained singer beneath the floor. The singer’s voice rose through the hot tiles, intimate and apparently bodiless, until a customer spilled scented oil through a crack and provoked language which the owner subsequently called an invocation. She thought of it as she entered the laundry. The same damp warmth clung to stone. Here it smelled of soap, wet wool and a stopped drain.

The room had three doors, as promised. Pello inspected all three before anyone sat. Benjamin inspected them after him. Neither man seemed offended by the duplication.

There was a long table used for folding linen. The Coin had brought five stools. Rosceline’s company had brought none.

“A generous arrangement,” she said.

Sariel moved two stools to the other side. Benjamin gave up his to Ilaro and sat on the edge of an empty washing trough. Daska perched beside him, tested the stone with her palm and asked whether it would survive them both.

“It survives full laundry,” he said.

“That depends what you put in the pockets.”

He smiled despite himself. Rosceline took a stool and spread her skirts where they would not meet the damp floor. She had worn the older dress. It was becoming a more useful garment with every job.

Sariel put a wrapped object on the table. She opened the cloth far enough to show a blue-and-white cheek and the pale lining of a mask.

“This stays with me.”

“I had expected an offer of tea first,” Rosceline said.

“The water’s off.”

It was a good answer. She allowed herself a smile and received nothing in return.

Kestra had laid out a drawing made on the back of a freight notice. Briska unfolded hers beside it. The two drawings disagreed over the width of the performance frame and agreed on almost everything that mattered.

“We need to establish the move downstairs,” Briska said. “Does it take the return with it?”

Kestra touched the heavy feet in her drawing. “The marked boards are fixed to the base. The position marks are on those boards. There is a prepared line in the glass rims and in the central mounting. The room doesn’t supply the position.”

“How do you know?”

“I examined the edge while you were having the fight.”

“You were having the fight as well.”

“I was looking under it.”

Thane pointed to a place on Briska’s plan. “This is where they fasten the carrying bars. The stage carpenter has the order. The base goes down whole, not in sections.”

Rosceline watched Briska accept the information. Her companion’s pleasure in a sound answer was sometimes indistinguishable from annoyance that someone else had supplied it.

Ilaro asked about the two women. They gave their accounts without names for the places Olyne had slept. Rosceline repeated what Zélie had told her, including the payment, the signed assurance and the refusal to come away from the conservatory. Elena listened with a face that grew more tired at the familiar parts.

“She asked you that too,” Rosceline said.

“About being arrested?”

“You have spoken.”

“Yes.”

“You might have told us.”

“You might have asked before trying to buy her silence.”

Rosceline felt heat rise under her collar. “That is not what I offered her.”

“Then you both had a better afternoon than she expected.”

It was said without satisfaction. That made it harder to answer. Rosceline moved to the arrangements.

Olyne would come under Kavra’s limited escort if the guild could secure the meeting. The impostor in her old body would already be there. Zélie’s yellow bird face remained in the sponsor’s dressing suite, kept in a shallow green box. Sariel held Olyne’s blue face. With the central mask intact, both bodies present and Zélie willing to perform the return sequence, they had a method that someone had practiced.

“She has not actually returned a pair herself,” Ilaro said.

“She has practiced the operation with the face covered,” Kestra answered. “She has watched Vaudrin use it. That is more than anyone else we have.”

“Then we should say exactly that.”

Kestra pressed her lips together. After a moment she nodded.

Rosceline leaned over the table. “We can do this without wasting the last night trying to rob one another. You bring the woman. We bring Zélie and the other face. Briska handles the mounting. Your scholar checks the positions. We complete the return, bring the women out and take the central mask to the Countess.”

Sariel shook her head.

“Then explain your preference.”

“We break it.”

“After the return.”

“After everyone who crossed is back.”

“You will have to demonstrate that breaking it is safer than keeping it secured.”

“Where?”

“In a guarded house.”

“Whose?”

Rosceline let the answer show in her expression. Sariel waited for her to say it.

“The Countess’s.”

“And who guards her?”

The question was old, obvious and well chosen. Rosceline had heard versions of it from people demanding guarantees no living arrangement could supply. She had also used it herself when somebody else stood to profit.

“Every object can be misused,” she said. “The purpose of custody is to make misuse more difficult. You are proposing an irreversible act on an instrument none of us completely understands.”

“You’re proposing to give it to someone we do understand.”

“You know what she did to you.”

Sariel’s face changed. Rosceline knew, a breath too late, how the sentence had sounded.

“I know what she did to Hest,” Sariel said. “And Orren. And the people who were still inside when the roof went.”

Benjamin put down the cup he had been turning between his hands. He had brought water, despite the closed laundry, and had not yet offered it around.

“Hest opened locks,” Sariel continued. “I have taught apprentices who have apprentices now. I still show them how she taught me to file a spring without taking the temper out. Orren used to make us carry things properly because he’d carried too many people who couldn’t walk afterward. They went to a room she had arranged for them to enter. The safe was empty. The heat came through the wall.”

Rosceline could see the room without wanting to. A safe placed conveniently. A place to kneel. The other people arriving where the planner had left room for them.

“They got out of that,” Sariel said. “They didn’t get out of the guildhouse.”

No one corrected her or supplied the better version. The laundry’s stopped drain gurgled behind the wall.

“I am not asking you to forgive her,” Rosceline said.

“You’re asking us to leave her this.” Sariel touched the blue mask through its wrapping. “The next time she wants someone to say yes, she won’t need to put his daughter in a room first.”

Celise’s careful handwriting came into Rosceline’s mind. No person of the returning company forms part of the commission. She had been pleased with the precision of that sentence.

She had no useful answer to Sariel that did not begin by pretending the Countess lacked imagination.

“There are other houses,” she said at last. “Other keepers.”

“Name one she cannot buy, threaten or wait out.”

“And you can assure me that no one will ever make another mask?”

“No,” Sariel said. “I can stop her getting this one.”

Rosceline looked toward Ilaro. He had put his pen down. Daska watched Sariel with the expression she had worn at Rell’s body after the killing was no longer something she could prevent. Rosceline felt a sudden, ugly wish to remind everyone in the room that Sariel had killed men herself. The wish passed before she spoke. She did not know which men, or why, and even as she reached for it she could hear how little it would answer.

Benjamin lifted the jug. “Water?”

Rosceline almost refused. Her mouth was dry enough to make the refusal absurd. She accepted a cup. He poured for Ilaro, then Elena, then offered the jug to Daska, who took it and passed it farther down the table.

For a little while the meeting concerned cups.

Thane had found the repaired crosspiece on Briska’s drawing. He asked whether she had allowed for the glass rim when the tool turned. She told him exactly how much clearance she had allowed. He pointed out that the glass hung lower when its hinge was worn. She shifted the drawing toward him and gave him her pencil.

Elena leaned across to Pello. “Have you found another boat?”

“A lighter. Not hired yet.”

“If we need to put her down for a moment, I need somewhere clear.”

“That is what the lighter is.”

“Before the boat.”

He studied the plan, then marked an alcove beside the loading door. “Here. But don’t put anybody across this passage. It’s the turn from the stair.”

She moved the mark with the end of her finger. “Here, then.”

He nodded.

Rosceline watched the work proceeding across an argument none of them had won. She wanted to take it as evidence that an accommodation remained possible. She had spent enough years obtaining accommodations to recognize how easily that wish could become an assumption about what other people would eventually surrender.

“We cannot agree about the object,” she said.

Sariel set down her cup. “No.”

“Can we agree that neither woman is to be forced out of the city in the other’s body?”

“Yes.”

“Neither paired face destroyed while they remain crossed. Neither woman handed to Sel or Vaudrin.”

“Yes.”

“And if one of us gets the central face first, we do not leave with it before they have had the chance to return.”

Sariel looked at her companions. Kestra nodded. Elena did not hesitate. Benjamin’s face remained troubled, but he nodded too. Thane lifted the pencil from Briska’s paper.

“We can agree to that,” Sariel said.

“It is not permission to steal from us afterward.”

“I didn’t ask permission.”

Rosceline laughed. She had not intended to. The sound was brief, annoyed and real.

“No. You have been scrupulously clear about that.”

They wrote the practical points down because the next night would not improve anyone’s memory. Both companies kept a copy. No one signed away a right to object. The papers contained names of doors, places to put injured people and the two masks each side would bring.

As the meeting broke up, Sariel rewrapped the blue-and-white face. Beneath the cloth Rosceline glimpsed a worn leather case with narrow tool pockets. One had been patched in a different hand from the rest.

She could have asked whether it had been Hest’s. She let the question go.

Outside, Daska waited until the others had reached the corner.

“You nearly had her convinced,” she said.

Rosceline looked at her sharply. Daska was not smiling.

“No,” Rosceline said. “I nearly had myself convinced that she ought to be.”

Daska walked beside her without answering. The laundry door closed behind them. A cart came rattling over the bridge, and both women moved into a doorway to let it pass. Rosceline smelled clean linen beneath the cart’s tarpaulin and envied whoever would sleep in it without first deciding whom to disappoint.

Part III — The guests become useful

Chapter 11 — A second boat

On the morning of the reconciliation, the lighter’s owner wanted payment before she heard why they needed it.

Pello said they needed to carry people and equipment from Ravenswood. The owner asked how many people. Briska looked at Pello. He looked back at her, then told the woman to allow for twelve.

“Alive?”

“We would prefer it,” Briska said.

The woman put down the brush with which she had been scouring a cookpot. Her name was Rossa. She had two boats, a bad knee and a husband whose principal occupation appeared to be making her repeat instructions already given quite clearly. He was sitting on an upturned bucket repairing a net none of her boats used.

“You can have the flat one,” she said. “With me aboard. I don’t lend a boat and hope it remembers the way home.”

Pello stepped onto the lighter’s deck. It dipped under him without rolling. Briska could see him judging the freeboard, the beam and the low coaming around the cargo space. There was room for a carrying board down the middle and people sitting along either side. No mast stood in the way.

“The tiller?” he asked.

“Works.”

He tried it. Rossa watched, then held out her hand for the deposit.

The hire would consume more than Briska had expected. Pello had brought the Countess’s written approval for the amount. Rossa read it, said she did not take payment in promises from the Heights and waited while Pello counted actual money from the common expense purse.

“After the evening meal,” he said. “At the loading door. Stay outside bowshot until I call you in.”

“You could have put that part first.”

“Would it have changed the price?”

“It just did.”

They negotiated the addition without assistance from Briska. She was glad to let a man who understood boats argue with a woman who owned one. Rossa’s husband looked up once, began to offer advice and returned to his net when she turned her head.

Mallow lay a little farther down the quay, its patched cover stretched on a temporary frame. Pello went aboard to fetch a measuring pole. He ran his hand along the new-painted gunwale before stepping off again. Briska pretended not to see. She knew the temptation to touch work that had finally become yours.

“You’re leaving that one here?”

“My cousin will use her for messages. He knows not to take the cargo hatch.”

“Why would he?”

“He needs one.”

Rossa laughed. Pello did not.

By the time they reached Ravenswood, the carrying bars had been removed from the performance frame and a carpenter was bolting its feet to the stage. The ballroom smelled of fresh-cut boards beneath yesterday’s wine. Bunches of white flowers lay on a table waiting to have their bruised stems hidden with ribbon. The sponsor’s costumes were coming down from the gallery suite to a ground-floor dressing room off the east service corridor. A maid carrying the green box waited for the carpenter to clear her doorway.

The steward met Briska at the service door with a sheet of paper held between two fingers.

“You are named as an inspecting mechanic.”

“I am.”

“There will be no repetition of the other evening.”

“Then ask your men not to fire at the boat.”

“They were responding to a theft.”

“Was the boat the stolen object?”

He folded the paper. Kavra’s request had been accepted reluctantly, with conditions sufficient to fill the lower half of the sheet. A private identification meeting before the closing dance. Two guild witnesses. The claimant and her escorts. An inspection of the fitted instruments by Selci and the mechanic named in the Countess’s household introduction. No removal of property without agreement from the host.

Briska read that last condition twice. It would have been polite to pretend it was not specifically for her.

Pello carried the tool case past the steward. On its outside hung the bronze lifting fork, now fitted with the broad crosspiece. Vaudrin saw it from the stage.

“My tool.”

“Your damaged tool,” Briska said. “Repaired. I have brought it back.”

“You removed it without asking.”

“I was leaving under some pressure.”

He came close enough for her to see the red veins in the whites of his eyes. He had not slept well either.

“You will leave it here.”

“It is listed among the instruments for the inspection.” She held up the guild paper. “You may stand beside me while I use it.”

The steward cleared his throat. Vaudrin looked at him, then stepped away.

The frame had traveled intact. So had the narrow boards joined to its base. Briska saw the marks now: two worn shapes where the players placed their feet, brass nails at the heels, a dull painted line that passed beneath the central mounting. The whole thing resembled a door through which people had repeatedly tried to walk in the wrong direction.

The glass wings opened inward toward the marked places. Turned on their hinges, their unpainted backs could face the occupants instead. Behind the crimson face was a silver tongue no longer than Briska’s thumb. It had two settled positions and a narrow stretch between them in which it resisted pressure. Zélie had described it accurately.

Briska did not turn it. She inspected the hardware around it, checked the lower saddle and closed the glass again.

“Your left hinge is cracking out of the wood,” she told Vaudrin. “It must be strapped before you put it under strain.”

“Do it.”

“I need the glass open.”

He unlocked it. She worked while he watched. Pello held the lamp; daylight from the ballroom windows did not reach the inner recess well enough. When she finished, the new iron strap showed plainly against the dark wood. Vaudrin asked whether it could be painted.

“After it has been used and inspected,” she said.

He swallowed his answer.

The Coin’s people arrived while the stewards were inspecting the afternoon seating. Sariel carried the flat blue bag under one arm. Benjamin carried a rolled blanket and a board the width of a narrow bed. Thane had brought the modified glass-door catch in a small cloth bundle. Elena was absent. Kestra came last, waiting while a young guild escort read their names off a paper.

Briska asked where their patient was.

“Coming with the other witness,” Sariel said.

It was enough of an answer to proceed with. Briska and Thane checked the door catch together. He had made a loop that could be pressed down with the heel of a hand while the catch was lifted by the other. She pointed out a projecting corner. He took the part back, filed it blunt and held it for her to test.

Neither apologized for having improved the other’s work.

Kestra stood at the turn from the rear stage stair to the service corridor. She wore a small bronze branching-road disc on blue wool. From her pocket she took a piece of chalk and a wrapped glass that she set where it would not be kicked.

“We need to walk the route,” she said.

“It’s thirty paces,” Briska answered.

“Then it won’t take us long.”

They carried the board between them: Benjamin at one end, Pello at the other, Daska trying the width at the turn. Briska followed with the case. Kestra walked beside them, stopping at each place where someone carrying a body might have to look down instead of ahead. She marked the safe line low on the wall and the corner of a fixed step.

The route ended at the treatment alcove near the canal door. Beyond that, ordinary hands would carry people over the threshold and down to the boat. No pale path would make the steps less steep.

“How long does it last?” Pello asked.

“About ten minutes. I can renew it at the start if the marks remain. If something falls across it, I have to check the ground again.”

“And the door?”

“Still has to be opened.”

“Good.”

She glanced at him. “Most people seem disappointed by that.”

He tried the loading door, which opened fully now, and let it shut on the latch.

Kestra rubbed the edge of the bronze disc. Briska noticed that its hole had been recently drilled and that the wool was almost new.

“A borrowed thing?” she asked.

“The first one was. I had to return it. This came through Three Oaks.”

“Does it work as well?”

“I do it as well. Some days.”

Briska liked her more for the answer than she intended to show.

They rehearsed the carry again. This time Daska lay on the board. She folded her hands over her stomach and instructed Benjamin not to display her feet to the expensive people. He carried her through the turn without striking either wall. At the alcove she sat up and announced that she had known funerals with worse service.

Then Vaudrin came to find out why his backstage passage had become a hospital.

“Someone might faint,” Rosceline said from behind him.

She had arrived unnoticed by Briska, dressed for the evening in the repaired gown. A narrow dark panel concealed the tear behind her knee. She turned so that it caught the light like a deliberate choice.

“Your entertainment has been described to me as unforgettable. We are preparing to believe it.”

Vaudrin looked at Daska on the carrying board. Daska smiled up at him.

“I am already overcome.”

He walked away. Rosceline waited until he had reached the ballroom.

“Do not antagonize him unnecessarily,” she said.

Briska looked at her.

“I said unnecessarily.”

Chapter 12 — The reconciliation

The house filled slowly, then all at once. Rosceline watched the moment at which waiting ceased to be fashionable and everyone who had been waiting in a boat decided to arrive.

The west windows stood open. Evening light made the canal look almost clean. Lamps burned inside glass globes along the walls, their flames sheltered from the draft. White flowers concealed the stage’s new supports. Somewhere under the perfume and hot wax was the good smell of butter browning in a pan.

She had eaten before coming. Daska had called that defeatist. Rosceline had called it experience.

Ilaro was in the little receiving room beside the north entrance, with the fittings list, a shaded lamp and permission to ask questions of the attendants. Briska remained backstage. Pello had gone to bring the lighter closer. The Coin’s patient had arrived under escort and been taken down to the costume store, where Elena had insisted on space and a chair with a back. Rosceline knew none of the details beyond those she needed. She found the restraint inconvenient and was trying to become used to it.

At the first dance, Benjamin asked Daska to stand with him.

Rosceline heard only the end of the exchange.

“You tried to hit me with a staff.”

“You were holding a sword.”

“I am not holding one now.”

“Then I expect you’ll be easier to lead.”

They went onto the floor. Benjamin placed his hand carefully at Daska’s waist, received some instruction too quiet to hear, and shifted it a little. Daska began smiling before the musicians reached the second phrase.

He could dance. That was not the same as having memorized the steps. When a couple beside them turned too widely, he moved Daska out of the collision without interrupting her figure. She looked up at him with surprise, then allowed him to try something more ambitious. He nearly succeeded. She laughed, caught his wrist and finished the turn herself.

Sariel stood near the edge of the floor with a folded cloth over one arm. A guest gave her an empty cup. She took it, set it on a tray and continued toward the sponsor’s suite. Rosceline watched the borrowed authority of that small errand carry her past a guard who would otherwise have asked her name.

She was good. It would have been pleasant to hire her for something in a city where neither woman knew the Countess.

“You approve of the dancing?”

Sel had appeared at her shoulder. Tonight she wore deep red. The color changed as she moved, dark along the body and almost luminous at the folds. Rosceline wondered which dyer had managed it and whether the supply of clean water had affected the price.

“I approve of anybody keeping the beat without arguing about it.”

“Then you will be disappointed when the guilds speak.”

Sel offered her a glass. Rosceline took it and touched it to her lips without drinking. She could see the stage reflected in a long mirror behind Sel. Sel could see the sponsor’s door in the dark window behind Rosceline. Neither chose to change position.

“Your mechanic is thorough,” Sel said.

“A defect I have tried to correct. She persists in wanting things to work.”

“The director says she takes instruction poorly.”

“Only inaccurate instruction. It is unfortunate how much of that is offered.”

Sel smiled. “And you?”

“I take admiration very well.”

“Instruction.”

“Less often.”

The singers began a short welcoming piece. Their voices rose cleanly above the dancers’ last steps. Rosceline could feel the room lean toward them. There was skill in the way Sel had placed the voices, a low one beneath two brighter lines, and a restraint about the final harmony that made people want it to continue.

“I knew a woman in Fairport who spoke of you,” Sel said when the song ended.

Rosceline kept her eyes on the dancers leaving the floor.

“I hope she had something original to contribute.”

“She said you disliked being mistaken for a woman with fewer accomplishments.”

“Most women do.”

“She also said you were very good at being somebody else.”

Rosceline turned her head. Sel had allowed the remark to sit between them without pointing at it. A less able woman would have touched her arm.

“It must be exhausting,” Sel added, “working for people who regard talent as a useful household possession.”

“You appear to manage.”

The smile stayed, but something in Sel’s eyes hardened.

“There are houses where a capable woman is introduced as a guest. I could arrange one.”

“For what?”

“Conversation to begin with.”

“Then you should invite Ilaro. He insists on beginning there.”

“I am inviting you.”

Across the room, Kavra removed a glove and scratched the skin along her wrist. Orun was refusing a second glass. Havret stood near the stage in a black fox mask pushed up on his head, making an attendant laugh with a story that used his whole body. Rosceline watched him take a backward step and avoid a musician’s case without looking. He had good control of where he ended.

“Your player is enjoying himself,” she said.

“He always enjoys the first part.”

“What part do you enjoy?”

Sel looked toward Olyne’s borrowed face, visible for a moment at the sponsor’s door. A maid said something; the face turned away.

“The part in which people discover they need you.”

Rosceline understood the pleasure well enough to resent its being offered as an introduction.

“An unreliable source of affection.”

“I didn’t say affection.”

The host came to claim Sel for a speech. She excused herself, leaving the glass in Rosceline’s hand. Rosceline set it on a tray and took water from a different servant.

She had wanted the introduction. That was the irritating thing. A room in which she was neither the Countess’s acquisition nor the author of a past its listener had already decided how to admire. She could hear herself talking there, the first flattering mistake corrected lightly, the second permitted because it was useful. She could also hear Sel asking for something afterward.

She went to find Daska.

The speeches were shorter than expected because the host’s youngest child began crying behind an open door and refused to be comforted. Each guild representative found a reason to conclude before the next wail. Daska regarded this as the evening’s best piece of organization.

Benjamin had gone back to the service passage. She stood with Rosceline beside a tray of little savory pies, holding one in each hand.

“He follows,” she said.

“So I saw.”

“In a dance. In a fight he waits until you move and tries to make the place you wanted narrower.”

“Did you ask him that over supper?”

“No. I was there when he did it.”

Rosceline took one of the pies. The crust broke beneath her teeth. It was hot enough to hurt and good enough that she did not immediately regret it.

“Sel has offered to introduce me somewhere,” she said after swallowing.

“Is it better paid?”

“Probably.”

“Did you say yes?”

“No.”

Daska waited.

“I did not say no either.”

“Then I’d like to know before we find ourselves working there.”

“That is why I’m telling you.”

Daska nodded and returned to her pie. Rosceline resented having prepared for an argument and then found herself grateful for its absence.

A young guild escort came through the room looking for Kavra. He found her near the flowers and spoke into her ear. She looked toward the sponsor’s door, then toward the stage. Rosceline set the uneaten half of her pie down.

At the far end of the ballroom, Vaudrin was turning the crimson frame a few degrees toward the chairs reserved for the participating patrons. The instrument had been screened during the speeches. Now its red surface caught every lamp in the room. The face seemed wet.

There were six patron chairs. One stood empty, its cushion still covered by the thin cloth used to keep dust off it. Rosceline could not see Estrenne’s sons anywhere among the people beside it.

Sariel appeared at the service door. She looked at the empty chair, then at the escort and Kavra. Her face gave Rosceline no answer she could safely name.

The first warning came from Ilaro: a folded card brought by the receiving-room attendant.

Meeting delayed. Ask for the woman now.

Rosceline handed it to Daska.

“Stay near the stair,” she said. “If Benjamin knows why, let him tell you.”

“And you?”

Rosceline looked at the sponsor’s door. Sel had put one of her own attendants beside it. The woman held a shawl that was not hers, and her attention moved between the ballroom and the room beyond with the regularity of someone listening for a signal.

“I have been meaning to complain about the chalk paste.”

She crossed the floor with her hand on the place where the stain had been. The music began again behind her, a little faster than the last dance, and she made her pace follow it.

Chapter 13 — The order of the dance

The attendant had taken Ilaro’s card. No answer had come back.

He waited long enough to read the list a second time, then put a clean sheet over the lower half. There were too many corrections visible at once. The person who had made them understood the performance and had therefore neglected to make them intelligible to anyone who did not.

Olyne’s name stood at the head of the patrons. Zélie’s appeared opposite it. Below came Orun and Havret, then Kavra and a player called Isotte. Estrenne’s line had a cross through it, made in a different ink. The remaining two pairs belonged to a wealthy charcoal factor and the host’s grown nephew. The nephew’s opposite number was Vaudrin himself.

Ilaro had noticed that on the first reading. He had not yet decided what it established beyond an appetite for being part of his own performance.

He asked the attendant who had made the cross.

“The caller, sir.”

“Bring her here.”

“She is busy.”

“So are we. Ask which place she would prefer to be busy in.”

The young man went. Ilaro closed the door halfway against the music. Through the gap he could see the lower end of the ballroom, where people were drifting toward supper and the participating chairs. He counted occupied chairs, lost his place when a server crossed his view, and began again using pencil marks on the edge of the page.

The caller arrived with a piece of chalk behind her ear and a string around her wrist from which three small keys hung. She was older than most of the players, younger than she sounded when she asked what had been lost now.

“I’m trying to establish who is actually taking part,” he said.

“Then look at the sheet.”

“I have. It says Estrenne is present.”

“It says Estrenne isn’t present.” She pointed to the cross.

“The cloak attendant has a claim ticket for her.”

“Her sons sent the cloak. They didn’t send the woman.”

“Do you know where she is?”

“No. Nor whether the cloak intends to perform without her. I’ve had enough people ask.”

He pushed the clean page toward her. “Who has already worn a fitted face tonight?”

“The players, in rehearsal. The guests have tried theirs in the side room. Except Kavra, because she says it burns.”

“Has the central face been uncovered during that?”

“No. The director keeps it screened until the figures.”

“Has anyone stood on the marked boards?”

She looked at him more carefully.

“That’s what the figures are.”

“Tonight.”

“Not yet.”

“What time is the private meeting?”

“I don’t call private meetings.”

“Who delayed it?”

“The hostess sent to say she was unwell.”

“Who brought the message?”

“Sel’s girl. The one with the red beads.”

He wrote that down. The caller watched him with growing irritation, but she stayed.

“What is your name?”

“Loude. If this is about the broken cup, I wasn’t there.”

“It isn’t.”

“That makes a change.”

She took the list from him and corrected two entries that had meant something else entirely. The pair assigned to a figure was not the same as the order in which the patrons would be presented. Vaudrin had moved Orun forward because the alchemist wanted to leave early. Kavra’s player would give a solo bow instead. Estrenne’s player had been sent to help change scenery. Olyne was to greet the room without wearing her mask, since the blue face had been stolen.

“Then Zélie will not enter.”

Loude looked toward the door. “Zélie hasn’t been coming down.”

“Where has she been?”

“I don’t know. I get told what parts to fill. If I asked why every player missed a call, we’d never put on the curtains.”

It was a practiced answer that did not quite conceal a real worry.

“Did you see her after the private demonstration?”

“Yes.”

“How was she?”

“Angry. Then frightened. Then she was put in a room and I was told not to disturb her.”

“Did that seem usual?”

“No.”

She said it with sudden force, then looked at the open door as if the word might have traveled farther than she meant.

Ilaro did not fill the pause. At last she asked what he thought had happened.

“I think people have been put in one another’s bodies.”

For a moment she almost laughed. Then the laughter failed to find any support in his face.

“The old taking,” she said softly.

“You know it?”

“I know what the figure is called. We perform it. I’ve performed a man’s execution three hundred times and nobody has cut my head off.”

“What follows the taking?”

“The giving back. Unless it’s the tragedy.”

“What does Vaudrin intend tonight?”

She took the revised sheet and turned it over. On the back, in a finer hand, was a short instruction. Ilaro had assumed it concerned scenery. Loude put her finger beneath it.

“Hold the borrowed station. No return call.”

They stood looking at the words.

“Has he changed it?” Ilaro asked.

“It wasn’t there yesterday.”

The music beyond the door stopped. Applause filled the opening, sharp enough to hurt. Ilaro waited for the first burst to pass. Loude went white.

“That’s early.”

“What is?”

“He’s called for the boards.”

Ilaro took the original sheet. “Come with me.”

“I have to call the players.”

“Tell them not to put on the masks.”

“You don’t understand. He’ll—”

“Tell them you can’t find their places. Anything. Then stay where I can see you.”

He had been too abrupt. He saw the woman draw away from his tone before she had considered his words. He lowered his voice.

“Please. I can explain it to him later. If I’m wrong, he can shout at both of us.”

Loude swallowed. She took the chalk from behind her ear, stared at it as though it were an object whose purpose she had forgotten, then pushed it into her pocket.

They went into the ballroom.

Kavra stood near the unoccupied chair. The cloth still covered its cushion. Ilaro showed her the altered instruction. She read it and looked toward the stage.

“Where is Sel?”

“I don’t know.”

“Where is Olyne?”

“I am trying to find out.”

The guildmistress beckoned an escort. She told him to go to the dressing suite and insist on the meeting. Then she took off the ornamental mask she had been carrying, handed it to her secretary and told him to place it in her bag.

The act did not stop the music beginning again. A short drum roll came from the gallery. Havret ran onto the little stage with his black fox face lowered. He bowed to Master Orun, who wore a silver fish and looked as if he had been persuaded to do something beneath his dignity for a sufficiently public reason.

Loude caught Ilaro’s sleeve.

“They’re the first.”

“The first tonight?”

“Yes.”

He put the original sheet inside the stiff outer pocket of his portfolio. He needed it undamaged, and he needed both hands free. He left the portfolio with Kavra’s secretary, telling him where the sheet was and asking him not to let anyone take it.

Then he went toward the east service door. Somebody tried to detain him with a glass. He declined, stumbled when a guest’s chair moved into his path and steadied himself against the back without apologizing. He could see Daska ahead of him. She had heard a shout from beneath the stage and was moving toward the narrow stair.

“Daska,” he called.

She turned.

“Stop the first pair. Orun and Havret. The first pair.”

She looked at the stage, then at the stair below it. A second shout came up from the store, followed by the hard sound of something striking wood.

“Get Rosceline,” she said, and went down.

Ilaro reached the backstage turn as the screen moved across his view of the frame. Briska was on the other side of it. He could hear her voice, angry and close.

“Take your hand off the catch.”

Then the curtain cords tightened, and the red face appeared between two reflections that did not quite move together.

Chapter 14 — Behind the screen

The woman guarding the sponsor’s door had put on the shawl she was supposed to be holding. That told Rosceline more about her expectations for the evening than her face did.

“I need to see Dame Olyne.”

“She is unwell.”

“How fortunate that I have come before it becomes serious.”

“She is not receiving.”

Rosceline stepped close enough that the woman had to decide whether to move or touch her. The woman chose to touch. Her fingers closed around Rosceline’s forearm.

There were several ways to break the grip. Rosceline chose the quiet one. She caught the woman’s smallest finger beneath her own, pressed it backward and held it at the point where pride began to become expensive.

“Open the door,” she said.

The woman’s breath caught. She lifted her other hand toward Rosceline’s face. Rosceline increased the pressure.

“You are employed to carry a shawl. Consider the salary.”

The hand dropped. The door opened.

Zélie stood beside the window. Olyne’s face looked gray under the cosmetics. A shallow green box lay on the table, its lid open. Inside was a yellow mask with a bird’s hooked nose and fine, soft lining darkened where a living face had rested against it.

“Don’t hurt her,” Zélie said.

Rosceline released the attendant. The woman backed into the corridor holding her hand against her chest.

“Send Sel,” Rosceline told her. “I would enjoy having this conversation with someone paid better.”

The woman went. Rosceline closed the door but left it unlatched.

“The guild witnesses are here. Your meeting has been delayed on the grounds that you are unwell. Did you ask for the delay?”

“No.”

“Will you come?”

Zélie looked at the box. Her ring lay beside it, wrapped in a small piece of clean linen. Without it, the finger had begun to look less angry.

“Is she downstairs?”

“Yes.”

“Does she know I want to return?”

“She knows you are frightened.”

“Those aren’t the same thing.”

“No. Tell her the other one yourself.”

A drum roll came from the ballroom. Zélie turned toward it with a startled, trained attention. Her hand moved toward her throat.

“That is the opening.”

“Then we had better be quick.”

“He was supposed to wait.”

“Come.”

Rosceline put the lid on the green box. She gave it to Zélie, who caught it with both hands and held it close enough to crease the front of Olyne’s gown.

At the door a guild witness met them: a solid woman in a dark collar, carrying the authority of someone who expected to be inconvenienced and paid afterward. Rosceline told her who she was bringing, what the woman wished to say and where the other claimant waited.

“I was told the sponsor would not attend,” the witness said.

“The sponsor was not consulted.”

Zélie nodded. “I want the meeting.”

The witness looked at her closely. She held out an arm. Zélie took it. They moved along the corridor toward the backstage turn.

A crash rose from below the stage. The witness stopped. Benjamin’s voice came up the narrow stair, sharp and immediate. Rosceline could not make out the words. Daska went past them so quickly that her repaired shoe made no sound. She vanished down the steps.

The witness drew Zélie back into the dressing suite.

“Stay here until the passage is clear.”

Rosceline wanted to object. Another man came up the stair backward, blood on his chin. He fell against the wall and put a hand out to stop her going past. She stepped around him and saw the rear of the performance frame through the parted screen.

Briska stood at its left side. Sariel was at the right, reaching through the open glass toward the crimson face.

Rosceline went to them.

“Not without the women,” she said.

“I am not leaving,” Sariel answered. “I’m taking it off.”

“Then give it to Briska.”

“Give her the tool. I’ll hold it.”

The bronze fork sat in its socket. Briska had the crosspiece in both hands. At the front of the stage, hidden from their sight by the folding screen, Havret was making the room laugh. Orun answered something. A gust from the western windows stirred the white flowers at their feet.

Rosceline heard Ilaro calling from the corridor. She could not hear the words.

Sariel’s fingers touched the red edge. Rosceline caught her wrist.

“Take your hand off it.”

“Take yours off me.”

“If you scratch the back—”

Sariel turned under the grip and hit Rosceline with her shoulder. The impact drove the breath from her. She struck the edge of a screen, caught it before it fell, and felt Sariel pull free.

Rosceline caught Sariel’s coat at the collar. She used her own weight, turning rather than hauling. Sariel stumbled one pace away from the frame. A small knife appeared in her hand.

“Put that away,” Rosceline said.

“Let go.”

Briska swore at both of them. The fork had jammed because Sariel’s first pull tilted the face against a hook while Rosceline’s collision shifted the screen into Briska’s elbow. It needed a moment of steady pressure and neither woman was giving her one.

Then Vaudrin’s voice came from the front of the frame.

“Look at your companion.”

Rosceline saw him through the gap beneath the crimson chin. His face had gone very still. One hand lay on the silver tongue behind the mounting.

“Briska,” she said.

The glass brightened without reflecting a brighter lamp.

Her mouth filled with a taste like a bitten coin. She heard Havret draw a breath on the far side, too deep and too sudden. Orun made a small sound. Their feet scraped the marked boards.

Vaudrin spoke a word Rosceline did not know. She knew the pressure behind it. It pulled at the place where a name was held ready for answering. For a moment the people in the ballroom seemed much farther away than the walls permitted.

Then the pressure passed through the glass.

Orun staggered out from behind the forward wing. The silver fish mask tilted against his nose. He tore it upward, breathing fast through his mouth.

“Stop,” he said. “You’ve got it wrong. That isn’t where—”

His voice was still the alchemist’s thin voice. His manner was Havret’s, quick and expansive, trying to fill an awful silence before it filled him. He looked down at his hands. He spread them toward the audience, a comic’s instinctive presentation of the ridiculous.

People laughed.

On the other marked board, the young actor lifted the black fox face and said, with terrifying precision, “Three to twenty. Three to twenty. Give me the calculation again.”

Rosceline let go of Sariel.

A guard rushed toward the older body. Havret, trapped in it, swung with a reach he did not know and missed. The guard caught his right wrist, turned it against the frame and slammed the hand down on the lower rail.

The crack reached Rosceline before the cry.

The audience’s laughter faltered. Sel spoke from somewhere beyond the lamps.

“The indignities of office,” she said warmly, and a few people, grateful for instruction, laughed again.

Rosceline drew her rapier.

The guard saw the blade come through the gap and tried to drag Havret in front of him. Rosceline thrust low, through the cloth of his sleeve where it bulged from the wrist. The point entered and came out. He screamed, released the broken hand and struck the side of the frame with his shoulder as he fell away.

The left glass wing lurched. Briska’s new iron strap held. One of the frame’s feet tore loose from the stage fastening with a splintering sound.

“Hold it!” Briska shouted.

Sariel threw her weight against the wooden upright. Rosceline caught the open glass by its brass rim. The edge bit the heel of her palm. She shifted her grip before it could cut deeply.

Vaudrin reached for the crimson face.

Briska turned the fork. This time the crosspiece gave her enough purchase to lift against the crooked hook. The central face rose a finger’s breadth, slipped free and came into her padded hands.

The sour metal taste vanished from Rosceline’s tongue.

“Back,” Briska said. “I have it.”

Vaudrin made a sound Rosceline had never heard from a man at a respectable entertainment. He reached past the glass for Briska’s throat. Sariel kicked his shin. It was not a graceful kick. It drove him against the screen, and he went through it into the front of the stage.

The audience recoiled. A woman cried out. Someone began clapping, then stopped when the guard with the pierced wrist crawled into view.

“Keep the glasses open,” Briska said. “Don’t let them hit.”

Rosceline obeyed. The edge of one pane trembled beside her cheek. In it she could see her mouth, bleeding where Sariel’s shoulder had struck it, and another reflection that seemed to finish the movement a fraction late.

Sariel had not let go of the upright. Her knife was back in its sheath.

“Did you take it in time?” she asked.

“After them,” Briska said. “Before anyone else stood there.”

“We need to be sure,” Rosceline said.

Havret was crouched beside the frame in Orun’s body, trying to protect the broken hand from his own coat. The real Orun stood in the young actor’s body and stared at the injury. His face had an expression Rosceline did not want to understand.

Sariel looked at the red mask in Briska’s hands, then at the canalward passage.

“We stay,” she said.

Rosceline wiped her lip with her wrist. The blood marked the silk.

“Yes,” she said. “Get something under this damned frame.”

Chapter 15 — Hold the frame

The frame wanted to fall toward the audience. Briska could feel the pull through the upright she held with her shoulder. The crimson face lay on a folded wool cloth in the open case at her feet. Its painted back faced upward, clear of every tool. She had never wanted a lid more badly.

“Thane,” she called.

“I’m here.”

“Under the left foot. The fastening has pulled.”

He knelt on the stage with a wedge in his good hand. The damaged hand held his sleeve out of the way. He drove the wedge beneath the foot, tested it, then shook his head.

“The board’s split.”

“I can see that.”

“It will go through if we lean on it.”

From the ballroom came the host’s voice demanding that everybody remain where they were. Several people attempted to obey in the doorway, preventing the people behind them from doing anything else. A lamp fell. Somebody put a cloth over it before it could set the flowers burning.

Sel’s voice rose above the confusion. Briska could not distinguish every word, but she heard intruders and theft, and then the reassuring instruction to let the household men deal with it.

A guard came toward the stage. Rosceline held up her rapier.

“Come slowly,” she said. “I have had a disappointing evening.”

The man stopped. He wore the house colors, not the troupe’s red cord. He looked at the injured alchemist, at the blood on the stage and at the broken screen through which Vaudrin had disappeared.

“Put the weapon down.”

“When your director stops reaching for people.”

“He’s not my director.”

“Then we are already making progress. Get your master.”

He backed away. Rosceline did not pursue him. Briska saw her checking the edge of the screen, the space behind the frame and the service turn all in the same few breaths.

Thane had found a length of stout timber under the stage’s outer edge. He tried to drag it free, failed with one hand, and called Sariel.

“Pull this. Toward me.”

Sariel gave up her place against the upright only when Briska nodded. Together they drew out a short support used to prop the decorative front. The flowers above it sagged. Thane turned the timber flat and fed it beneath the split board while Sariel lifted the foot enough to admit it.

The weight settled across sound boards on either side of the split. Briska’s shoulder eased. She stayed where she was until Thane had blocked the lower end against a fixed rail.

“Now,” he said.

She stood clear. The frame held.

Her arm throbbed from the old bruise. A small cut beneath her hair was leaking sweat and blood into one eye. She wiped it with the back of her wrist and looked at the glass. The strap she had fitted that morning was twisted but unbroken. The pane had survived. Its painted rim had not scraped the upright.

“Can you turn it?” Sariel asked.

“When I have checked the hinge.”

“Check it quickly.”

Briska looked at her. Sariel looked back, waiting. There was no useful time in being offended.

She took a narrow gauge from her case. The crimson mask lay beside it. Her hand shook enough that she stopped, pressed her palm against the wood and waited for it to steady.

Thane saw. He held out his good hand.

“Give me the gauge. Tell me the clearance.”

She did.

The young man in Orun’s body was on his knees with his injured hand wrapped in a table napkin. The napkin had begun turning red. He kept trying to use the hand to pull off the fish mask, although the face already lay beside him.

“Leave it,” Briska told him.

“I can’t feel where it is.”

“The hand?”

He shook his head. His good hand touched his own jaw, then the loose flesh beneath it.

Across from him, the real Orun gripped the young actor’s throat with the fingers of that body. Not hard enough to strangle himself. Hard enough to feel that something was there.

“Master Orun,” Briska said.

Both men looked at her.

She swore softly.

“You,” she told the younger face. “Take your hand down.”

“I cannot make the pitch settle.”

“Then don’t speak until you have to.”

A bitter expression passed across the boy’s handsome face. “That is very easy advice from where you are standing.”

It was. She went to fetch Elena.

At the narrow stair below the stage, Daska stood with her back to the wall and one foot across the first tread. Her staff was a curtain pole, stripped of its decorative end. Benjamin was below her. Briska could see his shoulder and part of his sword arm, not the room beyond him. Someone was moving heavily under the stage. A chair scraped. Elena’s voice said, “Wait. I said wait.”

Briska did not go down. The stair would hold one moving person at a time, and she had no business adding herself to it.

“Elena. We have a broken hand.”

“Whose?”

“Orun’s body. The player is in it.”

There was a silence. Then Elena appeared on the lower treads. Blood darkened the front of her apron now. She carried her small bag and looked past Briska to the light on the stage.

“Can he walk?”

“I think so.”

“Bring him to the alcove. Keep the two masks with the people.”

“His face is on the floor.”

“The fitted mask. The painted one.”

Briska nodded, embarrassed by the momentary confusion. Elena had already turned to tell Benjamin something. Briska heard only the last words: “Not up yet. There isn’t room.”

She returned to the frame. Sariel was wrapping the fish face in a clean cloth. Rosceline had sent the house guard back with an instruction to clear the east passage. Thane tested the hinge, reported the clearance and waited for Briska to check his work rather than asking her to accept it.

It was correct.

Briska crouched beside Havret in the older body. “We are going to stand. Your good arm around my shoulder.”

“I don’t know how heavy I am.”

“I do. Slowly.”

He got one foot under him, then the other. His knee folded at a point he had not expected. She caught him and nearly lost her own balance. Pello appeared on his other side and took most of the weight.

They brought him around the rear screen. The corridor beyond looked longer than it had that morning. People were trying to get into it from the ballroom while household guards tried to make them use the north entrance instead. Somebody had abandoned a whole tray of glasses on the floor.

Pello told a servant to pick it up.

“I’m not—”

“Before someone falls on it.”

The servant picked it up.

At the alcove Elena opened the napkin without disturbing the hand more than she had to. Havret watched her with Orun’s frightened eyes. Briska did not look closely. She had hit a man’s arm with an iron bar the other night and seen enough of what happened afterward to know she would be thinking about this hand later.

Pello went to the loading door. Through the open gap Briska saw Rossa’s lighter beyond the edge of the wharf. Mallow was farther out, her little cover bright against the dark water. The cousin at her stern held a pole and watched the windows above.

“Bring the flat boat in,” Pello called.

Rossa pointed toward the public side of the house. A man with a crossbow stood at an upper window. He had not aimed yet.

Pello closed the door halfway and looked at Briska.

“We can move the red face in Mallow. Get it away from here.”

“And them?”

“The lighter waits.”

“They need the face here to return.”

“I know.”

He looked down the corridor. A shout came from the narrow stair. Daska struck the doorframe with her pole and told someone to keep their hands where she could see them.

“Then I need that bow taken off the window,” Pello said.

Briska went to find the house guard. This time she did not threaten him. She took him to the alcove and made him look at the man whose hand Elena was splinting, then at the young actor saying that his name was Orun and demanding the household summon his own physician.

The guard asked which man was hurt.

“The body you know,” Briska said. “Look at it.”

He did. Whatever he had thought of the entertainment left his face.

He ran toward the main stair. A short time later the crossbow withdrew from the window. Pello opened the door and signaled with his whole arm. Rossa brought the lighter alongside without wasting a breath on her opinion of the delay.

The boat’s low deck rose and settled beside the steps. It was slower, larger and much harder to hide than the escape Pello had first described. It also held still when he tested the carrying board against the gunwale.

Elena looked up. “That will do.”

Pello nodded and returned to the frame for the second man.

Chapter 16 — The missing partner

Ilaro found Loude beneath the musicians’ stair, holding the hand of a player who would not stop laughing.

The laughter was wrong for the woman’s face. It kept escaping between her attempts to breathe. She had a green-and-ivory mask on her lap and a smear of someone else’s blood on the front of her dress.

“Isotte,” Loude said. “She hasn’t been on the boards. I was with her.”

“Did she wear the face?”

“For the bow.”

“With the central face uncovered?”

“Not on the boards.”

Ilaro crouched, slower than he wished, and asked the woman whether she had pain anywhere. She shook her head. He asked her what had happened. The answer came between bursts of laughter: she had seen the man’s hand crushed, and then she had seen another man look at it as though it were his, and she could not make the room stay ordinary again.

He did not ask her to stop laughing.

“Go to the receiving room,” he told Loude. “Stay with her. No masks on either of you.”

“I have the others waiting.”

“Tell them the performance is finished.”

“The director hasn’t called—”

“I know. You can tell them.”

She looked down at the woman’s hand in hers. Then she took the mask from the player’s lap and gave it to him.

“This is hers. Keep it away from him.”

Ilaro wrapped it in a clean table cloth. He had not expected to spend the evening holding a face as if it were something that might bruise.

Kavra’s secretary found him with the portfolio. The man had ink on two fingers and an expression of barely controlled offense at having been told to abandon the rest of the guild’s papers.

“The mistress says you are to come.”

“Where is she?”

“By the service door.”

“Bring the unused masks with you. Hers first.”

The secretary opened his mouth to protest, looked at the wrapped shape in Ilaro’s hands and shut it. He went back for the bag.

In the east corridor the movement had become purposeful. Household guards stood at the ballroom opening, asking the guests to leave through the main doors. They did not ask the troupe men. The difference in treatment was beginning to draw objections. Ilaro heard a player say that he had been paid to juggle, not to be arrested with a man who owned a red face. A guard told him to put the juggling knives down before they discussed it.

Kavra held a cloth to a small cut above her wrist. The wound had come from a broken glass, someone told him. It was ordinary enough that he welcomed it.

“Which of these people are still in the wrong bodies?” she asked.

“I am establishing that.”

“Can you be certain?”

“Not by looking. We have the order, witnesses to the frame, the fitted faces and the people themselves. I need them together.”

She looked past him at Orun’s young face. The alchemist stood by the treatment alcove, speaking very quietly to Pello. He had stopped touching his throat.

“What will you need from me?”

“Keep the unused faces off people. Don’t let anyone move the frame or the red mask. Put a guard on the director when they find him. And have the guild witnesses stay for the return.”

“If this is another demonstration—”

“Then I should like witnesses to it.”

She accepted the answer without liking it. Her secretary arrived with the bag. Inside lay her fitted mask, untouched since she had declined it. Ilaro placed Isotte’s beside it. The paired linings carried matching small loops stitched in blue thread. They were not proof of anything by themselves; he checked the names on the fitting paper as well.

Kestra came up from the narrow stair. She had a smear of dust across one cheek and a cut at the edge of her sleeve. She was not bleeding through it. Sariel followed her with the blue-and-white face held against its cloth wrapping.

“Where is Zélie?” Kestra asked.

“Dressing room, last I heard.”

“Still?”

He did not like the word any more than she did.

They moved to a cleared serving table. Ilaro set out the revised performance sheet and the fitting record. Sariel laid down Olyne’s face. Pello brought the silver fish and the black fox, carrying them by their ties so that the linings touched nothing. He had taken the young man to Elena and left both bodies within sight of one another.

“Four people,” Ilaro said. “Two pairs known to have crossed.”

“And four other pairs prepared,” Kestra said. “Some wearing their masks already. We need to know who stood where.”

Loude’s account put Orun and Havret first tonight. Briska had removed the central face immediately afterward. Rosceline had held the left glass; Sariel had held the upright. None had seen another pair take position in that interval.

“What about before?” Kestra asked.

“Loude was present through the rehearsals. The central face was screened. She saw the screen removed for this first figure. She isn’t relying on the announced order.”

He tapped the crossed-out entry for Estrenne.

“The order says she was here for the earlier presentation. Her chair is empty. Her cloak is here. That is all the attendant knew.”

“She isn’t here,” Sariel said.

“How do you know?”

“I know.”

“Did you see her leave?”

Sariel looked at him, then at Kestra. “I saw her somewhere else this afternoon. She is not coming.”

He accepted the statement as far as it went and marked it as hers. He did not need the somewhere else to know that the stage list had not been performed as written.

Kavra’s secretary brought the remaining masks in their padded rack. The actor paired with Estrenne had not put his on. The charcoal factor had worn his at the table but never reached the stage; two guests and a wine steward confirmed it. The host’s nephew was with his father, furiously insisting that he had refused the whole thing once his aunt began objecting. Vaudrin’s own fitted face remained in its compartment, the tie still pinned to keep it flat.

“None of those pairs crossed,” Ilaro said.

Kestra checked each name against the lining. Her pace was deliberate, neither soothing nor hurried. He was grateful that she did not keep asking whether he was certain while he was doing the work of becoming less uncertain.

“Then Olyne and Zélie,” she said. “Orun and Havret. The others stay off the boards.”

“Yes. Bring the two—”

A brass tray hit the corridor floor. The sound burst close to his ear. Ilaro shut his eyes. He had been about to give an order and could not find where it began.

He heard someone calling his name. He held up a hand, asking for a moment.

“Bring the two pairs,” Kestra said, quietly. “One at a time. You said the others stay off the boards.”

The sentence returned to him with the missing beginning attached.

“The injured one first?” she asked.

He shook his head. “Ask Elena which can stand. Zélie has to explain the return. We need her before either.”

Kestra went to Elena. Sariel took Olyne’s mask back into its cloth. Pello picked up the fish and fox and carried them to the frame, placing them on separate clean towels at its foot. Ilaro watched each object reach the place intended for it. He could hold the order again now.

The guild witness who had been with Zélie came down the corridor alone.

“Where is she?” he asked.

“Sel said the room was unsafe. She was taking her to the garden door.”

“Which door?”

“The loading side. I thought—”

He was already moving. His first quick step made the floor tilt. He stopped before he fell and caught Sariel’s sleeve instead.

“Sel has her. Loading side.”

Sariel took one look at his face and went. He heard her call Rosceline’s name.

Ilaro returned to the table and put both hands on its edge. The papers lay where he had left them. He had four people to return, eight faces to keep unused and an ordinary young woman in an ordinary old body being led toward a boat.

He could not go faster by wanting it. He called the nearest house guard and gave the direction again.

Chapter 17 — A life with servants

Sel had put the green box under her arm. Her other hand rested lightly on Zélie’s elbow.

They stood on the covered landing above the canal steps. Rossa’s lighter lay to the south of them, its bow held off the stone by Pello’s cousin, who had left Mallow tied behind it. A smaller boat waited beneath the landing’s northern edge. Rosceline could see the top of a man’s head and the dull red cord around his hat.

Sel had arranged a departure of her own.

“Let her arm go,” Rosceline said.

Sel turned. “You should be inside.”

“So should you. The quality of the evening has suffered in your absence.”

Zélie looked from one woman to the other. Her face had been cleaned in the dressing room. Without the earlier paint, Olyne’s tiredness showed along the cheeks and beneath the eyes. A body had spent the last several days being slept in badly by a stranger.

“I am taking her somewhere quiet,” Sel said.

“Ask her where.”

Zélie looked down at the little boat. “She has rooms.”

“Most people of her profession do.”

“Rooms where no one has to know.”

Rosceline stopped at the top of the steps. The upper landing was wide enough for four people if all four intended to stand still. The lower steps narrowed against the wall. The north side had no rail. She saw the water lift beneath the boatman’s pole, black under the wharf, violet where the last light caught dye drifting from a yard upstream.

“Zélie,” she said. “The frame is standing. We have the faces. The other woman is alive.”

“And very angry,” Sel added.

Rosceline kept her eyes on Zélie. “We can attempt the return.”

“You can attempt many things,” Sel said. “Who pays for the ones you get wrong?”

From inside came a cry, quickly quieted. Zélie’s shoulders tightened beneath Sel’s hand.

“She is right about Olyne,” Zélie said.

“I know.”

“She will tell them I signed. That I went along with it.”

“You did sign.”

“I thought you were trying to help me.”

“I am trying to tell you what I can actually help with.”

Sel’s smile was almost tender. “How scrupulous. She will have the comfort of knowing you warned her.”

Rosceline wanted to put the point of her rapier under Sel’s chin and make the conversation simpler. The narrow landing and Zélie’s body between them prevented it. She suspected Sel had chosen the place for several reasons.

“Ilaro has the fitting dates,” Rosceline said. “Mestrel kept both signatures. There are people who heard what you were promised. I will pay for an advocate to speak with you before you answer a charge. I will not tell you that the charge cannot be brought.”

“And the money afterward?” Sel asked. “The work? The room in which she used to sleep has already been let.”

Zélie flinched.

“You did that,” Rosceline said.

“The lodging was paid through the engagement. The engagement has become difficult.”

Sariel appeared in the loading doorway behind them. She saw the small boat, Sel’s hand and the green box. Her gaze moved to the leather folio hanging from the boatman’s shoulder. It had a brass clasp and a pale corner rubbed through at the fold.

Sel saw her looking.

“There is room for another passenger,” she said.

Sariel’s face became very still.

Rosceline did not know what the offer meant. She could see that it meant something. She placed the fact aside because Zélie had moved one foot toward the lower step.

Sel turned back to her.

“You can remain as you are. There are places where no one has met Dame Olyne, but a letter bearing her signet will open a house. You know how they listen now. You need not go back to begging for an audition from a man who cannot remember which of you he paid.”

The words had force. Rosceline felt the same low warmth that had passed into her fingers at the first supper. It came with the smell of Sel’s perfume, orange peel and something bitter. She saw Zélie’s breathing slow.

“Is it an offer she can refuse?” Rosceline asked.

“Of course.”

“Then take your hand away.”

Sel did not move.

Rosceline struck the stone beside her own foot with the point of her rapier. The scrape was ugly, loud and entirely without grace. Zélie startled. Sel looked down before she could stop herself.

“Do not put your voice in the place she is trying to answer from,” Rosceline said.

For the first time Sel looked at her without the prepared warmth.

“You have used a voice before.”

“Yes. I know what I’m hearing.”

Zélie pulled her arm away. Sel let it go a moment too late for the release to seem voluntary.

The three women stood in the damp evening air. A boat passed beyond the mouth of the small inlet, carrying ordinary people home with baskets in their laps. Nobody aboard looked up.

“I like being listened to,” Zélie said.

Rosceline waited.

“I liked the garden. I liked sending for a plate and not wondering whether I had asked for too much. I liked that no one told me to wait until they had finished with someone else.”

“You could keep that,” Sel said.

Zélie looked down at Olyne’s hands. The pale band from the removed ring stood out beneath the lamplight. She rubbed it with her thumb.

“I have been trying to remember how I used to bite a thread,” she said. “I keep putting it against the wrong tooth.”

Rosceline felt her own mouth close. The cut in her lip stung.

“It sounds foolish.”

“No,” she said.

“I want to go back.”

Sel opened her mouth. Zélie spoke over her.

“I want to go back before I get better at this.”

She reached for the green box. Sel held it away.

Rosceline moved at the same time Sariel did. Sel pushed the box into Rosceline’s chest, hard enough to foul her sword arm, and caught Zélie’s wrist with her free hand. She pulled the older body down the first step toward the waiting boat.

Zélie resisted. Her shoe slipped on the wet stone.

For one breath she hung sideways over the edge, held only by Sel’s grip. Then the boatman reached up, seized her other arm and tried to drag her aboard.

Sariel drew her knife. She slashed the strap of the folio as the boatman bent. The leather case fell against the gunwale, struck its brass clasp and burst open.

Papers spilled across the boat. One bundle slid into the water. Sariel reached for it.

The boatman pulled Zélie again.

Sariel abandoned the papers and caught the woman’s coat at the back of her neck. She dropped to one knee on the steps, taking the weight against her thigh. Zélie cried out. Her feet struck the wall. Sel let go of her wrist and stepped across her toward the boat.

Rosceline had the green box under her left arm. She thrust with her right. The blade passed through Sel’s loose upper sleeve and cut the flesh beneath. Sel hissed, ducked and caught the boatman’s shoulder. He shoved the boat away from the step before Rosceline could recover her point.

Sariel had both hands in Zélie’s coat now. She could not fight and hold her. Rosceline set the box on the landing, knelt and seized the older woman under the other arm.

“Feet against the stone,” she said. “Push.”

Zélie pushed. They hauled her up the first step, then the second. Her skirt caught beneath one shoe. Sariel cut the hem without asking. They dragged her onto the landing and stayed kneeling beside her while the little boat scraped its way out from under the wharf.

Sel had one hand clamped over her wounded shoulder. Her red dress looked black in the dark. The boatman used his pole to thrust them into the main canal, behind the passing hull of a grain barge.

Rosceline could have thrown the green box. She could have thrown her knife. Neither was likely to stop the boat, and one was needed for a woman breathing hard against her knee.

Sariel stared at the water.

The pale bundle had opened. Sheets turned beneath the surface, caught the light and went dark again. One struck a mooring post and hung there, folding against it. Sariel leaned toward it as if the length of her arm might change.

Pello reached the landing and caught her shoulder.

“Not from here.”

She pushed his hand away. He did not touch her again.

For a moment Rosceline thought she would go into the canal. Instead Sariel closed her eyes, drew a long breath through her nose and looked down at Zélie.

“Can you stand?”

Zélie shook her head.

“Then we’ll lift.”

Her voice was controlled so tightly that Rosceline said nothing about the papers. She helped Sariel raise the woman until Pello could take the heavier share. Rossa shouted from the lighter that they could use her deck if they meant to stop arguing on the stairs.

“Back inside,” Rosceline said. “She has chosen.”

Pello nodded. Together they took Zélie through the loading door. Sariel collected the green box. At the threshold she turned once more toward the water, then brought it with her.

Rosceline came last, rapier low in her hand. A red smear from Sel’s shoulder marked the blade. She wiped it on the torn strip of Olyne’s skirt and left the cloth on the stone, where the next rise of canal water would reach it.

Chapter 18 — The service passage

The first attempt to close the glass put pressure on the wrong corner. Briska stopped before the sound became a break.

“Back,” she told Thane.

He eased the catch. The pane settled into the strap she had fitted that morning. Beneath it, the painted line along the rim remained continuous. She put the lamp close, moved it slowly and checked the whole length. There was a hairline flaw at the top from the earlier fight. It did not cross the line. She could not promise that it would remain so if anyone struck the frame again.

“Something is lifting the lower hinge,” Thane said.

She felt along the wood. A sliver from the broken fixing had wedged under the brass plate. She could reach it with the fine bent pick that had gone under the gallery screens three nights earlier and never come back.

“Of course,” she said.

Thane took a tool from his roll. Its hook was too thick. She said so. He looked at it, handed her a thinner blank and held the lamp while she bent it against the edge of her iron bar.

It took longer than using the right tool. It worked.

The sliver came free. She laid it on a clean scrap beside the frame, then shut and reopened the glass. The hinge moved without lifting.

Thane breathed out.

“Now the silver tongue?”

“Not until she is here to tell us where she wants it.”

Sariel came back from the landing, gave Zélie the green box and brought the blue face from the serving table to Briska’s tools. They laid the crimson face in its padded case beneath the stage table, behind the wrapped blue face. Briska could see it whenever she looked down. She had put the case where no door opened over it and where a man reaching for it would have to show his whole arm.

It did not prevent Vaudrin from trying.

He came through the screen’s torn side with the silver head of his cane in one hand and the narrow blade drawn from it in the other. Briska saw the blade before she saw his face. She threw the lamp at his feet.

The oil did not catch. The lamp hit the stage, rolled and went out. Its iron edge struck his ankle. He stumbled and cut at her anyway. The point passed through the cloth at her upper left arm without finding much flesh, but the warmth afterward told her it had found some.

Thane kicked the case farther under the table. Vaudrin turned toward him.

Briska swung the iron bar. He parried with the cane blade, too lightly for the weight coming at it. The blade bent and glanced off. The shock traveled into her bruised forearm; she cried out despite herself.

Vaudrin hit her across the cheek with the silver head in his other hand.

The stage tipped. Her mouth struck the inside of her teeth. She caught the table before she fell into the case. Vaudrin put a foot against the low rung and shoved her back, reaching for the red face.

Benjamin caught him by the collar.

The director turned with surprising speed. His bent blade cut across Benjamin’s upper left arm. Benjamin kept his hold, drove Vaudrin against the screen’s wooden edge and took a hard blow from the silver cane head across his shoulder.

Daska came from the service passage with the curtain pole. She struck Vaudrin behind the knee. His leg folded. Benjamin released him before the falling weight could pull him into the blade.

Vaudrin went off the stage into the gap between the platform and the nearest patron chair. He came up with a hand on the chair back, still holding the weapon. Beyond him the household was trying to clear the last guests through the north doors.

“Come on, then,” he said to Benjamin.

He stepped backward toward the nearest frightened cluster. One of the women caught her skirts against a chair and nearly fell. Vaudrin put his free hand on her shoulder.

Briska saw Benjamin prepare to go after him. She saw the change when he looked toward the passage where Elena was bringing their patient up.

He stayed.

“Let her go,” he said.

Vaudrin smiled. He expected Benjamin to need something other than a successful withdrawal.

Rosceline came out of the service turn behind him.

She did not announce herself. Her rapier entered the outside of his sword arm above the elbow. He cried out, released the woman and swung toward Rosceline with the cane head. She stepped back. Daska’s pole caught his wrist from the side. The silver head struck the stage and bounced under a chair.

Benjamin kicked the bent blade away. Daska took Vaudrin down face-first across the upholstered seat, one arm held behind him. She knelt on the floor so her weight went through the joint instead of onto his spine.

“Stop,” she said.

He tried once more. She shifted her grip. His breath became a series of little noises.

“Stop.”

He stopped.

A household guard came running. Kavra followed with her secretary and the host. Rosceline held the bent blade out by its handle, offering it with the fastidiousness of a person presenting a dirty spoon.

“This is his,” she said.

The guard took it. The host looked at the blood soaking the chair, then at the director’s face pressed into its pale silk.

“My guests—”

“Are leaving,” Kavra said. “Keep them doing that.”

She ordered two guards to stay with Vaudrin. Elena came far enough to see the wound and told them how to hold a cloth against it until she could spare a hand. The director watched her as though her attention were another thing being withheld from him unfairly.

Briska sat on the stage edge. Her cheek had begun to swell. She put her tongue against the inside of it and tasted blood.

Thane brought her the lamp, refilled and relit.

“Can you see clearly?”

“Yes.”

“With both eyes?”

She closed one, then the other. The room remained where it belonged.

“Yes.”

He held out a clean cloth. She took it.

“I wanted this evening’s principal difficulty,” she said through the cloth, “to be a badly fitted hinge.”

“I thought it was.”

She looked at him. He was smiling a little, with exhaustion beneath it. She found herself laughing, which hurt her mouth enough that she stopped immediately.

The original Olyne came up the narrow stair in Zélie’s young body, supported by Elena and one of the guild escorts. Benjamin stood at the turn while they passed. Daska took over his place for the moment he needed to press a cloth to his own arm.

Olyne saw her older body waiting beside the stage and stopped so suddenly that Elena nearly lost her balance.

Zélie stood within that body, clutching the green box. There was canal mud along the ruined hem of her gown. She had lost one shoe. Her hands trembled against the lid.

“You,” Olyne said.

Zélie did not move.

Elena spoke close to Olyne’s ear. Briska could not hear the words. The older woman’s anger came through the young face so nakedly that Briska looked away, giving her a privacy the room could not otherwise supply.

Pello brought Havret in Orun’s body from the alcove. The broken hand had been splinted and hung from a broad strip of cloth. Havret had become quiet. The real Orun followed, a young man with old, exact displeasure in the line of his mouth.

Ilaro checked the paired masks again with Kestra. They had the unused faces in a rack guarded by Kavra’s secretary. The original paper lay in Ilaro’s open portfolio beside it. He asked Loude to remain where she could see the frame and say if any part of the sequence differed from the return she had practiced.

Zélie opened the green box. She took out the yellow bird face and turned it in her hands. The inside had the shape of the cheeks and nose she no longer possessed.

Briska lifted the crimson face from its cloth.

The red surface held the lamplight. She turned it carefully so the painted back cleared every edge of the mounting. It seated on the upper hooks with a faint, soft click. Thane eased the lower saddle into place using the crosspiece they had made between them.

For a moment Briska felt that the face was waiting to be pleased.

She withdrew the tool.

“Tell me what you need,” she said to Zélie.

Chapter 19 — Their own faces

Rosceline stood where Zélie could see her without looking through glass.

There was no practical reason it had to be Rosceline. Elena stood nearer the injured body. Kestra knew more about fitted workings. But Zélie had asked, very quietly, and Rosceline had found herself taking the place before she had thought of a reason to decline it.

The older woman’s body wore the blue-and-white face. Across from her, Olyne stood in the younger body with the yellow bird mask fitted to its cheeks. The arrangement looked backward until Rosceline made herself remember which thing had been made for which skin.

“Not tight,” Zélie told Sariel, who was fastening the bird’s ties. “It has to rest, not pull.”

Sariel loosened the upper tie. Olyne touched the bird’s beak and lowered her hand with an effort.

“My ankle,” she said.

Elena placed a folded cloth under the younger body’s heel to bring it level on the board. “Keep the weight where we practiced. You can lean on me until she says.”

Zélie looked at the silver tongue behind the central face. Briska held her hand near it, waiting.

“All the way toward me,” Zélie said. “Until it settles.”

Briska turned it. Halfway, the small metal piece resisted. She paused just long enough to judge the resistance and continued with steady pressure. It settled into the second position.

Zélie nodded. “Now the glass. Plain side.”

Thane released the changed catch. The two wings turned until their unpainted surfaces faced the marked places. The lamplight changed. Rosceline could still see the same two women, but the depth behind each reflection seemed greater than the space available within the frame.

She looked at the floor to steady herself. The brass heel marks remained where they had been. Olyne’s borrowed foot rested beside one, slightly swollen above its bandage.

Loude stood behind Zélie with a folded prompt sheet. She was not reading from it. She had performed this figure often enough to know the shape of its pauses.

“Have I missed anything?” Zélie asked her.

“The hands.”

Zélie swallowed. She placed one hand lightly against the throat of the body she occupied. Across from her, Olyne copied the movement with visible reluctance.

“You have to let me finish,” Zélie told her.

“I have been waiting for you to finish for days.”

The words hurt. Rosceline saw it even through the blue mask. Zélie’s shoulders drew upward, then lowered as she made herself breathe.

“Afterward,” Rosceline said to Olyne.

The young face turned toward her. For a moment its borrowed owner seemed about to refuse the whole arrangement because somebody had addressed her as a person who might need correcting.

Then Elena murmured something near her ear, and she looked back at the glass.

Zélie spoke the first line in Thepyrgosi. Rosceline understood enough of it to recognize an ordinary invitation worn thin by repetition: come back to the place prepared for you. Olyne answered with her own name.

The room’s sounds drew away.

Rosceline could see people moving beyond the stage. A guard adjusted his hold on Vaudrin. Kavra’s secretary bent over a paper. Their movement continued without the small accompanying noises she expected. She could hear instead a breath that did not belong to anyone near her, a shallow, impatient breath like a person waiting on the other side of a shut door.

Zélie’s second line came less surely. She stopped, found it and began again. Loude did not prompt her until she looked for help.

“Your own name,” Loude said.

Zélie gave it.

The blue face tipped forward. In the opposite glass, the yellow beak seemed to lengthen until it touched its own reflection. Rosceline knew that could not have happened. She also saw Olyne’s borrowed hand leave the younger throat, reach out and close on nothing.

“Stay,” Elena said.

The injured foot shifted. Sariel caught the younger woman’s elbow and kept the heel by its mark. Zélie made a low sound in the older throat. Her voice broke. Rosceline had the terrible impression that someone had tried to speak while carrying water in her mouth.

Then both women inhaled.

The sound was loud, ragged and human. The younger body sagged against Sariel. The older one stepped backward off its marked board and put both hands against its face.

Briska did not touch the silver tongue until Zélie told her to.

The instruction came from the younger voice.

Rosceline turned toward it.

Zélie had the bird mask halfway off. Tears ran from under it. She looked down at the bandaged ankle, flexed her free toes and gave a little cry that was almost laughter.

“That hurts.”

Elena helped her sit on the waiting chair. “It was hurt before.”

“I know. I know.”

She touched the scratch beneath her jaw with two fingertips. She pressed her tongue against a tooth, frowned and laughed again, crying harder as she did it.

Olyne removed the blue face. She held it away from her as though it were something taken out of a wound. Her eyes moved over her hands, her clothes and the torn hem. She looked for the ring before she looked at Zélie.

“It is in the dressing room,” Rosceline said. “Wrapped in linen beside the mirror.”

Olyne nodded. The information seemed to let her proceed with everything else.

She sat heavily in the second chair. The two women did not embrace.

Havret asked whether it would hurt.

Elena answered before anyone could improve the truth. “The hand will still hurt the person whose hand it is.”

Orun looked at his own splinted fingers hanging from another man’s shoulder. He had taken off the actor’s coat because it was too warm, then put it back on because the younger body became cold quickly when it stood still. The changes were small and had worn him down.

“Do it,” he said.

Zélie tried to stand. Her ankle refused the first attempt. Benjamin brought her chair close enough to the frame that she could direct the return without putting weight on it. Loude knelt beside her. Briska checked the tongue and opened the plain sides of the glass again.

They brought the men to their places. The silver fish went onto Orun’s body, still inhabited by Havret; the black fox rested on Havret’s younger face, still carrying the alchemist. Elena kept the injured hand supported in its sling. No one asked the older body to imitate a gesture it could not make. Zélie showed Havret how to place his good hand at the throat and leave the other untouched.

The second return was slower.

Havret answered too soon. Zélie stopped and had him wait for the whole invitation. Orun remained silent until she asked his name directly. His voice came out louder than he intended from the actor’s chest. He looked ashamed of that, which made Rosceline unexpectedly angry on his behalf.

The glass filled with the same impossible depth. This time she kept her eyes on the brass heel marks. One of Havret’s borrowed shoes had a worn patch at the toe. She could see every cracked line in it while the room grew distant around her.

Then the older man groaned.

His injured hand jerked in the sling. Elena caught the supporting cloth before it could pull the broken fingers. On the other board the young actor swayed, staring at two sound hands held before his face.

“Orun?” Elena said.

The older man nodded, his breath coming through his teeth.

“Havret?”

The younger man lowered his hands. “Yes.”

He looked at the sling and tried to say something. Nothing came out. He knelt to retrieve the silver fish mask where it had fallen from Orun’s face and held it by the ties until Pello took it from him.

The four returned people sat within sight of the frame. Ilaro asked each for the name they meant to be called, the last thing they remembered before the first exchange and whether anything had changed that they could not explain. He did not make them finish long accounts. Kavra’s witnesses wrote what they could and asked to speak separately later.

Kestra checked the unused masks against the list one last time. The four prepared pairs had never crossed. Loude confirmed it. The players who remained confirmed what they themselves had done, sometimes angrily, sometimes through tears. No one found a missing guest who required another return.

Briska turned the silver tongue to rest. She lifted the crimson face from the hooks with the crosspiece and held it in its cloth.

Rosceline reached for it.

Briska hesitated, then gave it to her.

It weighed less than Rosceline expected. The back was warm without being hot. She could see the painted characters where the lamplight reached them, each line drawn with an attention she understood even though she could not read it. Someone had held this object for hours and made it capable of doing what she had just seen.

Pello stood at the service turn. Beyond him the way to the canal was clear.

Sariel stepped into it.

“You know what I’m asking,” she said.

“I know what you intend.”

“I would rather not fight you for it.”

Rosceline’s lip had swollen enough that it hurt to speak. Her gown was ruined in three different places. She held a valuable object with two thousand marks agreed for its recovery and a quite separate value in the eyes of the woman who had commissioned it.

“We have brought them back,” she said. “We have stopped the rest. We could finish the work we accepted.”

Daska stood beside the table. “We haven’t agreed to finish that part.”

“We did agree. We signed it.”

“We also learned more.”

“People always learn more after an agreement becomes inconvenient.”

“Yes,” Daska said. “Sometimes it is why they stop.”

Rosceline looked at the others. Ilaro did not hide behind his papers. Briska’s cheek was swelling into an ugly bruise. Pello had a hand on the loading-door key, ready to use it but waiting to hear where they meant to go.

“It is fifteen hundred marks,” Rosceline said.

“I know,” Briska answered.

“Three hundred each.”

“I can divide it.”

“And whatever work follows it.”

That quieted them more than the money had.

The Countess had kept her bargain. Four recoveries. Four fees. A book passed over in the presence of witnesses, with its ownership written plainly. Rosceline had held that fact against everything else she knew because it had been done to her, for once, in the form promised.

Sariel’s blue mask lay on the table. Its cloth wrapping had fallen open beside the leather tool case. Rosceline remembered the room described in the laundry: a safe, a place to kneel, a kindness in the arrangement of the lamp. She found that she could not place this crimson face in the Countess’s study without also placing a chair in front of it.

“Could we entrust it to the guilds?” she asked.

Kavra, still close enough to hear, looked at the object. Orun’s pain had made him pale; it had not made him stop listening.

“On what terms?” Kavra said.

Rosceline almost laughed.

There would be terms. There would be a custodian, an appointed successor, a reason to inspect the thing in private. She could negotiate them beautifully. She could spend the remainder of her life discovering who had been omitted.

She turned the mask over in its cloth so its painted back faced upward.

“Briska. If we break it, will something come out?”

“I don’t know of anything housed inside it. The working runs through the painted metal and the glass. The tongue is at rest. Everyone who crossed is back. That is the safest time we have.”

Kestra nodded. “Break the connected lines. Then the fitted faces and the rims. Keep the fragments apart until we have checked them.”

No one promised it would be entirely simple. Rosceline was grateful for that, though she would have preferred the promise.

She held the crimson face out to Sariel.

Sariel took it with both hands. She did not say thank you.

Briska cleared a patch of bare stage. She laid the mask painted side up, placed a narrow iron punch over the central junction and gave Sariel the hammer. Sariel waited until everyone had moved away from the glass.

The first blow made a short, flat note. The red face bent.

Rosceline flinched. She had not expected to.

The second blow broke the painted line. The lamps guttered together although no door had opened. Something moved across Rosceline’s tongue like the last taste of a word she could not remember. Then it was gone.

Sariel struck again. The mouthless face split through the brow.

Briska turned the pieces with tongs and told her where to strike next. The glass rims were scored and broken separately. The fitted masks went beneath the hammer in their pairs, their personal linings cut through before the shaped faces were shattered. Olyne watched hers break. Havret turned away from the sound of the fish face going under the iron.

At the end the stage held lacquer chips, ruined glass, strips of lining and several pieces of red metal too small to be mistaken for an intact face. Kestra examined the disconnected marks. Briska wrapped the principal pieces in separate cloths for the report. The guild witnesses watched the packing and marked what had been destroyed.

Rosceline put the wrapped central fragments inside the carrying case herself.

“Your case will need cleaning,” she told Briska.

“Yes.”

“I shall pay for that.”

Briska looked at her. The bruised cheek made her expression difficult to read.

“It is a case,” she said. “Come and help me lift it.”

Part IV — No one’s guests

Chapter 20 — Separate doors

The house looked smaller once the guests had left.

Ilaro stood beside the loading door while a servant took the white flowers off the nearest wall. The stems had stained the cloth beneath them green. Most of the lamps were still burning. No one knew which ones belonged to the household and which to the troupe, and the steward had begun trying to settle it before somebody carried away the wrong glass.

At the treatment alcove, Elena loosened Orun’s sling and checked the hand again. He watched her without giving advice. Havret stood far enough away that he would not be mistaken for waiting for the same treatment, but close enough to hear every word.

“Will he use it?” the actor asked.

Elena did not look up. “We need the physician and time.”

“I mean—”

“I know what you mean.”

He stopped. After a moment he went to fetch the clean water she had asked someone else to bring.

Zélie lay on the carrying board with a folded blanket beneath her head. Her own young face had lost the strange, impatient authority Ilaro had first met behind Mestrel’s screen. She looked younger now than she had in the shop, and much more tired. Olyne sat beside her with one stockinged foot resting on a box. The older body had acquired a bruised knee on the canal steps. Zélie watched her rub it, then looked down at her own bandaged ankle.

Olyne asked where the ring was.

A maid brought it in the linen wrapping, with the other things left in the dressing suite. Olyne held it in her palm for a long time. She did not force it back onto the swollen finger.

Ilaro let the maid tell him where she had found it. He recorded the answer, then put his pen away when he saw Zélie watching.

“Not everything is an inquiry,” he said.

“It feels like one.”

“Yes.”

He sat on an empty box while the carrying arrangements were finished. The lamplight seemed too bright even beneath its shade. He had a headache across the whole right side of his head now, and a sour taste left from too much water drunk without food. Someone put bread beside him. He looked up to thank Pello and found it had been Kestra.

“You haven’t eaten since the speeches,” she said.

“Neither have you.”

“I had half an apple.”

He did not argue with the distinction. She sat on the opposite end of the box, leaving the papers between them. They were quiet for several breaths before she asked what he intended to report.

“What I saw. What each of them told me. Where the objects were. Who saw the returns.”

“And the rest?”

“Which rest?”

Her eyes moved toward Sariel, who was speaking to the guild escort at the doorway.

“I don’t know your rest,” he said. “If it matters to this account, you can give me the parts you want entered.”

Kestra looked down at the paper. One corner had become damp. She moved it away from his cup.

“I want the refusal recorded,” she said. “That neither woman was willing to remain as she was.”

“I heard Zélie say it. I did not hear Olyne at the landing.”

“Olyne has said little else since we met her.”

“Then she can say it once to the witnesses when she is ready.”

Kestra nodded. He could feel her wanting more from an account than it could safely hold. He remembered that appetite in himself and had not entirely lost it.

The guildmistress came to them with her secretary. She had obtained a six-day safe-conduct for the five members of the returning company, limited to their lodging, the witnesses’ offices and necessary movements concerning the case. It would not erase old entries or compel other employers to give them work. The paper carried two seals. The Alchemists’ witness had insisted on a copy before allowing the second to be set.

Sariel read it in the doorway. She asked a question about the roads out of the city. Kavra’s secretary pointed to the relevant line. Sariel made him write one more name beside the description of the permitted wharf.

Ilaro could not hear which name. He did not leave his box to find out.

Vaudrin went out through the street entrance between household guards and a guild officer. His wounded arm had been bound. He walked under his own power and made each man beside him feel that assisting him was a task they were performing badly.

When he passed the open service door he saw the carrying case containing the broken central face.

“You haven’t destroyed what you think,” he said.

Briska looked at him. “The connected surface is in seven pieces. The rims are cut. If you mean the idea, you can keep it to yourself until someone is paid to listen.”

The officer told Vaudrin to continue walking. He did.

Ilaro wrote down the threat because it had been spoken, not because he had any reason to certify it as prophecy. He asked the officer to preserve the director’s prompt books and private instruction sheets. The man said those would be listed. Ilaro asked who would list them and recorded that name as well.

Kestra went back to the marked turn behind the stage. She cleared a little fallen plaster from the chalk, took the bronze disc in her hand and asked for a minute without anyone crossing the passage. Daska kept the door while she spoke too quietly for Ilaro to hear.

A pale seam appeared along the scuffed boards. Zélie, raised on the carrying board, kept turning her head toward it. The doorway still troubled her; twice she had reached for a jamb farther away than she thought. Now her gaze followed the seam around the turn to the alcove. She let her hands lie on the blanket.

Ilaro walked behind the bearers. The light ended where Kestra had said it would, short of the canal steps. Pello still had to lift the latch, and Daska warned the men carrying Zélie about the worn edge of the first stone.

At the canal door, Pello counted people onto Rossa’s lighter. The five Coin regulars went with Olyne and Zélie, then Orun and Havret. Daska sat near Benjamin, whose cut arm had begun to stiffen. Pello took the forward station to help Rossa through the crowded water. Eleven passengers and the owner, with the carrying board laid down the middle and the tools kept clear of the patients’ feet.

There was no room for anyone else. Briska looked at the remaining space, looked at her case and did not argue.

Mallow came alongside for Ilaro, Briska and Rosceline. Pello’s cousin had left the cargo hatch where it belonged. He told Ilaro that this was evidence of exceptional restraint. Ilaro was too tired to decide whether a response was expected.

Rosceline helped him down the steps. She did not touch him until he held out a hand. The dark panel in her gown was almost indistinguishable from the blood and canal water around it.

The lighter went first. Rossa had been paid to transport people from Ravenswood and was now being asked to find the back wharf of a physician who did not like boats arriving after midnight. She expressed her views across the water with enough precision that the physician’s assistant was waiting when they arrived.

They spent the next part of the night in rooms that smelled of clean linen and boiled instruments. Orun was taken inside. Havret waited. Zélie slept while Elena spoke with the physician’s apprentice. Olyne wrote a short message in her own hand, stopped when the bruised finger cramped, and asked Kestra to finish the address.

The two versions of her signature lay beside one another when Ilaro passed the table. He did not need to point out the difference to anyone who had watched the return. He asked that both be kept.

Before dawn, Sariel came to the outer room. Her company had found transport onward to their own lodging. She returned Ilaro’s copy of the practical agreement from the laundry, now marked with water and a thumbprint at one corner.

“Keep yours,” he said.

“This is the one we wrote for you. You left it on the stage.”

He looked at the handwriting and saw that she was right.

“Thank you.”

She nodded. Rosceline stood beside the window with the carrying case at her feet. Sariel looked toward her and hesitated.

The words Ilaro expected did not come. Sariel asked whether the Countess would be told where the patient was staying.

“No,” Rosceline said. “She will be told that the returns were completed before the instrument was broken.”

“And if she asks?”

“She may ask.”

Sariel considered that. Then she went to fetch the others.

Ilaro watched them leave separately from the people they had spent the night helping. Benjamin carried his own sword. Thane carried a small tool roll and nothing he could not manage with the hands he had. Elena had her bag over her shoulder and a little food wrapped in cloth. Kestra stopped to ask the physician’s apprentice a question. Sariel waited by the door until she had finished.

Pello returned from paying the boatmen. He put the receipts in the side pocket of Ilaro’s portfolio and asked whether he wanted to go home.

“Very much.”

The morning light was beginning to reach the upper window. Ilaro closed the portfolio before he had thought of one more question.

Chapter 21 — What was promised

Rosceline slept for three hours and dreamed of being asked to identify a woman she could see only from behind.

When she woke, the room was ordinary. The chair carried yesterday’s ruined dress. Her clean stockings hung over the edge of a basin. On the small shelf opposite the bed stood Sarem’s book in its fitted box. Someone downstairs was complaining about the price of onions. She lay still until the complaint ended, then got up.

She did not open the box.

The day’s dress was dark and plain enough that she resented it. Her lip had swollen. She could paint around the damage or allow the Countess to see it. She spent too long deciding, then used less paint than she wanted and went to meet the others.

Briska had the carrying case. Ilaro had the accounts and witness notes. Daska wore her repaired shoes, openly and with satisfaction. Pello carried nothing except the things he always carried.

They went to the Heights on foot.

The Countess received them in the smaller room. Celise had set a cloth over the table. There were five chairs, water, a pot of coffee and bread cut into manageable pieces. The arrangement made Rosceline’s anger harder to use. She would have preferred something she could call careless.

Briska opened the case.

The principal fragments lay separately wrapped. She unfolded each cloth and turned the pieces so their disconnected marks could be seen. A strip of one broken glass rim lay beside them. The rest had been retained under the guild witnesses’ seals.

The Countess looked without touching.

“How many crossed?”

“Two pairs in all,” Ilaro said. “The first before our commission. Orun and the player Havret during the final performance. All four returned.”

“Estrenne?”

“She did not attend.”

“Where was she?”

“We did not see her.”

The Countess glanced at Rosceline. Rosceline held the look and supplied no better answer.

“Vaudrin?”

“Held under the guild inquiry. Wounded. Treated.”

“And Sel?”

“Gone by canal,” Rosceline said. “With a cut shoulder and considerably less luggage than she intended.”

The Countess took a slow breath. “You have made enemies.”

“We were not short of them when we began.”

“No. But some were conveniently occupied with other people.”

She turned one of the cloths without lifting the metal on it. The crimson outer surface caught the light, still beautiful on the broken curve. Rosceline could see the Countess assessing what had been destroyed and how much of its value could be recovered from the people who had seen it work.

“The instrument was intact after the returns?”

“Yes.”

“And could have been brought out?”

Rosceline answered before Ilaro could feel obliged to. “Yes.”

“Who decided to break it?”

“We did.”

“Which of you?”

“All five are here,” Daska said. “You can ask us separately if you want to spend the morning.”

The Countess looked at her. Then she looked back at Rosceline.

“It was my agreement.”

“It was ours as well,” Rosceline said. “That is why we have brought you the account.”

For a moment she thought the Countess might offer the balance anyway. The thought disgusted her with its readiness. She could already imagine accepting the money as an acknowledgment that the essential work had been done, as if the difference between preventing use and acquiring the means of use had not occupied the last three days.

The Countess drew the original terms from a drawer.

“The advance was earned by the attempt. It remains yours. The agreed expenses will be paid against the account. The outstanding fifteen hundred marks required delivery of the original in working condition.”

“I know what it required.”

“Then I will not insult you by saying it again.”

She put the terms down between them. Celise opened the accounts.

The boat hire was paid at the approved amount. The additional charge Rossa had negotiated on the quay was not approved, and the five would cover it. The Countess paid the damaged scowman’s earlier demand because she had already accepted it. The dress’s first repair and Briska’s original inspection materials were included. The later gown, the tools lost in the fighting and the physician’s treatment of the crew were their own expenses.

Pello checked the boat entries. He corrected one figure where a wet corner had blurred the ink. Celise looked at the original slip, changed the figure and thanked him. The ordinary care of the transaction was almost unbearable to Rosceline.

“You are being very fair,” she said.

The Countess looked up.

“Would you prefer me to be theatrical?”

“No. I have had enough of that.”

“Then take the fairness where it is offered.”

Rosceline wanted to tell her about the older body hanging over the canal, about Zélie trying to find the tooth with which she bit a thread, about a young man seeing his own broken hand on someone else. The Countess would listen. She might be moved. It would not oblige her to surrender her interest in having such an instrument available.

Rosceline left the descriptions in Ilaro’s account, where they belonged to the people who had given them.

Celise counted out the expenses. The Countess asked Briska whether the disconnected metal was dangerous to touch. Briska said she had found no remaining connection but advised keeping the pieces separately wrapped until someone qualified had inspected them.

“May I retain these?” the Countess asked.

Ilaro answered. “They are part of the witnesses’ record. These can be left for inspection, with their seals unchanged. The guilds retain the rest. None is a functioning surface.”

Briska added, “And none can be made one again by putting the edges together. We removed the connecting strips.”

The Countess smiled faintly. “You were thorough.”

“Yes,” Briska said.

The smile faded into something less easy to name. The Countess folded the cloth over the largest fragment and let it rest.

When they stood to leave, she asked Rosceline to remain a moment.

The others paused. Rosceline felt the old movement in herself: the wish for a private room in which everything could be made more advantageous by saying the right thing without witnesses.

“Does it concern another commission?” she asked.

“It concerns a possible introduction.”

“Then send it in writing. I shall decide whether I wish to attend.”

The Countess inclined her head. “As you wish.”

It was not a threat. Rosceline did not let the absence of one feel like pardon.

They divided the expense money at Briska’s bench. It repaid money already spent and left no unexpected fortune. Daska set aside her share of Rossa’s addition before Briska could begin calculating it aloud. Pello took the boat slips and went to make certain the people who had lent them vessels knew they had been paid.

Rosceline remained with Ilaro while Briska checked the case lining for glass.

“Do you think she would have used it?” she asked.

Ilaro sat back. He was pale with fatigue, but the question reached him whole.

“I think she wanted the choice.”

“So did I.”

He looked at her. She could have changed the sentence into something safer. She left it.

“I liked having it in my hands,” she said. “After all that trouble. I could have gone down the steps with it. I knew the boat was there.”

“I know.”

“You do not have to sound so entirely unsurprised.”

“I was at the steps.”

That drew a laugh out of her, brief and sore. She sat down beside him. Briska went on working. A shard no larger than a nail paring came out of the case’s corner seam. She held it in the tongs, showed it to them and dropped it into a thick pot with the others.

Rosceline asked whether Daska was still angry with her.

“Ask Daska,” Ilaro said.

“I have had quite enough straightforward advice for one morning.”

“Then wait until the afternoon.”

She looked toward the open door. Daska was outside talking to a woman who had come to borrow a pan. Her laugh carried into the workshop. Rosceline let it. She took the empty cups to the wash basin and found, beneath the ordinary task, a relief that had no pleasant account of herself attached to it.

Chapter 22 — Useful things

The sale had been advertised before anyone lost possession of their face.

Briska had the notice in her pocket, folded around a small rule. She had marked three lots. The stage contractor wanted his hired furnishings off the Ravenswood wharf by noon, and the household wanted anyone connected with the entertainment somewhere else. Between them they had produced a considerable assortment of things that might be useful if purchased cheaply and described without enthusiasm.

The confiscated goods were not for sale. A clerk stood beside the warehouse door explaining this to a succession of disappointed men. Inside, the painted screens, lamps and ordinary stage machinery had been stacked beneath the contractor’s numbered cards. Briska could smell hot glue, river mud and the damp velvet of curtains that should have been spread out days ago.

Four days had passed since the report to the Countess. The bruise on her cheek had acquired a yellow edge. Her left sleeve was pinned loosely over the dressing, which made reaching into her coat inconvenient. She had therefore put her money in the right pocket and would have preferred everyone to stop asking whether she needed help.

The first person to offer was Thane.

He stood beside a cast-iron clamp fixed to a short wooden bench. One broad pedal operated the jaws. He had worked the pedal and was now looking underneath at the return spring.

“That one?” Briska said.

“That one.”

“I was going to look at it.”

“You still can.”

She crouched beside the bench. He shifted enough to give her room without removing his hand from the edge. The clamp was old, well made and badly maintained. Its jaws were faced with split leather. There was a hairline crack in the wooden seat, easily pinned. The spring had been replaced with something too stiff, probably because a stagehand had found it lying about and was pleased to discover it almost fitted.

“The return is wrong,” she said.

“I know.”

“It will tire your ankle before your hand.”

“I know that as well.”

“You could have said.”

“You were enjoying yourself.”

She looked at him. He looked back without smiling, then bent to examine the underside of the seat again.

Benjamin waited by the next lot with a carrying strap over his sound shoulder. His left arm was bandaged beneath his coat. Kestra was reading the conditions of sale. Sariel and Elena were nowhere Briska could see. She did not ask where they were.

Beyond the machinery, Rosceline was holding a length of black lace against her wrist. Daska stood beside her with a small painted moon under one arm.

“We have no use for that,” Briska called.

Daska turned the moon so that its face regarded her. One eye had been retouched by a less capable painter than the other.

“It is looking at you.”

“It can look at someone with storage.”

Daska put it back. Rosceline did not put down the lace.

The auctioneer had a voice polished by years of selling things over dock noise. He could make a broken hinge sound like an opportunity without actually saying it worked.

Briska let two lots pass. The brass lamp brackets were thinner than they looked. The hinges had been painted while closed. Pello bought a folded length of sound canvas, having already held it against the light and put a finger into every hole he found. There were three. He bid as if there had been seven.

Then the clamp came up.

Briska offered eight marks. A carpenter at the door offered nine. Thane said ten.

She had allowed herself twelve. At twelve she could replace the spring, reface the jaws and still call it a sensible purchase. She raised a finger.

“Eleven,” the auctioneer said.

The carpenter shook his head. Thane said twelve.

Briska looked at the bench. She could fit it beneath the west window. Her present vise would then be free for metalwork, and the ridiculous arrangement of knee, elbow and padded box she used for gluing would cease to be necessary. She had needed something like it for two years.

Rosceline came up beside her.

“Do you want another mark?”

“No.”

“I have several.”

Briska shook her head. Thane’s twelve stood. The auctioneer waited, looked at her, then struck the bench with his little wooden hammer.

Thane paid from a counted purse. He tucked the receipt inside his coat before he tried the pedal again.

“You will need a pin through that seat,” Briska told him.

“I saw the crack.”

“Not a screw. It will split along the grain.”

“A pin.”

She took out the rule and laid it along the casting. He watched the measurement, then held the rule while she checked the width beneath the jaw. She wrote two figures on the back of the sale notice and tore off the corner.

“For the leather,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“Firm leather. Not whatever comes off the first dead shoe.”

Benjamin coughed. Briska did not look at him.

They needed to take the pedal arm off before the bench would fit their handcart. Thane had the proper spanner but the bolt had been seated too deep to reach squarely. Briska lent him hers, which had a narrow head. He worked with his left hand while Benjamin kept the bench steady. Neither asked her to do it for them.

When the arm came free, Thane set the spanner on the seat and went to fetch the cart.

“Mine,” Briska said, taking it back before Benjamin could wrap the bench in sacking.

“I had not moved.”

“You were about to.”

He grinned at her, then winced as he drew the strap across the wrong shoulder. She waited while he arranged it again.

Kestra joined them with the stamped receipt. The three went through the warehouse door into the sunlight. At the threshold Thane turned and lifted his left hand briefly. Briska lifted the spanner. Then the cart wheel struck a loose stone and all three became occupied with keeping the clamp upright.

Ilaro had found a place beyond the reach of the auctioneer’s voice.

He sat on a little stool near the open waterside doors, his coat folded beneath him. Someone had set a basket of costume shoes nearby. A red heel protruded through the wicker. He was looking across the canal at a man cleaning shutters with a long wet brush. The brush left pale vertical strokes; water ran down the wall in crooked lines.

Briska stopped beside him.

“Any better?”

“Here.”

She stood where her shadow would not fall across his eyes. On the other bank, the man missed the corner of the upper shutter. Ilaro watched him lower the brush and choose whether to lift it again. Eventually he did.

“Did you buy the thing?” Ilaro asked.

“Someone else did.”

“Is it useful?”

“Yes.”

He nodded. She did not make him ask who had bought it.

Daska brought them small hot cakes in a paper twist. She had gone outside for food and returned without the moon. Briska took one. The cake burned her fingertips and tasted of fennel, cheap oil and the first thing she had eaten all morning without leaning over a repair.

Rosceline appeared with the black lace. There was less of it than before.

“They sell it by length,” she said when Daska looked at it.

“Cruel people.”

“Unnecessarily exact.”

A boy wearing the contractor’s badge came toward them with a numbered card.

“Lot sixty-two,” he said.

Rosceline glanced at the basket. “An optimistic description of those shoes.”

“The stool, madam.”

Ilaro began to stand.

“Sit,” she said, and turned to the boy. “We are using it.”

“Yes, madam. It is for sale.”

“I had understood that the seating was provided.”

“So did three other people.”

Behind him the auctioneer called for lot sixty-two. The boy looked over his shoulder, anxious now. Ilaro put his hands on his knees.

Rosceline raised her voice. “Two marks.”

“Three,” said a woman beside the costume rack.

Rosceline turned. The woman held a little boy whose feet almost reached her knees. She looked at the stool with frank intention.

“Four,” Rosceline said.

The woman considered it. Her son wriggled. She put him down and he immediately sat on the floor.

“Yours,” she said.

The auctioneer’s hammer struck. Rosceline opened her purse with an expression Briska would have enjoyed more had her own clamp not cost twelve marks and belonged to someone else.

“I was going to get up,” Ilaro said.

“You may still do so. I have purchased the stool, not your occupancy.”

He settled back. Daska gave the boy a hot cake before he went for the receipt.

Pello came through the room carrying his roll of canvas. Briska took the other end while he retied the cord. The sunlight showed the three small holes again. He had already marked each with chalk.

They waited by the water while Rosceline disputed whether the sale included the stool’s cushion. There was no cushion. She had mistaken Ilaro’s folded coat for one, and had to accept the explanation in front of Daska.

At last the receipt arrived. Rosceline handed it to Ilaro. He read it, folded it once and put it in his breast pocket.

The others began gathering their purchases. He stayed seated until they were ready to go.
