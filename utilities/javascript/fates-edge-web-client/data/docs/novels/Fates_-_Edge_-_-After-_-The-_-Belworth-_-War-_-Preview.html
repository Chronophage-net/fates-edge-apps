The Duke’s Morning

The hawk had put a foot through Ouen’s letter.

It was his father’s letter, strictly speaking, but it had been addressed to him and nobody had told him anything about the state of the northern roads that could not have waited until the bird was settled. She stood on his glove with the torn corner caught beneath one talon. When he tried to draw it free she tightened her grip.

“Leave it,” he told the man beside the desk.

Halden left it. He had not been touching the letter.

Ouen worked the paper loose with his other hand. The hawk turned her hooded head towards the sound. She had cast well that morning and stood lightly on the glove; he could feel the small adjustments of her feet as he moved. It would be a good day. There was very little wind.

“His Grace asks for forty horses this month,” Halden said.

“I can read.”

“Yes, my lord.”

Ouen flattened the letter against the desk. His father had written three lines himself, beneath the secretary’s much longer account. The personal lines concerned the household and his grandson. None asked how Ouen thought the war should be conducted.

“And fodder for a hundred,” Halden said.

Ouen looked up. “He has other horses.”

“He does. The instruction is to hold the fodder here.”

“Then hold it.”

“In which store, my lord?”

“The stores.”

Halden laid another sheet beside the torn letter. He had drawn the three buildings in outline and written their contents inside them. Ouen could have understood it from the door. There was something especially disagreeable about that.

“The lower granary will have to be cleared. The hay cannot go into the upper one until the roof is finished.”

“Why isn’t it finished?”

“The slates arrived yesterday.”

“They were ordered last month.”

“Yes, my lord. The carrier came back by a different road.”

Everyone came back by a different road now. They told you which one, and why, and whom they had paid to use it, until an explanation of a roof required knowing the affairs of three cantons. His father appeared to enjoy this. Ouen had seen him keep a carter standing beside the supper table for half an hour, asking questions while the man twisted his cap into a rope.

“Get another roofer.”

“The men are working already. I need authority to move the grain from the lower fields into the stone granary beside the mill.”

“Then why are we discussing the roof?”

“To explain why we need the other building.”

The hawk shifted. Ouen raised his glove a little, giving her the perch she wanted. She settled at once.

“There,” he said, though neither man had asked about her.

Halden waited.

Ouen had once asked to see the household correspondence, certain that Halden was reporting to his grandmother. He had been given the keys and spent a miserable afternoon finding requests for lamp oil, a dispute over a damaged boat, and letters to his father saying things Halden had already said to him. There had been no secret account of Ouen’s shortcomings. There had not even been a private complaint about the falconers’ expenses.

He could not forgive the man for making him spend the afternoon that way.

“My father left me in charge here.”

“He did, my lord.”

“Then I shall decide when the grain moves.”

“Certainly.”

He heard himself. He heard the shape of the sentence, which was the shape of a boy arguing with his tutor, and he hated it, and he could not stop.

“I don’t need every cart brought to me.”

“No. Only the use of the mill granary. It has been reserved for His Grace’s rents.”

“You’ve said that.”

Halden drew the plan back from the bird’s reach.

“The teams are harnessed. They can begin when you return.”

Ouen had been going to say he had not decided whether to go out. His cloak was over his shoulder. Two horses waited in the court, with a third for the falconer, and the bird was on his fist.

“See that they are ready,” he said.

His son was on the steps with his nurse, wearing one of Ouen’s old gloves. It reached almost to the child’s elbow. He held his arm out rigidly, his face solemn with the effort.

“Not yet,” Ouen said.

“I can.”

“She would hurt you.”

“I won’t mind.”

“You would mind very much.”

He bent and let the boy touch the smooth leather of the hood. The nurse drew closer. Ouen kept the hawk turned away from the small bare hand; he could feel her attention change even beneath the hood.

“Gently.”

The boy touched it with one finger, then looked up at him.

“She can’t see.”

“No.”

“Take it off.”

“Out in the field.”

“I’ll come.”

“Another day.”

The boy’s face crumpled. Ouen wanted to promise tomorrow, but his wife had asked him not to promise days he would forget. He told the nurse to have a glove made that fitted. The boy might carry a wooden bird until he was older. He could paint it himself.

“Like yours?” the child asked.

“Better. Mine bites.”

That was accepted. Ouen kissed the top of his head and went down to the horses feeling that he had handled something well.

Beyond the lower fields, the ditches were full. The men waiting with the grain carts watched him pass. One took off his cap. Ouen returned the greeting with his free hand and turned towards the meadow where the falconer had seen quarry that morning.

The bird flew beautifully.

He knew she would before she left the glove. He had watched the way she held herself in the last few yards, felt her feet gather, waited until the turn of her head gave him the line she wanted. Released, she rose above the willows and made a broad circle in the clear air.

The falconer said something about the light. Ouen did not answer. He was watching the hawk.

She took the quarry beyond the ditch, on ground too soft for the horses. He dismounted before the groom reached him and went across on foot, choosing the firmer tufts. Mud filled one shoe. He scarcely felt it. The hawk crouched over her kill with her wings spread and watched him approach.

He came slowly for the last few paces. There was no point hurrying a bird into defending what she had caught. He spoke to her, offered the food he had brought, and let her exchange one thing for another when she was ready.

When she stepped onto the glove, the falconer nodded.

“Well taken, my lord.”

Ouen looked back across the ditch. The horses stood against the pale sky, the men beside them watching. For a moment he wished his father had been among them. Valdais would have known what it had taken to recover her without a struggle. He would have said so, too. Then he would have asked why the ditch had not been cleared, and by supper Ouen would have been answering questions about the mill.

His father could be attentive to women in the same exhausting way, remembering a small wish and sending halfway across Adria to satisfy it. He could do it for Taliya and for the woman she had asked him to give up, in the same week. Ouen had once suggested that the presents might be sent by different servants. Valdais had told him to mind his own household. He had said it in front of people.

Ouen turned his attention to the bird.

They stayed out longer than he had intended. On the way home he remembered the wooden hawk and told the groom to speak to the carpenter. Then he remembered his wet shoe. The discomfort became considerable as soon as he began attending to it.

The grain carts were still beside the lower fields.

At the house, Halden waited under the arch. He had moved a chair into the sun and was examining a torn harness strap with the carter who had brought it. When Ouen rode in, both men stood.

“Good sport, my lord?”

“Very.”

The falconer came to receive the bird. Ouen transferred her carefully, easing the jesses clear of his fingers. There was blood on the glove and a small feather caught against its seam. He picked the feather off before turning towards the door.

“My lord. The grain from the lower fields.”

“Move it.”

“Very good, my lord.”
