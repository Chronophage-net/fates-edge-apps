What the Water Kept

The priestess does not ask why. She does not ask what was done, nor to whom, nor whether it was right. She asks only whether the one who drinks has asked. The water does not weigh the deed. It weighs the asking.

From the wall of the Sacrarium, beneath Heugen

Part One The Lotus Gate

Chapter 1 The cup at the gate

The man had drunk at the gate an hour ago and still had the cup in his hand.

It was one of the plain ones, unglazed clay with a lotus pressed into the base, and he held it the way people held them for the first day, as if it might be needed again. The warden who brought him to the stall had tried twice to take it from him. The second time he had let her get as far as his wrist before his fingers closed. She had given up and left him there with a nod to Mirrah, which meant: a name, when he’s ready, and the Council’s rate.

“Sit down,” Mirrah said. “It takes a while.”

He sat on the stool across the bench from her. He was perhaps forty, sunburned across the nose and one forearm, the other forearm pale, as though he had spent a long time with one arm out of a window. His boots had been good once. Someone had cut the cuff of his left trouser leg to make room for a bandage and the bandage was gone now, leaving the cuff.

Mirrah set out the tray.

Thirty lozenges of river glass lay in it on a bed of felt, each one the size of a thumbnail and the color of the lotus water at dusk, between grey and violet. Each was blank. She had cut and polished them over the winter, when trade was slow and the barges came in half empty, and she had cut them a little thicker this year because the last batch had chipped.

“You choose a sound,” she said. “I cut it. Sister Hadde binds it at the Petal Steps tomorrow, or tonight if you want to wait. After that it’s yours.”

He looked at the tray. Then at her.

“A sound.”

“A name. Whatever you’d like to be called. Most people take something short.”

“I don’t know what I’d like to be called.”

“No,” she said. “Nobody does, the first day.”

She took down the board from its peg behind her. It was cedar, gone dark with hands, and she had painted on it the names other people had chosen in the eleven years she had kept the stall, or the ones they had let her keep a record of, which was most of them. Short names, mostly. Some were words. Bread. Ford. Sorrel. One man had chosen Tuesday and been very pleased with it. She turned the board so he could read it and moved her finger slowly down the first column, not because she thought he could not read but because a man with a cup in his hand often lost his place.

He read half of the first column and stopped.

“These are other people’s.”

“You can take one if you like it. Nobody owns them.”

“I’d rather not.”

“Then we’ll find another.”

He put the cup down on the bench at last, carefully, between the tray and the edge, and looked at his hands as though they had done it without him.

Outside the stall the Mask Market had reached the slow part of the afternoon. The masks on the cord across the alley turned a little in the river wind, plaster and lacquer and painted linen, faces with no one behind them looking politely at the faces across from them. Old Brannoc two stalls down was selling lotus-ink by the thimble and complaining to the woman beside him about the price of gum. A child ran through the alley with a hoop and nobody shouted at her. At the far end, where the market opened onto the water steps, a barge was being unloaded, and every time a sack came off it the men at the bottom counted aloud.

On every doorpost Mirrah could see from her stool there was a lotus, painted or carved or in some cases simply scratched with a nail. It meant the same thing on all of them. I do not ask your past.

“Where did you come from?” she said. “Upriver or down?”

“Up.” He frowned. “I think up. The boat came down.”

“Then up.” She left it there. The question had been to settle where his eyes should go, not to take anything from him, and he seemed to understand that; his shoulders came down a little. “People from upriver often like a hard sound at the front. Down-river people like it soft. I don’t know why.”

“Hard,” he said, after a while.

“Ket. Dov. Tarn.”

He shook his head at each.

“Oren,” she said.

He said it under his breath. Then again, aloud. He looked at the cup.

“Oren,” he said.

“Oren.”

She chose a lozenge from the third row, where the glass had come out clearest, and fixed it in the jaw of the vise with a scrap of leather between to keep it from cracking. The wheel was the small one, the copper wheel, that she kept for names. She wet it from the little pot of oil and grit at her elbow and set the treadle going with her foot. The wheel sang.

It was a quiet sound when it was going well. When it went badly it whined, and the whine meant the stone was wrong or the angle was wrong or the oil had dried. She listened for it while she worked, as she would have listened for someone on the stair.

The O was easy. A circle was the first thing anyone learned on a wheel. The r was harder because it had a tail, and the tail had to stop exactly where she meant it to or the glass would run a crack along the line of it, quietly, over the next few days, and the man would find his name split in two one morning. She stopped it exactly. Her hand knew where. She had never been able to say how it knew, and she had stopped trying.

When she looked up, Oren was watching her hands.

“You do that well.”

“I’ve done it a long time.”

“Who taught you?”

It was not a question people asked in Heugen, and she saw him hear himself ask it. He put his hand flat on the bench as though he could take it back that way.

“I don’t know,” she said, which was true. “I came here able to do it.”

He nodded slowly. Then, with the care of someone setting down something that might spill, he said, “So did I. I can do this.”

He picked up a length of the twine she used for hanging charms and, without looking at it, tied something in it: two loops through each other and a turn, drawn tight with a jerk of both wrists. He held it up. It was a knot she had seen on the river, on the cargo nets, a knot that held more the harder it was pulled.

“I don’t know what it’s called,” he said.

“The bargemen will know.”

“I don’t want to ask them.”

“Then don’t.”

He laid the knot on the bench beside the cup.

She finished the e and the n and took the lozenge from the vise and held it up to the light from the alley. Oren. The letters sat in the glass the way she liked them to, a little below the surface, so that the name seemed to be under water. She washed the grit from it in the bowl and dried it on her sleeve and drilled the hole for the cord with the bow-drill, slowly, because a hole was where glass broke, and threaded it, and held it out.

He took it in both hands.

“Hadde will bind it,” she said. “She’s at the Steps until the second bell. You’ll know her. She has a thorn in her hair.”

He nodded without hearing her.

“That’s two shells,” she said. “The Council pays the other three. If you can’t pay the two today, pay me when you can.”

“I have money.” He had: he took it from an inside pocket, a little roll of coins in a twist of oiled cloth, and counted out two shells and then, after a moment, a third, and pushed them across to her. She pushed the third back.

“Two.”

“Take it.”

“If you want to give it away, give it at the gate,” she said. “They feed people at the gate who came without anything. I’m not the gate.”

He looked at the coin. Then he put it back in the twist of cloth with the others, and the twist back in his pocket, and she saw that he had decided to remember the gate and give it there, which she thought he probably would.

He stood. He was taller than she had thought. He held the charm in one hand and with the other picked up the cup, and then stood looking at it.

“I should give this back.”

“Leave it here. I’ll take it to the warden.”

“Thank you.” He set it down again. At the mouth of the stall he turned. “Do they keep a record? At the gate. Of who came in.”

“No,” Mirrah said. “They keep nothing.”

He nodded and went out into the alley with his name in his fist, and the masks turned on their cord above him in the wind, and he was gone among the stalls before she had finished wiping the wheel.

She closed early.

The light came off the river in the late afternoon and made everything gold for an hour, and in that hour no one bought anything, because people in Heugen went down to the water to watch it. Mirrah swept the grit from the bench into the tin she kept for it. She oiled the copper wheel. She wrapped the lozenges in their felt and put the tray on the high shelf out of the damp, and she took the cedar board down from its peg and held it a moment before she put it back, and added nothing to it, because Oren had not said she could.

The knot was still on the bench. She untied it, after a struggle, and wound the twine back on its spool.

The cup she carried in her apron pocket down through the market to the water steps, where the warden who had brought him was sitting on the bottom step eating a pear. Her name was Aun. She took the cup and turned it over and looked at the lotus in its base, as though she were checking that it was the right cup. It was the right cup. They were all the right cup.

“He kept it a long time,” Aun said.

“Some do.”

“He’ll be all right.”

“Yes.”

Aun finished her pear and threw the core in the river. A fish took it at once, a flash of white belly and gone. “Brannoc says there’s a barge in from Hasra with talk on it.”

“There’s always talk on barges.”

“Chains,” Aun said. “Something about chains on a road. He wouldn’t say what. You know what he’s like when he wants to be asked.”

Mirrah did know. She let it go by.

She walked home the long way, through the Lotus Wells Quarter, because the long way was cooler at this hour and because she liked to. The lanes there were narrow and shaded with balconies, and every few streets the lane widened round a well, and at each well there was a stone kerb worn smooth and a lotus-warden on a stool beside it with a tray of cups. At dusk people came. They came in ones and twos, mostly, and spoke quietly to the warden, and drank, and sat on the kerb for a while afterwards with their hands in their laps. Some of them wept. Most did not. A woman with a basket of laundry on her hip drank, and set the cup down, and shifted the basket to the other hip, and went on with her laundry.

Mirrah walked on.

She had drunk once, eleven years ago, at the river gate, from a cup like Oren’s. She remembered that. She remembered the Aster Keeper’s hands around hers on the cup, big hands, cool from the water, and the Keeper saying slowly in a voice like somebody’s grandmother. She remembered that the river had been high and brown and that she had been very cold for a reason she did not know. She remembered nothing before that, and she had not, in eleven years, wanted to.

People asked sometimes whether it troubled her. Newcomers asked, the first few weeks, before they learned the manners of the place. She told them the truth, which was that it did not. Her hands could cut glass. Her feet knew the lanes. She had friends and a stall and a room with a window on a courtyard with a fig tree in it, and none of it needed anything from before.

A smith on the corner of the dyers’ lane dropped a bar of iron on his step as she passed.

It rang. She was against the wall of the opposite house with her hand at her side before the ringing stopped, and her hand had gone to the pocket of her apron where she kept the small knife she used for opening felt. She did not remember putting it there. She stood with her back to the plaster and her heart going.

The smith was looking at her.

“Sorry,” he said. “Sorry, sister. Slipped.”

“It’s all right.”

“Frighten you?”

“No,” she said. “Startled me.”

He picked the bar up and took it inside. After a moment she took her hand out of her apron pocket. The knife was still in it, folded. She looked at it and put it back in the pocket and walked on, and by the next well she had stopped thinking about it, as she had the other times.

Her room was at the top of a stair on Fig Court. She unlocked the door. Inside, on a nail by the frame where she would see it every time she came in, hung her own charm on its faded cord: a lozenge of river glass a little greener than the ones she cut now, from some other batch, some other river, with Mirrah cut into it in a hand that was not hers. The tail of the r went a fraction too far. There was a fine crack running from the end of it into the glass. It had been there as long as she had owned it. It had never got any longer.

She touched it, as she did every evening, and went in, and shut the door.

Chapter 2 The writ-case

The writ-case had been made for a taller woman.

It hung from Sehra’s shoulder on a strap of old red leather and knocked against her hip at every step, so that by the fourth day out of Galanina she had a bruise there the size of a palm. She could have shortened the strap. The notary who had carried the case before her had been a head taller and had worn it for thirty years, and the holes he had punched in the strap were his holes, and she had not yet been able to bring herself to punch her own.

The host walked south along the Paladian in a column that took most of a morning to pass any one place. Sehra walked near the middle of it, with the clerks and the auditors and the two carts of record-chests, behind the penitents and in front of the cooks. The penitents went barefoot. They had been told they need not, and most of them had kept their sandals on for the first day and taken them off on the second, and by now the road behind them was marked every few paces with small dark prints where somebody’s foot had opened. Brother Kael, who was in charge of them, walked at the back of their file with a jar of salve and a sack of clean rags and let them go on.

To the left of the road the river ran wide and brown and slow. To the right was Galanina’s desert, flat and pale and full of light, so that if you looked at it too long the edges of things began to shake.

At the tenth bell the column halted to water the animals, and Sehra sat on a milestone and drank from her flask and took the case off her shoulder for the length of a breath before somebody came looking for her.

It was a soldier from the vanguard, a boy with a new beard and a chained halo stitched on his shoulder that had not yet faded in the sun. He had a sheet of paper in his hand.

“Notary,” he said. “The sergeant says I’m to ask you what this means.”

She took the paper. It was a copy of the General Order, the one Radan had issued at Galanina before the column left, and it had been folded so many times that the creases had begun to wear through. Somebody had underlined one sentence in charcoal.

No soldier of the Host shall take from any person of Heugen any thing, by force or by bargain, save what is named in a writ.

“It means you take nothing that isn’t written down,” she said.

“Nothing at all?”

“Nothing at all.”

“Not food? If they offer it?”

“If they offer it freely, and you pay for it, that isn’t taking. But you ask the sergeant first.”

He nodded. He looked at the paper, and she saw him read the sentence again, moving his lips.

“What’s a writ?”

“A writ is a judgment that somebody has written down so that it can be carried,” she said. “It says who, and what, and why, and what the remedy is. If a thing isn’t in a writ, the Host has no right to it, however much it wants it.”

“And if it is in the writ?”

“Then the Host has the duty to see it done.”

“Right.” He thought about that. “Who writes them?”

“Courts. Judges. Sometimes notaries, when the judge has already ruled and someone has to copy it fair.”

“You, then.”

“Sometimes me.”

He took the paper back and folded it along its old creases, and touched the halo on his shoulder as soldiers did, not quite a salute, and went back up the column. She watched him go. He had not asked her what the remedy would be for Heugen, and she was glad, because she would have had to tell him.

In the evenings, after the camp was made, she read.

That had been the old notary’s custom and she had kept it. When the cookfires were lit and the first food was in people’s hands, she climbed onto the tailboard of one of the record-carts with a lamp beside her and read the writs aloud. Not all of them every night: there were too many for that. One or two, or the General Order again, or a page from the founding judgment, so that the people who carried the Covenant’s banner would know what was in it.

At first a dozen came to listen. By the fourth night it was a hundred. Some came because it was something to do. Some came because they could not read and wanted to know what they were marching for. Some came, she thought, because the old notary had had a voice like a well-tuned bell, and hers was not like that at all, it was quiet and somewhat dry, and they had to come close to hear it, and coming close in the dark with strangers had its own pleasure.

On the fourth night she read the founding judgment.

She knew it without the page. She had known it by heart since she was eleven, when her grandmother had made her learn it in the back room in Galanina where the family kept its small image of the Arbiter: a painted board, older than her grandmother, with the green flame of the household lamp in front of it, showing a figure on a high seat with one hand open, palm up, toward a kneeling woman, and the sword lying across his knees with its hilt toward her. The hand before the sword, her grandmother had said, tapping the board with one finger. Remember the order of it.

“In the matter of Daramet of the Sidhi, plaintiff,” Sehra read, “against the House of Ourel, respondent. The plaintiff complains that the respondent has taken from her the field her mother sowed and the well her mother dug, and has driven her from them, and that when she brought her complaint to the respondent’s own steward she was told she had no standing to bring it, being a stranger in that country and a woman and poor.”

She read the findings. She read the respondent’s answer, which had been long and full of precedent, and the judgment, which had been short. She read the remedy: the field and the well returned to the plaintiff, and the respondent to dig her a second well at his own cost, so that the next stranger who comes to that country may drink. When she finished there was a silence around the cart, and then somebody near the front said, “That’s all?”

“That’s all.”

“No battle?”

“No battle. A hearing.”

“I thought there’d be a battle,” the voice said, and some people laughed, and she smiled too, because everyone thought that the first time.

“The battle is what you have when nobody will give you the hearing,” she said. “The Covenant exists so that you don’t need one.”

A man near the back said, in a voice that carried, “Then why are we marching with siege engines?”

It went quiet.

She could not see him. She could see the fire behind him and the shapes of heads between, and a few faces turning to look for him, and she could see, at the edge of the light, the long low shapes of the engines under their tarpaulins, which the column had dragged all the way from Galanina at enormous cost and which had not, as far as she knew, yet been unwrapped.

“Because Heugen has refused every hearing,” she said. “For as long as there has been a Heugen. The people there have been named in writs, again and again, in courts all along the river, and when the writs were carried there the city said what it always says, which is that it does not ask anyone’s past. That’s not a hearing. That’s a door shut in a plaintiff’s face.”

Nobody answered. After a moment she said goodnight, and climbed down, and took the lamp, and the crowd broke up around the fires.

The Prelate-Seneschal was standing by the second cart when she reached it.

She had not seen him come. He was a narrow, grey man with a soft voice and very clean hands, and he had a way of being where he had not been a moment earlier that the soldiers joked about and that she had decided, early on, was simply a matter of his walking quietly. He wore the plain grey of an auditor with nothing to show his rank except the halo, which was silver where everyone else’s was thread.

“Notary.”

“Prelate.”

“That was well answered.”

“Thank you.”

“It was also not quite true,” Radan said mildly, “and I think you know it. The siege engines are here because I do not believe Heugen will give a hearing even now. I would rather you told them that.” He looked at the fire. “They’ll trust you more for it later.”

She considered this.

“Yes,” she said. “I’ll tell them tomorrow.”

“Good.” He held out a hand for the lamp, and she gave it to him, and he lifted it so that it shone on the record-chests in the cart. “Cruciant’s scouts are back. We’re nine days out. I’d like the Writ of Abrogation fair-copied before we reach the river gate, and the schedule of persons with it. You’ll be reading it.”

“At the gate?”

“In the square inside the gate, if they let us in under the white flag. They will. They always let the first delegation in. It’s the second they shut the gate on.” He gave the lamp back. “The schedule is long. I’m sorry for that.”

It took her three nights.

The schedule of persons was not one document but a hundred, gathered from the record-chests and from letters that had come into Galanina for years: copies of judgments from circuit courts along the Paladian and the Hasra and the coast, writs of arrest and writs of restitution and, here and there, the formal complaint of somebody’s widow, written out by a notary of some town Sehra had never heard of in a hand that grew smaller towards the bottom of the page as the money for paper ran out. Each of them named somebody who had been traced to Heugen, or was thought to have gone there. Each of them had been carried to Heugen at some time, and returned unanswered. Every one of those courts sat under the Covenant’s protection, as Galanina did, and a writ of any of them ran in all the others; that was what the Covenant was, more than its banners or its hymns: a promise that a judgment given in one town would be honored in the next.

Her task was to copy them fair onto the long roll that would be attached to the Writ: name, place, charge, the court that had heard it, the remedy owed. That was all. It was the work she had been doing since she was nineteen, and she was good at it, and for the first night it went as such work always went, steadily and without much thought, the lamp hissing and the camp asleep around her and the reed going quietly from inkpot to roll and back.

On the second night she began to read them.

She had not meant to. The old notary had told her not to; you’ll copy worse if you read, he had said, because you’ll start to have opinions about the commas. But it was hard not to, by lamplight, alone, with the names going by under her hand one after another. A man of Hasra, for the burning of a granary in which two people had died. A woman of the coast, for the sale of her own children. A man of Ourel, for debts owed to forty households, all of whom had been ruined. A steward who had beaten a boy to death. A judge who had taken bribes for twenty years and then, when found out, taken his family and his strongbox and gone downriver in the night.

A great many of them were murderers. A great many more were not. There were debtors, deserters, runaway servants, a dozen apprentices who had broken their indentures, wives who had left husbands and taken the household silver with them. There were a few who seemed to have done nothing at all except be on a list that someone had made, and she marked those in the margin of her working copy for Radan to look at.

Every one of them had a remedy. That was the thing she kept coming back to. Somebody, at the other end of each of these papers, was owed something. A field. A child’s life, in the only coin a court could pay it in. A name cleared, a debt paid, a granary rebuilt. For as long as there had been a Heugen, those people had waited at the far end of the papers while the people the papers named sat in a city by the river and drank, and forgot, and were told that this was mercy. She thought of Daramet of the Sidhi, being told she had no standing to complain. She thought the widows on these papers had been told much the same thing, by a city that would not even pretend to listen.

On the third night, late, she came to a short entry in a cramped and careful hand on paper so cheap the ink had feathered.

Isaut Vell, of Ostrin on the Paladian, apprentice glass-cutter, aged about twenty-five years. For the death of her master, Ansel Garrow, glazier of that town, by a glass knife, in his own workshop. The accused fled by river the same night. Remedy: restitution to the household of the deceased, and the accused to be returned to stand before the circuit court at Ostrin.

It was an ordinary entry. She had copied a dozen like it that night. She dipped the reed and began to write Isaut Vell on the roll, and her eye, which was tired, went on reading by itself to the bottom of the old page, where somebody had added a line in a different ink.

Cf. plaint of the same, against the same, Ostrin court, the spring previous: refused, for want of standing.

She stopped writing.

She read it again. Then she looked through the bundle for the plaint it referred to, and it was not there, and she had not expected it to be; nobody kept plaints that had been refused. There was only the line. Somebody, a clerk of the Ostrin court, had thought it worth noting that the woman who had killed her master had, the spring before, tried to bring a complaint against him and been turned away. He had not said what the complaint was. He had only made a note, in a different ink, as though it had been bothering him.

Sehra sat for a while with the reed in her hand.

Then, because the schedule required the whole of the record and she had been trained to copy the whole of the record, she copied the entry fair, every word, the name and the charge and the remedy, and at the bottom, in the same careful hand as the rest, the line about the refused plaint. She blotted it. She went on to the next.

It was nearly dawn when she finished. She rolled the schedule and tied it with the red cord, and put it in the writ-case on top of the Writ of Abrogation, and closed the case, and sat on the tailboard of the cart with the case across her knees, watching the light come up grey over the desert. Somewhere behind her a penitent was crying in her sleep. Brother Kael’s voice said something low, and she stopped.

The strap of the writ-case lay across Sehra’s knees. She took the small awl from her kit, and measured the strap against her own shoulder, and punched a new hole in it, a hand’s width above the old notary’s last one.

It went through more easily than she had expected.

Chapter 3 Rumors of chains

Brannoc had decided to be asked.

He sat at his stall all morning with his thimbles of lotus-ink set out in their rows and his hands folded on his stomach, and every time somebody stopped to buy he sighed. It was a long sigh, a sigh with a story folded in it, and when the customer had gone he looked up and down the alley to see who had heard. Mirrah had heard. Everyone in that end of the Mask Market had heard by the third sigh. Nobody asked. In Heugen you could go a long time without asking, and Brannoc, who had lived there longer than most, knew it, and sighed anyway, as a man might go on knocking at a door he knew to be empty because the knocking was the point.

It was the potter’s boy who asked in the end, because he was nine and had not learned yet.

“What’s the matter with you, Brannoc?”

“Nothing’s the matter with me,” Brannoc said at once. “Nothing in the world. I’m only thinking about what I heard on the water yesterday, that’s all, and keeping it to myself, which is more than some people manage.”

The boy waited. So, by then, did half the alley.

“Chains,” Brannoc said.

He told it with his hands. A barge had come down from Hasra with salt and cloth, and the bargemen had come ashore at the water steps for beer, and one of them had been on the river road above Hasra ten days ago when the road was closed. Closed for a morning, while an army went past. Not soldiers only: carts, and men in grey, and a long file of barefoot people singing, and banners with a ring on them, a ring with a chain hanging from it. Things on wheels under tarpaulin that took twenty oxen each. It had taken the whole morning to pass, the bargeman said, and when it had passed the road was dark with blood from the barefoot people’s feet.

“Going where?” said the potter’s boy.

Brannoc spread his hands, and looked at the river.

Somebody laughed. It was a short laugh and nobody joined it. The woman who sold veils at the stall across the alley said, “There’ve been armies on the Hasra road before,” and Brannoc agreed there had, and the alley went back slowly to its business.

Old Pell did not go back to his.

He had the stall beside the veil-seller’s, where he mended masks: a cracked cheek, a lost ribbon, a nose knocked off in a fall. He had two fingers missing from his left hand and did fine work with the other eight. In eleven years Mirrah had heard him say perhaps a hundred words, most of them prices. Now, while the alley talked, he set down the mask he was working on, a lacquered fox, and covered his pots of glue, and took his apron off and folded it, and put his tools one by one in the wooden box he carried them home in, and put the box under his arm, and went. He did not hurry. He went down the alley toward the water steps, and at the corner he turned the other way, toward the Wells Quarter, and was gone.

The fox mask lay on his bench with one ear glued and the other not.

“What’s wrong with him?” the potter’s boy asked.

“Nothing,” said the veil-seller. “Go and help your father.”

By the afternoon it was everywhere.

Mirrah went to the bathhouse on Copper Lane at the fourth bell, as she did on the days when the wheel had put grit in her hair, and the women in the warm room were talking about it in the low steady way women talk in bathhouses, with their eyes half closed. Not chains now; the ring with the chain had become a halo with a chain, and the barefoot people were penitents, and the men in grey were auditors. Somebody had a cousin who had been in Galanina in the spring and seen the banners being sewn. Somebody else said that was nonsense, Galanina was a free city and sewed nothing for anybody. The name was said once, quietly, by a woman Mirrah had never seen, and then not again.

“They read things,” said the woman. “That’s what they do. They come into a town with a list and they stand in the square and read out what people did.”

“Did where?”

“Before. Before they came here.”

The warm room went still. The water dripped from the spout into the basin, and somebody turned over on her bench, and the woman who had spoken looked round at all of them, and Mirrah saw that she was frightened and that she had said it to see whether anybody else was.

“They can’t read what isn’t there,” said the bath-mistress from the doorway. She was a broad woman with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder and a ladle in her hand, and she had run the bathhouse on Copper Lane since before Mirrah came. “You drank, didn’t you? Then it’s gone. They can read their list till their tongues fall out. It’ll be a list of strangers.”

“Is it gone?”

“Of course it’s gone.”

“My grandmother used to say,” the frightened woman said, “that nothing goes. That it’s only water.”

“Your grandmother,” said the bath-mistress, “is not here, and the hot water is going cold while you talk about her,” and she ladled water onto the stones so that the steam came up in a cloud and hid them all from each other, and after that nobody said anything about it.

Mirrah lay in the steam with her eyes shut. Her hair was full of grit. Her hands were tired as they always were after a long morning on the copper wheel, a tiredness that started in the second joint of the thumb and spread backward. She thought about Oren tying his knot. She thought about a list of strangers. She found that she was trying to imagine a list with her name on it, and could not, because she did not know which name it would be.

That was a strange thought. She looked at it for a moment, in the steam, the way she would have looked at a flaw in a piece of glass before deciding whether to cut around it.

Then she let it go, and washed her hair, and went out.

The Petal Steps went down from the old temple yard to the river in forty broad shallow flights, worn into dips in the middle by two hundred years of feet, or a hundred, or however many it was. Nobody agreed. At the top, under the temple’s broken portico, the lotus-priestesses kept their tables, and at dusk people came up the steps with new charms to have them bound, and down them afterwards with the charms around their necks, and at the bottom the river went by gold and then grey and then black.

Mirrah went up because Oren had sent word by the potter’s boy that his name was bound and he wanted her to see it.

She found him halfway down the steps on the way back, with the charm on its cord around his neck and his hand closed over it, as though it might be taken. He showed it to her. Sister Hadde’s binding was a thread of white wax run round the edge of the glass and pressed with her thumb, and the print of the thumb was in the wax, small and whorled.

“She asked me to say it three times,” Oren said. “And then she said it back. And then it was mine.” He looked at the glass. “It doesn’t feel like anything.”

“It doesn’t, at first.”

“Does it after?”

“After a while you stop noticing,” Mirrah said. “That’s what it feels like.”

He nodded, and thanked her, and told her he had given the third shell at the gate, and went on down toward the river with his hand still over the charm. She watched him go. He walked more easily than he had the day before. That was usual. The second day was usually easier.

At the top of the steps Sister Hadde was packing up her table. She was a small, neat woman with her grey hair coiled at the back of her head and pinned there with a long black thorn, which she touched, now and then, while she worked, as other people touched a ring. She had bound every name Mirrah had cut for eleven years. She nodded to Mirrah without stopping, and wrapped her pot of wax in a cloth, and went in under the portico.

Sister Ilvane was sitting on the top step with a bowl of tea.

“You’ve come to see him off,” she said. “Sit. There’s more in the pot.”

Mirrah sat. Ilvane poured her tea from the little clay pot at her side. It had lotus petals in it, as all the tea on the Steps did, and it tasted as it always did, green and faintly sweet and with something at the back of it like river water. Ilvane drank hers in small sips, holding the bowl in both hands. She was perhaps fifty, and tall, with a long plain face that became handsome when she was amused and a voice that was always a little hoarse, as though she had been talking for a long time. She had been at the Steps longer than Hadde. She bought one of Mirrah’s charms every winter for the children of the temple yard, and haggled over it, and paid more than she had haggled for, every time, without remarking on it.

“You’ve heard,” Ilvane said.

“The chains.”

“The chains, the halo, the penitents with their feet.” Ilvane turned her bowl a little in her hands. “It’s true. All of it. The Covenant has a host on the river road and it’s coming here. It’ll be here before the next new moon.”

Mirrah looked at her.

“How do you know?”

“Because I asked a man who knows,” said Ilvane, “and he told me the truth, which is the only way anyone ever knows anything.” She smiled. “Everyone else in the city has spent the day hearing it and deciding not to believe it. That’s the Heugen way. You hear a thing and you decide what you’ll carry. But a thing you decide not to carry doesn’t stop being true, little—” She stopped, and drank her tea, and began again. “It doesn’t stop being true. It only stops being yours.”

The light was going off the river. Below them on the steps people were still coming up with charms, slowly, in ones and twos, and the priestess at the farthest table, a young one, was lighting her lamp.

“Can I ask you something?” said Ilvane.

It was not a question people asked in Heugen. Mirrah heard it as she had heard Oren’s who taught you, the day before: a door opened in a wall where there had not been a door.

“Yes,” she said.

“What would you do if your worst deed were read aloud? In the square. With your name in front of it.”

Mirrah thought about it. She tried to think about it honestly, because Ilvane had asked honestly, and because there was something in the question that made her want to have an answer to give back.

“I don’t have one,” she said at last.

“No,” said Ilvane. “You don’t have it. That isn’t quite the same thing as not having done one.”

She said it gently. She said it the way someone tells you there is a smut on your cheek, kindly, so that you can wipe it off before anyone else sees. Mirrah found that she had put her hand to her face.

“Everybody in this city has one,” Ilvane said. “Most people in the world have one, come to that. The difference here is that people have put theirs down. They came up these steps and drank and put it down, and walked on lighter, and that is a good thing. I’ve spent my life on these steps, and I’ll tell you honestly: I have seen it be a good thing more times than I could count. People who couldn’t walk, walking. People who couldn’t sleep, sleeping.” She set her bowl down on the step. “But something you’ve put down isn’t gone. It’s only somewhere else. And when people come with a list, they come to pick things up.”

“They can’t pick up what isn’t there,” Mirrah said. It was the bath-mistress’s line. She heard herself use it and disliked the sound of it in her own mouth.

Ilvane looked at her for a moment with her long plain face, and then it changed, and became handsome, and she laughed.

“No,” she said. “No, they can’t. You’re right. Drink your tea.”

Mirrah drank her tea.

They sat until the lamps were lit all along the top of the steps and the river below was black. Ilvane talked about other things: the price of wax, which was rising; the temple roof, which was falling; a cat that had had kittens in the vestry and could not be persuaded to leave. Mirrah listened and answered and was glad, she found, that the conversation had gone back to cats. When she rose to go Ilvane rose too, and took the bowls, and said goodnight, and then, at the top of the first flight, turned back.

“If you ever want to know,” she said, “what it would be, if they read it. Come and ask me. I won’t tell you anything you don’t ask.”

“Do you know it?”

“No,” said Ilvane. “But I know how people find out.”

Mirrah walked home through the Wells Quarter in the dark.

The lanes were quieter than they had been the night before. At the first well there was nobody drinking at all. At the second there were four, sitting on the kerb together, saying nothing, and the warden had set out more cups than usual on her tray, as though she expected more people later. At the third there was nobody, and the warden had gone to her supper, and the tray of cups stood on the stool by the kerb with a cloth half over it.

One of the cups had been knocked over. Mirrah stopped to set it upright, because she could not walk past a fallen cup, she never had been able to, and as she lifted it something slid in it and chimed against the clay.

It was a coin. An old one, silver, worn nearly smooth, with something on one face that might once have been a ship. People left coins at the wells sometimes, as thanks. The wardens kept them for the gate. She tipped it into her palm to put it with the others in the warden’s dish.

It was warm.

The night was cool. The cup had been standing in the open since sundown, and the clay of it was cold against her fingers, and the coin lay in her palm as warm as if somebody had been holding it in a closed hand all day and had only now put it down. She stood in the lane with it. She waited for the warmth to fade, and it stayed, and after a while she put it in the warden’s dish with the others, where it lay among the cold coins looking like any of them, and set the cup straight on the tray, and drew the cloth over the tray, and walked on.

She could still feel the warmth in her palm when she reached Fig Court. She could feel it when she climbed the stair, and when she touched her charm on its nail by the door, and the glass of the charm was cold, and the cold and the warm sat in her hand together and would not mix.

She held her hand under the water in the basin until she could not feel either.

Chapter 4 The Aster Keeper

The Aster Keeper’s house had nine doors and she used one of them.

It stood above the river gate, built into the wall itself, so that its back rooms were the gate’s old guardrooms and its front rooms looked out over the first wells of the city: the wide stone basin inside the gate where newcomers drank, and beyond it, stepping up the slope of the town, the six lesser wells of the Gate Quarter, each in its own small square. The nine doors opened onto the basin and the squares and the wall-walk and the stair down to the water. All but one of them had been bricked up, long before Mirrah’s time, and painted over with lotuses, so that from outside the house seemed to be all doors and none. The one that opened was low and green and stood at the top of a short flight of steps from the basin, and there was always a bucket beside it.

Mirrah knocked with the coin still on her mind.

She had not slept well. She had dreamed of nothing she could remember and woken twice with her right hand closed in a fist, and in the morning the palm had looked like any palm, and she had gone to the stall and opened it and sat for an hour without customers, turning a blank lozenge over and over in her fingers, before she understood that she was going to come here and that she had not decided to.

The Keeper opened the door herself.

“Mirrah,” she said. “Come in. Mind the bucket.”

She was as big as Mirrah remembered from the first day, and the first day was the only time Mirrah had stood this close to her. Big through the shoulders and the hips, with grey hair cropped short like a man’s and forearms like a bargeman’s, and a long apron of oiled canvas over a dress of no particular color. Her hands, when she put one on Mirrah’s arm to steer her round the bucket, were cool. Everything about the Keeper was cool, Mirrah thought: her hands and her voice and the room behind her, which was stone-floored and smelled of wet stone, and her eyes, which were pale and very steady, and looked at you the way the water in the basin looked at the sky.

“Sit,” said the Keeper. “I’m mending a rope.”

She was. A coil of it lay on the table by the window, wet and grey and as thick as Mirrah’s wrist, and the Keeper sat down to it again and took up the end where she had been working and went on splicing, her big fingers parting the strands and tucking them under and drawing them tight. Mirrah sat on the bench across from her. Through the window she could see the basin and the queue that had already formed at it, a dozen people with bundles and the dust of the river road on them, and a warden going down the line with a tray of cups.

“They’re early this year,” the Keeper said, without looking up. “And more of them. They’ve heard.”

“Heard what?”

“What you heard. What everybody’s heard. That something’s coming down the river road with a list.” She drew a strand tight. “When there’s a list, people come here before it arrives. They think that if they drink before the list is read, they won’t be on it.”

“Will they?”

“Be on it?” The Keeper’s mouth moved. “That depends what’s on it. I don’t make the lists.”

Mirrah watched the queue. A woman at the front took her cup from the warden and held it, as Oren had held his, and looked at the water in it, and drank. The warden said something. The woman nodded and sat down on the basin’s edge with her bundle in her lap and closed her eyes.

“I remember you doing that,” Mirrah said. “With me. You said slowly.”

“I say it to everybody. People gulp.” The Keeper’s fingers paused. “You were cold. I remember that. It was the end of the flood season and the river was in the gate, nearly, and you came in on a raft of somebody’s barrels, wet to the neck. You couldn’t stop shaking. I had to hold the cup for you.”

Mirrah had not known about the barrels. She sat with that.

“I didn’t come about that,” she said.

“No.”

“I came because—” She stopped. The question she had come to ask had seemed simple on the stair at Fig Court and on the long walk down through the Gate Quarter, and now, in this cool stone room, it would not come out in any form that was not a breach of manners. She tried another. “Sister Ilvane said a thing yesterday. On the Steps.”

“Ilvane says a great many things.”

“She said that something you put down isn’t gone. It’s only somewhere else.”

The Keeper’s hands stopped.

She sat for a moment with the rope across her knees, looking at the splice, and then she put the rope down on the table and wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, the backs and then the fronts, as though there were more on them than water.

“Did she say where?” she asked.

“No.”

“No. She wouldn’t.” The Keeper looked out of the window at the queue. “What did you say to her?”

“I said they can’t pick up what isn’t there.”

“Good answer.”

“Is it true?”

The Keeper turned and looked at her, and for the first time since the door had opened Mirrah felt the thing about her that the Gate Quarter children whispered about, the thing that made the wardens stand up straighter when she came down the steps. Not anger. Something cooler than anger and much larger. It was like standing at the edge of the basin at night and knowing, suddenly, that you could not see the bottom.

“I have kept these wells for thirty-one years,” the Keeper said. “My mother kept them before me and her aunt before her. In all that time no one has ever been made to drink. Do you understand that? No one. They come to the gate, and they ask, and if they ask we give, and if they don’t ask they walk in with their whole lives on their backs and nobody here says a word to them about it, ever. That’s the law of this place. It’s the only law it’s got.”

“I know.”

“You asked. Eleven years ago, wet to the neck, you asked. You said take it, and I said all of it?, and you said yes, and I held the cup.” The Keeper’s voice did not rise. It had no need to. “Whatever was in it is not your business now. You gave it. A thing you give isn’t yours to ask after.”

Mirrah held still.

“Ilvane,” said the Keeper, more quietly, “is a good priestess and she has bound more names on those steps than I could count. And she likes very much to tell people true things that frighten them. I’ve never yet decided whether she does it to help. Don’t let her frighten you. There’s a list coming, that’s true. When it comes, it’ll be a list of strangers.”

The bath-mistress had said exactly that. Mirrah thought of the woman in the steam room whose grandmother had said nothing goes, and of the coin in the cup, and opened her mouth to say something about the coin, and found that she would rather not, in this room, to this woman. She could not have said why.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Don’t thank me. Help me with this rope. My hands are getting old.”

They were not. But Mirrah held the end while the Keeper finished the splice, and handed her the marlinspike when she reached for it, and the work went on in silence for a while, and the silence was not unfriendly. Outside, the queue moved up. The woman on the basin’s edge opened her eyes and looked about her and then, after a while, smiled at nothing and stood up and went on into the city with her bundle, and somebody else sat down where she had been.

When the rope was done the Keeper took it down to the water stair, and Mirrah, because she had been given no sign to leave, went with her.

The stair went down inside the wall, dark and wet, lit by slits that let in thin blades of daylight, to a landing where the river came in under an arch and lay in a black pool against the steps. A boat was tied there, and a winch, and a great wooden wheel set into the wall with buckets on its rim, old and grey and furred with green. The Keeper hung the rope on its hook beside the winch.

“Is this where the water comes from?” Mirrah asked. “For the basin?”

“Some of it.”

“Where does the rest come from?”

“From underneath,” the Keeper said. “Everything here comes from underneath.”

On the far side of the landing, where the steps ran down into the pool, there was a second arch, lower, with an iron gate across it, and beyond the gate nothing but dark and the sound of water moving somewhere a long way off. The gate had a lock on it as big as a fist, new, bright, the only new thing in that place. The Keeper looked anywhere but at it. Mirrah looked at it for both of them.

“My mother used to bring me down here when I was small,” the Keeper said. “She said: this is the river, and that’s the town, and don’t confuse them.”

“Which is which?”

“That’s the joke,” the Keeper said. “There isn’t an answer.” She turned, and started up the stair. “Come on. Somebody’s knocking.”

Somebody was. They heard it when they were halfway up, a slow knock, three times, on the green door. When the Keeper opened it, a man was standing on the steps outside with his back to the basin and the queue, and a veil over his face.

It was a fine veil, grey silk, falling from a band at the brow to the collarbone, so that his face was a shape behind it and nothing more. Everybody in Heugen knew the veil. Speaker-of-Petals spoke for the Lotus Council at the gate and the Council chamber and the river, and had done so for nine years, and in that time, as far as anybody knew, had never once been seen without it. The children said he had no face. The bargemen said he had been a prince somewhere. Mirrah had seen him a dozen times, crossing the Market with his secretary behind him, and he had always seemed to her a pleasant, tired man with good boots, walking a little stiffly, as if one of his knees were bad.

“Keeper,” he said. His voice was low and quite ordinary. “Forgive me. The Council would like a word. About the gate, and about the—” He stopped.

He had seen Mirrah behind the Keeper’s shoulder.

That was the thing she noticed afterwards, turning it over on the walk home: not anything he did, but how completely he went still, as though every part of him had stopped at once, and the veil, which had been stirring a little in the wind off the river, hung perfectly still in front of his face.

“I’ll go,” Mirrah said.

“No,” said Speaker-of-Petals. “No, don’t—forgive me.” The veil moved again. “I didn’t know the Keeper had company. Good morning.”

“Good morning.”

“You’re the glass-cutter,” he said. “In the Mask Market.”

“Yes.”

“I’ve seen your work.” He paused. “It’s very fine.”

He was looking at her hands, not her face. She had seen glass-cutters in the market look at each other’s hands like that, across a stall, before they looked at anything else.

“Thank you.”

The Keeper looked from one of them to the other with her pale steady eyes and said nothing at all.

“I’ll come back,” Speaker-of-Petals said to her. “This afternoon. It’s not urgent.” It was plainly urgent. “Good morning,” he said again, to Mirrah, and turned, and went down the steps and across the basin, past the queue, walking stiffly, one knee bad, without looking back.

The Keeper shut the green door.

“Well,” she said.

“Do I know him?”

It came out before she could stop it, and it was the wrong question, a Heugen question turned inside out, and she felt her face go hot. The Keeper looked at her for a long moment.

“Everybody knows him,” she said. “He’s the Speaker.”

“That’s not what I meant.”

“I know it isn’t.” The Keeper put her hand on the latch. She left the door shut. “Go back to your stall, Mirrah. Cut your names. If there’s a list, it’ll come, and it’ll be read, and then it’ll go away again, the way they always do. Don’t go looking for things you gave. And don’t go up the Steps for tea.”

“Ilvane’s my friend.”

“Ilvane’s everybody’s friend,” the Keeper said. “That’s what I don’t like about her.” She opened the door. The light came in off the basin, and the noise of the queue, and the smell of river water. “Go on.”

Mirrah went.

At the bottom of the steps she looked back. The green door was shut. Beside it stood the bucket, and in the window above it the Keeper was sitting again at her table with her hands flat on the wood and her head bent, not working at anything, quite still, like somebody listening for a sound a long way off underneath.

Chapter 5 The white flag

They let the delegation in at the second bell, exactly as Radan had said they would.

Sehra had not quite believed him. She had stood in the road below the river gate with the writ-case on her shoulder and the white flag cracking in the wind above her head, looking up at the wall, and the wall had looked back at her with its painted lotuses and its arrow-slits and the round mouths of its old drains, and it had seemed to her the most shut thing she had ever seen. Then a small door had opened in the great door, and a woman in a lotus-warden’s sash had stepped out and said, “How many?” and Radan had said, “Six,” and the woman had counted them, and nodded, and stood aside.

Inside the gate there was a basin.

Sehra had read of it in the record-chests. She had not been prepared for how large it was, or how still: a wide shallow square of water, stone-kerbed, with steps going down into it at the corners, and a queue of people along one side of it with bundles on their backs, and wardens going up and down the queue with trays of cups. The queue turned to look at the delegation as it came in. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of people at a well who see somebody arrive with a jar, and wonder whether there will be enough.

“Don’t stop,” Radan said quietly, at her side.

They crossed the basin square. Sehra kept her eyes in front of her. Behind her came Marshal Cruciant, who had insisted on coming and had then said nothing for the whole of the ride down, and the Seal-Bearer in her black gown, and two auditors carrying the small portable desk. The white flag went ahead with the boy from the vanguard holding it, the one who had asked her about writs. He had volunteered. He was holding the pole so tightly that his knuckles had gone pale.

The streets beyond the square were narrow and bright and full of people who stopped to watch them pass. There were lotuses on every doorpost. There were masks on cords across the lanes, turning slowly in the wind. There was a smell of river and spice and something faint and green, like crushed stems, that she could not name. A child ran out in front of them with a hoop, saw them, stopped, and ran back. A man selling ink in thimbles watched them go by with his hands folded on his stomach and an expression of the deepest satisfaction, as though he had been proved right about something.

It was not what she had expected. She had expected, if she was honest, a city that looked ashamed of itself. It looked like Galanina on a market day.

The Lotus Council received them in the square inside the second gate, in the open, under an awning of faded violet canvas, which Radan said afterwards was a courtesy and Cruciant said was a precaution. A long table had been set out on trestles. Behind it sat seven people in masks, plain lacquered masks with only slits for the eyes, and behind them stood a crowd that filled the square and the lanes leading into it and the balconies above, so many people and so silent that Sehra could hear the canvas of the awning flapping.

In front of the table, standing, was a man with a grey silk veil over his face.

“I speak for the Council,” he said. His voice was ordinary, and carried. “You are welcome under the white flag. You will be fed and you will leave by the gate you came in by, before dark. Say what you have come to say.”

Radan stepped forward and bowed: a real bow, Sehra noted, not the stiff nod he gave the sergeants.

“I am Radan, Prelate-Seneschal of the Host of the Covenant,” he said. “I have come to read a writ. My notary will read it.” He stepped back. “Notary.”

She had practised it. She had practised it every night of the last nine, on the tailboard of the record-cart, to the empty dark after the fires were out, until she could say it without the page. She took the page out of the case anyway, and unrolled it, because the page was the law and her memory was not, and she read.

“By the Covenant of Mykkiel, and by the judgment of the courts of the Covenant at Galanina, at Hasra, at Ostrin, at Hollin’s Ford, at the Salt Gate and at forty-one other courts named in the schedule attached: to the city called Heugen, and to the Council that governs it, and to all persons dwelling in it.

“Whereas for the whole time of its existence this city has received persons named in writs of the Covenant’s courts, and has refused to answer those writs, and has given those persons the means to put away the memory of their deeds, so that they cannot be brought to answer;

“And whereas those to whom remedies are owed have been denied them, and have waited, and have died waiting;

“It is ordered that the city of Heugen be vacated within thirty days, that its wells be sealed, that its records be surrendered, and that the persons named in the schedule attached be delivered to the Host, to be returned to the courts that named them and there heard, and the remedies owed to others be paid.”

She stopped. The square was perfectly silent. Somewhere above them on a balcony a baby began to cry and was hushed.

“The schedule,” said the man in the veil, “is long?”

“Six hundred and eleven names,” Sehra said.

There was a sound from the crowd then. It was not loud. It was the sound of a great many people breathing in.

“We do not ask anybody’s past,” the Speaker said. He said it quite gently, as a man states a rule of the house to a guest who has broken it without knowing. “Here, that is the law. Whatever these people did, wherever they did it, they came here and asked to be rid of it, and were. The people on your schedule are not here. There are only the people they became.”

“Then let them answer for the people they were,” said Radan.

“They can’t. They don’t know them.”

“No,” said Radan. “But we do.”

He turned to Sehra and held out his hand, and she gave him the second paper, the one he had chosen from the schedule the night before and had her fair-copy separately on good vellum. She had asked him why that one. He had said: because it is bad enough to be believed and long enough ago that it cannot be anyone’s child.

He gave it back to her.

“Read it,” he said.

She read it.

It was the case of a woman of Hollin’s Ford, a ferry-keeper’s widow, who twenty years ago had taken in three children of a family drowned in the floods, and been paid by the parish to keep them, and kept the money, and kept the children in a shed, and when the youngest died of the cold had buried it under the shed floor and gone on taking the money for three. She had been found out in the spring when the shed flooded. She had gone down the river the same night. The court at Hollin’s Ford had named her and named the two children who lived, who were grown now, and named the remedy: the money returned to the parish, and the woman to stand before the court and say where the third child lay so that it could be buried properly in the churchyard with its family.

Sehra read it all, slowly, in her dry quiet voice that people had to come close to hear. She read the description of the woman as the court had taken it from the children: tall, a burn scar on the inside of the left wrist from the ferry’s lamp, a way of standing with her weight on one hip. She read the name.

She did not know which of them it was. She had not looked for her. But when she reached the end and raised her eyes from the page, she saw the crowd moving at its edge, near the mouth of a lane to the left of the awning, the way water moves round a stone. People were stepping back. In the space they had made there was a woman.

She was tall. She was standing with her weight on one hip, with a basket of laundry on it, and she was looking down at the inside of her own left wrist. People were looking at her. She raised her head and looked at them looking, and Sehra saw the moment when she understood what they were looking at.

She did not cry out. She put the basket down on the stones, very carefully, and stood beside it, and said, in a voice Sehra could hear across the whole of the square: “Was it me?”

Nobody answered.

“Please,” the woman said. She was looking at the people near her, one face after another. “Somebody. Was it me?”

The Speaker had turned to look at her. Behind the veil Sehra could not see his face, but she saw his hands, which had been folded in front of him, come apart and hang at his sides.

“I don’t know,” the woman said. “I don’t know. I don’t know.” She sat down on the stones beside her laundry, and put her arms over her head, and said it into her own knees, over and over, and the people who had stepped away from her did not step back, and then one of them did, an old man with a fox mask pushed up on top of his head, who sat down on the stones beside her and put his hand on her back and said nothing at all.

Sehra stood with the vellum in her hands.

She had read hundreds of writs. She had read them on doorsteps and in market squares and once, in Hasra, at a bedside, to a man who had died before she finished. She had seen people weep and curse and run and fall down. She had never before read a deed to a person who did not know it was theirs. The woman on the stones had not been accused. She had been told. And she did not know, now, whether what she had been told was true, and there was nobody in the whole of this city who could tell her, because she had asked them all not to.

Radan was speaking. Sehra heard him from a long way off, and then nearer.

“—thirty days,” he was saying, to the masked Council behind the table. “The Host will camp at the Reed Fields above the gate. The writ and the schedule will be left with the Speaker. You may answer when you choose. We have waited a long time. We can wait thirty days more.”

“And if we don’t answer?” said one of the masks. A woman’s voice, old.

Radan looked at Cruciant. Cruciant, who had not said a word since Galanina, looked at the walls of the square: the balconies, the lanes, the way the second gate stood open behind them with the river light coming through it. Then he looked back at the old woman’s mask.

“Then I’ll come back,” he said, “without the flag.”

They were fed, as the Speaker had promised: bread and cheese and figs and cold water, brought out to them by wardens on trays, under the violet awning, while the crowd broke up slowly round them and the Council went away through a door behind the table. The woman with the laundry was gone. Sehra had not seen her go. The old man with the fox mask was gone too.

Sehra left her food. She sat on the bench with the writ-case on her knees and the vellum rolled inside it, and drank the water, which tasted of stone and was very cold.

The boy from the vanguard sat down beside her with the white flag leaning on his shoulder.

“Notary,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Was it her?”

She looked at him. He was holding a fig and had not eaten it.

“The description fits,” she said.

“But was it her?”

“I don’t know,” Sehra said. “That’s what the court at Hollin’s Ford would find out. If she went back to it. That’s what a hearing is for.”

He thought about that.

“She didn’t know,” he said. “She really didn’t know.”

“No.”

“Then who are we reading it to?”

She had no answer. She drank her water. After a while he ate the fig, because a soldier does not waste food, and then, because he was nineteen and it had been a long morning, he ate two more, and she let him have hers.

Chapter 6 The schedule of persons

The Speaker came back for the schedule himself.

Sehra had thought he would send someone. The Council had gone in through the door behind the table and not come out, and the crowd had thinned until there were only the wardens clearing the trays, and children on the balconies watching the Covenant’s people eat, and the old woman who swept the square sweeping round their feet. The sun had moved off the awning. Radan sat with his clean hands folded on the table and his eyes half closed. Cruciant had gone to stand at the second gate and look at the street beyond it, and stood there so long that a warden went over and asked him if he was lost.

Then the door behind the table opened and the Speaker came out alone, without his secretary, and crossed the square to them, walking a little stiffly.

“Notary,” he said. “You have something for me.”

She stood. By the law of the Covenant a writ had to pass from the hand of the notary into the hand of the person who answered for its object. It could not be left on a table, or given to a servant, or thrown over a wall, though all three had been tried by notaries in a hurry and all three had been ruled void. The receiving party had to take it, and see that it was whole, and acknowledge it with a mark. The old notary had told her that the moment of the passing was the most important moment in the whole life of a writ, and that she should always look at the face of the person taking it, because she would learn more from that face than from anything they said afterwards.

She could not see the Speaker’s face.

She took the Writ from the case and gave it to him, and he took it in both hands and looked at the seals, and nodded. Then she took out the schedule, rolled and tied with its red cord, and he took that too.

“You must see that it’s whole,” she said. “I’m sorry. It’s required.”

“Of course.”

He untied the cord. He did it with care, as a man undoes a knot he means to tie again. His fingertips were seamed with old white burns, and the pads of the first two were hard and shiny; she had seen hands like that in the glass-street in Galanina, on the glaziers who lifted panes out of the annealing oven bare-handed. Then, holding the top of the roll in his left hand, he let the rest of it fall open down the front of his body to the ground, and the sheets went down and down in their long fair-copied column, six hundred and eleven names in her hand, past his knees, onto the stones, and lay there in a curl.

He began to roll it up again from the bottom. That was the custom: you rolled it up, and as you rolled you looked, so that you could say afterward that you had seen the whole of it. Most people did it quickly. A clerk of the Hasra court had once rolled up one of her schedules in the time it took her to blot her receipt.

The Speaker did it slowly.

She watched his hands. She could not help it; she had copied every line of what was passing under them, and some part of her was still copying, still following the column down, so that she knew, as the paper came up, where on it his eyes would be. The first night’s names. The burners of granaries. The forty ruined households. He did not stop at them. The second night’s: the steward, the judge with the strongbox. He did not stop. The third night’s.

He stopped.

It was not much. His hands stopped rolling, that was all, for the space of a breath, with a span of the third night’s sheet lying flat between them, about a third of the way down. Then they went on. But she had been watching his hands since Galanina, it seemed to her, or since much longer, since she was nineteen and had first been told to watch the hands; and in the breath in which they stopped she saw his thumb move on the paper, a very small movement, as though it had touched something hot.

He rolled the rest of the schedule quickly. He tied the red cord. He held the roll against his chest.

“It’s whole,” he said.

“I’ll need your mark,” she said. “On the receipt.”

She had the receipt ready, written out on the little desk the auditors had carried: Received of the Notary of the Host, the Writ of Abrogation and its schedule of six hundred and eleven persons, whole, at Heugen, the — and the date, and a space. She held out the reed.

“A name,” she said, “and your office.”

He took the reed. He held it over the space for a moment. Then he wrote, in a clear unhurried hand: Speaker-of-Petals, for the Lotus Council of Heugen.

“Your name,” Sehra said, as gently as she could. “Your own.”

“That is my name.”

“The receipt requires—”

“I know what it requires,” said the Speaker. “I’ve read the Covenant’s law, Notary. I read it for a long time before I came here. It requires a name by which the person who receives can be called to account for what he received. That is the name by which I can be called to account. If you want me, ask at the Council door for the Speaker, and I’ll come.” He handed back the reed. “Any other name you wrote down would be for somebody you’ll never find.”

She looked at the receipt. Radan, at the table, had opened his eyes.

“It’ll do,” Radan said. “Seal it, Notary.”

She sealed it.

They left by the river gate an hour before dark, as the Speaker had said they would, and the small door shut behind them, and the bar went down with a sound that she felt in her teeth.

The Reed Fields lay a mile upriver of the gate, on the flat ground between the road and the water, where the Host had been coming in all afternoon and making its camp. From the gate road she could see it spread out across the fields in the evening light: the lines of tents going up, and the horse-lines, and the cookfires being lit one after another, and the long low shapes of the engines under their tarpaulins, which were being unwrapped at last. Men were standing on them, folding back the canvas, calling to each other. The light caught on iron.

Cruciant rode beside her for a while without speaking. Then he said, “You were watching him.”

“The Speaker?”

“When he rolled it up. You were watching his hands.”

“It’s what notaries do.”

“Is it.” He looked at the camp, the engines. “I was watching the gate. Did you know the bar on that small door is new? Not this year. But newer than the gate. Somebody put a better bar on it, some time in the last ten years. Somebody expected to need it.” He was quiet for a while. “What did you see?”

She thought about the hands. The small movement of the thumb.

“He stopped,” she said. “Once. About a third of the way down the last sheet.”

“At a name?”

“I couldn’t tell which. There’d have been a dozen on that span.”

Cruciant nodded slowly.

“Write them down,” he said. “The dozen. Tonight, while you remember where it was.”

“Why?”

“Because he’s the only man in that city who’s going to answer us,” Cruciant said, “and I want to know what he’s afraid of.” He touched his heels to his horse and rode on ahead of her, toward the engines.

She wrote them down that night, by the lamp on the tailboard of the record-cart, after the reading.

She read no writ that night. She read the General Order again, and then, because she had promised Radan she would, she told them what he had told her to tell them: that the engines were here because the Prelate did not believe Heugen would give a hearing even now. They took it quietly. A few of them nodded, as if they had known. The boy from the vanguard was at the front, with the white flag furled and leaning against the cart wheel beside him. He asked her nothing, and when she climbed down he was gone.

She found the place in her working copy easily. The third night: she remembered the feathered ink, the cheap paper, the cramped careful hands of the small courts. A third of the way down. She counted the entries on the span that would have lain flat between his hands and wrote the names on a scrap of paper, one under another.

A man of Hasra, a deserter. A woman of the coast, a debtor. Two apprentices of Ostrin who had broken their indentures, a boy and a girl, cousins. A ferryman of Reedmouth who had drowned a passenger for her purse. A carter. A wet-nurse. A man of no fixed town who had sold the same mule to four people. Isaut Vell, of Ostrin on the Paladian, apprentice glass-cutter, for the death of her master by a glass knife; and below her, in a different ink, cf. plaint of the same, against the same, refused, for want of standing.

There were eleven names on the scrap when she was done. She looked at them for a long time.

Her eye kept going back to the one with the refused plaint. She had read that line by lamplight on the third night out and been troubled by it, and so now, of the eleven, it was the one that looked like the answer. She made herself read the other ten again, slowly, one by one, as if any of them might be the one.

She folded the scrap in half, and then in half again, and put it in the writ-case under the strap, where it would be the first thing she touched every morning.

Then she sat on the tailboard in the dark with the case on her knees, and listened to the camp going quiet around her, and to the river, and to the sound of somebody up at the engines, very late, still working, a slow tapping of a hammer on iron that went on and on and stopped only when she had nearly fallen asleep sitting up.

Down the river, under the black line of the wall, there was a single light in the high window above the gate. It was still burning when she went to bed.

Part Two Under Siege

Chapter 7 Chains across the river

The chain came down the river road on eleven carts, and it took two days to lay.

Sehra watched it go across from the bank below the Reed Fields, because the engineers had asked for a notary to witness the laying, and because once she had seen the first of it she could not stop watching. Each link was as long as her forearm and as thick as her wrist, black iron, forged in Galanina over the winter by smiths who had been told only that the Host would need a great deal of chain. They had stamped every tenth link with the chained halo. The engineers ran it out from a winch on the near bank, across a line of moored barges that they had taken from the river traders at a fair price paid in Covenant scrip, to a second winch on the far bank, where a company of the vanguard had dug in among the reeds. When the last link was made fast the chain lay across the river just under the surface, and the brown water broke over it in a long low ripple, as though something very large were lying asleep on the bottom.

A second chain went across below the city, at the bend. After that nothing came down the Paladian to Heugen, and nothing went up from it.

“It’s a lawful blockade,” the chief engineer said to her, while she wrote. He was a heavy, cheerful man from the coast with burned forearms and a great deal of respect for paperwork. “Writ of Interdiction, signed by the Prelate. We’re not taking anything. We’re only stopping it.”

“I know.”

“I only say so because you looked as if you were wondering.”

She had been wondering about the barges. They lay at their moorings along the chain with nobody aboard, rocking a little, their owners paid off and gone home up the river road with Covenant scrip in their purses and their livelihoods tied to a chain across the Paladian. She wrote down the names of the barges, all nineteen, because the engineer had not, and he watched her do it with his head on one side.

“Nobody will ever ask for that,” he said.

“Somebody might.”

On the third morning the engines began.

There were four of them. She had seen them unwrapped on the first evening, great frames of oak and iron on wheels taller than a man, with a long arm on each that the crews winched down against a twisted skein of rope as thick as a tree, and a sling at the end of the arm. Cruciant had sited them along the edge of the Reed Fields, facing not the wall, which was too thick to be worth the stones, but the land outside it.

Heugen had wells beyond its walls. She had not understood that from the records. The city had grown out past its own wall long ago, as river cities did, and along the bank above and below the gate there were orchards and lotus-ponds and drying-yards, and a scatter of low houses, and every half mile or so a well: an old stone kerb in a little square of beaten earth, with a lotus carved on its lip and a wooden roof over it. The people of the outer houses had gone inside the walls when the Host came. The wells were still there.

“Those are the outlying wells,” Cruciant said. He had come to stand beside her on the bank while the first engine was being wound. He had a slate in his hand and a stub of chalk. “Seven of them on this side. I want them broken.”

“The Writ says sealed.”

“A well that’s broken is sealed.” He kept his eyes on the well. “I’m not going to send men to brick up seven wells under the eyes of a city that doesn’t want them bricked. I’ll break them from here.”

“The water’s theirs.”

“Not now. Read your Writ, Notary.”

She had read it. She had copied it. She knew that it said the wells of Heugen were to be sealed, and she knew that it did not say how, and she knew, standing there with her case on her shoulder and the wind off the river in her face, that Cruciant was not wrong.

The engine crew finished winding. The sling was loaded with a stone the size of a sheep. The master of the crew looked at Cruciant, and Cruciant looked at the nearest well, which stood in an orchard half a mile downriver, with its wooden roof showing white among the trees, and nodded.

The arm went up with a sound like a door slamming in a high wind. The stone went up and up, turning, slowly, it seemed, as if it were thinking about where to go. It came down in the orchard short of the well and threw up a fountain of earth and branches, and a long time after, it seemed to her, the sound of it arrived, a dull flat crump that she felt in her feet.

“Short,” said Cruciant, and made a mark on his slate.

The second stone took the roof off the well. The fourth went into it.

She had thought it would look like something breaking. It did not. The stone went down out of sight under the trees and the white roof was gone and there was a moment when nothing happened at all, and then, out of the place where the well had been, there came up a spout of water.

It went up a great height, higher than the trees, white at first and then grey, and stood there in the morning air as straight as a column, and did not fall.

The crew stopped winding. Down the bank, men were standing up from their work to look. The column of water stood above the orchard, quite still, catching the low sun, so that for a moment it seemed to be made not of water at all but of light, and in the light there were shapes, shifting, the way shapes shift in the spray of a waterfall: a wall, a door, the shoulder of someone turning away, a hand.

Then it fell. It came down all at once into the trees with a long rushing roar, and the shapes went with it, and there was only the orchard and the broken roof and the water running out from under the trees in a dozen bright threads toward the river.

The chief engineer had come up beside her. He was not cheerful now.

“What was that?” he said.

“Pressure,” said Cruciant. He had not moved. “There’s water under that whole bank. You break the cap and it comes up.” He made a mark on his slate. “Next one.”

“Marshal,” the engineer said, “I’ve broken wells before. I’ve never seen one do that.”

“Then you’ve seen something new,” said Cruciant. “Next one.”

They broke three that morning. Each of them did the same. The column went up, and stood, and the shapes moved in it, and then it fell, and there was water running in the orchards and the lotus-ponds and the drying-yards, more water than any well could have held. After the third the crews worked without talking. A penitent who had been watching from the edge of the camp began to sing one of the Covenant hymns, the old one about the Arbiter’s open hand, quite loudly and badly, and kept singing it until Brother Kael came and took her by the arm and led her away.

At noon Cruciant called a halt.

“Four left on this side,” he said, to Sehra, not to the crews. “Tomorrow.”

“What did you see in it?” she asked him.

He looked at her for the first time that morning.

“Nothing,” he said. “I saw water. Pressure, and water, and the sun in it. What did you see?”

She said nothing. He waited, and then nodded, as though she had, and turned and walked back toward the engines with his slate under his arm.

That evening there was no reading in the camp.

She went instead to the bank below the first broken well, alone, with a lamp, because she had been thinking about it all afternoon and could not stop. The orchard was a mire. The water had stopped running and lay in sheets among the trees, black and still, reflecting the lamp. The well itself was a ragged hole in the earth with the kerb-stones tumbled round it and the broken beams of the roof sticking up out of it, and the hole was full to the brim with water, and the water was perfectly calm.

She knelt on one of the kerb-stones and held the lamp over it.

It was clear. That was what she noticed first: it was not brown like the river or grey like the standing water in the orchard but clear as glass, so clear that she could see down into it a long way, past the tumbled stones, past the roots of the trees where they had grown into the shaft, down into a darkness that the lamp did not reach. And it was not empty. Down at the edge of the lamplight there were things in it, small pale things, drifting slowly, like motes in a sunbeam in a shut room. Petals, she thought at first. Lotus petals. But they were not quite the shape of petals, and they turned as they drifted, and when they turned she thought she could see that they had something on them, marks, like the marks on the glass charms she had seen hanging in the lanes of the city. Like writing.

She leaned closer.

The water moved. Not a ripple: the whole surface of it rose a finger’s breadth, all at once, smoothly, as if something far down had taken a breath, and it touched the edge of the kerb-stone where her hand was resting, and was cold, so cold that it hurt.

She took her hand back. The water sank to where it had been. The pale things went on drifting.

After a while she stood up, and took the lamp, and walked back through the mire of the orchard to the camp, and sat on the tailboard of the record-cart and wrote out the morning’s witness in full: the four engines, the three wells, the columns of water, the time each stood before it fell. She wrote what Cruciant had said: pressure. She left out what she had seen in the columns, because she was not sure that she had seen it, and a notary did not write down what she was not sure of.

Then she took the scrap of paper with the eleven names out from under the strap of the writ-case, and unfolded it, and read it through, and folded it, and put it back.

Her right hand was still cold. It stayed cold until morning.

Chapter 8 The copyist

The papers came over the wall on the fourth morning, in bundles tied with red cord, wrapped round stones.

Mirrah heard the first of them land. She was at the stall early, because she could not sleep for the noise from beyond the walls: the dull flat sound that had begun the day before and went on at intervals all morning, like a great door being shut somewhere far off, again and again. Then there was a different sound, much nearer, a smack and a clatter on the roofs of the Mask Market, and a child shouting, and when she went out into the alley there was a stone lying in the gutter with paper tied round it, and Brannoc was standing over it with his hands on his knees.

“Don’t touch it,” said the veil-seller from across the way.

Brannoc touched it. He untied the cord and unwrapped the paper from round the stone and held it up, and it was a long sheet closely written in a small clear hand, and at the top, in larger letters, SCHEDULE OF PERSONS ATTACHED TO THE WRIT OF ABROGATION. SHEET FOURTEEN OF THIRTY-ONE.

“Give it here,” said the veil-seller.

“I’m reading it.”

“You can’t read.”

“I can read persons,” said Brannoc, but he gave it to her, and she read the first lines of it aloud, and stopped, and folded it in half quickly as though it were hot.

By midmorning there were wardens in every street with sacks, picking the bundles up from the gutters and the roofs and the well-squares, and carrying them away to be burned. People handed them over. Most people. Mirrah saw a man in the next alley put one inside his shirt when he thought nobody was looking, and saw the warden see him do it, and saw the warden walk on.

She did not pick any up. One landed in the gutter in front of her stall at noon, and she looked at it, at the red cord and the corner of paper showing under the cord, and went back inside and sat down at the wheel, and when she came out again an hour later it was gone.

Wenna came after dark.

She knocked on the door at the top of the stair on Fig Court as she always knocked, two quick and one slow, which was the knock that let the ward on the door know it was her. Wenna had set the ward. She had set the wards on every door in Fig Court and the four courts round it, years ago, and she renewed them each spring with a pinch of salt and a few words and a thread of red wool tied round the latch, and the people of Fig Court paid her in eggs and mending and, in Mirrah’s case, a charm every year for each of her three grandsons. She was a small brown woman with a great deal of grey hair under a scarf and a slight limp from a fall on the wall stairs, and she had never, in eleven years, come up the stair after dark.

“Can I come in?” she said.

“Of course.”

Wenna came in. She stood inside the door with her back against it and looked round the room as though she were checking the corners for something, and then she looked at the charm on its nail by the frame, and then at Mirrah.

“Sit down,” Mirrah said. “There’s tea.”

“No. Thank you. I can’t stay.” Wenna put her hand inside her shawl and took out a folded paper. Not one of the long sheets from the stones: a smaller one, a half-sheet of the coarse grey paper the threshold-witches used for their own accounts, written over in a round careful hand. “I copied them. Some of them. Before the wardens burned them.”

“Why?”

“Because people have a right to know what a paper says about them,” said Wenna, “before somebody else tells them in the street.” She held out the half-sheet. “We’ve been going round. All of us, all the circles. We copied what we could and we’ve been taking them round to the people we thought they might be. Quietly. So nobody’s surprised in the square.”

Mirrah did not take it.

“You think I’m on it.”

“I don’t think anything,” Wenna said. “I’m not saying it’s you. I’m saying there’s an entry on a paper that came over the wall, and I’ve known you eleven years, and I’d want somebody to show me.” She held the paper out a moment longer. Then she put it down on the table by the door, open, and stepped back from it, as people step back from a bowl they’ve set down for a dog that might bite.

“It’s the fourth one down,” she said.

Mirrah looked at the table.

She left it there a while. She stood where she was, in the middle of her own room, with the lamp on the shelf behind her and Wenna by the door and the paper on the table between them, and she found that she was thinking, quite calmly, about the warm coin in the cup, and about Ilvane on the steps saying a thing you decide not to carry doesn’t stop being true, and about the Keeper saying don’t go looking for things you gave. She had not gone looking. It had come over the wall on a stone.

She went to the table.

The first entry was a man of Hasra, a deserter. The second was a woman of the coast, a debtor. The third was two apprentices of a town she had never heard of, cousins, who had broken their indentures. The fourth was written in the same round careful hand as the others, but Wenna had pressed harder on it, so that the letters stood up off the coarse paper and could almost have been read by touch.

Isaut Vell, of Ostrin on the Paladian, apprentice glass-cutter, aged about twenty-five years (eleven years past). For the death of her master, Ansel Garrow, glazier of that town, by a glass knife, in his own workshop. The accused fled by river the same night. Remedy: restitution to the household of the deceased, and the accused to be returned to stand before the circuit court at Ostrin.

And then, below, squeezed in at the bottom of the entry where Wenna had nearly run out of room:

Cf. plaint of the same, against the same, Ostrin court, the spring previous: refused, for want of standing.

Mirrah read it twice.

The name meant nothing. That was the first thing, and she was distantly surprised by it; she had thought, when she let herself think about it at all, that a name would be like a key, and that if anyone ever said hers the lock would turn. Isaut. She said it inside her head. It was a name. It was a little old-fashioned, a river name. It did not turn anything.

Ostrin meant nothing. Ansel Garrow meant nothing. Glazier meant—her eye went to her own hand, lying on the table beside the paper, the second joint of the thumb where the tiredness always started—something, perhaps, or nothing.

It was the last line that she could not stop reading.

Refused, for want of standing.

She did not know what a plaint was, not precisely. She knew what refused meant and she knew what standing meant. And something in the joining of them, in that short dry sentence that some clerk had squeezed in at the bottom in a different ink, went into her and went cold, the way the coin had gone warm, and spread. She stood with her hand flat on the table and felt it spread up her arm and across her chest, and she could not have said what it was. It was not memory. There was nothing in it. It was only cold, and the shape of a door shut in a face, and she did not know whose face.

“Mirrah,” Wenna said.

“Yes.”

“Sit down.”

She sat on the bed. Wenna came and sat beside her, not touching her, with her hands in her lap.

“I’m not saying it’s you,” Wenna said again. “There were three glass-cutters on the sheets I copied. One of them was a man. There’ll be more on the sheets I didn’t see. Lots of people came down the river eleven years ago.”

“Wet to the neck,” Mirrah said. “On a raft of barrels.”

Wenna said nothing.

“I asked the Keeper how I came,” Mirrah said. “She told me. The end of the flood season.” She looked at her hands. “It says the same night. By river.”

“Lots of people—”

“I know.”

They sat. Below in the court, somebody was drawing water from the well under the fig tree; she heard the rope on the wheel, and the bucket knocking the side of the shaft, and the slosh of it coming up. A dog barked twice and stopped.

“What will you do?” Wenna asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t have to do anything. The paper’s only a paper. They can’t take you off the street for a paper; the Speaker won’t let them, and they can’t get in.” Wenna was quiet a moment. “If you want, I’ll put a stronger ward on the door. There’s one I know. It won’t keep a person out, but it’ll tell you who’s on the stair.”

“Thank you.”

“I’ll do it tomorrow.” She got up. At the door she turned and looked at the charm on its nail, and then at Mirrah, and said, “Burn the paper, if you like. Or keep it. It’s yours. I only copied it.”

“Wenna.”

“Yes?”

“Who else have you shown?”

Wenna’s face changed a little. “Eleven people tonight,” she said. “Some of them cried. One of them laughed. One of them hit me.” She touched her cheek, and Mirrah saw for the first time that there was a mark on it. “He didn’t mean it. He was sorry after.”

“Why are you doing this?”

Wenna thought about it.

“Because someone came to my door when I was nineteen,” she said, “and told me what a paper said about me before the man who’d written it got there. And I had a night to decide what to do. That’s all. Only a night. But I had it.” She opened the door. “Goodnight, Mirrah. Lock this.”

Mirrah did not burn the paper.

She sat on the bed with it for a long time after the knock had gone down the stair, and then she got up and put on her shoes and her shawl and went out, and down, and across Fig Court in the dark, and through the empty lanes to the Mask Market, and unlocked the stall.

She lit the lamp. She took the tray of blank lozenges down from the high shelf and unwrapped the felt, and chose one from the third row, where the glass had come out clearest, and fixed it in the jaw of the vise with a scrap of leather between. She wet the copper wheel. She set the treadle going.

The wheel sang.

She cut it slowly. I, which was nothing, a single stroke. s, which was the hardest letter there was, two curves turning against each other, and the glass liked it least of any letter and would split along it if you let it. She did not let it. Her hand knew where. a, u, t.

It came out perfectly. It sat in the glass the way she liked them to, a little below the surface, so that the name seemed to be under water.

She took it out of the vise and washed it in the bowl and held it up to the lamp. Isaut. It looked like any other name she had cut, on any other night, for any stranger who had come down the river and drunk and needed something to be called.

She did not drill the hole. She wrapped it in a scrap of felt, small and tight, and put it in the drawer under the bench, at the back, behind the spare drill-bits and the little pot of polishing rouge, and shut the drawer, and oiled the wheel, and put out the lamp.

On the way home she passed three wells. At every one of them, though it was nearly midnight, there were people sitting on the kerb.

Chapter 9 Refused

The basin inside the river gate was higher than Mirrah had ever seen it.

She noticed it from the top of the steps, in the grey before sunrise, while she was still deciding whether to go down. The water stood a hand’s breadth below the lip of the kerb, where on every other morning in eleven years it had stood an arm’s length below, and it was not still. Nothing moved on it. There was no wind. But the surface had a tremor in it, a fine continuous shiver, like the skin of a horse that has been stung, and every so often, at no interval she could find, the whole of it rose a finger’s breadth and sank again.

The queue was already there. It was longer than she had ever seen it too: it went the length of the basin and round the corner into the first of the Gate Quarter squares, and it was not the queue she knew, newcomers with the dust of the river road on them. These people had no bundles. They had come from inside the city. She knew some of them. The bath-mistress from Copper Lane was there, near the front, with her sleeves rolled down for once and her arms folded. A man who sold her drill-bits. A woman she had cut a name for four years ago, who had come in on a salt barge with a baby and no husband and had chosen the name Ash.

Nobody was drinking. The wardens stood at the head of the queue with their trays of cups, and the cups were empty, and the wardens were looking at the green door.

Mirrah went down the steps and past the queue, and nobody stopped her, because she was not carrying a cup and people in Heugen let each other be, and climbed the short flight to the green door, and knocked.

The Keeper opened it.

She looked as if she had not slept. Her apron was wet from the hem to the knee and the sleeves of her dress were wet to the elbow, and there was a smell on her of the deep stair, cold stone and old water, and a smear of green on one forearm like the green on the buckets of the great wheel. She looked at Mirrah. She looked past her at the queue. She did not say come in.

“I want to drink,” Mirrah said.

“I know.”

“I want—” She had rehearsed it on the walk down, as you rehearse a thing you are going to say to a judge, and now it came out wrong, in pieces. “There’s a paper. It came over the wall. I’ve read it. I don’t want to have read it. I want to give it back.”

“You can’t give it back,” said the Keeper. “You never had it.”

“I’ve got it now.”

“You’ve got a paper. That’s not the same.” The Keeper stayed in the doorway. “A paper isn’t a memory, Mirrah. It’s ink. The water doesn’t take ink. You could drink the basin dry and that paper would still be lying wherever you left it, saying what it says. The only thing you’d lose is having read it. And then you’d find it again, or someone would show you, and you’d read it again for the first time.” She paused. “Do you want to do that every week until they go away?”

“Then give me—” Mirrah stopped.

She had been going to say give me the rest of it. She had been going to say if there’s anything left, take that. She did not know what she meant by it. She stood on the step with her mouth open and the Keeper looking at her with those pale steady eyes, and behind her the queue had gone quiet, as the square had gone quiet when the woman with the laundry asked her question, and she understood that they were listening.

“No,” said the Keeper.

She said it to Mirrah. But she said it loudly enough that it went out past her over the basin and the queue and round the corner into the square, and Mirrah heard it go, and heard the queue hear it.

“No,” the Keeper said again, more quietly. “Not now. Not you, and not anyone, until I say. Go home.”

“You’ve never refused anyone.”

“I know.”

“It’s the only law it’s got. You said. If they ask, you give.”

“I said that,” said the Keeper. “And I’m saying this. The water’s heavy. Something’s broken it outside, and it’s heavy, and it’s moving, and I’ve been down in it since midnight trying to find out where. I’m not giving anybody a cup of it until I know what’s in the cup.” She put her wet hand on the latch. “Go home, Mirrah. Go and cut your names. If you want to know what I’m afraid of, I’m afraid of that.” She lifted her chin at the basin, the shivering water, the lip it was a hand’s breadth below. “Not you.”

She shut the door.

Mirrah stood on the step.

Behind her, the queue was breaking up. It broke up slowly. People stood a while longer looking at the green door, and then at each other, and then, one by one, they began to leave: a few toward the square, most of them back up the steps the way they had come. Nobody spoke to her. The bath-mistress, going past the foot of the green door’s steps, stopped and looked up at her, and Mirrah saw on her broad red face an expression she had never seen on it before, which was fear, and then, under the fear, something that might have been blame.

“Did you ask for us all?” the bath-mistress said.

“No.”

“She said not anyone.”

“I only asked for me.”

The bath-mistress looked at her a moment longer. Then she unfolded her arms and pushed her sleeves up to the shoulder, as if she had remembered who she was, and went off up the steps toward Copper Lane without another word.

The wardens were putting the cups back in their crates. One of them was Aun, who had taken Oren’s cup from her on the water steps a hundred years ago, or nine days. Aun kept her eyes on the cups. She stacked the cups in the crate one inside another, carefully, so they would not chip, and when she had finished she sat down on the crate with her hands between her knees and looked at the basin.

Mirrah went and sat beside her.

“She’s never done that,” Aun said. “Not in my time. Not in my mother’s.”

“I know.”

“What’s she seen down there?”

“She didn’t say.”

The water rose a finger’s breadth, all at once, and sank. Aun flinched. They sat and watched it, and after a while it did it again.

“People will say it was you,” Aun said. Not unkindly. As if telling her where it might rain.

“Yes.”

“Was it?”

“I asked,” said Mirrah. “That’s all. Same as everyone.” She looked at the green door. “She’d have said no to anyone. She said so. She’d been down there all night.”

“People will still say it.”

“I know.”

Aun nodded slowly, and they sat a while longer, and then the noise began again beyond the walls, the great door shutting somewhere far off, and they both looked toward the sound, and as it came the water in the basin shivered all over at once, from end to end, like water in a cup on a table when somebody heavy walks across the floor.

She walked, instead of going to the stall.

She walked up through the Gate Quarter and round the edge of the Wells Quarter and along the top of the Petal Steps without stopping, and down through the dyers’ lanes, and round again, as she had sometimes walked in her first year, when she had not yet learned the city and had wanted to make her feet learn it for her. At every well there were people. At every well the warden had set the cups out on the tray as usual, and at every well nobody was drinking; word had gone round already. People sat on the kerbs, as they did in the evenings, with their hands in their lap. Some of them looked down into the water. Some of them looked at her as she went by, and she could not tell, any more, whether they would have looked anyway.

At the well in Fig Court, under the fig tree, the water had risen too. It stood almost to the lip of the shaft. She could have put her hand down and touched it.

She did not.

She went up to her room and sat on the bed, and took the half-sheet of grey paper out of her shawl, where she had put it last night before she went down to the stall, and unfolded it and read it again. Isaut Vell. Refused, for want of standing. And the cold came up her arm again, slowly, as it had the night before, and she let it, this time, and sat with it, and tried to see the face of the door it was shut in. She could not. There was nothing. There was only the cold, and the shape, and under the shape, very faint, something like the smell of a room where glass is worked, hot metal and grit and oil.

That might have been her own stall. It might have been anything.

She folded the paper and put it under the pillow, and lay down with her shoes on, and slept without meaning to until the afternoon.

When she woke, the light in the court had gone gold, and somebody was sitting on the edge of the well under the fig tree, looking up at her window.

It was a child. A girl of nine or ten, thin, in a brown dress too short in the sleeves, with her hair cut short and ragged as if she had done it herself. Mirrah did not know her. She knew every child in Fig Court and the four courts round it, because she cut their names, and this one was not one of them.

The girl looked up at the window. Mirrah looked down. Neither of them moved.

Then the girl got up off the edge of the well, unhurriedly, and dusted her hands on her skirt, and walked out of the court by the lane that led toward the river.

Where she had been sitting, on the kerb of the well, there were two wet handprints, small, side by side, drying slowly in the last of the sun.

Chapter 10 The naming trials

The first boat came down on the sixth night, and the vanguard took it at the chain.

Sehra heard about it at breakfast. A skiff, the sergeant said, a little flat river skiff with no light, poled down along the far bank in the dark by a man who knew the river, carrying a woman and two children and a sack of bread. It had gone into the chain under the surface without seeing it and stopped dead, and the man had gone over the bow, and the vanguard had heard him shout and gone out in a boat and pulled him out of the river and brought them all in. Nobody was hurt. The children had been given porridge. They were in a tent at the end of the auditors’ lines with a guard on it, and the Prelate wanted the Notary.

“Why?” said Sehra.

“The woman’s on the schedule,” said the sergeant. “Or she might be. The Prelate wants a trial.”

The Seal-Bearer heard it.

That was the first thing Sehra was glad of, that morning. Radan had meant to hear it himself, she learned afterwards; the Seal-Bearer had gone to his tent before breakfast and talked to him for the length of a bell, and when she came out she was to hear the trials, all of them, and the Prelate was to sit at the side of the tent and not speak. The soldiers said she had threatened to go back to Galanina. The auditors said she had quoted the founding judgment at him for the length of a bell and he had given in to stop her. Sehra never asked which. The Seal-Bearer was a tall old woman of the coast with a face like a closed book and a voice that never rose, and she had been a judge of the Covenant for forty years, and she had sat on the bench at Galanina the year Sehra came to be examined for her notary’s ring and had asked her one question, and Sehra had got it wrong, and the Seal-Bearer had passed her anyway, for reasons she had never explained. Sehra had spent twenty-one years wondering what the right answer was.

The trial was held in the auditors’ great tent, with the sides rolled up so that anyone who wished could see. The Seal-Bearer sat at a table at one end. Sehra sat at a small table beside her with the writ-case and the working copy of the schedule and a clean sheet of vellum. The Prelate sat at the side, on a camp stool, with his clean hands folded.

They brought the woman in.

She was perhaps thirty-five, and small and tired, with her dark hair still wet from the river and a blanket round her shoulders over a dress that had been good once. She sat on the stool they gave her and looked at the Seal-Bearer and then at the Prelate and then, for a long time, at Sehra, as if she had decided Sehra was the one to watch.

“What is your name?” the Seal-Bearer asked.

“Lis.”

“Is that the name you were given at your birth?”

“It’s my name.”

“Did you drink, in Heugen?”

The woman hesitated. Then she nodded.

“When?”

“Seven years ago.”

“From where did you come to Heugen?”

“I don’t know,” Lis said. “Downriver. The coast, they told me. I came in on a salt barge.”

The Seal-Bearer turned to Sehra. “Notary.”

Sehra had found it the night before, after the sergeant told her the woman’s description; she had sat up with the working copy and gone through the coast entries one by one. There were nine women who might have been her. Eight of them were too old or too young or had come too long ago or too lately, or were described with marks the woman did not have. One was not.

She read it. Of the Salt Gate. Married. For abandoning her household, and taking from it two hundred marks in coin which were the dowry of her husband’s sister, and a child of four years. Remedy: the coin returned to the sister, and the child returned to the father, and the accused to stand before the court at the Salt Gate.

“The child would be eleven now,” said the Seal-Bearer. “A girl or a boy?”

“A girl,” said Sehra.

The woman on the stool had gone still. Her hands were holding the edges of the blanket together at her throat, and her knuckles had gone white, and she was looking at Sehra.

“The children with you,” the Seal-Bearer said. “Are they yours?”

“Yes.”

“Both?”

“Yes.”

“How old is the elder?”

“Eleven.” Lis’s voice cracked. “She’s eleven. She’s—I’ve got her name on a charm, it’s—” She stopped. She looked at the Prelate and back at the Seal-Bearer. “Does it say why?”

“The writ does not,” said the Seal-Bearer.

“Does it say anything about him? The husband? Does it say what he—” She stopped again. She put her hand flat on her own breastbone, under the blanket, as if something there hurt. “I don’t know why I’m asking that. I don’t know anything about him. I don’t know his name.”

“His name is in the writ,” said the Seal-Bearer. “Do you want to hear it?”

For a while the woman said nothing. The tent was full of the sounds of the camp outside, a farrier somewhere hammering, a horse complaining, a long way off the noise of an engine being wound.

“No,” Lis said.

“Then I will not read it,” said the Seal-Bearer.

At the side of the tent the Prelate moved, a small movement, as if he were about to speak; and the Seal-Bearer, without looking at him, raised one finger from the table, and he did not.

“This is not a hearing,” the Seal-Bearer said to the woman. “I want you to understand that. A hearing can only be held by the court that named you, at the Salt Gate, and only after you have been brought there. This is a naming. The Host has the right, under its Writ, to tell you what you have been accused of and to keep you until you can be taken to answer it. That is all. It has done that now.” She folded her hands. “You will stay in the camp, with your children, under guard. You will be fed and nobody will harm you. When the Host goes home, you will go with it, to the Salt Gate. Do you understand?”

The woman nodded.

“Is there anything you wish to say?”

The woman looked at Sehra again.

“Thank you,” she said.

Sehra waited, not understanding.

“I’ve always been afraid of something,” Lis said slowly, “and I’ve never known what. Seven years. I wake up at night and I’m afraid and there’s nothing there. And now there is.” She looked down at her hands on the blanket. “It isn’t less frightening. But it’s got a shape. That’s better. I didn’t think it would be better, but it is.” She looked up again. “So thank you.”

They took her out. At the flap of the tent she stopped and looked back, not at the Seal-Bearer or the Prelate, but at Sehra, and then she went.

There were four more that week.

The vanguard took two boats at the upper chain and one at the lower, and on the ninth night a man swam it, alone, in the dark, and nearly made it past, and was pulled out of the reeds below the camp half drowned and coughing. Sehra found them on the schedule, three of the four, and the Seal-Bearer heard them in the open tent while the Prelate sat at the side, and Sehra read.

A man of Hasra, a deserter, who listened to the description of his own desertion with his head on one side, like a man hearing a story about a cousin, and at the end said, “Well, I’m sorry for it,” and asked if there would be bread. A woman of the coast, a debtor, who wept all through the reading and at the end could not remember any of it, and had to be read it twice. An old man whom Sehra could not find on the schedule at all, and who said, when the Seal-Bearer told him so, that he had not thought he would be, he had only wanted to see his sister in Ourel before he died, and might he go on? The Seal-Bearer let him go on. The Prelate stood up and left the tent, and came back, and sat down.

The swimmer was the last.

He was young. Not much older than the boy from the vanguard, Sehra thought, with a narrow face and long arms and a scar across the knuckles of his right hand. He sat on the stool in a borrowed shirt with his own wet one in his lap and looked at the floor while she read.

He had been a soldier too. Not of the Covenant. Of a lord upriver, in a war that had been over for ten years now, about a boundary that had since been moved twice. He had been one of a company that had come at night to a village on the wrong side of the boundary and had been ordered to fire it, and had fired it, and he had stood at the door of one of the houses with a pike, as ordered, so that nobody came out. Nobody had. The court that named him was the court of the lord on the other side of the boundary, and the remedy it named was the remedy every court of that country named for that crime, and Sehra read that too.

He listened to all of it with his eyes on the floor.

At the end the Seal-Bearer said what she had said to the others: that this was a naming, not a hearing, and that he would be kept, and fed, and taken in the end to answer. He nodded. She asked him if there was anything he wished to say.

He looked up at Sehra.

“Was there a dog,” he said.

She looked at the page. “It doesn’t say.”

“I keep dreaming about a dog,” he said. “At a door. That’s all. Seven years I’ve been dreaming about it. I thought it was nothing.” He looked at the scar on his knuckles. “Was there a dog?”

“It doesn’t say,” Sehra said again, because it did not, and because she could not think of anything else.

He nodded. They took him out.

In the morning the guard on his tent was asleep and the tent was empty, and at midday the engineers found him at the upper chain, where the river had carried him down against the links in the night, and held him there, with his long arms through the iron as if he had been trying to climb it.

There was no reading in the camp that night either.

She sat instead on the bank below the first broken well, in the dark, without a lamp, and watched the water in the orchard. It had not drained away. It lay among the trees in long black sheets, and the trees were standing in it to their knees, and the soldiers had begun to call the place the Mirror and to avoid it. One of them had washed his shirt in it two days ago. Afterwards he had sat down on the bank in his wet shirt and wept, and told his sergeant, when the sergeant came, that he had been in a kitchen with a blue-tiled floor and a woman singing, and that he had never in his life been in a kitchen with a blue-tiled floor, and he wanted to go back to it. The sergeant had sent him to Brother Kael. Brother Kael had told the camp to keep out of the orchard water. Nobody had needed telling twice.

Somebody came and sat down beside her on the bank. It was the boy from the vanguard. He had a blanket over his shoulders, and for a while he said nothing.

“What’s your name?” Sehra said.

He looked at her, surprised. “Haven’t got one, Notary. Not a proper one. I was a foundling at the halo house in Galanina. They put me on their roll as the boy from the third bench, and when I signed on, the sergeant wrote boy.” He pulled the blanket tighter. “Nobody’s ever needed to call me anything else.”

She took her own book from the case and opened it on her knee, though it was too dark to see the page, and wrote by feel, slowly, in the careful hand she used for writs.

“Davo,” she said. “It was my grandfather’s name. It means someone who carries a thing for somebody else.”

“Is that allowed?”

“It’s written down,” said Sehra. “That makes it allowed.”

He said it under his breath. Then again, aloud.

“Davo,” he said.

“I was on the chain this morning,” he said at last. “When they brought him up.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I knew him,” the boy said. “Not knew him. I’d seen him. At the tent. I took him his bread.” He was quiet. “He asked me if I’d ever been to his country. I said no. He said it was flat. Very flat. He said you could see a house burning from a day away.” The boy pulled the blanket tighter. “He wasn’t crazy. He was all right. He asked about my mother.”

Sehra said nothing. There was nothing in the founding judgment for this, and nothing in the General Order, and nothing in any writ she had ever copied. In twenty-one years of reading writs aloud she had never once been there when a remedy was paid. She read, and passed the paper from hand to hand, and went on to the next town, and somebody else, somewhere else, returned the field or the money or the child. She had always thought that was the shape of the work. Sitting on the bank in the dark, she found that she wanted, more than she had wanted anything since she was nineteen, to see one paid. There was only the boy beside her, and the dark water among the trees, and the far black line of the wall, with one light burning in the high window above the gate.

“The woman said thank you,” the boy said. “The first one. Lis. I heard her.”

“Yes.”

“And he—” He stopped. “Same writ. Same reading. Same everything. And one says thank you, and one goes in the river.” He looked at her. “Which one was it for, Notary? The reading. Which one was it supposed to be for?”

“The people at the other end,” Sehra said. “The ones who are owed.”

He thought about that.

“His village,” he said.

“Yes.”

“They’re all dead,” said the boy. “It says so. That’s the crime. They’re all dead, and there’s nobody at the other end to be owed.” He stood up. “So who did we read it to?”

He went away up the bank toward the fires. She sat on in the dark. After a while she took the scrap of paper with the eleven names out from under the strap of the writ-case and held it in her hand, without reading it, because she could not see to read; only held it, as she might have held somebody’s hand in a room where somebody was dying.

Chapter 11 The girl who follows

Wenna was found at the foot of the wall stairs on the eleventh morning, with her scarf over her face.

The stairs went up the inside of the wall behind the dyers’ lanes, steep stone steps without a rail, to the wall-walk where the threshold-witches went at the turn of every season to renew the ward on the gate. Wenna had fallen on them once before, years ago, and limped ever since. Everybody knew that. Everybody knew, too, that the stones had been coming down outside the walls day and night for a week, the great flat crump of them shaking the ground, so that pots walked off shelves in the dyers’ lanes and the water jumped in the wells. The wall must have shaken, people said. She must have been on the stairs when it shook. Her bad leg.

Mirrah heard it from the potter’s boy, who had heard it from the veil-seller, who had seen them carry her down to the Wells Quarter on a door. She shut the stall and went to Fig Court and stood at the bottom of her own stair.

Wenna had come the day after she brought the paper, as she had promised, and set the new ward on the door. She had knelt on the top step with her bad leg out stiff beside her and worked for the best part of an hour, with salt and a red thread and a little brass bell no bigger than a fingernail that she hung inside the frame where the charm was, and when she had finished she had said, There. If anyone comes up who isn’t you, that’ll ring. Not loud. You’ll hear it. And Mirrah had paid her in a charm for the youngest grandson, which she had cut the week before, and Wenna had put it in her shawl and gone down the stair and across the court with her hand on the fig tree for balance, the way she always did, and that was all.

The little bell was there inside the frame. Mirrah touched it. It did not ring for her.

She went up and sat on the bed and did not cry, and after a while she understood that she was not going to, and that she was sitting with her hands in her lap like the people on the kerbs of the wells, and she got up.

She went up the Petal Steps at dusk, because the Keeper had told her not to.

The Steps were full. She had never seen them so full. The priestesses’ tables under the broken portico had been pushed back against the columns to make room, and the top of the steps was crowded with people sitting, standing, waiting, and the line of them went down the first three flights. Nobody had come to have a name bound. Nobody was carrying a charm. In the middle of the crowd, at a trestle under a lamp, there was a great iron pot on a tripod over a brazier, and Sister Ilvane was standing behind it with a ladle.

“Bread’s on the left,” she was saying. “Salt in the dish. Take a bowl and give it back, we haven’t enough bowls. No, it’s free. It’s free. Put that away.”

The man in front of her put his coin away. She ladled soup into his bowl, and he took a heel of bread and a pinch of salt and went and sat on the steps to eat it, and the next person came up, and the next.

Mirrah stood at the edge of the crowd and watched.

There had been rationing in the Gate Quarter since the third day of the chain. The Council had shut the granaries and given out bread by the household, a loaf a day for two people and half a loaf more for each child, from the doors of the old customs house on the river. People grumbled and queued. Mirrah had queued, the same as everyone. She had not known there was soup on the Steps.

Most of the people eating were like the people in the queue at the customs house: tired, and hungry, and talking to each other in low voices about the chain, the stones, the wells. But not all of them. On the second flight of steps, a little apart from the others, there were five or six people sitting in a row who were not talking to anybody. They held their bowls in both hands and ate slowly. They were thin, much thinner than the rationing could have made anyone in ten days, and their clothes were good and hung on them as if they had been made for somebody larger. They were the most polite people she had ever seen. When the woman next to them dropped her bread, three of them reached for it at once, and the one who reached it first gave it back to her with a little bow.

One of them looked up at Mirrah as she came past.

He was an old man with a gentle face, clean-shaven, his white hair combed with care. He had finished his soup. He was holding the empty bowl in his lap.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I’m sorry to ask. You wouldn’t have a name you could spare?”

Mirrah stopped.

“A name?”

“Any name. It needn’t be a good one.” He smiled at her. He had a beautiful smile, very sweet, very tired. “I had one. I gave it away, you see, and then I gave away the one after that. I’ve been meaning to get another.” He looked at her hands. “You cut them, don’t you? In the market. I’ve seen you.”

“Yes.”

“Then you’d have one to spare,” he said. “Only a small one.”

She had nothing to say to that. He waited, still smiling, quite patient, and when no answer came he nodded, as if she had, and said, “Never mind. Never mind. Thank you anyway,” and looked down at his empty bowl.

“Mirrah.”

Ilvane was beside her. She had given the ladle to another priestess and come round the end of the trestle, wiping her hands on a cloth, and she put one hand on Mirrah’s arm and drew her gently away, up the last flight and in under the broken portico, where it was quieter.

“Don’t mind him,” Ilvane said. “He asks everybody. He doesn’t mean any harm.”

“Who is he?”

“Somebody I’ve been feeding for a long time.” Ilvane looked back at the old man, at the row of thin polite people on the second flight. “They’ve had a hard few years, those ones. They come every evening. Sit down. You’ve heard about Wenna.”

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry. She was a good woman. She fixed the ward on our vestry door last spring and wouldn’t take anything for it.” Ilvane sat down on the plinth of a column and pulled Mirrah down beside her. Up close, she smelled of soup and smoke and, under it, of the lotus tea, green and faintly sweet. “You’ve had a paper.”

Mirrah looked at her.

“Wenna showed me some of what she was showing people,” Ilvane said. “I asked her to. I’ve been helping them, a little; I know who came in when, from the binding-book. She told me she’d been to you.” She kept her hand on Mirrah’s arm. “I won’t ask what it said. I told you I’d only tell you what you asked.”

“Then tell me what’s going to happen.”

Ilvane looked at her for a moment. Then she told her.

She told her that the Covenant had taken five people at the chain in the last week, and read them their deeds in the camp, and that one of them, a young man, had gone into the river afterwards and been found against the chain in the morning. She told her that the Host had the whole schedule, six hundred and eleven names, and that every one of them had a description beside it, and that they would be read, all of them, in the square, if the city fell, and that the people standing in the square to hear them would be the people those six hundred and eleven had lived beside for ten years and twenty and thirty. She told her that the Council had had the schedule for twelve days and had not said one word to anyone about what was on it, and that the Speaker had it locked in a chest in the Council chamber and slept beside the chest, and that the Council was splitting, three ways, and that one way wanted to give the Covenant what it asked for, and one way wanted to give it nothing, and one way wanted to break the wells so there would be nothing for anybody to give. She told her that the Keeper would not give her another cup, nor anyone; that the Keeper was down in the water every night now and came up wet to the shoulders and spoke to nobody.

All of it was true. Mirrah knew it was true while Ilvane was saying it. She had known most of it already, in pieces, from the bathhouse and the queue and the potter’s boy, and nobody had said it to her straight, and Ilvane said it straight. It was like having a splinter drawn: it hurt, and then there was the small clean relief of it out.

“So that’s what’s going to happen,” Ilvane said. “If nothing changes.”

“And will it change?”

“I don’t know. Nobody knows that.” Ilvane turned the cloth over in her hands. Below them, on the steps, somebody laughed at something, and the sound came up thin and strange in the dusk. “But there may yet be a way. For some people. Not for everybody.”

Mirrah waited.

“A name can come off a paper,” Ilvane said. “If you know how. It’s old work. Older than the wells. Not many people can do it any more. But a name that’s come off a paper can’t be read in a square, because it isn’t there to be read. Nobody can point at anyone.” She looked at Mirrah. “Nothing is destroyed, little sister. It’s only moved.”

She said it simply, as a priestess says the words of a binding. Little sister. It was what the priestesses called each other. Mirrah had heard Ilvane say it to Hadde a hundred times, across the tables on the Steps, pass me the wax, little sister. She had never heard her say it to anyone who was not a priestess.

“And the price?” Mirrah said.

“Something you’ve already given away,” said Ilvane. “You won’t miss it. You haven’t had her for eleven years.” She stood up, and folded the cloth, and put it over her arm. “That’s all you need to know tonight. Go home. Sleep. If you want to talk about it again, I’m here every evening, and I won’t come looking for you, and I won’t ask.” She touched Mirrah’s shoulder, lightly. “Eat something first. There’s still soup.”

She went back down to the pot. Mirrah watched her take the ladle from the other priestess and begin again: bread on the left, salt in the dish, it’s free.

She ate nothing. She sat on the plinth of the column until the light was gone and the lamps were lit along the top of the Steps, and the line had dwindled, and the thin polite people on the second flight had risen one by one and bowed to Ilvane and gone away into the dark. She was trying to remember the words exactly. Something you’ve already given away. That was true; she had given it all. You won’t miss it. That was true. You haven’t had her for eleven years.

Her. She had said it, and then her.

Mirrah sat with that, and turned it, as she had turned the thought of a list with a name on it in the bathhouse steam, and could make nothing of it, and did not ask. It had been a slip, she thought. Ilvane was tired. Ilvane had been ladling soup for three hours.

She walked home through the Wells Quarter in the dark.

At the second well, somebody was sitting on the kerb. It was the girl from Fig Court: thin, in the brown dress too short in the sleeves, her ragged hair falling over her face. She was dabbling one hand in the water. When Mirrah came past, she took the hand out and got up and fell in beside her, a pace behind, without a word.

Mirrah stopped. The girl stopped.

“Where do you live?” Mirrah said.

The girl looked at her. Her face, under the hair, was a narrow brown face with a pointed chin and a smudge of grit along one cheekbone, and her eyes were dark, and there was nothing in them at all that Mirrah could read. It was not an unfriendly face. It was a face that was waiting for something.

“Are you lost?” said Mirrah.

The girl did not answer. Mirrah walked on. After a moment she heard the light steps behind her again, bare feet on the stones, keeping exactly a pace back, stopping when she stopped, going on when she went on. Past the third well. Past the dyers’ corner, where the smith had dropped his iron. Into Fig Court, under the fig tree.

At the foot of the stair Mirrah turned.

The girl was standing by the well with her wet hand held a little away from her side, so that it dripped on the stones. In the light from somebody’s window her face looked older than Mirrah had thought, or younger; it was hard to tell.

“Goodnight,” Mirrah said.

The girl looked at her for a long moment.

“Night, Sau,” she said.

It was a small voice, rough, as if it had not been used much. Mirrah stood at the foot of the stair with her hand on the rail. Sau. It was not a name. It was a scrap of a name, the end bitten off, the kind of thing a mother calls a child across a yard, or a sister calls a sister. She had never heard it in her life.

Her heart had gone hard and quick in her chest, as it had when the iron rang on the dyers’ step.

“What did you call me?” she said.

But the girl had turned, unhurried, and gone out of the court by the river lane, and her bare feet made no sound on the stones going away. Mirrah stood a long while at the foot of the stair. Then she climbed it, and at the top, as she put her hand to the latch, the little brass bell inside the frame rang once, softly, as Wenna had said it would when somebody who was not her was on the stair.

She turned round. The stair was empty. The court was empty.

The bell did not ring again.

Chapter 12 The towers burn

The drying towers went up on the fourteenth night, all nine of them, one after another, from the downstream end.

Mirrah was on the wall when the first one caught. Half the city was on the wall by then. Since the chain went across and the stones began, people had taken to climbing the wall-walk in the evenings to look out at the Host’s fires on the Reed Fields, and at the orchards where the broken wells had flooded, and at the engines, which in the dusk looked like great birds asleep on one leg. The wardens had tried to keep people off the walk for the first few nights and then given up. Mirrah had not come before. She came that night because she could not stay in her room with the little brass bell inside the door frame, waiting to see whether it would ring.

The towers stood along the bank below the walls, among the drying-yards: tall narrow structures of lath and cane, open-sided, five stories high, where the lotus was hung in bundles to dry after the summer cut. This year’s crop was in them. Nobody had been able to bring it in. When the Host came the drying-yard workers had gone inside the walls with everyone else and left the lotus hanging, and for fourteen days it had hung there drying, out of reach, and on still evenings the smell of it had come up over the wall, green and sweet.

The first fire came over from the Reed Fields in a long high arc, slowly, like the stones, but trailing a tail of sparks. It went down among the towers at the downstream end. For a moment nothing happened. Then the tower nearest it bloomed.

There was no other word. It went up from the bottom like a flower opening, a great slow upward rush of flame, and the bundles of dried lotus caught one after another up the inside of it, story by story, and as they caught the flame changed color. It went from yellow to rose to violet, a deep clear violet like the lotus water at dusk, and the smoke that came off it was violet too, and thick, and rolled up into the night sky in a slow column and leaned toward the city on the wind.

On the wall-walk, nobody spoke.

A second fire came over. A third. They took the second tower and the fourth; the third caught from the second. By the time the fifth went up the downstream end of the bank was a wall of violet flame, and the smoke had reached the wall and was coming over it, low and heavy, rolling down into the streets behind them like water over a weir.

Mirrah breathed it before she could stop.

It was sweet. It was the smell of the tea on the Petal Steps, and of the cups at the gate, and of the river at dusk, all at once and much stronger, so that it went straight to the back of her head and sat there. For a moment she did not know where she was. She was standing on a wall in the dark with violet fire below her and she did not know what the fire was or why she was there or whose the hand was that was gripping her arm. It was her own hand. She was holding her own arm, hard, above the elbow, the way she would have held a child back from a ledge.

Then it passed. She was on the wall at Heugen, and the drying towers were burning, and the smoke was in her eyes.

Beside her a woman was screaming.

It was not loud. It was a thin high continuous sound, like a kettle, and the woman making it was standing quite still with her arms at her sides and her face turned to the small boy at her feet, who was holding her skirt. He was perhaps four. He was looking up at her. She was looking down at him, and screaming, and Mirrah saw in her face that she had no idea who he was.

“Mama,” the boy said.

The woman stopped screaming. She looked at him. She put her hand out, slowly, and touched his hair, as you might touch a strange dog to see if it would bite, and then took the hand back and looked at it.

All along the wall it was the same. Mirrah saw it go down the walk like a wind going through a field: people turning to the people beside them with blank faces, people stepping back from each other, a man holding a baby out at arm’s length and staring at it, an old woman sitting down on the walk and folding her hands and saying, quite calmly, I don’t know this place, I don’t know this place. The children did not forget. That was the strangest thing about it, afterwards, when people tried to talk about it. The children all knew their mothers. They stood at the feet of their mothers and their fathers and held on and said their names, Mama, Mama, Da, and the mothers and fathers looked down at them out of faces that had nothing in them.

It lasted perhaps the time it takes to boil water.

Then the wind turned. The smoke leaned the other way, out over the river, toward the Reed Fields, and the air on the wall-walk cleared, and one by one, all along the walk, people came back into their faces.

The woman beside Mirrah went down on her knees and took the boy in her arms and held him so hard he cried out. She was saying something into his hair. It was his name, over and over. Mirrah could not hear what the name was. She did not try to.

By morning the towers were black bones on the bank, still smoking, and the whole of the year’s lotus was gone.

The Council sat at noon, in the open square inside the second gate, under the violet awning, and the city came to hear it. Mirrah came. She had not slept. Nobody had slept; she could see it on every face in the square, and she could see something else, which was that nobody would look at anybody else for long. People who had stood on the wall and looked at their children without knowing them could not now look at their children without remembering it, and the children knew it, and held on.

The seven masks sat behind the long table. The Speaker stood in front of it. He looked, Mirrah thought, like a man who had been up all night with a sick child; there was nothing to see of his face behind the grey silk, but his shoulders were wrong, and he kept his weight off his bad knee by leaning, very slightly, on the back of the nearest chair.

“The Council has heard three counsels,” he said. “It will hear them again, here, so that you know what they are. Then it will choose.”

The first mask to speak was the old woman who had asked the Host what would happen if they did not answer. She spoke for surrender. She said it plainly: that the city had no army and no allies and no food beyond the season, that the lotus was gone and the river was shut, that the Covenant had promised in writing that no one would be harmed who was not on its schedule, and that the people on its schedule had come to Heugen of their own will and must answer of their own will, as everyone must in the end. She said she was sorry. She said it twice. There was no sound from the square while she spoke, and no sound when she had finished.

The second mask was a man’s, with a soldier’s voice. He spoke for the third counsel, the one Ilvane had told Mirrah of on the Steps.

“They have come for the wells,” he said. “Not for us. For the wells. Every writ on that schedule depends on one thing, which is that what we gave the water can be got back out of it. That is the whole of their claim. Without it they have a list of strangers. So take it away from them.” He laid his hands on the table. “The wells are fed from below. Everyone in this city knows it, though nobody says it. There are cisterns under the Gate Quarter as old as the city, and older. Break them. Let the river into them. Let what’s in the water go into the river and down to the sea, where nobody can read it, nobody can fetch it up, nobody can ever stand in a square and point. Then let them come in. Let them read their list. It’ll be a list of people who don’t exist.”

There was a sound from the square then, a low rough sound like a crowd of people breathing out at once. Mirrah could not tell whether it was agreement. She did not think it was. She thought it was people hearing, for the first time aloud, a thing they had been told all their lives in this city was not true: that the water kept what it was given.

She looked round, without meaning to, for the Keeper. The Keeper was not there.

Ilvane was.

She was standing at the edge of the crowd near the mouth of the lane that led up to the Petal Steps, with her hands folded in front of her, in her plain priestess’s grey, listening. She was not looking at the soldier-mask. She was looking at the people in the crowd, one face after another, as she had looked at the faces in the soup line, kindly and attentively, as if she were counting them. When the soldier-mask finished speaking she nodded. It was the smallest nod, the nod of a teacher whose pupil has said the lesson right.

Then the Speaker spoke for the second counsel, and Mirrah stopped watching Ilvane.

“The second counsel,” he said, “is that we give them neither our people nor our wells. That we open the gate to their court, but not to their army. That anyone named on their schedule who wishes to answer may go out and answer, freely, and anyone who does not wish to may stay, and that whatever any person gave to the water stays in the water until that same person asks for it back, and not before.” He paused. “It is the hardest counsel and I don’t know if they’ll take it. I don’t know if they’ve ever been offered it. I only know that it’s the only one of the three that keeps our law.”

“Which law?” somebody called from the crowd. It was a man’s voice. It was hoarse.

“The only one we’ve got,” said the Speaker. “That no one here has ever been made to do anything with their own past but what they asked.”

The square was quiet. Then, from somewhere in the middle of it, somebody began to laugh.

It was the old man from the soup line, the one with the beautiful smile, who had asked Mirrah for a name. He was sitting on the edge of the dry fountain in the middle of the square, in his good clothes that were too big for him, and he was laughing, quietly and helplessly, with his hands over his face. Nobody near him moved. When he had finished laughing he took his hands down and folded them in his lap and looked up at the Speaker with his sweet, tired face, perfectly composed.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I’m sorry. Go on.”

For a moment the Speaker said nothing more. Then he said, to the masks behind him, “The Council will choose tonight,” and the seven masks rose together and went in through the door behind the table, and the Speaker followed them, leaning on the chair-backs as he went.

Mirrah walked home through the streets with the smell of burnt lotus in everything, in the plaster and the masks on their cords and the water of the wells.

On the corner of the dyers’ lane the smith was sitting on his step with his hammer across his knees, not working. He nodded to her as she went by. “Sister,” he said. She nodded back. Two lanes further on, a woman was standing at an open door holding a small girl by the hand and talking to her neighbor across the lane, and as Mirrah passed she heard the woman say, I knew him, though. I knew his face. I just didn’t know what he was for, and the neighbor said, I know, I know, love, and the small girl looked up at Mirrah as she went by.

It was not her girl. Her girl was sitting on the kerb of the well in Fig Court, under the fig tree, dabbling her hand in the water.

Mirrah stopped at the edge of the court. The girl looked up. They looked at each other across the court, as they had the first evening, with the fig tree between them and the light going.

“Who are you?” Mirrah said.

The girl took her hand out of the water and looked at it, at the wet fingers, and then at Mirrah.

“Sau,” she said, as if it were an answer. “You forgot to eat again.”

Then she got up, and went out by the river lane, and Mirrah stood in Fig Court in the smell of the burnt towers with her heart going hard and quick, and realised that it was true, she had not eaten all day, and that nobody had told her to eat in eleven years.

Chapter 13 Whether it should be

The wind turned at the fourth bell of the night, and the smoke came over the Reed Fields.

Sehra was awake. She had been awake since the first fire went over, standing on the bank below the engines with half the camp, watching the towers go up one after another along the river under the black wall, the flames going yellow and rose and then that deep impossible violet, the smoke leaning away toward the city. She had heard the Seal-Bearer arguing with the Prelate in his tent before the first fire was loaded, the old woman’s voice not rising and not stopping, and then the flap of the tent thrown back and the Seal-Bearer coming out and walking away toward her own lines without looking at anyone. The Writ said the wells. It did not say the lotus. The Prelate had said that the lotus was the draught, and the draught was the well, and a well was not sealed while its draught hung drying on the bank. He had signed the order himself. Cruciant had loaded the engines himself. Nobody had said anything.

Then the wind turned, and the smoke came back across the river toward them, low and heavy and sweet.

She breathed it.

Afterwards she could not say how long it lasted. She knew that she was standing on a riverbank in the dark, among strangers, holding a leather case on a red strap, and that she did not know what the case was. She opened it. There were papers in it, rolled and tied. She took one out and looked at it, and there was writing on it in a small clear hand, and she could read the writing, every word, and none of it meant anything. By the Covenant of Mykkiel. She did not know what a covenant was, or who Mykkiel was. She did not know who had written this in such a careful hand, or why, or why she was holding it in the middle of the night on a riverbank with violet fire across the water and men around her weeping.

She stood there with the paper in her hands. And what she felt, she remembered afterwards with great clarity, was not fear. It was lightness. It was like setting down a weight she had carried so long she had forgotten it was a weight, and straightening, and finding that her back did not hurt.

Then the wind turned again. The smoke went out over the water. She was standing on the bank below the engines with the Writ of Abrogation in her hands, and she was Sehra, notary of Galanina, and she remembered everything, and her back hurt.

All around her people were coming back into their faces. A soldier sat down hard on the ground and put his head between his knees. Brother Kael was walking among the penitents with his jar of salve and his rags, touching each of them on the shoulder as he passed and saying their names. Davo was standing a little way off with his hands over his mouth.

She rolled the Writ and tied it and put it back in the case, and her hands were steady, and she did not understand why that frightened her more than anything else that night.

The Council’s answer came at noon the next day, by a warden in a sash who walked out of the small door in the river gate alone, with a white rag on a stick, and came up the road to the Reed Fields, and asked for the Notary.

Sehra received it. That was the law: the passing from hand to hand. The warden was a young woman with short hair and a pear in her other hand, which she seemed to have forgotten she was holding. She gave Sehra the letter, sealed in violet wax with a lotus, and stood waiting while Sehra broke the seal and saw that it was whole, and made her mark on the receipt the warden held out: Received of the Lotus Council of Heugen, by the hand of— and looked up.

“Aun,” said the warden.

Sehra wrote it. The warden looked at the receipt with an odd expression, as if she had not expected to see her name written down and was not sure what she thought about it. Then she put it in her sash, and took a bite of her pear, and walked back down the road to the gate.

The Prelate read the letter in the open tent, with the Seal-Bearer and Cruciant and Sehra standing by. He read it twice. Then he gave it to Sehra and said, “Read it out, please, Notary. So that it’s on the record that it was read.”

She read it out.

It was short. The Council of Heugen did not accept the Writ of Abrogation. It would not vacate the city, nor seal its wells, nor surrender its records, nor deliver any person to the Host. But it would open the river gate, by day, to the courts of the Covenant, and to their judges and notaries, unarmed, so that any person named in the schedule who wished to answer before them might do so, in the city, freely, and without being taken from it. And any person who did not wish to answer would not be made to. And whatever any person had given to the water of Heugen would remain in it, until that person, and no other, asked for it back.

Given under the lotus, it ended, by the Council, in the voice of the Speaker-of-Petals.

There was silence in the tent.

“It’s a hearing,” Sehra said. She had not meant to say it aloud.

The Prelate looked at her.

“It’s an offer of a hearing,” she said. “A real one. With courts, and judges. In the city.”

“It is an offer,” said Radan, “of a court that has no power to bring the accused before it.” He said it quite gently. “Notary, you know as well as I do what a summons is worth when the person summoned may decline it. Six hundred and eleven people on that schedule. How many do you think will walk through the gate and stand before a judge and say: yes, that was me, I did that, take me back to Hollin’s Ford? Five? Ten? And the rest will stay behind their walls and drink, and the people at the other end of those writs will go on waiting, as they’ve waited all along, and the Covenant will have made a treaty with the thing it came here to end.” He took the letter back from her, and folded it, and laid it on the table. “I’m sorry. I mean that. It’s a clever answer and I think it was meant in good faith. But the Covenant does not offer its courts to a city on condition that the city decides who comes to them.”

“The founding judgment—” Sehra began.

“The founding judgment,” said Radan, “was given against a respondent who was in the court. Daramet’s field was returned to her because the House of Ourel was made to stand before the Arbiter and answer. If the House of Ourel had been allowed to stay home and drink, Daramet would have died on the road.” He looked at the Seal-Bearer. “Would you say otherwise?”

The Seal-Bearer looked at him for a long moment with her face like a closed book.

“No,” she said. “I would not say otherwise.” She paused. “But I would say that you burned their lotus last night without a writ, and the Arbiter’s hand was not open when you did it.”

Radan inclined his head, as if she had made a fair point at chess.

“Answer them,” he said to Sehra. “The Host declines the Council’s offer. The Writ stands. Thirty days from the reading, less fourteen. Sixteen days.” He stood up. “Marshal. A word.”

Cruciant found her that night at the engines.

She had gone there because she wanted to be away from the record-cart, where people came to find her, and because the engines at night were the loneliest place in the camp. The crews slept beside them under the frames, wrapped in blankets, and the great arms stood up against the stars, and there was nobody awake but the sentries, who knew her by now and let her pass. She sat on the axle of the nearest one with the writ-case on her knees and the Council’s letter inside it, folded, beside her own answer, which she had written out fair that afternoon and sent back by a sergeant with a white rag on a stick.

Cruciant sat down beside her on the axle. He had a flask. He drank from it and offered it to her, and she shook her head, and he drank again.

“Sixteen days,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I could do it in ten.” He said it flatly, as he said everything. “The wall’s thick, but it’s old, and they’ve let the drains silt up along the river side. There’s a place below the gate where I can bring the wall down into the water in three days with all four engines. After that it’s streets. Narrow streets. I’ll lose men in them. But they’ve got no soldiers, only wardens, and a few old men who were soldiers once somewhere else. By the end of the season I’ll have the square and the gate and the Council chamber, and the Prelate will have his reading.” He drank again. “That’s what I told him tonight. He was pleased.”

Sehra said nothing.

“I wasn’t in the smoke,” Cruciant said. “Last night. When it came over. I was up on this frame here, checking the skein. It came over me up there and I didn’t know where I was. I was sitting on a beam of wood in the dark with my hands on a rope as thick as my leg, and I didn’t know what it was for.” He turned the flask in his hands. “Do you know what I thought? I thought: what a fine piece of work. Somebody built this. Somebody thought it out, the frame, the arm, the twist in the rope. It’s beautiful. I sat there and I admired it like a boy looking at a bridge.” He was quiet a moment. “Then it came back. What it was for. What I was for. And I’ve been building things like it for twenty-two years and I’ve never once, not once, looked at one without knowing what it was for.”

“Marshal—”

“I’m going to ask you something,” he said. “Not as the Marshal. You can tell me it isn’t my business.”

“Ask.”

“I can win this,” Cruciant said. “I’ve just told you how. Ten days. I’ll take the city and the Prelate will read his list in the square, and some of the people on it will go home to the courts that named them, and some will go in the river like that boy, and the wells will be broken and the city will be empty in a year. I can do that. I know how.” He looked at her in the dark. “You know the law. You’ve read every page of every writ in that case. You read that woman her deed in their square and you watched her sit down on the stones. You read that soldier his and he went in the river.” He paused. “Should it be won?”

She had known he was going to ask it. She had known since he sat down. And still, when he asked it, she found that she had no answer, none, not in the founding judgment or the General Order or the Writ or the long red-corded schedule in her case, not in anything she had ever copied in her careful hand since she was nineteen years old. She sat on the axle of the engine with her hands flat on the leather of the case and looked for an answer, and there was nothing there but the memory of standing on the bank in the violet smoke with a paper in her hand that meant nothing, and how light it had been.

“I don’t know,” she said.

Cruciant nodded slowly, as he had nodded on the gate road when she told him the Speaker had stopped.

“No,” he said. “Nor do I.” He stood up. “Ten days. Unless somebody gives me a reason. Goodnight, Notary.”

He went away along the line of the engines, stepping over the sleeping crews.

She sat on the axle a while longer. Then she went back to the record-cart, and lit the lamp, and took a clean sheet of vellum from the case, and wrote a letter.

It was to the clerk of the circuit court at Ostrin on the Paladian, a town she had never seen, four days upriver. She wrote it in her best hand, and in the form of a notary’s request between courts, which a clerk could not refuse without cause. She asked for the record of a plaint brought before that court eleven springs ago by an apprentice glass-cutter named Isaut Vell against her master, the glazier Ansel Garrow, and refused for want of standing. She asked for whatever had been written down of it: the plaint itself, if it had been kept; the clerk’s note of its substance, if it had not; the name of the judge who had refused it, and the grounds.

She sealed it. In the morning she gave it to a rider going upriver with the Host’s dispatches, and paid him a mark of her own money to carry it the rest of the way to Ostrin and wait for an answer.

She did not tell the Prelate. She did not tell Cruciant. She told herself, on the walk back from the horse-lines in the grey light, that it was only a notary’s diligence, that a record ought to be complete, that the line in a different ink at the bottom of the entry had been bothering her since the third night out of Galanina and this was the proper way to settle it.

Then she went back to the record-cart and copied the request into her own book, with the date, in case anybody ever asked.

Part Three The Wells Below

Chapter 14 The riverfront

The wall came down into the river at the end of the third day, a little after the sixth bell, with a sound like a sigh.

Sehra had been on the bank since dawn. They all had: the engineers and the crews and half the vanguard, and the Prelate in his grey with his hands folded, and the Seal-Bearer in her black, standing a little apart from him. For three days the four engines had thrown their stones at one place, a stretch of the river wall below the gate where Cruciant said the old drains had silted, and for three days the wall had taken them. Each stone struck with the same flat crump and threw up a burst of dust and chips, and the dust settled, and the wall stood. The crews had stopped cheering on the first morning. By the third they worked in silence, winding and loading and loosing, and nobody looked at the wall between stones, because there was never anything to see.

Then, late on the third day, one of the stones went in.

Not through. In. It struck the wall low, near the waterline, and instead of the burst of dust there was a soft wet sound and the stone simply disappeared, as if the wall had opened its mouth and taken it. For a moment nothing else happened. Then a crack ran up the face of the wall from the place where the stone had gone, slowly, branching as it went, like frost across a pane. Water came out of the crack. Brown at first, and then not brown: clear, and a great deal of it, more than any drain could have held, coming out of the face of the wall in a sheet. And the whole stretch of the wall above the crack leaned outward over the river, slowly, and slid down into the water with that long soft sigh, and the river took it.

Where it had been there was a gap the width of a street, and a slope of rubble going down into the Paladian, and beyond the gap the backs of warehouses, and narrow lanes, and people.

“Boats,” said Cruciant.

They went in at dawn.

Sehra watched from the bank with her case on her shoulder, because the Prelate had told her to witness it, and because the law said a city was not taken until a notary had seen it taken. The boats went out from the Reed Fields in the grey light, forty of them, flat river-boats full of the vanguard, low in the water, poled across to the slope of rubble below the gap. She could see the soldiers jump out into the shallows and go up the rubble on their hands and feet. She could see the white flag of the Covenant being carried up among them, and the halo banners, and Davo with the flag, because he had asked to carry it again and the sergeant had let him.

They went in through the gap and were gone into the lanes.

For a long while there was only noise. It came back across the water thin and confused: shouting, and a bell ringing somewhere inside the city, and something that might have been the crash of barrels, and once a sound she did not want to name. Cruciant stood beside her on the bank with his slate, and wrote nothing on it. The Prelate stood a little behind them both. The Seal-Bearer had gone back to her tent.

The first boats came back before the third bell.

They came back fuller than they had gone. The soldiers in them were wet, many of them to the chest, and some of them were hurt, and some of them were carrying others. They came ashore below the camp and sat down on the bank in rows, and the surgeons went among them, and Brother Kael with his jar and his rags, and nobody shouted orders. Sehra went down among them because she could not stay on the bank. She did not know what she meant to do. She had no salve and no rags. She had a case of writs.

Davo was sitting at the end of a row with the white flag across his knees.

He was soaked to the neck. His hands were bleeding, both palms, scraped raw. He was looking at the flag. When she knelt in front of him he looked up and it took him a moment to know her.

“Notary,” he said.

“Are you hurt?”

“My hands.” He looked at them. “I held on to the pole. When we went down.”

“Went down?”

He told her. He told it as people tell a dream, carefully, in the order it happened, as though if he got the order wrong it would stop making sense. They had come up through the gap into a street of warehouses. There had been barricades, barrels and carts and doors laid flat, and behind the barricades there had been wardens, and old men, a few dozen of them, with poles and boat-hooks and two or three swords. Not soldiers. Old men. One of them had a mask pushed up on top of his head, a fox, and fought with a mallet. They had pushed the barricade back down the street. They had pushed it back two streets, three, and the old men had given ground and given ground, and the vanguard had come into a little square with a well in it, and the ground had opened.

“Just opened,” the boy said. “The whole square. It went down under us like a floor going through. We fell into water. Deep water. Cold.” He was shaking. “There were steps. Under the water, going down. And arches. And walls with carvings on them. And lamps, Notary, there were lamps down there, old ones, on brackets, but they weren’t lit, it was all dark except where the light came down from the hole. And it went on. It went on under the whole city. I swam and I swam and it went on.” He looked at the flag. “I held on to the pole. That’s how they found me. The flag was sticking up out of the hole and they pulled it.”

“You’re safe now.”

“There’s another city,” the boy said. “Under that one. With steps and streets and everything. All full of water.” He looked at her. “Who built it?”

She did not know. She put her hand on his wet shoulder for a moment, and then the surgeon came, and she stood up and went back along the row.

Cruciant called them back at noon.

He did it without a horn. He sent a boat across with a sergeant in it, and the sergeant went up through the gap and was gone for a while, and then they began to come back, boat after boat, slowly, the wounded first, until the last of them was on the bank and the gap in the wall stood empty with the river lapping at the rubble below it. Inside the city the bell had stopped ringing.

“Eleven drowned,” he said that evening, in the Prelate’s tent, reading from his slate. “Thirty-four hurt. The wardens took some of ours prisoner and let them go again an hour after, which I didn’t expect. The riverfront’s ours as far as the second lane. Past that the ground’s no good.” He set the slate down. “The whole quarter’s hollow underneath. Cisterns. Galleries. I don’t know what. The square went in under forty men. I’m not sending another forty in on top of it until I know what’s down there.”

“The city is built on its wells,” said Radan.

“The city’s built on a hole,” said Cruciant. “Full of water. I’ve seen siege mines go in under walls. I’ve never seen a whole quarter standing on nothing.”

The Prelate was quiet for a while, with his clean hands folded on the table.

“Then we’ll go underneath,” he said. “The Writ says the wells are to be sealed. If they’re fed from below, they’ll be sealed from below. The boy said there were steps going down. Find where they come out.” He turned to Sehra. “And Notary, you’ll go with them. I want it witnessed.”

She looked at him.

“Under the city?”

“Wherever the wells are fed from,” said Radan. “Seal-Bearer, you’ll give her a writ for it. A Writ of Entry. Lawful, in every particular.”

The Seal-Bearer looked at him over the table for a long moment with her face like a closed book.

“I’ll give her a writ,” she said at last. “And I’ll go with her.”

“There’s no need—”

“There is every need,” said the Seal-Bearer. “You burned their lotus without a writ, Prelate. You will not send a notary down into a dead city in the dark without a judge beside her to say what she may and may not seal.” She stood. “Find your steps, Marshal. Tell me when.”

Sehra did not sleep that night either.

She sat on the tailboard of the record-cart with the lamp, and the writ-case open beside her, and the Seal-Bearer’s Writ of Entry drying on the boards, and she thought about the boy in the water under the city, swimming and swimming in the dark while the lamps hung unlit on their brackets. A city under the city. She thought about the columns of water from the broken wells, and the shapes in them, and the pale things drifting in the shaft under the orchard, marked like writing. She thought about the Council’s letter: whatever any person has given to the water of Heugen will remain in it.

Remain. Not is gone. Not is destroyed. She had read that letter aloud in the Prelate’s tent and heard the word and let it pass, because she had been listening for the offer of a hearing and had heard that instead.

She took out the letter and read it again by the lamp, and there it was. Will remain in it, until that person, and no other, asks for it back.

Somebody in that city had known all along. Somebody had written it down, in a letter to the Covenant, under the lotus seal, in the voice of the Speaker-of-Petals. Somebody had told them, in so many words, that the thing they had marched three weeks down the river to restore had never been lost, and they had read it and not heard it.

She sat with the letter in her hands until the lamp burned down.

Then she took the scrap of paper with the eleven names out from under the strap, and unfolded it, and looked at it a while, and folded it again and put it inside the Writ of Entry, so that it would go down into the dark with her.

Chapter 15 The descent

The bell on the river gate rang for a whole morning and then stopped, and in the quiet afterwards Mirrah could hear the water.

She was on the water steps at the bottom of the Mask Market when it stopped, with the potter’s boy and Brannoc and half the alley, because there was nowhere else to be. The fighting had come up from the riverfront at dawn, three streets away, and nobody in the market had known what to do except stand on the steps and listen to it: the shouting, and the crash of barrels, and the bell going and going. Some of the old men had gone. Old Pell had gone first, with his fox mask pushed up on his head and a mallet from his own toolbox in his hand, walking down the alley toward the noise as if he were going to mend something. Mirrah had watched him go. She had thought about going with him and found that her feet would not.

Then, at the second bell, there had been a sound she had never heard before, a great soft heavy sound like a cart-load of wet sand being tipped, and after it the shouting had changed. It had become screaming, and then calling, names called over and over, and then it had gone further off, and at last there was only the bell.

Now the bell had stopped, and there was the water.

It was everywhere. She could hear it under the steps, under the stones of the alley, under her feet: a deep slow moving sound, like a river in a cellar, that she had never heard in eleven years and that she understood, now that she heard it, had always been there. The well at the top of the alley was overflowing. Water came up over its kerb in a smooth clear sheet and ran down between the stalls and over the lip of the water steps into the river, and it was not river water. It was clear as glass.

“The Pellen Square’s gone,” said someone coming up the steps. A warden, wet to the knees. “The whole square. Gone down. There’s a hole there you could put a temple in.”

“Who’s in it?”

“Theirs. Forty of theirs. And Pell.”

Mirrah turned and ran.

She did not know where she was running until she was there.

The basin inside the river gate was full to the lip. The water stood level with the kerb and trembled, and there was nobody at it, no queue, no wardens with trays. There was only a crowd at the foot of the steps up to the green door, perhaps twenty men, with sledges and crowbars and coils of rope over their shoulders, and in front of them a broad heavy man with no mask on, whom she had last seen behind a mask at the Council table speaking for the third counsel. He had a soldier’s face, grey and square, and he was holding a key-ring the size of a horse’s bridle, and he was shouting up the steps.

At the top of the steps, in front of the green door, the Aster Keeper stood with her arms folded.

“—no use,” the soldier was shouting. “The square’s gone in. The city’s falling into its own water. They’ll be in the Gate Quarter by tomorrow. Let us finish it.”

“No.”

“There’s nobody else to ask. The Council’s sitting with its doors shut and the Speaker’s at the hole in the Pellen Square pulling bodies out of the water with his own hands. It’s us or them. The key, Keeper.”

“No.”

“Then we’ll go in by the square,” the soldier said. “We’ll go down the hole and find the sluices from below, and it’ll take us all night and some of my men will drown doing it, and that’ll be on you.” He held up the key-ring. “Or you give me the one key on this that I haven’t got, and we do it in an hour, and nobody drowns but the water.”

The Keeper did not move. She stood with her arms folded and looked down at him with her pale steady eyes. Then she looked past him, at the crowd, and over the crowd, and found Mirrah at the edge of the basin.

“Mirrah,” she said. “Come here.”

The men turned. Mirrah went up the steps through them. Nobody stopped her. At the top the Keeper took her by the arm, her big cool hand closing just above the elbow, the way Mirrah had held her own arm on the wall in the violet smoke, and drew her in through the green door, and shut it, and shot the bolt.

On the other side the soldier began to hammer.

“Will it hold?” said Mirrah.

“Long enough.” The Keeper was already moving, through the stone-floored room, past the table with the rope on it, to the door at the back that went down. “Bring that lamp. And the other one. Have you eaten?”

“What?”

“Have you eaten anything today?”

“No.”

The Keeper stopped at the head of the water stair, picked up a heel of bread from a shelf by the door, and put it in Mirrah’s hand.

“Eat it on the way,” she said. “You’ll want it.”

The water stair was wet to the third step.

It had never been wet before. Mirrah had walked down it with the Keeper on her first visit and the steps had been dry to the landing, and now the river had come up the inside of the wall and stood black and still over the bottom of the flight, and the boat at the landing was afloat at the level of the winch, and the great wheel with its buckets stood in water to the hub. The Keeper waded down into it to her knees without looking. Mirrah followed. The water was cold, but not so cold as it had been in the basin. It had the river in it.

At the far side of the landing, the low arch with the iron gate stood with the water halfway up the bars.

The Keeper took a key from inside her apron. It was on a cord round her neck, a long iron key, plain, older than the lock. She waded to the gate and found the lock under the water by touch and fitted the key to it and turned it, and it turned with a sound that came up through the water like a bell struck under a pillow, and the gate swung inward.

“In,” she said.

“Where are we going?”

“Down.” The Keeper held out a hand for one of the lamps. “They want to open the sluices and let the river through. Do you understand what that means?”

“It’ll empty the cisterns.”

“It’ll wash them out. All of them. Into the river and down to the sea. Everything that’s ever been given to this water, by anyone, for as long as there’s been a city here.” The Keeper’s face, in the lamplight, was perfectly calm. “Every one of those people asked to be rid of it. Not one of them asked for it to be thrown in the sea. It’s theirs. It’s kept for them. That’s the only promise this place has ever made, and the Covenant says it’s a lie, and now our own Council wants to make it one.” She lifted the lamp. “The sluices are below the Sacrarium. I can close them from there and they won’t be able to open them from anywhere else. If I get there first.”

“Why me?”

The Keeper looked at her.

“Because you’re the only person who’s come to my door in eleven years to ask about what she gave,” she said, “and been refused, and come back anyway. And because you’ve got good hands, and there’s a gate down there I can’t open by myself any more.” She turned. “And because some of what’s down there is yours. If they’re going to drown it, you’ve a right to see it first.”

She went in under the arch. Mirrah ate the heel of bread in three bites, and followed.

On the other side of the gate there was a stair.

It went down through the rock in a long straight flight, wide enough for three people abreast, with a channel cut down the middle of it for water, and the water was running in the channel, not fast, clear and quiet, going down. The walls were dressed stone. Every twenty steps there was a bracket on the wall with an old lamp in it, bronze, green with age, and the Keeper stopped at each one and lit it from her own, and the lamps caught, one after another, and threw their small yellow light back up the stair behind them so that when Mirrah looked back she could see the whole flight going up into the dark like a road.

At the bottom there was a gallery.

It was the size of the Council square. Bigger. The roof was so high that the lamps did not reach it, and it was held up by pillars, rows of them, going away into the dark, and they were not stone. They were roots. Great pale roots as thick as the pillars of the temple portico, ivory-colored, smooth, coming down out of the dark overhead and going into the floor, and between them, arching from one to the next, other roots, thinner, like ribs. The floor of the gallery was water. Still water, black, clear when the lamp was held over it, with stone paths running across it on low arches from pillar to pillar.

“Lotus,” the Keeper said.

“What?”

“The roots. They’re lotus.” She was looking up into the dark. “The first lotus in Heugen was planted in the first well. That’s what they say. It grew down. It’s been growing down ever since.”

Something was falling from the dark above them. Mirrah saw it in the lamplight: a pale flake, drifting, turning slowly as it came down, like snow on a windless night. It settled on the water and floated there. Another came. Another. They were coming down all through the gallery, in ones and twos, from somewhere high up among the roots, and settling on the black water, and floating.

She knelt on the edge of the stone path and looked at the nearest one.

It was not a petal. It was the shape of a petal, and the color, but it was thin and stiff and faintly translucent, like the flake that comes off glass when you strike it wrong, and there was something on it. Marks. She held the lamp closer. The marks were small and fine, and they were not writing, not quite; they were the shape writing would have if you could see it from a great way off, or from underneath.

“Don’t touch it,” said the Keeper.

“What is it?”

“Not yours.” The Keeper put her hand on Mirrah’s shoulder. “That one came down from the Pellen Square. They’re all coming down from there, since it broke. The water’s carrying them. Come on.”

Mirrah stood up.

On the far side of the gallery, where the stone path went in under another arch, somebody was standing on the steps.

It was the girl. She was standing quite still at the head of the next flight down, in her brown dress too short in the sleeves, with her ragged hair over her face, and she was dry. The lamps did not reach her. Mirrah could see her anyway, a small pale shape against the dark of the arch, as clearly as if she were carrying a light of her own.

The Keeper had seen her too. She had stopped. Her hand was still on Mirrah’s shoulder, and Mirrah felt it tighten.

“Who’s that?”

“She follows me,” said Mirrah. “She’s followed me for days.”

The Keeper said nothing for a long moment. The pale flakes drifted down between them and the girl and settled on the water. The girl stood on the step and waited, as she had waited by the well in Fig Court, with her wet hand held a little away from her side.

“That’s not a child,” the Keeper said quietly.

“Then what is she?”

The Keeper took her hand off Mirrah’s shoulder. She lifted the lamp and looked across the black water at the girl on the step, and Mirrah saw on her face, for the first time since the green door had opened eleven years ago, something that was not calm.

“I don’t know,” the Keeper said. “But she knows the way down. Look. She’s waiting for us.”

Chapter 16 The kneeling knight

The girl went down without a light, and the lamps on the walls lit themselves ahead of her.

That was how it seemed. Mirrah knew it could not be so. She knew the Keeper was lighting them, one after another, as they came to the brackets, as she had lit them on the first stair. But the girl was always a flight ahead, and every time Mirrah raised her eyes from the steps the next lamp below was already burning and the girl was standing under it, small and dry and patient, looking back, and then the girl turned and went on down and the light seemed to go with her.

They went down a long way.

The stairs were not all alike. Some of them were dressed stone, square and even, like the first flight from the iron gate. Some were older, worn into dips in the middle like the Petal Steps, and some were older than that, hardly steps at all, only ledges in the rock with a groove worn along them where the water ran. The galleries between the stairs grew smaller as they went down. The great one with the lotus-root pillars gave way to lower rooms, and then to passages, with the roots coming through the walls and the roof like the knuckles of a buried hand, and everywhere the water: in the channels down the middle of the steps, in pools on the landings, in thin bright threads coming out of cracks in the walls and running down to join the rest. It was all going down. All of it, the same way they were going.

From far above, every so often, there came a sound like a door being struck.

“They’re through the green door,” the Keeper said, on a landing, when the sound had come three times. She did not stop. “They’ll be on the water stair. They’ve got the key-ring; it won’t be long before one of them finds a way round the iron gate. There’s a culvert from the old customs house.” She was breathing hard now. She was a big woman and the stairs were steep and she had been down in the water every night for a week. “They don’t know the way, though. We do.”

“She does,” said Mirrah.

The Keeper looked down the next flight at the girl, who was waiting under a lamp.

“Yes,” she said. “She does.”

At the bottom of the oldest stair there was a room with a carving in it, and the girl had stopped.

It was a small room, square, with the water standing a hand deep over the whole of its floor and a low arch going on out of the far side of it into the dark. Three of its walls were bare rock. The fourth was dressed smooth, a single great slab, and on the slab, from floor to roof, somebody had cut a picture.

The Keeper lit the lamp on the bracket by the door and held her own up beside it, and the picture came out of the rock.

A man was kneeling. He was kneeling on broken stones, among broken walls, a heap of rubble that went up on either side of him to the edges of the slab, and he was a knight; Mirrah could see the mail on his arms and the long surcoat over it, with some device on the shoulder worn almost away: a figure, a broken curve, something coming down through it. His sword lay on the stones in front of him with its hilt toward him. His hands were over his face.

Above him, at the top of the slab, there was a figure.

It was not clearly a man or a woman. It was robed, and the robes fell away from it in long folds that the cutter had made seem to move, as though there were a wind in them, and it had no face that Mirrah could see, only the shape of a head bent forward and down. It was coming down out of the top of the picture. Its arms were a little out from its sides, the hands open, the way a dancer holds her arms coming down from a leap. And one foot was reaching down, the toes pointed, past the knight’s bowed head, past his shoulder, to the stones beside him, and the very tip of it was just touching the ground.

Where it touched, the cutter had made a little hollow in the rock. A small round hollow, smooth, no bigger than the bowl of a cup. There was water in it.

“That’s the founding,” said the Keeper.

Mirrah had seen it before. Not this; the shape of it. It was on the gate. It was painted on the tiles in the bathhouse on Copper Lane and pressed into the bottom of the clay cups at the basin and the wells, the toe coming down to touch the ground and the little ring of water where it touched. Every child in Heugen could draw it. She had not known there was a first one.

“There was a city here before,” the Keeper said. “In the crusades. Not ours. Somebody’s. The crusaders took it, and emptied it, and went on, and were beaten, and came back through it running, and one of them stopped.” She was looking at the knight. “He’d seen things, they say, and done things, that he couldn’t carry. And he knelt down in what was left and asked to be rid of it. And the angel came down and touched the ground with one toe, and there was water, and he drank, and he got up and went out into the world and told people there was a place where you could set it down. And they came.” She lowered the lamp a little. “That’s what we tell the children. That’s what the gate says. That’s all there is.”

The girl had gone to stand in front of the carving. She was standing in the water up to her ankles, with her back to them, looking up at the kneeling knight.

“Who were they?” Mirrah said.

“Who?”

“The people in the city before.”

“There’s no story about them,” the Keeper said. “There’s him, and the angel, and the water. Nobody’s ever said who was here before.”

Mirrah took the lamp from her.

She did not think about it. Her hands did it. She took the lamp from the Keeper’s hand and crouched down in the water in front of the slab, and held the lamp low, close to the rock, almost touching it, the way she held a lamp to a sheet of glass at the stall when she wanted to see a scratch on it too fine for straight light: so that the light came across the surface from the side and every mark threw a shadow.

The rubble round the kneeling knight was not rubble.

She had thought it was. In the straight light from the bracket it looked like a heap of broken stones going up to the edges of the slab on either side, cut roughly, as a cutter cuts stones to fill a space. In the low light it was something else. There were lines in it. Faint lines, so faint and fine that they were hardly deeper than the grain of the rock, lines that the lamp on the bracket washed out completely and the lamp at the level of the water brought up like frost on a window.

Walls. Doorways. Streets. A market, with awnings. A well, with a woman bending over it with a jar. Children in a lane. A man leading a goat. Roofs, and on the roofs people, sitting, as people sat on roofs in the evening, and on one roof a pair of figures with their heads together, and in one doorway a very small figure standing with its hand on the doorpost, looking out. Hundreds of them. More than hundreds. The whole of the rubble on both sides of the knight, from the floor of the slab to the place where the angel’s robes began, was full of them, a city, cut so lightly that you could only see it if you put your face to the water and held the light along the stone, and every one of them so small that a fingertip would have covered three.

None of the doorposts had a lotus.

Mirrah stayed crouched in the water with the lamp. She heard the Keeper come and crouch beside her, heavily, and heard her breath stop.

“I’ve never seen that,” the Keeper said.

“It’s very faint.”

“I’ve stood in this room a hundred times.” The Keeper’s voice was low. “My mother brought me here when I was nine. She held the lamp up there, on the bracket, and told me the story, and I never—” She stopped. “Nobody ever held it low.”

“Somebody cut them,” said Mirrah. “On purpose. Light enough that nobody would see.” She moved the lamp along the stone, slowly. The tiny city came up out of the rock under it and sank back as it passed. “Somebody who remembered.”

“Or somebody who wanted them kept,” said the Keeper, “and couldn’t bear them looked at.”

Mirrah thought about that. She thought about the people at the wells in the evening, sitting on the kerbs with their hands in their laps. She thought about her own charm on its nail by her door, with the crack running from the tail of the r.

The girl had moved. She was standing beside them in the water now, quite close, looking down at the lamp and the faint lines it brought out of the stone. She put out one wet hand. She laid it flat on the rock, low down, near the floor, on a place where Mirrah could see in the light a lane, and a doorway, and the very small figure standing in the doorway with its hand on the post.

Then she took her hand away and looked at Mirrah, and there was something in her narrow brown face that had not been there before. Not a smile. Something like a smile, in the way that the faint city was something like rubble.

“Sau,” she said. “Come on. It’s late.”

She turned and went out through the low arch on the far side of the room, into the dark.

The Keeper got up out of the water. She took her lamp back from Mirrah without a word and stood for a moment in front of the carving, looking at the knight with his hands over his face and the angel coming down above him and the small round hollow full of water where the toe touched the ground. Then she lifted the lamp from the bracket by the door, and blew it out, and set it back, so that the room was dark behind them except for the lamp in her own hand.

“Let it be,” she said, to nobody, or to the slab. “Whoever you were.”

They went out through the arch after the girl. Far above them, faint now, somebody was hammering on iron.

Chapter 17 The Vault of Unwritten Sins

The masks hung in a spiral, and the spiral went down into the dark further than the lamp could follow.

The room beyond the low arch was round, and tall, and the floor of it was not a floor but a ramp, a long shallow ramp of stone that went round and round the inside of the wall, down, like the thread of a screw. The middle of the room was empty. Not empty: it was full of air, a great round well of dark air that went down from the level of the arch to somewhere the lamp could not reach, and across that air, from the outer wall to a pillar of lotus root that rose up out of the depths in the very middle, there were cords. Thousands of cords. They were strung across the space like the cords of masks across the lanes of the Mask Market, and from every one of them hung a mask.

They were clay. Unglazed, the color of the cups at the basin. Each one was the size of a face and the shape of a face, with holes for the eyes and a hole for the mouth, and each one was different. Mirrah held the lamp up and the nearest ones came out of the dark: an old man’s face, sunken at the cheeks; a woman’s, broad and frowning; a young man’s, with a split lip; a child’s, round, with the mouth open. They turned a little on their cords in some draught she could not feel, slowly, all of them together, so that the faces looked at her and away and back.

“What are they?” she said.

“What people did,” said the Keeper. “The things they did. Not the things done to them.” She had stopped at the head of the ramp, at the very edge of the dark air, and was looking out across the masks with her lamp held low. “My mother called it the Vault of Unwritten Sins. I always thought that was too grand. It’s just where they hang.”

“Who made them?”

“Nobody made them.” The Keeper looked at her. “When somebody comes to the gate and drinks, and what they’re asking to be rid of is a thing they did, then some time after, a day or a week, there’s a new one here. On a new cord. Nobody puts it there. The water does.” She began to walk down the ramp, keeping close to the outer wall, and Mirrah followed. “It doesn’t judge them, if that’s what you’re asking. It doesn’t sort them. It doesn’t know if what you did was wicked or only something you couldn’t bear to have done. There are masks here of people who killed their children and masks of people who stole bread for them and masks of people who ran away from a fire and left somebody behind, and they all hang on the same kind of cord. The water doesn’t ask why. It never has.” She glanced back at the masks. “That’s what the Covenant can’t understand about us. They think it’s a court. It isn’t. It’s a well.”

The girl had gone ahead of them down the ramp and was standing a full turn below, at the wall, with her back to the masks, waiting. She kept her face to the stone as if the masks were not there.

They went down.

The ramp was wet and Mirrah walked with one hand on the wall. The masks hung close beside her on that side, the nearest cords anchored to iron rings set in the stone at the height of her shoulder, and as she went down they turned toward her and away, and she tried not to look at them, and looked. A face with one eye shut. A face with a beard. A face that was hardly a face, the clay slumped and runnelled as though it had been left out in rain. She thought, with the part of her mind that was always thinking about glass, that somebody who knew clay could have told a great deal from the way each one had fired. Some were hard and dark. Some were pale and soft-looking and had crumbled at the edges. Some had cracks in them.

On the second turn down, her shoulder brushed one.

She never meant to. The ramp narrowed there, and a mask hung low on its cord, lower than the others, and her shoulder touched the rim of it as she passed, and it swung out over the dark and back, and touched her cheek.

She was standing in a boat.

It was night and there was a river under the boat, fast and black and loud with flood, and there was a man in the water beside the boat holding on to the gunwale with both hands. He was saying her name. Not her name: his brother’s name, the name of the person whose hands she was in, whose hands were holding the oar, and the boat was low in the water and full of sacks, and if she pulled him in they would both go down, the sacks and the boat and the grain in the sacks that the whole house was owed for, and she—he—the hands on the oar—

“Mirrah.”

The Keeper had her by both shoulders. She was standing on the ramp with her back against the wall and her face wet, and the mask was swinging slowly on its cord out over the dark, a young man’s face with a long jaw, and turning, and turning back.

“Not yours,” the Keeper said. “Not yours. Don’t touch what isn’t yours.”

“He let go,” Mirrah said. “He—they—” She could still feel the oar. Her hands were shaking. “He held the oar and the man let go.”

“I know. I know. It’s not yours. Breathe.”

She breathed. The Keeper held her by the shoulders until the shaking stopped, and then let go, and stood back, and looked at her with something in her pale face that might have been pity.

“Everybody touches one,” she said. “The first time. My mother made sure I did. She said I ought to know what I was keeping.” She picked up the lamp from where she had set it on the ramp. “Walk on the inside. Keep your hands to yourself. We haven’t got long.”

On the fourth turn down, the Keeper stopped.

The cords there were different. Mirrah saw it a moment after she did. Along that stretch of the ramp the iron rings in the wall still had cords tied to them, and the cords went out across the dark to the lotus pillar in the middle as they did everywhere else, but nothing hung from them. They were empty. Ten of them. Twenty. More, further down, a whole gap in the turning faces, like a gap in a mouth where teeth have been drawn.

The Keeper went to the nearest ring and took hold of the cord where it was tied and drew it in, hand over hand, out of the dark. It came easily. There was no weight on it. When the end came in over the edge of the ramp, Mirrah saw that it had been cut, cleanly, with a knife, and that just above the cut there was a smear of something white on the cord, gone hard.

The Keeper looked at it for a long moment.

“Wax,” she said.

“Whose?”

The Keeper gave no answer. She drew in the next cord, and it was cut too, and the next. On the fourth there was a little dried bead of the white wax at the knot, pressed flat, and in the flat bead, small and whorled, the print of a thumb.

Mirrah knew that print. She had seen it in the wax round the edge of Oren’s charm, on the Petal Steps, a hundred years ago. She had seen it round the edge of every charm she had cut for eleven years. She stood on the wet ramp in the dark with the masks turning beside her and looked at the small whorled print in the bead of wax, and found that she was not surprised, and that this frightened her more than if she had been.

“Hadde binds the names,” she said.

“I know who binds the names.”

“Why would she—”

“I don’t know.” The Keeper let the cord fall. It slid away out over the dark, slack, and hung there. “I don’t know what anybody would want with them. They’re nothing. They’re clay. They’re not even the thing; they’re only the shape of it.” She stood with her big hands open at her sides. “Thirty-one years,” she said. “Thirty-one years I’ve come down these stairs, and I never once came this far round, because there’s nothing to do here. You don’t tend them. You let them hang.” Her voice stayed level. “Somebody’s been coming down here behind me with a knife.”

Far above, on the stair they had come down, a voice called out. A man’s voice, made hollow by the stone. It was too far off to make out words. Another answered it.

The Keeper turned her head toward the sound and listened.

“That’s them,” she said. “They’ve found the stair.” She looked down the ramp, into the dark, where the girl was still standing a turn below with her face to the wall, waiting. “The Sacrarium’s at the bottom. The sluices are under it.” She picked up the lamp. “Later I’ll find out who’s been cutting my cords. Later I’ll find out everything. Now we go down.”

They went down. Mirrah kept to the inside of the ramp and her hands to herself, and kept her eyes off the masks, and on every turn she counted the empty cords, and stopped counting at eighty, and never told the Keeper the number.

Chapter 18 The Sacrarium

At the bottom of the spiral there was a court, and nobody had sat in it for a hundred years.

Mirrah knew it was a court before the Keeper said so. She had never been in one, not that she remembered, but her body knew the shape of the room as it had known the iron on the dyers’ step, or as her hand knew where to stop the tail of an r. A round room, smaller than the vault above, with a floor of pale stone worn smooth and three rings of stone benches going round the wall, one above another, like the seats round a cockpit. At one side, raised on three steps, a long stone table. Behind the table, set into the wall, three tall seats with high backs. In front of the table, on the floor of the room, alone, a single stool of stone with a hollow worn in the seat.

On the table there was a pair of scales.

They were bronze, gone green, and large, the beam as long as Mirrah’s arm, and both pans were empty and both hung level, perfectly level, as though nothing had been weighed in them for a hundred years or as though everything had.

“The Sacrarium,” the Keeper said. She stood at the foot of the steps with her lamp and looked at the three high seats. “Where they used to hear people. Before.”

“Before what?”

“Before us. Before the gate.” The Keeper went up the steps and set her lamp on the table beside the scales, and the light went round the room and up over the rings of benches, and Mirrah saw that the walls above the benches were cut all over with writing. Line after line of it, close and even, going round the whole room. “My mother’s aunt said that in the first days, when the prophet was still alive, nobody drank at the gate. They came down here. They sat on that stool and the priestesses sat in those seats and heard them. And when they’d heard them, they gave them the cup, or they didn’t.”

“Didn’t?”

“Read the wall,” said the Keeper.

Mirrah took the other lamp and went to the wall.

The writing was old. The letters were the same letters she cut every day, but older in their shape, longer in the stem, like the letters on the oldest of the wardens’ cups at the gate, and some of the words were spelled in ways she had never seen. She could read it. She went along the wall with the lamp, reading, and the Keeper stood at the table and let her.

Here the water is given only by the hand that holds the cup.

Let no one drink who is brought to the cup by another. Let no husband bring a wife, nor a master a servant, nor a lord a soldier, nor a mother a child, nor any person any person, to set down what the one who brings them wishes set down.

Let the priestess ask three times. Let her ask: what will you set down. Let her ask: is it yours to set down. Let her ask: do you ask it.

Let the one who drinks answer with their own mouth. If they answer with another’s words, let them not drink.

What the cup takes goes down. The water comes up to fill the place it left. Let no one fear that the well will fail.

This is the Sacrament of Consent, and it is the whole of the law of this water, and there is no other.

There was a great deal more. There were rules for the priestesses, about how long they should sit and how they should listen and what they should do if the one on the stool wept or could not speak. There were the words of the cup, the old form, longer than the form the Keeper used at the gate, but the same at the end. All of it? And the answer: Yes. And under that, cut deeper than the rest, as if the cutter had wanted it to last longer than everything around it:

The priestess does not ask why. She does not ask what was done, nor to whom, nor whether it was right. She asks only whether the one who drinks has asked. The water does not weigh the deed. It weighs the asking.

Mirrah stood in front of that for a long while.

“It doesn’t say anything about the people they hurt,” she said at last.

“No.”

“Some of the people who drank had done terrible things. The masks. You said. People who killed their children.”

“Yes.”

“And some of them had had terrible things done to them. And drank to forget that.” She was thinking of the woman in the square with the laundry, sitting on the stones saying I don’t know. She was thinking of the soup line, and the thin polite people on the second flight. She was thinking of the cold that had come up her own arm from three words on a paper. Refused. Want of standing. “And the water took both. And never asked which.”

“It was never meant to ask,” said the Keeper. “That was the point. Anybody can ask what did you do. The courts all along the river ask that, every day. The Covenant’s marching down the road to ask it. Nobody asks do you want to carry this. Only here.” She put her hand on the beam of the scales, very gently, and it did not move. “That was the whole of it. That anyone who came would be asked that one thing and nothing else, and would be believed.”

“Then why did they stop? Coming down here. Asking three times.”

The Keeper was quiet.

“Because it was slow,” she said. “That’s what my mother’s aunt said. Because people came faster than they could be heard. Because the prophet had been all over the river by then, telling people there was a place where they could set it down, and he’d never told anyone about the stool or the three questions or the priestesses. He told them about the cup.” She looked at the empty seats. “So the priestesses came up to the gate. And they asked the questions there. And then they asked one. And then my family had the gate, and we ask: all of it? And that’s all.”

“He never told anyone about the stool,” Mirrah said.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I don’t know.” The Keeper took her hand off the scales. “Maybe he’d forgotten it. He drank too.”

The sluices were under the Sacrarium, through a door behind the three seats.

It was a low door of bronze, green like the scales, and it was stuck. The Keeper put her shoulder to it and it moved a finger’s width and stopped, and she stood back breathing hard, and said, “That’s the gate I can’t do any more. There’s a bar inside. It’s swollen. You have to lift it and slide it at the same time, and it wants two hands that know how to hold a thing without breaking it.”

Mirrah put her hands through the gap. She felt the bar: bronze, cold, slick with water, and swollen in its brackets as glass swells in a vise when you’ve tightened it on a hot day. She knew exactly what it wanted. She lifted it a hair’s breadth and eased it toward her and let it settle and lifted again, gently, the way she freed a lozenge from the jaw of the vise when it had seized, never forcing, and on the fourth lift it slid, and the door swung open.

The Keeper looked at her hands.

“Good,” she said.

Beyond the door there was a room full of water and noise. The roar of it came at them out of the dark, and the lamp showed a long stone chamber with a channel down the middle as wide as a lane, and water running in the channel fast and clear and loud, and at the far end of the chamber, set across the channel, three great gates of bronze on chains, with wheels to raise and lower them. Two of the gates were down. The third was half up, and the water was going under it in a smooth heavy tongue into the dark beyond.

“That’s the river side,” the Keeper said, pointing past the gates into the dark. “If those three go up, the river comes in through there and through here and up into everything above. Every cistern. Every gallery. It’ll wash it all out and take it down to the sea.” She waded into the channel to the thigh and took hold of the wheel of the third gate. “Help me.”

They lowered it together. It came down slowly, the chain grinding over the wheel, the bronze gate sinking into the tongue of water until the water heaped up against it and then stopped, and there was only the noise of it beating on the other side.

The Keeper went along the chains with her hands, checking each one. Then she took the long iron key from round her neck and fitted it to a lock on the frame of the middle wheel, and turned it, and Mirrah heard something heavy move inside the stone.

“Now they’re locked,” the Keeper said. “From here. Nobody can raise them from upstream. They’d have to come down here and do it, and they’d have to get through that door to do it, and they won’t know about the bar.” She stood in the water with the key in her hand and her shoulders sagged, all at once, as if something holding them up had been let go. “That’s done.”

Mirrah was not listening.

She was looking at the wall of the sluice chamber beside the door, low down, near the water, where the lamp she had set on the ledge was throwing its light along the stone. There was a hole there. It was not old. The edges of it were sharp and pale, the stone freshly broken, and there were chisel marks round it, crisp, the kind of marks that would have been worn smooth in a year of running water. Beyond the hole there was a channel. A small one, no wider than a gutter, cut into the rock, going away from the sluice chamber into the dark at a slant, downward; and water was running into it out of the main channel in a thin bright thread, and going away.

“Keeper.”

The Keeper came. She looked at the hole for a long time with the lamp held close.

“I didn’t dig that,” she said.

“Nobody digs anything down here.”

“I didn’t dig that,” the Keeper said again. She put her hand into the hole and felt along the edge of the little channel, and drew it out wet, and held it to the lamp. There was something caught between her fingers. A thin black splinter, hard as horn, longer than a needle, with a point at one end.

A thorn.

The Keeper held it in her palm and looked at it, and Mirrah saw her face, and looked away.

“Where does it go?” said Mirrah.

“Down. Somewhere I’ve never been.” The Keeper closed her hand over the thorn. “There’s a cistern under the old customs house, they say. The first one. Older than the gate. My mother’s aunt said it was sealed when she was a girl and nobody’s been in it since.” She looked down the little channel into the dark, where the thin thread of water was running away from her, from her locked gates and her sluices and her thirty-one years, slant-wise, into the rock. “Somebody’s been in it.”

From somewhere behind them, back through the Sacrarium and up the spiral, there was a crash, and men’s voices, nearer than before.

The Keeper turned toward the sound.

“They’re in the vault,” she said. “They’ll be down here soon, and they’ll find the door open.” She looked at Mirrah. “I’m staying with the gates. If they want them, they’ll have to take the key off me, and they’ll find that harder than the door. You—”

She stopped.

The girl was standing in the little channel.

Mirrah had not seen her come. She was standing ankle-deep in the thin bright thread of water where it went away into the rock, small and dry and patient, with her back to them, facing down the slant into the dark. She did not turn round. She did not need to. Mirrah knew what she was waiting for.

“Go on,” the Keeper said quietly. “If she’s going, go. See where it ends.” She held out the lamp. “And Mirrah. Whatever you find down there, it was given. Remember that. Whatever it is, somebody asked.”

Mirrah took the lamp.

“So did I,” she said.

“Yes,” said the Keeper. “So did you.”

Chapter 19 What she gave

The channel was too low to walk in, so she went down it on her knees, with the lamp held out in front of her and the girl going ahead.

It was not far, as distance went. It felt endless. The rock pressed close on either side and overhead, and the thin thread of water ran under her knees, cold, going the same way she was going, and every few lengths there were the chisel marks, crisp and pale, and here and there on the stone a smear of white wax where somebody had steadied a candle. The girl went ahead without a light. When Mirrah raised her head, the small pale shape was always there, a little further down the slant, waiting, and then going on.

Then the rock opened, and Mirrah could stand.

She was in a dome. It was smaller than the Sacrarium and much older; the stone was not dressed at all, only hollowed, as if somebody had blown a bubble in the rock, and the floor was dry except for a groove where the thread of water came in and ran across it and out again through a second channel on the far side. The air smelled of candle-smoke and old wax and, under it, faint and green and sweet, of lotus.

There were shelves round the walls. Somebody had cut them into the rock, rough ledges at the height of her chest, going round the whole of the dome, and they were full.

The masks were there. She saw them first because they were faces and the lamp found them: clay faces, the color of the cups, lying on the ledges in rows, side by side, looking up at the roof. Eighty of them. More. Some still had a length of cut cord through the hole at the top. Next to the masks there were jars, small round lidded jars of the kind the dyers kept their mordants in, dozens of them, and when she lifted the lid of the nearest one it was full of pale stiff flakes, the shape of petals, marked faintly all over like writing seen from underneath.

And next to the jars, on the ledge nearest the channel she had come in by, there were the charms.

They were hers. Not one of them was a charm she had made for herself; all of them were charms she had made for other people. She knew them at once, as you know your own hand across a room: river glass, the color of lotus water at dusk, a little thicker than the ones she cut now, from batches she remembered cutting, winters ago. They were threaded on cords in bundles of ten and twenty, and the white wax of Hadde’s binding was still on the rims of most of them, with the small whorled print of Hadde’s thumb. Mirrah picked up a bundle and held it to the lamp.

They were blank.

The names had been taken out of them. Not broken out; that would have cracked the glass. Taken out, ground away from inside, with a care and skill that she knew, because she would have needed every hour of her eleven years at the wheel to do it, so that the glass was clear right through where the name had been and the lozenge was as smooth and empty as the day she had cut it from the sheet.

She stood holding them.

A name can come off a paper, Ilvane had said, on the Petal Steps, in the dusk, kindly. If you know how. And on the second flight of the Steps the thin polite people sat every evening in a row, holding their bowls in both hands, and one of them, an old man with a gentle face and a beautiful smile, had asked her, so courteously, whether she had a name she could spare. He had had one, he said. He had given it away, and then he had given away the one after that.

On the floor of the dome, against the wall, there was a little stone dish with a lamp beside it, burnt out, and in the dish a heel of bread, gone hard, and a pinch of salt.

Mirrah put the bundle of charms back on the ledge, exactly where it had been, and wiped her hands on her apron, and wiped them again.

The girl was standing at the mouth of the second channel.

The second channel went down too, but not far.

It came out, after twenty lengths of crawling, at the edge of a little round room no bigger than Mirrah’s own room on Fig Court. The whole floor of it was a pool. The water came right up to the mouth of the channel and lay there, perfectly still, perfectly clear, so clear that when she held the lamp out over it she could see to the bottom, which was not far, a man’s height down, pale sand. The air in the room was cold. It smelled of nothing at all.

The chisel marks stopped a hand’s breadth from the pool.

She could see them. Whoever had cut the channel had cut it down to here, to the very edge of the little room, and stopped, and there was a last bead of white wax on the rock where the last candle had stood. A hand’s breadth more and the channel would have broken through into the pool, and the water in it would have gone out, slant-wise, up, along the thread, to the dome with the shelves.

The girl sat down on the edge of the pool with her feet in the water.

Mirrah knelt beside her and looked down.

There was a mask lying on the sand at the bottom of the pool. It lay face up. The water over it was so clear that it seemed to be lying in air, and the lamplight went down to it without bending, and Mirrah could see it as plainly as if it were on the ledge in the dome. A young woman’s face, narrow, with a pointed chin. Clay, unglazed, the color of the cups. Fired hard. There was a crack in it, a fine crack running from the corner of the mouth down across the chin, and it was a crack she knew; it was the crack that runs from the tail of an r that has been carried a fraction too far.

Round the mask, drifting slowly in the still water, there were petals. Not many. A dozen. Pale and stiff and marked all over with writing seen from underneath. They turned as they drifted, slowly, and settled, and lifted again.

The girl looked down at the mask, and then up at Mirrah, and her narrow brown face with its pointed chin was the same face as the mask’s, only younger. Ten years younger, perhaps. Fifteen. It was the face the mask’s face had been, before.

“That’s you,” the girl said.

Mirrah did not answer. She could not.

“You put it down,” the girl said. “And me. You put me down too.” She swung her feet in the water. “You said all of it.”

“I don’t remember,” Mirrah said.

“I know. That’s what all of it means.”

Mirrah looked from the mask to the chisel marks that stopped a hand’s breadth from the water, and back. Somebody had been cutting toward this. Toward her. She thought of the bread and salt in the dish, and the blank charms on their cords, and Ilvane at the soup pot saying it’s free, put that away. She was still looking at the marks when the water moved.

The water in the pool moved.

It did not ripple. It rose: the whole surface of it, all at once, a finger’s breadth, as the basin had risen under the green door. And then it did not sink. It went on rising. It came up out of the pool in a smooth clear hump, as if something under the sand at the bottom were lifting its back, and the sand came up with it, pale, streaming, swirling up through the water in long coils, and the coils were moving, slowly, round and round each other, like rope being twisted.

It came up between Mirrah and the mask.

It had no face. It had something where a face might have been, a long blunt end of streaming sand and water turned toward her, and in the sand there were shapes, shifting like shapes in spray: an eye, a mouth, a hand, a doorway, a woman bending over a well with a jar. The tiny people from the carving. They were moving in it. They moved in it as the petals moved in the pool, drifting and turning and settling and lifting, and every one of them was looking at her.

It made a sound.

It was not a voice. It was the sound of water running in a channel a long way off, and sand moving, and under it, faint, something like words, the way the marks on the petals were something like writing. She knew some of them. She heard refused. She heard standing. She heard her own voice, a voice she had never heard, younger and rougher, saying please, saying I want to bring a complaint, saying it to somebody behind a high desk, and somebody saying no.

It leaned toward her.

She could not move. The girl beside her had stopped swinging her feet and was sitting quite still. The streaming sand came closer, cold, and the shapes in it turned their small faces to her, and the sound of the far water and the far voices filled the little room, and under all of it, at last, words she could hear, in a voice very like the girl’s.

You can ask again.

You asked once. You can ask again. Go back up. Go back to the wheel and the fig tree, and put it down, and be light. I’m not keeping you.

It was not threatening her. It was offering. That was all it wanted. It wanted the rest.

She opened her mouth. She did not know what she was going to say until she said it.

“I asked.”

The thing stopped.

“I asked,” Mirrah said again, louder. Her voice shook. She made it stop shaking. “I don’t remember what. I don’t remember why. But I know I asked. I came to the gate wet to the neck and I said take it, and she said all of it?, and I said yes. Nobody brought me. Nobody made me. I asked with my own mouth.” She was on her knees at the edge of the pool with the lamp in both hands, and she held it up, toward the blunt end of streaming sand where a face might have been. “That’s mine. That’s the one thing I’ve got that’s mine. I asked.”

For a moment nothing happened.

Then the sand fell.

It fell all at once, as the column of water had fallen in the orchard, with a long rushing whisper, back into the pool. The shapes went with it. The water closed over the place where it had been and heaved once and was still, and the sand settled slowly to the bottom in a pale haze, and through the haze, after a while, Mirrah could see the mask again, lying face up on the sand with the crack running from the corner of its mouth, and the petals drifting round it.

The girl was looking at her.

She sat at the edge of the pool for a long time.

She thought about the dome with the shelves, and the charms with the names ground out of them, and the bread and salt in the dish by the lamp. She thought about the chisel marks that stopped a hand’s breadth from the pool. She thought about Ilvane on the plinth of the column under the broken portico, in the dusk, with the soup-cloth over her arm, saying: Something you’ve already given away. You won’t miss it. You haven’t had her for eleven years.

Her.

She had said it, and then her, and Mirrah had sat on the plinth in the lamplight and turned the slip over and made nothing of it, because Ilvane was tired, because Ilvane had been ladling soup for three hours, because Ilvane had been right about everything else.

Something you’ve already given away.

She looked at the girl sitting beside her with her feet in the water. Thin, in a brown dress too short in the sleeves. Ragged hair. A smudge of grit along one cheekbone, the kind of grit you get from a wheel. Ten years old. Eleven. The girl she had been, before Ostrin, before the glazier’s workshop and the high desk and the word refused. The girl she had poured into a cup at the gate with all the rest of it and walked away from, and who had come up out of the water when the wells were broken and walked the city looking for her, and found her, and followed her home, and told her to eat.

That was the price. Not the mask. Not the petals. Her.

A name can come off a paper, Ilvane had said. Nothing is destroyed, little sister. It’s only moved. And the names had come off the charms in the dome, every one, ground out of the glass with such care that there was nothing left, and the people who had worn them sat on the second flight of the Steps every evening asking strangers for a name they could spare. That was the other half. Not off the writ: off everything. Nobody would ever be able to read her name in a square, or point at her, or haul her back up the river to Ostrin. And nobody would ever be able to call her to a court again, or hear her, or let her bring a complaint, ever, anywhere. She would have no standing in the world at all. Not want of it. None.

Little sister. A priestess’s joke. Ilvane said it to Hadde across the tables. Pass me the wax, little sister.

Mirrah put her hands over her face.

She had been going to do it. She knew that now. She had sat on the plinth and not asked the price because Ilvane had been right about everything, and she had gone home and lain awake, and if the wall had not come down into the river and the Keeper had not taken her by the arm on the green door’s steps, she would have gone back up the Petal Steps one evening soon and sat down by the soup pot and said yes.

She had been going to sell her own little sister.

When she took her hands away from her face the girl was still there, looking at her, with her feet in the water and her wet hand held a little away from her side.

“Sau,” the girl said. “You’re crying.”

“I know.”

“Why?”

“Because I nearly gave you away again,” she said. “And I didn’t know your name.”

The girl considered this, as children consider things, frowning a little, swinging one foot.

“It’s your name,” she said. “Same as mine. You know it. It’s on the paper.”

She did know it. She had cut it into a lozenge of river glass at midnight with her own hands and wrapped it in felt and put it at the back of a drawer. She had known it for nine days. She had refused, every one of those nine days, to let it be hers.

She looked at the mask lying on the sand at the bottom of the pool, with the crack running from the corner of its mouth.

“Isaut,” she said.

It did not turn anything. It was not a key. It was a name, a little old-fashioned, a river name, and she said it to the water and to the girl and to the mask on the sand, and nothing came back, no memory, no room, no face behind a high desk. Only the name, and the cold of the little room, and the lamp in her hands.

But it was hers. She had given it away, once, wet to the neck, and she had asked to, and now she was asking for it back.

Isaut stood up. She took the lamp. She looked down at the girl, who was looking up at her, and held out her free hand, and after a moment the girl took it. Her hand was cold and wet and very small, and it was real; it closed on Isaut’s fingers with a child’s grip, hard.

“Come on,” Isaut said. “It’s late.”

Chapter 20 The culvert

The culvert ran under the old customs house, and the chief engineer said it was the oldest thing he had ever crawled through.

“Older than the wall,” he said, on his hands and knees in front of Sehra, with a lamp on a hook at his belt and his broad back filling the whole of the tunnel. “Older than the gate. Look at the stone. Nobody’s dressed stone like that in five hundred years. I’d like to know who did.” He said it with a kind of reverence, as he had talked about paperwork on the first day of the chain. “Mind your head, Notary. It drops here.”

It dropped there. Sehra minded her head.

There were fourteen of them. The engineer and four of his men with picks and crowbars and a sack of mortar between them, which the Seal-Bearer had looked at at the head of the culvert and said they would not be using without her leave. Six soldiers of the vanguard. The boy, who had asked to come and whom the sergeant had refused and the Seal-Bearer had overruled: he’s been down there once already; he knows what water feels like in the dark. The Seal-Bearer herself, who was sixty-eight years old and had come down the rope into the culvert’s mouth hand over hand like a sailor and refused all help. And Sehra, with the writ-case on her back on its strap, punched in her own hole, and the Writ of Entry inside it, and inside the Writ the scrap of paper with eleven names.

The culvert came out into a gallery full of lotus roots.

She had heard about it from the boy. She had not believed it, quite; she had thought it was the kind of thing a frightened man sees in the water. But it was exactly as he had said. A great hall held up by pillars of pale root, ivory-colored, coming down out of a dark too high for the lamps, and a floor of black water, and stone paths running across it on low arches, and the lamps on their brackets, old bronze, green, unlit. And falling through the lamplight, slowly, from somewhere far above, pale flakes like petals, turning as they came down, settling on the water.

The soldiers stopped at the edge of the gallery and looked up. Nobody spoke.

“Don’t touch those,” said the Seal-Bearer.

“Why not?” said one of them.

“Because they aren’t yours,” said the old judge. “Light the lamps, Engineer. All of them you can reach. I want to see where I’m putting my feet.”

They went down for a long time.

There were stairs, many stairs, and galleries between them that grew smaller as they went down, and everywhere the water, running down the channels in the middle of the steps, going the same way they were going. Twice they heard voices, far off, men calling to each other somewhere above or to the side, hollow in the stone. The engineer thought they were the Council’s men, from what the prisoners had said; there was a faction in the city that wanted to open the sluices and wash the whole of the underneath out into the river. “They’ll drown themselves doing it,” he said, “and us with them,” and the Seal-Bearer told him to stop talking about drowning in front of the boy, and he stopped.

On the oldest stair, where the steps were hardly steps at all, only ledges with a groove worn along them, they came to a small square room with a carving in it.

The Seal-Bearer stopped at the door. She stood there for a long while with her lamp held up, looking at the slab, and then she went in, slowly, through the water, which was a hand deep over the floor, and stood in front of it.

Sehra came in beside her.

A knight kneeling in rubble, with his hands over his face, and his sword lying in front of him with the hilt turned toward him. Above him, coming down out of the top of the stone, a robed figure without a face, arms a little out from its sides like a dancer coming down from a leap, and one foot reaching down past the knight’s head to touch the ground beside him. A small round hollow in the rock where the toe touched. Water in it.

“I know this,” Sehra said. “It’s on their gate. On the cups.”

“It’s on everything they’ve got,” said the Seal-Bearer. “This will be where they got it from.” She looked at the knight for a long time. “Hold your lamp up to his shoulder, child. There’s something there.”

Sehra held the lamp up.

There was a device on the knight’s surcoat, at the shoulder, worn almost away. A figure, a broken curve, something coming down through it. She looked at it for a long time with the lamp close, and something moved in her memory, the way a fish moves under the surface of a pond: the long roll of the crusade orders in the record-chests at Galanina, which she had copied fair once, when she was twenty, on the old notary’s orders, for practice. Hundreds of devices, one after another down the roll, each with the name of its order beside it. Some of the names had been struck through, with a single line, in red. She could see the red lines. She could see that some of the devices beside them had been figures, with broken curves, and something coming down through them.

She could not see which.

“I’ve seen it,” she said. “On the roll of the orders. I don’t remember which one.”

“Find out,” said the Seal-Bearer. “When we’re back.” She was looking now not at the knight but at the rubble around him, the heap of rough-cut stone going up on either side to the edges of the slab, and she had a strange expression on her closed face. “Give me your arm.”

Sehra gave her her arm. The old woman lowered herself down onto her knees in the water in front of the carving, slowly, with a small grunt at the bottom, and sat back on her heels with the water round her waist and her black gown floating out round her, and rested her lamp on her knee.

It was low. It was almost on the water.

“Ah,” said the Seal-Bearer.

Sehra looked.

In the low light the rubble was full of people. She would not have seen them if the old woman had not knelt. They were cut so faintly into the stone that in the light from the door they were nothing, only the grain of the rock, but with the lamp on the Seal-Bearer’s knee throwing its light along the surface they came up out of it like frost: streets, and doorways, and a market with awnings, and a woman at a well with a jar, and children, and a man leading a goat, and people on the roofs, hundreds of them, a whole city, each one no bigger than the end of a finger. In one doorway, low down, a very small figure stood with its hand on the post, looking out.

None of the doorposts had a lotus.

Behind Sehra, at the door, the boy had come in, and the engineer, and two of the soldiers, and they stood in the water and looked, and nobody said anything.

“Somebody remembered them,” said the Seal-Bearer at last. “That city. Whoever it was that was here before the knight came back and knelt in it. Somebody remembered, and couldn’t bear to forget, and couldn’t bear to have it looked at, and cut it so light that you’d have to kneel to see it.” She was quiet a moment. “And then everyone in this place stood here with the lamp up on the bracket and told the story of the angel, and never once got down on their knees.”

“There’s nothing in the record,” Sehra said. “About a city here before. Nothing. I’ve read everything in the chests.”

“No,” said the Seal-Bearer. “There wouldn’t be. The crusade that emptied it wouldn’t have written it down.” She put her hand on the stone, very gently, low down, on the doorway with the small figure in it. “And the people who might have written it down were the people in it.”

She took her hand away. She looked up at Sehra.

“Child,” she said, “what does the Writ say we’re down here to do?”

“Seal the wells. From below.”

“And what are the wells for?”

Sehra did not answer.

“I’ll tell you what I think,” said the Seal-Bearer. “I think we came three weeks down a river to restore what was owed to people who were harmed, and to make the people who harmed them answer. And I think the first harm in this place, the oldest one, the one everything else is built on, is this.” She touched the stone again. “And nobody’s owed for it. Nobody’s on the schedule for it. Nobody will ever stand before a court and answer for it, because the man who did it knelt here and drank and became a prophet, and the people he did it to are a few hundred marks on a wall that you can only see by kneeling in the water.” She held out her arm. “Help me up.”

Sehra helped her up. The old woman stood dripping in the little room with her lamp, and looked at the knight a moment longer, and then turned to the engineer.

“You won’t be needing the mortar, Engineer,” she said. “We’ll go on down and see what else there is. We’ll seal nothing in this place until I’ve understood it, and I begin to think I may be some time understanding it.”

“Yes, Seal-Bearer.”

They went out through the low arch on the far side of the room, into the dark. Sehra was last. At the arch she stopped, and turned, and held her lamp low once more, almost to the water, and looked at the faint city on the stone, and at the small figure in the doorway with its hand on the post.

Then, from somewhere ahead and below, far down, there came up through the stone a sound she did not expect.

It was a child’s voice. It said something she could not make out, and a woman’s voice answered it, low, close to the child’s, as a mother answers a child walking beside her in the dark. The words were lost in the stone. But the tone of them came up clear: patient, and tired, and kind.

The boy had heard it too. He was standing in the arch with his lamp, looking down.

“Who’s down there?” he said.

Sehra put her hand to the strap of the writ-case, where the scrap of paper with eleven names lay folded inside the Writ of Entry.

“I don’t know,” she said. “Let’s go and see.”

Part Four The Two Courts

Chapter 21 Salt and glass

The Keeper was sitting on the wheel of the middle sluice with the key in her lap, and Sister Hadde was standing in the water in front of her with a soldier’s knife at her back.

Isaut saw them from the mouth of the little channel, before anyone saw her. The sluice chamber was full of lamps now. The broad grey soldier who had stood at the foot of the green door’s steps was there with a dozen of his men, wet to the waist, with their sledges and crowbars and coils of rope laid down along the edge of the channel where they had dropped them; and the lamps they had brought made the long room bright, and loud with the beating of the river on the far side of the three bronze gates. Nobody was working at the gates. Everyone was looking at Hadde.

She was small and neat and grey, as she always was. Her hair was coiled at the back of her head and pinned with the long black thorn. The front of her grey priestess’s gown was dark with water to the breast, and there was a smear of white wax on one sleeve, and she held her hands folded in front of her, exactly as she held them at her table on the Petal Steps while she waited for somebody to say a new name three times.

“She brought us,” the grey soldier was saying to the Keeper. “She came to the customs house at noon and said she knew a way down that didn’t need your key. A culvert, and then a channel. She said she’d been down it before.” His square face was stiff. “She said the Sister on the Steps had sent her to help.”

“Which Sister?” said the Keeper.

“Ilvane.”

The Keeper looked at Hadde, and Hadde looked back, mild and patient.

“You cut my cords,” the Keeper said.

“Yes,” said Hadde.

“Eighty of them.”

“Ninety-one.”

“What for?”

“For what was on them,” Hadde said. She said it without any heat at all, as she would have told a newcomer the fee for a binding. “They were hanging there doing nothing. Nobody had come for them in forty years. Some of them a hundred. A thing nobody comes for is a thing that’s been thrown away, Keeper. You know that. You’ve said it to me yourself. A thing you give isn’t yours to ask after.”

“I said it isn’t the giver’s to ask after,” said the Keeper. “I never said it was yours.”

“Somebody’s,” said Hadde. “Or nobody’s. And nobody’s is a waste.” She looked down at her folded hands. “I’ve seen what happens to things nobody remembers, Keeper. I grew up behind an army. Whole towns of things nobody wanted, lying in the road. Somebody ought to have them. Somebody ought to want them for something.”

The grey soldier had gone still.

“The names,” the Keeper said. “The charms. She found them.” She lifted her chin toward the mouth of the little channel, and every head in the chamber turned, and saw Isaut standing there with the lamp in one hand and the girl’s small wet hand in the other. “Bundles of them. Ground blank. In a hole under the customs house, with bread and salt.”

“Yes,” said Hadde.

“And Wenna.”

Hadde was quiet for a moment. It was the first time she had been quiet before answering.

“Wenna was going round with her copies,” she said. “Showing people. That was kind of her. But she had begun asking who had bought a name off the Steps this year, and who had sold one, and writing it down. She had a list.” Hadde looked at the Keeper with her mild grey eyes. “A list of people who’d been relieved. Can you imagine? Going about the city with a list. Next week she’d have shown it to someone in a square.” She folded her hands a little tighter. “The wall was shaking. She had a bad leg. It wasn’t hard.”

Nobody spoke.

The grey soldier was looking at Hadde, and Isaut saw in his face that he had come down into the dark to drown two hundred years of other people’s pasts for what he believed was the city’s sake, and had brought this woman with him to show him the way, and had only now understood whose way it was.

“Bind her,” he said to one of his men. His voice was hoarse. “Hands. Not too tight.” He looked at the Keeper. “I didn’t know.”

“I know you didn’t,” said the Keeper. “That’s what she’s for.”

They were binding Hadde’s wrists with a length of the soldiers’ rope when the water in the channel began to rise.

It came up slowly at first, as the basin had risen, a finger’s breadth, then another, then another, without stopping. The river beat on the far side of the locked gates as it had beaten all along. This was not the river. This was coming from the other way, from upstream, from the stair they had all come down, and it was clear.

“That’s not mine,” said the Keeper, getting up off the wheel.

“What is it?”

“Above us. Something’s broken above us.” She was listening, with her head on one side, to a sound Isaut could not hear. “The gallery. The big one. The roots.” She turned to the grey soldier. “Is anyone else down here? Anyone but you?”

“No,” said the soldier. “Not ours.”

“Then whose are those?”

Lamps. Isaut saw them a moment after the Keeper did: lamps, coming down the far stair into the Sacrarium beyond the bronze door, a dozen or more, swinging, and voices with them, and the sound of men splashing through deep water in boots. A voice called something sharp and the lamps stopped. Then one of them came on alone, slowly, to the bronze door, and through it, and stopped at the edge of the sluice chamber, held up high.

It was a woman. She was not old; perhaps forty, dark-skinned, with her hair cut short like a man’s under a soaked grey cap, and a leather case on a red strap across her back. She was soaked to the hips. She stood in the doorway with the lamp and looked at all of them: at the Keeper, and the soldiers, and Hadde with her wrists half-bound, and the rising water. And then at Isaut, with the girl’s hand in hers, at the mouth of the little channel.

She looked at Isaut for a long time.

“Who’s in charge here?” she said.

It was a dry voice, quiet, the kind of voice people would have to come close to hear. Behind her in the Sacrarium Isaut could see other lamps now, and soldiers, and the chained halo on their shoulders, and among them a tall old woman in a black gown floating out around her in the water.

“Nobody,” said the Keeper. “I keep the wells. These men came to break them. That woman”—Hadde—“has been robbing them. And you, I think, came to seal them.”

“I came to see what was here before I sealed anything,” said the woman in the doorway. “The Seal-Bearer won’t let me seal what I don’t understand.” She glanced at the rising water. “Is that going to keep coming up?”

“Yes.”

“How far?”

“Into everything,” said the Keeper. “There’s one place it won’t go. Below us. Under the dunes. The water there runs the wrong way; it can’t fill.” She looked from the doorway to the soldiers to Hadde to Isaut. “If you want to live through the next hour, all of you, follow me. Bring her,” she said to the soldiers, about Hadde. “And bring your lamps.”

The Root-Vault was the last room of all, and it was made of salt and glass.

The way down to it was a shaft, a narrow round shaft with a stair cut into its wall, going down in a spiral like the ramp in the Vault of Unwritten Sins but much steeper and much smaller, so that they went down it one at a time with a hand on the wall. Isaut went behind the Keeper, with the girl behind her. After them came the grey soldier and his men with Hadde between them, and after them the woman with the leather case, and the old woman in black, and the Covenant soldiers and engineers, and last of all a young soldier with a furled white flag on a pole, which he would not leave behind.

At the bottom of the shaft the wall opened, and they came out into the vault one by one and stopped.

It was a lotus. That was Isaut’s first thought, and it stayed her thought afterwards, when she tried to describe it: a great flower turned upside down, so that they had come in at its stem and were standing in its heart, looking up at the petals curving away overhead. The petals were salt. White salt, crystalline, in long smooth ribbed sheets that went up and up and out and curved over far above, and between them, filling the spaces where one petal overlapped the next, there was glass. Clear glass, thick, in great curved panes, and behind the glass, everywhere, water. Not standing water. Running water, running in thin bright sheets up the inside of the glass, from the floor to the roof, upward, against all sense, so that the whole vault was full of the sound of it and the light of the lamps went up the walls with it in long wavering bands.

The floor was glass too, a single great disc of it, old, scratched and clouded, and under the glass there was nothing; darkness, a long way down.

Words were cut into the salt. Isaut saw them as her lamp went round: lines of the old long-stemmed letters, going round each salt petal from floor to roof in a spiral, and the letters were cut deep, and the light caught in them. She could read some. Here the covenant was sworn. Here let no word be false. Here what is spoken is weighed.

“What is this place?” said one of the Covenant soldiers.

“Where the founders swore,” said the Keeper. She was standing in the middle of the glass floor with her lamp held up, looking at the salt petals. “The first ones. Before the gate. Before the priestesses and the stool.” She lowered the lamp. “My mother’s aunt said they made it so that nobody could lie in it. Say something false in here and the glass clouds.”

“That’s a story,” said the grey soldier.

The pane of glass nearest to him, between two petals of salt, went white.

It went white from the middle outward, like breath on a cold window, and the water running up behind it vanished into the whiteness, and the sound of the water in that pane stopped. For a moment nobody moved. Then the white began to clear, slowly, from the edges in, and the water came back, running upward, and the sound with it.

The soldier stared at the glass.

“It’s not a story,” the Keeper said. “Try not to say anything you don’t mean.”

The woman with the leather case had come to stand at the edge of the glass floor. She had taken off her soaked cap and was holding it in her hand, and she was looking not at the salt or the glass or the upward water, but at Isaut.

She came across the floor. Her boots left wet prints on the old glass. She stopped an arm’s length away, and Isaut saw her look at the girl, and at the girl’s small wet hand in Isaut’s, and back up.

“My name is Sehra,” the woman said. “I’m a notary of the Covenant. I copied the schedule of persons that was read in your square. I’ve been carrying a list of eleven names in my case since the night we read it.” She took a breath. “I think one of them may be yours.”

Around the vault the glass stayed clear. The water ran upward behind it, bright, unbroken, and in the long wavering bands of lamplight on the salt the old words went round and round.

“It is,” said Isaut.

The glass stayed clear.

Chapter 22 A court with no judge

The Speaker came down the shaft a quarter of an hour after them, alone, with a lamp in his teeth.

Sehra heard him before she saw him: the scrape of boots on the spiral stair, slow, uneven, one leg favored, and a man’s breath coming hard. Then the lamp swung out of the mouth of the shaft into the vault, and a hand took it from his mouth, and he stepped down onto the glass floor and stood there with the water running off him. His grey silk veil was soaked through and clung to the shape of his face, so that for the first time she could see that he had a face under it: a long jaw, a straight nose, the hollows of eyes. Nothing more. The silk was still between it and the light.

“They said the Keeper had come down,” he said. “The men at the green door. They said she’d taken a glass-cutter with her.” He looked round the vault, at the salt and the upward water and the crowd of people standing on the glass, and his eyes, or the hollows of them, stopped on the woman with the girl’s hand in hers. Then moved on. “I came to see that nobody drowned.”

“Nobody’s drowned,” said the Keeper.

“Good.” He set his lamp down on the glass. His hands were shaking, and he folded them in front of him to stop it, and Sehra saw again, in the lamplight, the old white seams across his fingertips. “Good.”

The Seal-Bearer had not sat down. There was nothing to sit on; the vault had no benches, no table, nothing but the glass floor and the salt petals curving up overhead. She stood in the middle of the floor in her black gown, which had stopped dripping and hung heavy and dark round her ankles, and looked up at the words going round the salt, and Sehra stood beside her and held the lamp so that she could read them.

The Keeper came and stood on her other side.

They did not look at each other at first. They stood side by side looking up at the words, two old women, one tall and narrow in black and one broad and heavy in an oiled apron, and Sehra could feel, standing between them, that they were measuring each other as two masons measure a stone before they lift it between them.

“You keep the wells,” said the Seal-Bearer.

“I keep them.”

“And you drink?”

“Never,” said the Keeper.

The glass round them stayed clear. The Seal-Bearer looked at it, and then, for the first time, at the Keeper.

“Never,” she said.

“Keepers don’t.” The Keeper’s broad face was perfectly calm. “My mother didn’t. Her aunt didn’t. You can’t keep a thing you’ve set down. Everything that’s come to my gate in thirty-one years, I’ve seen. Every face. Every one of them shaking, or weeping, or wet to the neck. I’ve held the cup for them and watched them drink and walked them to the kerb and sat them down. And then they went into the city lighter, and I went back up my steps.” She looked down at the glass under her feet. “Somebody has to remember that they came. That’s all a Keeper is. The one who doesn’t put it down.”

The Seal-Bearer was quiet for a long time.

“I’ve sat on the bench at Galanina for forty years,” she said at last. “I’ve heard every kind of thing a person can do to another person. I’ve never once been allowed to forget any of it. I didn’t know there was anybody else in the world who carried it on purpose.”

“There’s me,” said the Keeper.

“Yes,” said the Seal-Bearer. “So I see.”

They looked at each other a moment longer, and then, without either of them saying anything, they turned back to the words on the salt, and Sehra, standing between them with the lamp, understood that something had been settled that she was not part of and would not be told about.

“Child,” the Seal-Bearer said, a little later. “The device. On the knight’s shoulder. Have you remembered?”

She had. She had remembered it on the spiral stair coming down, with her hand on the wall and the water rising below her, as a word comes back to you just after you’ve stopped looking for it. The long roll of the crusade orders in the record-chests at Galanina. A whole sheet of them near the end, the old ones, the orders that had marched in the crusades and come home and been reckoned. Devices down the left side, names down the right. And three-quarters of the way down that sheet, a figure, a broken curve, something coming down through it; and beside it, struck through with a single line of red, a name.

“The Order of the Lost Angel,” she said.

The glass stayed clear.

“Struck from the roll,” she said. “There was a note in the margin. I copied it. Struck for the emptying of the city at—and then a name I couldn’t read, the ink had gone—and for desertion of the field.” She was quiet a moment. “I thought it was two charges. I think now it may have been the same man.”

“The knight,” said the Seal-Bearer.

“Or his order. And him the one who ran.”

The Keeper had turned to look at her.

“The angel was Aveh’s,” the Keeper said. “Everyone in Heugen knows that. The toe, and the water. The Veiled One.”

“Yes,” said Sehra, and the glass stayed clear. “The angel was. The order wasn’t. They had their own.” She looked up at the salt. “He deserted his own angel, and knelt down in the city they’d emptied, and asked for something else. And something else answered him.”

Nobody spoke for a while. On the far side of the vault, Hadde was sitting on the glass floor with her bound wrists in her lap and two of the grey soldier’s men standing over her, and she had raised her head when Sehra said the name of the order and was looking at her now, mild and attentive, as a teacher looks at a pupil who has got halfway to an answer.

“You knew,” Sehra said to her.

“Everyone of ours knows,” said Hadde. “We came with them. When they came south. We came behind the order, the way we come behind every army, to see what was left after.” She tilted her head. “And what was left was a well, and a man who’d set down everything he’d done in it. And then another man. And then a whole city of them. We’ve never had such a harvest, before or since.”

The glass stayed clear. Sehra watched it stay clear and felt something cold go down her back.

“It doesn’t cloud for her,” she said.

“No,” said the Keeper. “It wouldn’t. That’s the worst of them. They never lie.”

It was the grey soldier who said it in the end.

He had been standing at the edge of the glass with his men since they came down, saying nothing, with his square face set hard and his eyes on Hadde. Now he turned round to the rest of them and said, too loudly, so that it rang off the salt:

“So who’s going to judge this?”

They all looked at him.

“Somebody’s got to,” he said. “Look at us. There’s her”—Hadde—“who killed a woman on the wall stairs and says so. There’s a city up there falling into its own wells. There’s an army at the gate with a list of six hundred people, and the woman who copied the list standing here, and one of the six hundred standing next to her holding hands with a—” He looked at the girl and stopped. “With a child. There’s me, who came down here to drown it all, and I don’t know any more if I should have. Somebody’s got to say what’s to be done. Who’s the judge?”

Everyone turned, as if a rope had pulled them, to the Seal-Bearer.

She stood in her heavy black gown on the glass floor and looked back at them.

“I am a judge of the Covenant,” she said. “Of the bench at Galanina. My writ runs in every court under the Covenant’s protection.” She looked round the salt and the glass and the upward water. “This is not a court under the Covenant’s protection. This is the oldest room in a city that has refused the Covenant for two hundred years. I have no standing here.”

The glass stayed clear.

“Then you,” the soldier said to the Keeper.

“I keep wells,” said the Keeper. “I don’t judge. I never have. I ask one question at the gate and I believe the answer.”

The glass stayed clear.

“The Speaker,” the soldier said. “He speaks for the Council.”

Ilvane was beside her. She had picked up her lamp.

“Now, little sister,” Ilvane said to Hadde, quite calmly, through the roar of the water. “While they’re all talking.”

And Hadde knelt and put her hands down into the hole in the floor, into the dark water under the glass, and began to draw.

Under the floor, far down, the pale thing turned, and turned, and began to rise.

Chapter 26 The second surfacing

“Up,” said the Keeper. “All of you. Now.”

Sehra heard it through the roar of the water. She was on her knees on the glass floor, she did not remember falling, with the writ-case under her and her arms round it, and the floor was heaving under her slowly, like the deck of a barge in a swell, and through it, far down in the dark, the pale thing was turning and rising.

She got up. Davo was beside her. He had the furled flag in one hand and her arm in the other, and he was pulling.

“Notary. The stair.”

The vault was full of water. Not standing; it was climbing. It came up through the burst panes and the cracks in the floor and went up the salt petals in ropes and sheets and fell back from the roof in a cold heavy rain, and the lamps were going out one after another, hissing, and in the light of the ones that were left the faces of the people in the vault came and went: the engineers at the mouth of the shaft, pushing each other up it; the grey soldier hauling one of his fallen men to his feet; the Prelate standing quite still in the middle of the floor with water pouring over him, looking down through the glass at what was coming up. The Speaker had Isaut by the arm. She had the girl. They were going to the shaft.

On the far side of the floor there was nobody. Where Hadde had knelt at her little hole there was only the hole, and the bead of wax, and the water going down into it in a smooth bright tongue. Ilvane’s lamp was gone.

“Prelate,” said the Seal-Bearer.

He looked up. The old woman was at the foot of the stair with her black gown streaming, and she held out her hand to him as she held it out from the bench to a witness who could not find the step, and after a moment he crossed the heaving glass and took it.

“Keeper,” said Sehra. “Keeper, come on.”

The Keeper had not moved.

She was standing over the hole in the floor where Hadde had been, with her feet planted on either side of it and the water pouring past her into the dark, and she had put down her lamp, and she was looking down. Not at the hole. Through the glass, at the thing below.

“Go up,” she said again. She did not look round. “I’m staying.”

“You can’t—”

“I can.” The Keeper turned her head then, and Sehra saw her face in the light of the last lamps, broad and wet and perfectly calm. “It eats what’s been put down and never answered for. That’s what it is. That’s all it is. Two hundred years of it. Everything anybody ever gave this water without a word said about it.” She looked down again. “I never put anything down. Not one thing. Every face that ever came to my gate, I’ve kept. It can’t eat me. I’m the only thing in this whole city it can’t eat.”

The glass stayed clear. In the middle of everything, with half the panes burst and the water roaring, the glass round the Keeper stayed clear.

“Go up,” said the Keeper. “Tell them to hold their hearing. Tell them I said so.” Her voice softened, a little. “Tell Mirrah I’m sorry I refused her. It was the right thing. I’m still sorry.”

“She’s Isaut now,” said Sehra.

“Is she.” The Keeper’s face moved. It was nearly a smile. “Tell Isaut, then.”

Davo was pulling at her arm. Sehra let him pull. She went backwards across the heaving floor to the mouth of the shaft with the writ-case against her chest, watching the Keeper, and the Keeper stood over the hole with the water rushing past her feet and the pale thing coming up through the dark below, and as Sehra reached the stair she heard her begin to speak.

She was not speaking to them. She was speaking down, through the glass, in her flat cool voice, the voice that said slowly at the gate.

“A man with a cup he wouldn’t put down,” the Keeper said. “Upriver. Sunburnt one side. He chose the name Oren, and he gave his third shell at the gate.” A breath. “A woman off a salt barge with a baby and no husband. She took the name Ash.” A breath. “A girl wet to the neck on a raft of barrels, eleven years ago, at the end of the flood. I had to hold the cup for her. She couldn’t stop shaking.”

Under the glass, the pale thing faltered.

Sehra saw it. She was on the bottom step of the shaft with Davo’s hand in her collar, and she looked back through the falling water and saw the great slow coil far down in the dark check, and turn, and turn back, as a fish turns from a hand put into the water.

“A man who’d been a soldier somewhere,” said the Keeper, “with two fingers off his left hand. A woman with a burn on her wrist from a ferry lamp. A boy who’d stolen a horse. An old man who’d sold one mule to four people.” Her voice did not change. “I remember all of you. I held the cup. You came. Somebody saw you come.”

Then Davo pulled, hard, and Sehra was on the stair, going up, round and round, with her hand on the wet wall and the case on her back and the roar of the water falling away below, and the Keeper’s voice going on under it, low and flat and steady, saying one face after another into the dark.

They came out through the green door into the grey before dawn, and the city was moving.

Sehra did not understand it at first. She came up the water stair behind the engineers, wet to the skin, and out through the Keeper’s house with its nine doors, and down the steps to the basin, and stood there, and the ground under her feet was moving. Not shaking, as it had shaken under the stones. Moving. A long slow swell, like the river under a moored boat, that went under the basin from the Gate Quarter toward the wall, and lifted the stones of the square a finger’s breadth as it passed, and let them down.

The basin was empty.

The water that had stood trembling a hand’s breadth from its lip for nine days was gone. There was only the stone bowl of it, and the steps going down at its corners into nothing, and at the bottom a black hole the width of a cart, and out of the hole a sound like breathing.

And across the city, the wells were standing.

She saw them from the steps, over the low roofs of the Gate Quarter: columns of water, a dozen of them, two dozen, white in the grey light, standing up out of the squares and lanes of Heugen as the columns had stood out of the broken wells in the orchards, as high as the drying towers had been, and quite still. Shapes moved in them. Faces, and hands, and doorways, and a woman bending to a well with a jar.

On the roofs, everywhere, there were people. They had climbed up in the night when the ground began to move, and they were standing on the roofs and the balconies and the wall-walk in their nightclothes, with children in their arms and blankets round them, watching the columns stand, and nobody was making a sound.

Then the swell came back under the city, the other way, from the wall toward the Gate Quarter. Sehra felt it lift the step under her feet and set it down. And under the city, a long way down, something very large turned over.

The columns fell.

All of them, at once, as if somebody had cut a string. The water came down out of the grey air into the squares and the lanes with a roar that went on and on, and ran in the streets ankle-deep, knee-deep, clear as glass, and poured down the steps of the basin and into the black hole at the bottom of it, and was gone. The swell went out under the wall toward the river, and the river heaved once along its whole length below the city, from the upper chain to the lower, so that the moored barges lifted on the chain and dropped, and then lay still.

The ground stopped moving.

On the roofs, slowly, people began to sit down.

Sehra stood on the basin steps until the light came.

The others were round her. The engineers, the grey soldier and his men, the Covenant soldiers sitting on the steps with their heads in their hands. The Seal-Bearer on the kerb of the empty basin with her wet gown heaped round her. The Prelate a little way off, standing alone, looking at nothing. The Speaker, with his soaked veil, sitting on the bottom step with his elbows on his knees. Isaut beside him, and the girl in her lap, awake, with her dark eyes on the black hole at the bottom of the basin.

The Keeper had not come up.

Sehra looked at the green door. It stood open. Nobody came through it.

“She’s holding it,” said Isaut. She had not taken her eyes off the hole. “Down there. That’s why it stopped.”

“For how long?”

“I don’t know.” Isaut’s arm tightened round the girl. “As long as she can remember them.”

From beyond the wall there came a sound Sehra knew: the long flat note of a Covenant horn, sounding the stand-to, once and then again. In the Reed Fields the Host was waking. In a few hours Cruciant would be on the bank with his slate, looking at a wall with a gap in it and a city that had stopped moving, and the four engines would be wound, and he would have nine days left of his ten.

She stood up. The writ-case was on her back, on its red strap with her own hole punched in it. She put her hand on it and felt the Writ of Entry inside, gone soft with water, and inside that the scrap of paper with eleven names, and under them both the whole long roll of the schedule.

“Davo,” she said.

He was beside her at once, with the flag.

“I need to go out to the camp,” she said. “To the Marshal. Now. Before he winds the engines.” She looked down at Isaut on the step, and the girl, and the Speaker, and the Seal-Bearer on the kerb, and the empty basin with the hole at the bottom of it where the Keeper was saying faces into the dark. “We need a day.”

“One day?” said Isaut.

“One,” said Sehra. “To hold a hearing in the square. If he’ll give it to me.”

“Will he?”

Sehra thought of Cruciant on the axle of his engine in the dark, with his flask, saying I sat there and I admired it like a boy looking at a bridge, and should it be won?, and ten days, unless somebody gives me a reason.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I’m going to ask.”

Part Five The Hearing

Chapter 27 One day

Cruciant was on the bank with his slate when they came out of the gap in the wall, and the rider from Ostrin was standing beside him.

Sehra saw the horse first. It was a post-horse from the Host’s dispatch line, lathered to the girth, standing with its head down among the reeds below the camp, and beside it the rider she had paid a mark of her own money nine days ago, mud to the thighs, with a flat oilcloth packet under his arm. He was talking to the Marshal. When he saw her coming across the rubble at the foot of the breach, with Davo behind her carrying the flag, he stopped talking and held up the packet.

“Notary,” he said. “From the court at Ostrin. The clerk said to put it into your hand and nobody else’s.”

She took it. It was heavier than she expected, and tied with cord, and sealed with a seal she did not know, a small one, brown wax, the seal of a clerk and not a court. She held it and did not open it.

Cruciant was looking at her. Past her, at the wall, with its gap and its slope of rubble going down into the river, and above it the roofs of the city, crowded with people sitting in the early sun.

“I watched it from here,” he said. “All night. The ground. The columns.” He looked at his slate. There was nothing written on it. “I had the engines wound at the second bell. I was going to put stones into the Gate Quarter while it was moving, because a city that’s moving is a city that can’t hold a barricade. And then the columns came up, and I stood here with my hand up to give the order, and I couldn’t.” He lowered the slate. “I don’t know why. I stood here like a fool with my hand up for an hour and watched them, and then they fell, and the whole river jumped, and I thought: whatever that is, I don’t want to put a stone into it.”

“It was something under the city,” Sehra said. “Something very old. The Keeper’s holding it.”

“The Keeper.”

“She stayed below. She’s holding it down.”

“How?”

“By remembering everyone who ever came to her gate,” said Sehra. “Out loud. One at a time.”

Cruciant looked at her for a long while.

“Where’s the Prelate?” he said.

“In the city. On the steps of the basin. He came up with us.” She hesitated. “Something happened to him down there, Marshal. In the vault. There’s a room where nobody can lie. He said he’d never doubted we were right to come. And the glass broke.”

Cruciant said nothing. He looked at the wall, and at the roofs, and at the people sitting on them in the sun.

“Marshal,” said Sehra. “I need a day.”

She told him all of it, there on the bank, with the post-horse cropping the reeds and Davo standing a little way off with the flag. She told him about the carving with the faint city in it and the Seal-Bearer kneeling in the water. About the masks on their cords, and the Sacrarium, and the words cut in the salt. About the woman named Isaut Vell, who had stood in the oldest room in Heugen and heard her deed read true and asked for a hearing, and about the Seal-Bearer’s question at Sehra’s examination twenty-one years ago, and the answer. About Hadde, and Wenna on the wall stairs, and Ilvane with her soup and her offer, and the channel cut in the floor of the vault.

He listened without interrupting. When she had finished he was quiet a long time.

“A hearing,” he said.

“In the square inside the gate. Today. With the whole city to witness it. I’ll call witnesses and take their words down and read them back, in public, so that it’s done and on the record and nobody can say it wasn’t.” She held up the oilcloth packet. “And I have the record from Ostrin. The complaint she brought. The one they refused.”

“And then what?”

“And then whatever the hearing finds.”

“The Prelate won’t sanction it.”

“I’m not asking the Prelate,” Sehra said. “I’m asking you. Not to wind the engines. Not to send anyone through the gap. For one day. That’s all.”

“One day,” said Cruciant. “I’ve got nine.”

“Then you’ll still have eight.”

He looked at her. Then, unexpectedly, the corner of his mouth went up.

“You said once you didn’t know,” he said. “On the axle. When I asked you if it should be won.”

“I still don’t know.”

“No. But you’ve found something you want instead.” He tucked the slate under his arm. “That’s a reason. I told you I’d wait for one.” He turned and called up the bank to the engine crews, not loud: “Stand down. Unwind them. Nobody touches a skein until I say.” And to a sergeant: “Pass the word along the lines. No boats. No one through the gap. Anyone who goes into the city today goes unarmed and as a witness, or not at all.” He turned back to Sehra. “One day.”

“Thank you.”

“I’m coming,” said Cruciant. “Unarmed. As a witness. I want to see this.” He looked at the packet in her hand. “Open that. You’ve been holding it like it’s hot.”

She opened it on the tailboard of the record-cart, with Cruciant standing by and Davo holding the flag.

There were three sheets inside. The first was a letter from the clerk of the Ostrin court, short, in a cramped and careful hand that she knew at once: the hand of the line in a different ink at the bottom of the entry. To the Notary of the Host, greeting. You ask for the plaint of Isaut Vell. It was refused and refused plaints are not kept. I kept it. I have kept it eleven years in a box under my bed, because I wrote it down for her and I believed it and I could not throw it away. I send it now. I ask you to give it to her if she lives. Benedek, clerk.

The second sheet was the plaint.

It was not long. It was in the same cramped hand, taken down from the girl’s mouth, and it said that Isaut Vell, apprentice glass-cutter, aged twenty-four years, bound to the glazier Ansel Garrow for seven years, of which five were served, complained against her master that he beat her with the cutter’s iron when the work went wrong and when it went right, and that he had kept back her wages for three years, and that he had told her he would break her hands if she spoke of it to anyone, so that she could never work glass again. She asked the court to hear her and to release her from her indenture and to make him pay what he owed. She named a witness: his son, Teodor, who has seen it.

At the bottom, in the clerk’s hand: Refused by the court, for want of standing: an apprentice may bring no claim against her master during her term. The witness named was sent for and declined to come.

The third sheet was a single line, in the same hand, on a scrap of paper torn from something else.

The son was in the workshop the night of the death. He told the watch he saw nothing. I did not believe him either.

Sehra read all three twice. Then she put them back in the oilcloth, carefully, and put the oilcloth in the writ-case beside the schedule, and closed the case.

“Well?” said Cruciant.

“She asked them first,” Sehra said. “She asked them to stop it before she did anything. They said she had no right to ask.” She stood up. “And the master had a son. Who saw.”

“Where’s the son?”

Sehra thought of the Speaker on the glass floor, with his folded hands and the soaked silk on his face, saying No, and the pane beside him going white from edge to edge like milk poured into water. She thought of the old seams of burn across his fingertips, and a glazier’s son lifting a pane out of the annealing oven bare-handed before he was tall enough to reach the bench.

“I think he’s going to tell us,” she said. “At the hearing. He said he would.”

They went back into the city at noon, through the gap, unarmed: Sehra with the writ-case, and Davo with the white flag unfurled, and Cruciant in his shirtsleeves with his slate under his arm, and behind them, because the word had gone along the lines and some of them had asked, forty soldiers of the vanguard without their swords, and Brother Kael with his jar and his rags, and a file of the barefoot penitents.

The Prelate met them at the second gate.

He had changed his clothes. Somebody in the city had given him a plain grey shirt and he had put it on, and he stood under the arch of the gate with his clean hands folded, mild and narrow and grey, and waited for them to come up to him.

“Marshal,” he said. “You’ve unwound the engines.”

“For a day.”

“Without my order.”

“Yes,” said Cruciant.

The Prelate looked at him. Then at Sehra, and the writ-case, and Davo with the flag, and the forty unarmed soldiers and the penitents behind them.

“The Notary means to hold a hearing,” he said. “In a city outside the Covenant. With no judge and no court. For a woman named on my schedule.”

“Yes, Prelate,” said Sehra.

“It has no standing in any law I serve.”

“No, Prelate.”

He was silent for a long while. Sehra watched his face, and thought of it on the glass floor, looking at the white crazed pane beside him with the crack running across it; and she saw, now, that the face had not closed again quite as well as he believed.

“I’ll attend,” the Prelate said at last. “As a witness. I’ll say nothing.” He paused. “I find I’d like to know what it finds.”

He turned and walked ahead of them through the gate toward the square.

Chapter 28 The gate square

They set the table at the edge of the empty basin, where the queue had stood.

It was the Council’s trestle, the long one from under the violet awning; somebody had carried it down through the streets in the morning and set it up on the stones with its back to the green door and its face to the square. There was one chair behind it, and the notary sat in it, with the leather case open on the table in front of her and a clean sheet of vellum and a reed and an inkpot, and a lamp, though it was noon. Isaut did not know why there was a lamp. Then she saw that it was the Keeper’s, the one from the shelf by the water stair, and that somebody had lit it and set it at the end of the table, and she understood.

There was no awning. There were no masks. The seven of the Council sat on a bench at one side of the table, in plain clothes, with their faces bare, and Isaut was surprised by how ordinary they were: an old woman, a man with a soldier’s square face, a lotus-priestess, a dyer with blue hands, three others she might have passed in the market every day for eleven years without knowing. On the other side sat the old judge in black, and the grey man with the silver halo in his borrowed shirt, and the Marshal with his slate on his knee. The boy with the flag stood behind the notary’s chair.

And the square was full.

It was fuller than it had been for the reading of the Writ. People stood in the square and in the lanes going into it and on every balcony and roof round it, and on the wall-walk above the river gate, and on the steps of the Keeper’s house, all the way up to the green door. Some of them had come straight from their roofs and were still in their nightclothes with blankets round them. Isaut saw Brannoc, and the veil-seller, and the bath-mistress from Copper Lane with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder. She saw Aun the warden with her crate of cups. She saw the woman from the laundry, the tall one with the burn on her wrist, standing at the mouth of the lane with her basket on her hip, and beside her an old man with a fox mask pushed up on top of his head, his arm in a sling, alive. She saw the thin polite people from the second flight of the Petal Steps, in a row at the back, quite still.

She did not see Ilvane. She looked.

The girl was holding her hand.

“This is not a court,” the notary said.

She did not stand. She sat at the table with her hands flat on the vellum and spoke in her dry quiet voice, and the square was so silent that Isaut thought everybody in it could hear her, even on the wall.

“I’m a notary of the Covenant. I have no standing to judge anyone in this city, and nor has anyone at this table. What we’re holding is a hearing. The kind that was held before there were courts. In the field, with the people of the place standing round it to see that it was done.” She looked at Isaut. “A woman named on the Covenant’s schedule has asked to be heard before she answers to it. She asked a court for that once, eleven years ago, and was refused. So we’re going to hear her now. I’m going to write down what’s said and read it back to you. When it’s written and read, it will have happened, and it can’t be refused again.”

She took a sheet from the case.

“I’ll read the charge first,” she said. “Then the complaint she brought before it. In that order.”

She read the charge. Isaut had heard it twice now, once from Wenna’s copy in her room and once in the vault where the glass weighed it, and she stood and heard it a third time in the square with the city round her: Isaut Vell, of Ostrin on the Paladian. For the death of her master, by a glass knife, in his own workshop. The square was silent. Nobody looked away from her. She found she did not want them to.

Then the notary took out the oilcloth packet, and unfolded the sheet inside it, and read the complaint.

Isaut had never heard it. She had not known it was kept. She stood in the square and listened to her own words, the words of a woman of twenty-four she did not remember being, taken down eleven years ago in a clerk’s cramped hand: that her master beat her with the cutter’s iron when the work went wrong and when it went right. That he had kept back her wages three years. That he had told her he would break her hands if she spoke of it, so that she could never work glass again.

Her hands moved.

She did not move them. They moved on their own, at her sides. They closed. Both of them, at once, into fists, hard, the thumbs folded inside the fingers, the way a person closes their hands to hide them. She looked down at them. The left one was holding the girl’s hand so tightly that the girl had gone still. She opened it.

She named a witness, the notary read. His son, Teodor, who has seen it.

Refused by the court, for want of standing. The witness named was sent for and declined to come.

The notary set the sheet down on the table.

“That’s the record,” she said. “As it was kept. A clerk at Ostrin kept it under his bed for eleven years because he couldn’t bear to throw it away.” She looked out at the square. “The complaint names a witness. Is he here?”

The Speaker stood up from the bottom step of the Keeper’s house, where he had been sitting all morning with his elbows on his knees.

He came across the square to the table. He walked stiffly, one knee bad. The crowd opened for him and closed behind him. He stopped in front of the table, a little to one side, so that he was not between the notary and Isaut, and he stood there with his hands at his sides and the grey silk veil, dry now, hanging from its band at his brow to his collarbone.

Then he reached up and unfastened the band, and took the veil off, and folded it, and laid it on the table beside the Keeper’s lamp.

He had an ordinary face. Isaut had expected, without knowing she expected it, something terrible behind the silk: a scar, a ruin, a face nobody could look at. It was only a man’s face, a long-jawed face of about forty, pale from nine years under the veil, with deep lines beside the mouth and grey coming into the hair at the temples. There was an old burn on one cheekbone, small and white. His eyes were dark, and he looked at her.

She knew him.

Not from memory. There was nothing; the water had it all. She knew him the way she had known her own name was a river name, a little old-fashioned; the way she knew where to stop the tail of an r. She knew the shape of him in a doorway. She knew it in her shoulders, which had come up round her ears, and in her hands, which had closed again.

“My name is Teodor Garrow,” he said. “I am the son of Ansel Garrow, glazier, of Ostrin. I’m the witness she named.”

The square was silent. On the bench, the seven of the Council sat with their bare faces turned to him. The grey man with the halo had not moved. The old judge in black had closed her eyes.

“I saw it,” Teodor said. “All of it. The iron. Three years of it. I was in the workshop every day. I was seventeen when she came to be bound and I was twenty-two when it ended, and I saw every day of it, and I said nothing. He beat me too, when I was smaller. I thought that was what masters did. I thought that was what fathers did.” His voice was quite steady. “When she went to the court, she asked me to come with her and say what I’d seen. I said I would. The morning of the hearing I didn’t go. I sat in the back of the workshop and listened to the bells and didn’t go. The court sent a man to fetch me and I told him I was sick.”

The notary was writing. The reed went steadily across the vellum.

“The night after they refused her, he found out she’d gone to them,” Teodor said. “Somebody told him in the tavern. He came home with the iron. He said he was going to do what he’d told her he’d do. Her hands.” He stopped. “She was at the bench. She had a glass knife in her hand. She’d been cutting with it. He came at her with the iron, and she—” He stopped again, and this time it was longer. “I was in the doorway. I saw it. It was quick.”

Isaut could not feel her hands at all.

“I told the watch I’d seen nothing,” Teodor said. “I told them I’d been asleep upstairs. Before the watch came, I took her down to the river. It was the end of the flood and the water was in the yard. There were barrels in the yard, empty ones, for the glass. I tied four of them together with rope and put her on them and pushed them out into the current. She was shaking so hard she couldn’t hold on. I tied her hands to the rope.” He looked down at his own hands. “That’s the last thing I did for her. I tied her hands to some barrels and pushed her into a flood.”

“Wet to the neck,” said Isaut. She heard herself say it. “On a raft of barrels.”

“Yes,” said Teodor.

The lamp burned at the end of the table. Nobody in the square made a sound.

“I came here two years after,” Teodor said. “I’d heard about the gate. Everybody on the river had. I came down on a salt barge and walked up to the basin and the Keeper held out the cup and asked me what I wanted to set down.” He looked at the empty basin, at the black hole at the bottom of it. “I said: the watching. Five years of watching and saying nothing. And the morning I didn’t go to the court. And the doorway. And she said all of it?, the way she says it to everyone, and I said—” He stopped. “I said no. Not all of it. Not her. I wanted to forget what I’d done. I didn’t want to forget that she’d been there. It seemed to me that if I forgot her too, there’d be nobody left in the world who knew that she’d asked.”

“And the Keeper let you,” said the notary.

“The Keeper said it was the first time anybody had ever told her no at the gate. She held the cup anyway. She said I’d asked, and that was all she weighed.” He was quiet a moment. “So I kept her. And a year after that, I was walking through the Mask Market and I saw her at a stall, cutting a name into glass for a newcomer. And she looked up, and looked straight through me, because she’d asked for all of it. And I couldn’t speak to her. That’s the law here. You don’t ask anyone’s past. You don’t tell anyone theirs. I’d lived by it for a year, and it had let me live, and I couldn’t break it on her.” He touched the folded veil on the table. “So I put this on, so that she wouldn’t ever look at me and wonder. And I asked the Council for work, and they gave me the Speaker’s, because I could read the Covenant’s law and nobody else here could. And for nine years I’ve walked past her stall and not looked.”

The notary had stopped writing. She was looking at him.

“You signed my receipt Speaker-of-Petals,” she said. “You said it was the name you could be called to account by.”

“It was,” said Teodor. “Until today.”

The Speaker’s chair at the Council stood empty. Teodor Garrow mended windows in the Gate Quarter now, with his veil folded away in a drawer somewhere, and when he passed Isaut in the street he nodded to her, and she nodded back. It was not more than that. It was not nothing.

The boat was a salt barge, and the woman who got off it was perhaps twenty, thin, with a bundle on her back and the dust of the river road on her, and she came up the water steps slowly, as they all came, looking at the basin and the lotuses on every doorpost and the masks on their cords across the lanes, and stopped at the edge of the basin and looked at the water.

Aun put down her pear and got up and went to her.

Isaut could not hear what they said at first. She saw Aun speak, and the woman shake her head, and Aun speak again, and the woman look at the tray of cups and then at the old judge at her table and then back at Aun. Then the woman said something, and Isaut heard it, because the woman’s voice had gone up the way voices do when they are frightened and trying not to be.

“What happens to it?”

Aun looked at her.

“What I give,” the woman said. “If I drink. Where does it go? Does it just—stop? Is it gone?”

Isaut saw Aun’s face change. Not much. It was the face of somebody who has been asked a question she has waited all winter to be asked and now finds she wants to answer carefully.

“No,” Aun said. “It’s not gone. Nothing’s ever gone, here. It goes down. Under the city. And the water comes up to fill the place it left, so the well never fails.” She held up one of the cups and turned it over so the woman could see the lotus pressed into the base, and the small round ring of water where the angel’s toe had touched the ground. “It’s kept. For you. Nobody else can have it. Nobody can read it or fetch it up or take it off you. And if you ever want it back, one day, you can come here and ask, and you’ll get it.” She paused. “One woman did.”

The woman looked at the cup.

“Did it hurt?” she said. “Getting it back?”

Aun glanced up the steps, at Isaut standing at the top of them in her grit-stained apron, and back.

“Ask her,” she said. “She’s the one.”

The woman looked up the steps at Isaut. Isaut looked back. She thought about saying something; she did not know what she would say. She lifted her hand a little, from her side, and let it fall.

The woman turned back to Aun.

“Before I drink,” she said. “Is there—do I have to say what it is? Somebody told me on the barge you have to say.”

“You don’t have to say anything you don’t want to,” said Aun. “But I have to ask you three things. That’s the law here now. It’s the old law. It was always the law; we just stopped asking.” She set the cup down on the table and looked at the woman, and asked them, slowly, one at a time, in the voice that said slowly at the gate, which was not the Keeper’s voice and not yet quite Aun’s own, but was getting there.

“What will you set down?”

“Is it yours to set down?”

“Do you ask it?”

The woman stood at the edge of the basin with her bundle on her back and the three questions in front of her, and thought. Isaut watched her think. It took a long time. Nobody hurried her. The old judge at the second table had put down her reed and folded her hands and was waiting, her face like a closed book, and Aun was waiting, and the newcomers who had come up the water steps behind the woman off the salt barge were waiting, and nobody said a word.

“Yes,” the woman said at last. “It’s mine. I ask it.” She looked at the cup. “Not all of it, though. Can I keep some?”

“You can keep whatever you like,” said Aun. “Tell me what.”

The woman began to tell her, low, so that only Aun could hear.

Isaut went back up through the Gate Quarter to the Mask Market.

The alley was opening. Brannoc was setting out his thimbles of lotus-ink and complaining to the veil-seller about the price of gum. Old Pell was at his bench with his arm out of the sling at last, gluing the other ear on a lacquered fox. A child ran through the alley with a hoop, and nobody shouted at her. At the far end, where the market opened onto the water steps, a barge was being unloaded, and every time a sack came off it the men at the bottom counted aloud.

There was somebody waiting at her stall.

He was sitting on the stool across the bench, with his hands folded in his lap, very straight and very thin, in good clothes made for somebody larger, with his white hair combed with great care. He had a beautiful smile. He smiled it at her as she came in under the awning.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I’m sorry to ask. I was told you might have a name to spare.”

Isaut sat down across the bench from him.

“I might,” she said. “It takes a while.”

She set out the tray. Thirty lozenges of river glass on their felt, the color of lotus water at dusk, cut over the winter, a little thicker than the old ones because the old ones had chipped. She took down the cedar board from its peg and turned it so he could read the names other people had chosen, and moved her finger slowly down the first column, because a man who has given his name away twice often loses his place.

He read the whole of the first column and the whole of the second and looked up.

“They’re all other people’s,” he said.

“You can take one if you like it. Nobody owns them.”

“I’d rather not.” He looked down at the tray. “I had a good one, I think. Once. I don’t remember it.”

“No,” said Isaut. “But you could ask for it. At the gate. It’s kept.”

He looked at her for a long while with his sweet, tired face.

“Is it?” he said.

“Yes.”

“Even the ones I gave to the Sister on the Steps?”

Isaut thought about the dome under the customs house, and the bundles of blank charms on their cords that the Seal-Bearer’s clerks had carried up in sacks in the winter and laid out on tables in the Council chamber, hundreds of them, and the slow work, still going on, of matching each one to the binding-book and the person it had been cut for.

“We’re looking,” she said. “It’ll take a long time. Some of them we might never find.” She picked up a lozenge from the third row, where the glass had come out clearest, and held it up to the light from the alley. “But you could have a new one while you wait. To be going on with. And when we find the old one, you can choose.”

He thought about it.

“Something short,” he said at last. “Hard at the front. I think I came from upriver.”

She fixed the lozenge in the jaw of the vise with a scrap of leather between, and wet the copper wheel from the little pot of oil and grit at her elbow, and set the treadle going with her foot. The wheel sang. It was a quiet sound when it was going well. She listened for it while she worked, as she would have listened for somebody on the stair, and it went well, and her hand knew where to stop.

That evening, when the stall was shut and the wheel was oiled and the light was going gold off the river, she climbed the stair on Fig Court and unlocked her door.

On the nail by the frame, where she would see it every time she came in, there were two charms now. One was a lozenge of river glass a little greener than the ones she cut, from some other batch, some other river, with Mirrah cut into it in a hand that was not hers, and a fine crack running from the tail of the r that went a fraction too far. The other was from the third row of her own tray, where the glass came out clearest, with Isaut cut into it in her own hand, a little below the surface, so that the name seemed to be under water. She had drilled the hole for it in the winter, slowly, because a hole was where glass broke.

Inside the frame, the little brass bell that Wenna had hung there hung quiet.

She touched both charms, as she did every evening, and went in, and shut the door, and remembered to eat.
